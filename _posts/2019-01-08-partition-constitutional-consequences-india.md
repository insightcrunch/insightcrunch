Partition and the Indian Constitution are usually shelved in different rooms of the founding library. Partition goes to the historians, who narrate the Radcliffe Line, the trains, the refugee columns and the killing fields of Punjab and Bengal between August and October 1947. The Constitution goes to the lawyers, who narrate a serene two-year seminar in the Constitution Hall in New Delhi, where the Constituent Assembly debated federalism, rights and citizenship as if the country outside the chamber were holding its breath in an orderly way. The shelving is convenient and it is false. The same men sat in both rooms, often on the same day. Vallabhbhai Patel chaired the committee that advised on minorities in the morning of a week in which, as Home Minister and Minister for States, he was also managing the accession of princely states and the first reports of communal massacres. Jawaharlal Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution in December 1946 and then, as Prime Minister of an interim government from 2 September 1946, spent the summer of 1947 negotiating the transfer of power that divided the subcontinent his Assembly was supposed to constitute. The Indian Constitution was not written despite Partition; it was written through Partition. That sentence is the claim this article defends, provision by provision, against the strongest alternative reading of the founding.

## Partition and the Indian Constitution: One Story, Not Two

The conventional treatment separates event from text. In the conventional treatment, Partition is the context of the founding: the noisy, bloody background against which a calmer intellectual achievement unfolded. The Constitution, on this telling, would have looked much the same without the division of British India into two dominions on 15 August 1947, because its real sources were the Government of India Act of 1935, the constitutional traditions of the Commonwealth, the American and Irish examples the Constitutional Adviser B.N. Rau collected on his tour of the autumn of 1947, and the social programme of the national movement reaching back to the Karachi Resolution of March 1931. Context explains atmosphere. It does not explain clauses.

The record refuses the separation. Between the Assembly's first sitting on 9 December 1946 and the adoption of the Constitution on 26 November 1949, the chamber changed its membership, its mandate, its federal blueprint and its working assumptions about the people it was constituting, and every one of those changes bears the date of a Partition decision. The June 1947 plan announced by Viceroy Louis Mountbatten on 3 June 1947 split the Assembly itself: members elected from territories that became Pakistan ceased to sit, and the body shrank from 389 members to 299. The Union Powers Committee, which in April 1947 had still imagined residuary powers resting with autonomous units under the Cabinet Mission scheme, reported on 5 July 1947 in favour of a strong centre, because the federation it had been designing for a united India no longer existed. The citizenship clauses debated in August 1949 were written around two administrative dates created by the migration itself, 1 March 1947 and 19 July 1948. The language formula adopted on 14 September 1949 carried the scar of two provinces, Punjab and Bengal, that Partition had cut in half. A context does not leave fingerprints of that kind on that many clauses. A determinant does.

Yasmin Khan, in The Great Partition of 2007, restored the human scale of the event: the estimates of displacement that historians work with run from ten to fifteen million people crossing the new borders in both directions, and the estimates of the dead run from several hundred thousand to figures above a million, with the uncertainty itself a measure of how completely the state's machinery of counting had broken down in the autumn of 1947. Khan's account is the strongest of the narrative treatments, and this article engages it as the narrative baseline. Yet even Khan's framing keeps Partition in the register of catastrophe and consequence, an event that happened to a founding that proceeded alongside it. The claim defended here is stronger and narrower. Four families of provisions in the Constitution, the security and emergency provisions, the citizenship architecture of Articles 5 to 11, the language settlement of Article 343 and its companion clauses, and the minority-protection provisions of Articles 25 to 30, were calibrated against the post-Partition reality in ways that can be traced clause by clause in the Constituent Assembly Debates and in the committee record. Where the trace exists, the determinant claim holds. Where the trace runs cold, this article says so, because the structural inheritances of the founding, the parliamentary system, the basic federal frame, the independent judiciary, plainly predate Partition and were not its product.

### What did Nehru pledge in the Tryst with Destiny speech on 14 August 1947?

On the night of 14 to 15 August 1947 Nehru pledged the sovereign Assembly to the service of India and her people, naming the tasks as the ending of poverty and ignorance and disease and inequality of opportunity. The pledge was made for a divided India, by a government whose first instruments were refugee administration and minority assurance.

The midnight speech is usually quoted for its poetry. Its constitutional weight lies elsewhere: the man who spoke it had already accepted, in the June plan, that the India being pledged was a divided India, and the service he described would be performed by a state whose standing equipment, refugee administration, border security, minority assurance, was Partition equipment. Poetry and administration were the same speech act, and the Assembly that heard it knew the difference between the two registers had collapsed in the streets of the capital it sat in.

The four dimensions organise everything that follows. First, though, the sequence of the summer of 1947 has to be set out, because the determinant argument is a chronological argument before it is a doctrinal one: the clauses moved when the country moved, and the order of the movement is the evidence.

## The Summer of 1947: The Sequence That Reordered the Assembly

On 20 February 1947 the Attlee government in London announced that Britain would transfer power by June 1948 and sent Mountbatten to replace Archibald Wavell as Viceroy. On 3 June 1947 Mountbatten announced the plan that bears his name: British India would be partitioned, the legislatures of Bengal and Punjab would vote on dividing their provinces, referendums would be held in the North-West Frontier Province and in the Sylhet district of Assam, and power would be transferred, ahead of the announced schedule, in August 1947. The Congress leadership accepted the plan through the All India Congress Committee resolution of 14 and 15 June 1947, a resolution moved in a hall where, as the record of the debate shows, the acceptance was understood by its own movers as the price of a strong, governable centre in the India that remained. Govind Ballabh Pant, moving the resolution, argued the June plan offered the only route to a strong central government for a united Indian Union of the territories that stayed. The trade was explicit at the time. Territorial wholeness was surrendered; central authority in the remainder was the compensation. Every later clause about emergency power and federal supremacy trades on that June bargain.

The machinery then moved at a speed no constitution-maker would choose. The Bengal and Punjab legislative assemblies voted for partition of their provinces on 20 June 1947 and in the days immediately following. The Sylhet referendum of 6 and 7 July 1947 took the district to East Pakistan, with a small portion retained in Assam after the boundary award. The North-West Frontier Province referendum of July 1947, boycotted by the Khudai Khidmatgar movement of Abdul Ghaffar Khan, took the province to Pakistan. The Indian Independence Act received royal assent on 18 July 1947. Its sections created two independent dominions from 15 August 1947, ended British paramountcy over the princely states, abolished the office of Secretary of State for India, and, in section 8, vested the Constituent Assembly of each dominion with full power to make laws for that dominion, free of the disallowance and reservation controls that had fettered Indian legislatures since 1919. The Assembly that had been convened under the Cabinet Mission Plan as a constitution-making body with a limited and contested mandate became, by statute, sovereign. Partition was the legal occasion of that sovereignty. A united-India Assembly under the Cabinet Mission scheme would have worked inside a three-tier federation negotiated with a Muslim League that retained a veto at the centre. The sovereign Assembly of August 1947 existed because that scheme had collapsed and been replaced by division.

Cyril Radcliffe, a London barrister who had never previously visited India, arrived in July 1947 to chair the two boundary commissions for Bengal and Punjab, each split evenly between Congress-nominated and League-nominated judges who deadlocked on nearly every disputed district. Radcliffe drew the lines in roughly five weeks, signed the awards on 12 and 13 August 1947, and left India on 17 August 1947, the day the awards were published, two days after independence. The delay in publication, decided by Mountbatten, kept the award from shadowing the independence celebrations, and it also meant that for two days the citizens of Lahore, Amritsar, Gurdaspur and Ferozepore celebrated or mourned inside a sovereignty whose borders nobody could state. The constitutional consequence was immediate and permanent: the founding generation learned, in the first week of the state's existence, that the territory of the republic was an administrative artefact drawn in haste, and that holding it together would be a continuing act of government rather than a settled premise. The strong-centre clauses of the Constitution are unintelligible except as the written form of that lesson.

The Assembly's own composition carried the same lesson. When the Assembly reconvened after the transfer of power, the members for territories assigned to Pakistan, eighty-six of them by the usual count, had withdrawn to form the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan in Karachi. The Indian Assembly's strength stood at 299. Its committees were reconstituted. The Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal Areas, chaired by Patel, lost its Muslim League members and with them the negotiating structure for which several of its early reports had been drafted. B.N. Rau's notes from August 1947, preserved in his papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, record the Constitutional Adviser working through the reorganisation of the federal scheme that Partition had forced: the grouping provisions of the Cabinet Mission Plan were dead, the federation had to be redesigned for a smaller and more homogeneous union, and the provinces that remained had to be held by a centre whose authority no longer depended on the consent architecture of 1946. Rau's notes are the under-cited primary source of this article's argument, and they matter because they are dated. They show the redesign happening in August 1947, in direct response to the June plan, months before the Drafting Committee under B.R. Ambedkar, appointed on 29 August 1947, began converting decisions into articles.

The compression of the timetable deserves its own sentence of analysis, because compression is part of the causation. The Attlee statement of 20 February 1947 had allowed sixteen months for the transfer; the June plan allowed ten weeks. In ten weeks the Assembly lost its Pakistani members, the Congress accepted a strong centre as the consolation for division, two provinces were voted apart, two referendums were held, a boundary was commissioned, drawn and withheld, and sovereignty changed hands. Institutional choices made at that speed are not seminar choices. They are triage, and the Constitution's most distinctive clauses, its permit dates, its emergency conversions, its deferred language explosion, read as triage decisions that a slower founding would have had the leisure to soften. The softening never happened, because the Assembly's later sessions, for all their deliberative calm, worked on drafts whose load-bearing decisions the ten weeks had already taken.

Violence then supplied the second half of the education. The killings that began in Calcutta in August 1946 and in Noakhali and Bihar in the autumn of 1946 became, after August 1947, a Punjab catastrophe and a Bengal haemorrhage. Delhi itself filled with refugees in September 1947, and communal killing reached the capital where the Assembly sat; the government proclaimed emergency administration in the city and Gandhi returned to a capital under curfew. The war over Kashmir began in October 1947 when tribal raiders, backed by Pakistan, entered the princely state, and Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947, an accession V.P. Menon flew to Srinagar to obtain and Indian troops were airlifted to hold on 27 October 1947. A Constituent Assembly that debated emergency clauses in 1949 did so with members who had governed through all of this, several of them, including Home Minister Patel, carrying direct administrative responsibility for it. The debates cannot be read as hypotheticals. They were after-action reviews conducted by the officers of the action.

## Austin Against Jayal: The Disagreement This Article Adjudicates

Granville Austin published The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation in 1966, and it remains the most accomplished statement of what this article calls the institutional-autonomy reading. Austin read the founding as an achievement of institutional craftsmanship animated by a national consensus: the founders, in his account, pursued national unity and integrity, social revolution and democracy as a single seamless web, and the provisions of the text are to be explained by the logic of those commitments and by the founders' skill in working them into law. Partition appears in Austin's account as tragedy and as background pressure, and he does not deny its weight. His architecture, though, treats the constitutional choices as separable in principle from the catastrophe: the Assembly had its philosophy before August 1947, in the Objectives Resolution of January 1947 and in the committee work of the spring, and it executed that philosophy through the crisis rather than deriving the philosophy from the crisis. On Austin's reading, a historian who explains Article 352 or the citizenship clauses by pointing at Partition has confused the occasion of a provision with its reason.

Niraja Gopal Jayal, in Citizenship and Its Discontents of 2013, reads the founding from the citizenship end, and the citizenship end cannot be separated from Partition at all. Jayal's layered-citizenship argument holds that the Constitution's apparently universal citizenship was built over older, deeper strata of belonging, religious, regional and gendered, and that the founding settlement managed those strata rather than abolishing them. For the citizenship clauses the Partition context is not an occasion but the substance: Articles 5 to 11 exist in their adopted form because millions of people crossed borders in 1947 and 1948 and the text had to say, with legal precision, which of them the new republic owned. Jayal's reading generalises less readily than Austin's, and she does not claim that every clause of the Constitution is a Partition clause. Her claim is that the founding's universalism was calibrated, at the exact points where belonging was decided, by the emergency of division.

The adjudication this article defends is Jayal's side, with Austin's larger point granted and fenced. Granted: the Assembly's institutional ambitions, parliamentary government, an independent judiciary with judicial review, a rights chapter with remedies under Article 32, universal adult franchise under Article 326, were carried into the hall in 1946 and would have been recognisable in a Constitution for an undivided India. Fenced: those ambitions do not explain the adopted form of the four provision families at issue. They do not explain why the citizenship article needed a permit date of 19 July 1948. They do not explain why the federation's residuary powers migrated to the centre between April and July 1947. They do not explain why the minority safeguards were redesigned in May 1949 to strip out political reservations that the Assembly's own Advisory Committee had recommended in August 1947. They do not explain why Hindi's official-language clause carried a fifteen-year English bridge whose politics were the politics of two partitioned provinces. In each case the explanation that fits the dates, the committee record and the debate text is the Partition explanation. The determinant claim is therefore a claim about calibrations, and it is worth distinguishing the two words with care, because [the founding period that the Partition determinant shaped](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) is misread whenever calibration is collapsed into either context or total authorship. Partition did not author the Constitution. Partition set the values of its most security-sensitive, belonging-sensitive dials.

One methodological note belongs here, because the counterfactual framework governs the later sections. To call Partition a determinant is to claim that, holding the founders and their philosophy constant, a founding without Partition would have produced measurably different clauses in the four families. The evidence for such a claim cannot be a rival text that was never written. It must be the textual archaeology of the text that was written: the April 1947 committee reports that assume a united federation, the Objectives Resolution's original promise of autonomous units with residuary powers, the draft clauses that changed after June 1947, and the debate speeches in which members named the post-Partition situation as their reason. That archaeology is what the four dimension sections perform. The standard of proof is the founders' own stated reasons, in the record, at the date of decision. Where a member said the quiet part aloud, this article quotes the occasion rather than paraphrasing a motive into existence.

## Dimension One: Security, Emergency and the Memory of the Autumn of 1947

The emergency provisions of Part Eighteen of the Constitution, Articles 352 to 360, are the provision family where the Partition explanation is most often resisted, because a rival explanation lies ready to hand: the Government of India Act of 1935 contained emergency powers, section 93 governor's rule and ordinance powers among them, and the founders copied freely from the 1935 Act elsewhere. Inheritance is real here, and this article's complication section returns to it. Yet inheritance explains the existence of emergency powers, not their adopted architecture: a proclamation by the President on the ground of war, external aggression or internal disturbance, laid before Parliament, lapsing unless approved by both Houses within two months, renewable in six-month increments, accompanied by Article 358's automatic suspension of Article 19 freedoms in a war emergency and Article 359's power to suspend the enforcement of other rights. When the Assembly debated the draft emergency articles in August and September 1949, recorded in Volume IX of the Constituent Assembly Debates, the members who spoke did not reach for the 1935 Act as their justification. They reached for the previous twenty-four months.

Read the setting of that debate. The Assembly that discussed draft Article 275, the ancestor of Article 352, in 1949 sat in a capital that had itself been under emergency administration in September 1947, when refugee columns and communal killing overwhelmed the ordinary machinery of Delhi and the government governed the city by extraordinary means while the ink of independence was still wet. The Kashmir war, which began with the tribal invasion of October 1947, was still formally unresolved, the ceasefire of 1 January 1949 being months old. The police action in Hyderabad of September 1948, Operation Polo, had been concluded inside the previous year. The Communist Party of India had announced its insurrectionary line at its Calcutta congress of February and March 1948, and the government of West Bengal was fighting a communist insurgency's opening phase in the same province that was absorbing the East Bengal refugee movement. Members who rose to defend a strong emergency clause were not describing a thought experiment about a future crisis. They were describing their own diaries.

The critics inside the chamber make the point from the other side, and their speeches are the best evidence that the clause was understood at the time as a Partition-generation instrument rather than a borrowed formality. H.V. Kamath, who had moved amendments against the emergency provisions in August 1949, warned the Assembly that the draft articles concentrated power in terms reminiscent of the constitutional breakdowns of Europe between the wars, and he pressed the point that a generation that had fought an authoritarian colonial state should hesitate before writing authoritarian capacity into its own charter. Shibban Lal Saxena and other members voiced the fear that emergency powers granted for genuine breakdown would be retained for convenience. Ambedkar, closing the debate for the Drafting Committee, did not deny the capacity. His defence was structural and experiential at once: the Constitution, he argued, was designed to work as a federal system in normal times and to become unitary when emergency required, and a polity that had watched provinces fail to protect life and property in 1947 could not responsibly write a charter that left the centre a spectator at the next failure. The hope he expressed for the cognate provision on the failure of constitutional machinery in a state, that it would remain a dead letter and never be called into operation, only makes sense as the remark of a man legislating for emergencies he had personally witnessed and hoped not to witness again.

Article 22, the preventive-detention clause that sits anomalously inside the Fundamental Rights chapter itself, carries the clearest single signature of the security calibration. When the Assembly debated the due-process and personal-liberty clauses in December 1948 and again on 15 and 16 September 1949, the members who defended the detention power, Patel's lieutenants in the Home Ministry's interest and Ambedkar on the floor, argued from the condition of the country: sabotage on the railways of the refugee routes, communal organisations stockpiling arms, a communist movement committed at the time to an insurrectionary path, and two new borders across which hostile intelligence services operated. K.M. Munshi and Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, neither of them authoritarians by record, treated the power as an unfortunate necessity of the founding moment rather than a principle of free government, and the clause survived with its safeguards, advisory-board review after three months, because the necessity argument, in September 1949, had the autumn of 1947 behind it. The first Preventive Detention Act of February 1950, enacted by the provisional Parliament weeks after the Constitution commenced, shows the calibration passing directly from text to statute without a pause for reflection: the founding generation did not experience the security provisions as a reserve for a distant contingency. They experienced them as the standing equipment of the state Partition had made necessary.

### Why does the Constitution permit preventive detention inside its rights chapter?

Article 22 permits preventive detention, with advisory-board review beyond three months, because the Assembly that adopted it on 15 and 16 September 1949 had governed through refugee-route sabotage, communal arming and an insurrectionary communist line since August 1947. K.M. Munshi framed the power as a founding necessity, not a principle of free government.

### How did Partition push the founders toward a strong Union centre?

The Objectives Resolution of January 1947 promised autonomous units holding residuary powers. After the plan of 3 June 1947, the Union Powers Committee reported on 5 July 1947 for a strong centre instead. The June debates state the trade openly: territory was divided, so the India that remained had to be governable from its centre.

The emergency architecture also reached into the fiscal and administrative clauses that surround Part Eighteen, and the reach was Partition-shaped as well. Article 354's power to modify the financial distribution between Union and states during an emergency, and the direction power in Article 353, assume a centre that may have to commandeer a province's administration at short notice; the assumption was written by men who had watched the Punjab provincial administration effectively cease to function as an impartial instrument in the late summer of 1947 and had improvised a Military Evacuation Organisation and an emergency committee of the cabinet to move millions of people across a border the state itself had failed to police. The East Punjab government's collapse and reconstruction is the unwritten preamble to Part Eighteen. Granville Austin himself, in his later work, acknowledged the founders' obsession with unity and integrity as the first of their commitments; the determinant reading simply dates that obsession precisely. It was not an abstract preference for unity. It was the preference of a cabinet that had buried the victims of disunity in the autumn of its first year in office.

The cognate clause on the failure of constitutional machinery in a state, which became Article 356, drew the same generational argument in miniature when the Assembly debated the draft article on 4 August 1949. Ambedkar, defending the power to supersede a provincial government, told the Assembly that he hoped the clause would remain a dead letter and that the President would never need to call it into operation; the hope is quoted whenever the clause's later career is criticised, and it is usually quoted as naivety. It reads differently in context. The Assembly had watched a provincial administration, the Punjab government of the late summer of 1947, cease to function as an impartial protector of its population, and it had replaced that government's functions with military evacuation machinery and direct central intervention. A dead-letter hope attached to a must-exist power is the exact emotional signature of 1949: the founders wrote the clause because 1947 had happened, and hoped against it because they remembered what needing it had cost. The clause's first actual use confirms the genealogy. President's Rule was proclaimed for the first time in Punjab on 20 June 1951, in the partitioned province itself, amid a Congress ministry's collapse and a rehabilitation administration still settling a displaced population. The first patient of the emergency medicine was the province whose condition had caused the medicine to be compounded.

The fiscal emergency of Article 360, under which the Union may direct state financial administration during a threat to the financial stability or credit of India, has never been proclaimed. Its presence in the text is sometimes treated as evidence of the founders' centralising excess. The determinant reading offers the more precise explanation: a government that had divided a treasury, a debt, a railway system and an army between two hostile dominions in the autumn of 1947, and had then fought over the division's arithmetic, including the payment of Pakistan's share of the cash balances in January 1948, wrote fiscal emergency into the charter because it had learned how quickly a state's credit becomes a weapon in a partition's aftermath. Restraint in use is a fact about later governments. The clause is a fact about the founding experience, and the founding experience was the unmaking and remaking of a state under emergency conditions.

## The Capital Under Strain: Delhi, the Fast and the Assassination

No section of this article's argument needs the capital's own record, and that is exactly why the record belongs here: it shows the conditions under which the constitution-makers worked, in the city where they worked, while the citizenship and minority clauses were still unwritten. Delhi in September 1947 received one of the largest refugee inflows of any Indian city, as Hindus and Sikhs fleeing West Punjab and the North-West Frontier Province arrived at a rate the municipal administration could not house, while Muslims of the old city fled in the opposite direction toward the camps at Purana Qila and Humayun's Tomb and onward to Pakistan. Communal killing reached the capital in the first week of September 1947. The government governed Delhi through emergency arrangements; Nehru and Patel toured the city's neighbourhoods in person; Gandhi, who had intended to go to Punjab, arrived in Delhi on 9 September 1947 and stayed, working from Birla House, because the capital of the new republic could not keep its own minorities alive without him. The Constituent Assembly's members debated provincial constitutions in a city under curfew, and several of them, as ministers, signed the emergency orders of the same administration whose powers they were supposedly designing in the abstract. The abstraction is a later invention. The founders were the emergency government.

Gandhi's last fast, from 13 to 18 January 1948, belongs in the constitutional record for two reasons. The first is its demands: the restoration of Delhi's mosques converted during the violence, the safety of Muslims travelling and worshipping in the capital, and the release to Pakistan of fifty-five crore rupees, Pakistan's agreed share of the undivided cash balances, which the Indian cabinet had been withholding while the Kashmir war continued. The cabinet's decision to make the payment was announced as the fast began, and the fast's success in extracting a peace pledge from Delhi's communal leadership showed the moral machinery that still ran parallel to the state's legal machinery in the founding winter. The second reason is the fast's ending in the longer story: twelve days after Gandhi broke the fast, on 30 January 1948, Nathuram Godse assassinated him at a prayer meeting at Birla House. The state responded as the security state the founders were building: the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh was banned by a government order of 4 February 1948, issued by Patel's Home Ministry, on the stated ground that its members had engaged in acts of violence and subversion, and communal organisations across the political spectrum faced a season of proscription and surveillance.

The constitutional consequence of the assassination winter is written into the freedoms chapter, and it is usually missed because the consequence is a set of qualifications rather than a grant. Article 19's freedoms of speech, assembly and association were adopted with their restriction clauses, permitting restrictions in the interests of public order, decency or morality and the security of the State, and the debates of 1948 and 1949 on those clauses were conducted by members who had buried Gandhi between sessions and who discussed the freedom of communal organisations as a question with a body count. The founders did not write an absolutist free-speech clause and then discover communal violence as an afterthought, on the American pattern that B.N. Rau's consultations had put before them. They wrote qualified freedoms from the start, because the founding's central political experience was that speech, organisation and procession, in the conditions Partition created, could be instruments of massacre. A reader who restores the winter of 1947 and 1948 to the freedoms debate understands the restriction clauses as the founders understood them: not as colonial survivals smuggled into a liberal text, but as Partition clauses, argued for in Partition terms, by the men who had run the refugee trains.

## Dimension Two: Citizenship Written for the Migrations

Part Two of the Constitution, Articles 5 to 11, is the provision family where the determinant argument needs no archaeology, because the text confesses on its face. A constitution written for a settled population does not need Article 6. Article 6 confers citizenship on a person who migrated to India from Pakistan, and it distinguishes two classes of migrant by a date: those who arrived before 19 July 1948 are citizens if they or a parent or grandparent were born in India as the Government of India Act of 1935 had defined it, and those who arrived on or after 19 July 1948 are citizens only if registered after six months of residence. The date is not symbolic. The permit system regulating movement from West Pakistan into India was introduced in July 1948, and the Constitution froze its commencement into the definition of citizenship itself. An administrative instrument created to manage the largest migration in the subcontinent's recorded history became a constitutional boundary between two classes of the republic's own citizens. No account of the founding as pure intellectual achievement explains why a constitution should contain a permit date. The Partition explanation explains it in one sentence.

Article 5, the general clause, deserves the same close reading. At the commencement of the Constitution, citizenship went to every person who had a domicile in the territory of India and who was born in that territory, or either of whose parents was born in that territory, or who had been ordinarily resident in that territory for not less than five years immediately before commencement. The layered alternatives, birth, descent, residence, were drafted for a population whose documentary condition Partition had scrambled: families in the refugee camps of Delhi, Kurukshetra and Amritsar held ration cards rather than birth certificates, and a clause that demanded proof of a single criterion would have excluded people the republic intended to include. When the Assembly debated the citizenship clauses in the sittings of August 1949, in the debates the brief directs to Volume VII of the Constituent Assembly Debates for the earlier citizenship discussions of December 1948 and January 1949 and concluded in the later session, members pressed the ministry on precisely this documentary problem: how would a displaced person from Lahore or Lyallpur prove a grandparent's birthplace to a registering officer? Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, answering for the Drafting Committee's approach, defended a frame that trusted declaration and residence where paper had burned. The clause is generous because the migration had been destructive of evidence. Generosity here is not a temperament. It is a calibration.

Article 7 performs the opposite movement and exposes the political nerve of the whole Part. A person who had migrated to Pakistan after 1 March 1947, the date chosen because it marked the onset of the Punjab disturbances that preceded formal Partition, was not to be deemed a citizen, even if he or she would have qualified under Article 5, unless that person returned to India under a permit for resettlement or permanent return and was registered. The clause was fought over in the Assembly because everyone in the chamber understood its human content: it governed the fate of Muslims who had left India in fear during the violence of 1947 and 1948 and then, finding the promised homeland a camp in Karachi or an unwelcome tenancy in Dhaka, attempted to come home. Members from the United Provinces and Bihar, where the question of returning migrants had become an administrative and communal controversy by 1949, pressed for the strictest reading. Nehru, whose correspondence of those months wrestled with the returning-migrant question district by district, defended a settlement that kept the door legally open through the permit proviso while conceding the political reality that unconditional return was no longer administrable. Article 7 is the constitutional form of that concession. It is also the clearest refutation of the context reading: a clause that decides which frightened families may resume citizenship is not background to the founding. It is the founding, operating on the exact population Partition had displaced.

Article 8 completes the architecture by reaching in the other direction, to the diaspora: a person of Indian origin ordinarily resident outside India could be registered as a citizen at an Indian diplomatic or consular office, whether or not that person had ever lived inside the new borders. Article 9 bars citizenship for anyone who had voluntarily acquired a foreign citizenship. Article 10 continues citizenship subject to the law Parliament would make, and Article 11 hands Parliament plenary power to regulate acquisition, termination and every other question of citizenship, the power under which the Citizenship Act of 1955 was later enacted. Jayal's layered reading finds its strongest textual support across this whole Part. The universal citizen of Article 5 sits on top of layers the text itself supplies: the migrant layer sorted by permit dates, the returnee layer sorted by intention and permission, the diaspora layer sorted by registration, and a parliamentary layer empowered to re-sort everyone by statute in the future. The layers are the Partition settlement, written in the grammar of universalism.

The floor debate on the citizenship clauses in August 1949 supplies the texture that the text alone conceals, and two interventions deserve recovery. Rohini Kumar Chaudhuri, speaking for Assam, pressed the Assembly on the eastern migration's open door: Assam's districts were receiving movement from East Bengal without any permit machinery, and he asked, in substance, whether a citizenship frame designed around the western permit system could hold in a province where the border was a river system crossed by kin. The question exposed the asymmetry this article has already noted, and the Drafting Committee's answer, in effect, was that Article 5's domicile route and Article 11's parliamentary power would have to carry the eastern case until Parliament legislated. The second intervention ran the other way: members who feared that Article 6's generosity would be exploited pressed for registration machinery and documentary proof, and the exchange forced the government benches to state the settlement's philosophy aloud, that the republic would rather risk including some who had no strict claim than exclude the displaced who had lost their papers in flight. A citizenship debate in which the burden of proof is argued from the refugee camp outward is a Partition debate, whatever its formal subject.

The settlement's treatment of intention also repays attention. Article 7's structure assumes that migration to Pakistan after 1 March 1947 carried a meaning, an abandonment of India, that migration before that date did not, and the return permit exists to let a migrant disprove the assumption. Nehru's government defended that assumption in the Assembly and in correspondence with chief ministers, while conceding the cases that broke it: the official who had taken a post in Karachi and resigned it, the family that had fled a riot in panic and walked back across the Punjab border in the spring of 1948, the divided household whose earning member stood on the wrong side of a line drawn in August. Each conceded case required the permit desk's discretion, which is to say that the Constitution, at the exact point of belonging, built discretion into the boundary and trusted administrators to soften it. Jayal's layered-citizenship reading is at its most persuasive on this material, because the layers are not metaphors. They are the strata of a sorting operation, and the sorting operation was the state's daily work in the winter the clauses were drafted.

Two further features of the citizenship settlement show the calibration working below the constitutional surface, in the electoral and administrative machinery that gave the text effect. The first electoral rolls of the republic, prepared through 1948 and 1949 for the general election that Sukumar Sen would conduct in 1951 and 1952, had to answer the citizenship questions before the Constitution commenced, and the preparation of the rolls in the refugee-settled districts of Delhi, Punjab and West Bengal became a vast administrative adjudication of belonging, conducted by enumeration staff with the displaced camps as their hardest districts. The second feature is asymmetry. The permit system, and with it the constitutional distinction of Article 6, governed the western migration; in the east, movement between India and East Pakistan remained comparatively unregulated through 1949 and into the following decade, so the eastern migrant entered the text through Article 5's domicile and residence routes rather than through Article 6 at all. The same Constitution therefore contains two migration regimes, one for each border, because Partition itself had produced two different migrations: a western exchange that was violent, rapid and nearly total, and an eastern movement that was slower, longer and never declared complete. A text that mirrors its event at that resolution is not contextualised by the event. It is determined by it, down to the difference between its clauses.

## Belonging Administered: Permits, Evacuee Property and the Abducted Persons Regime

The determinant argument gains strength when it is tested against the dominion-era statute book, the laws the Assembly's members were making in their other capacity as the Dominion Legislature between August 1947 and January 1950, because those statutes show the same hands solving the same problems the Constitution was calibrating. The permit system of July 1948 began as executive control of influx from West Pakistan and hardened into the Influx from Pakistan (Control) legislation of 1949, which made entry without a permit an offence and return without a resettlement permit a ground for exclusion; Article 7's proviso is the constitutional shadow of that statute's permit desk. The evacuee-property regime, built through ordinances of 1949 and consolidated in the Administration of Evacuee Property Act of 1950, vested the abandoned houses, shops and lands of departed Muslims in a Custodian, initially as a trusteeship pending return, and increasingly, as return became politically impossible, as a permanent redistribution of urban property to incoming refugees. A state deciding who owns a vacated house in Karol Bagh or Aminabad is deciding the material content of citizenship, whatever the constitutional text says, and the founders knew it, because the evacuee-property debates were among the most bitterly contested of the provisional Parliament's early sessions.

The Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act of December 1949 exposes the gendered layer of the same settlement. Under the Inter-Dominion agreements of late 1947, both dominions had committed to recovering women abducted during the violence, and the 1949 Act gave the Indian state power to recover and restore women, and children born to them, across the new border, in many cases against the women's own stated wishes where officials judged the stated wish to be a product of captivity. Social workers such as Mridula Sarabhai administered the programme in Punjab, and the files of the recovery operation record women pleading, in terms, to be left in the families where the violence had placed them. Jayal's argument about gendered belonging, that the founding state treated women's citizenship as derivative of family and community in a way it never treated men's, is written across this statute more plainly than across any constitutional clause. The determinant reading absorbs the point without strain. Partition created the abducted woman as a legal problem; the founding state solved the problem by deciding her belonging for her; and the Constitution she lived under had been drafted, in its citizenship Part, by the same political order that was running the recovery camps. The distance between Article 6's permit date and the recovery tribunal's order is the distance between a text and its administration, and both were set by the same event.

The Nehru-Liaquat Pact of 8 April 1950, signed in Delhi by Nehru and Pakistan's Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan after the renewed Bengal crisis of early 1950 drove fresh waves of migration in both directions, belongs to the same record. The pact guaranteed minorities in each dominion equality of citizenship irrespective of religion, freedom of movement and occupation, and security of life and property, and it set up minority commissions to police the guarantees. Its constitutional relevance is direct: the guarantees the pact promised Pakistan's Hindus were the guarantees Articles 25 to 30 and Article 29 promised India's Muslims, and the pact's existence confesses that in 1950 the founders regarded those guarantees as requiring international reinforcement. Shyama Prasad Mookerjee resigned from Nehru's cabinet over the pact, holding that it papered over the failure of protection in East Bengal. The resignation is useful evidence precisely because it came from a critic: even the pact's opponents debated minority protection as a Partition problem to be solved by constitutional and diplomatic machinery, not as an abstraction. [The Patel-Nehru-Ambedkar triangulation that operated under post-Partition conditions](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) was, in these years, a triangulation among three administrators of a divided country, and its constitutional output cannot be priced without the division.

## Dimension Three: The Language Settlement in Two Divided Provinces

Article 343 declares that the official language of the Union shall be Hindi in the Devanagari script, and that English shall continue for the purposes for which it was used before commencement for fifteen years, with Parliament empowered to extend it. The formula, negotiated by K.M. Munshi and N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar and adopted on 14 September 1949 after the most emotionally charged debate of the Assembly's final session, is usually explained as a north-south compromise: Hindi for the Hindi-speaking provinces, time and English for the sceptical south. That explanation is true and incomplete, because the two provinces where language had become an existential political question by 1949 were not in the south. They were the two provinces Partition had cut: Bengal and Punjab.

Begin with Punjab. The undivided province had conducted its politics in a three-language settlement of practice: Urdu as the language of administration and of the Muslim majority's public life, Punjabi in Gurmukhi script as the sacred and increasingly the political language of the Sikhs, and Hindi in Devanagari as the language of the Hindu public sphere organised by the Arya Samaj and its educational network. Partition removed Urdu's constituency and its official standing at a stroke, and left Hindi and Punjabi facing each other inside East Punjab with no third term to mediate. Through 1948 and 1949 the province's politics reorganised around the script question with startling speed: the demand for a Punjabi Suba, a Punjabi-speaking state, gathered force under Master Tara Singh and the Akali Dal; the Punjab government under Gopi Chand Bhargava promoted Hindi; and the census and education policies of the state became proxy battles over which language a Sikh or Hindu child would be registered under. When the Assembly debated the language clauses in September 1949, the members from East Punjab who spoke were not discussing philology. They were reporting from a province where the choice between Hindi and Punjabi had become the choice between two futures for the Sikh community inside India, a community whose suspicion of majoritarian absorption was itself a Partition product, formed in the massacres and the flight from West Punjab. Seth Govind Das pressed for Hindi with the fervour of the Hindi movement; Sikh and Punjabi members answered with the arithmetic of survival. The fifteen-year English bridge in Article 343(2) bought time for all of these fires, not only for Madras.

Bengal supplies the second half of the calibration. Partition divided a province whose language had been its proudest political possession, and it placed the Bengali language question on both sides of the new border at once: in West Bengal, a Hindu-majority state absorbing millions of Bengali-speaking refugees from the east, and in East Pakistan, where the Bengali-speaking Muslim majority confronted a Pakistani state that declared Urdu its sole national language in 1948, a declaration Muhammad Ali Jinnah repeated in Dhaka in March 1948 to open hostility. The Dhaka students who protested the Urdu-only policy in March 1948 were demonstrating, for any Indian constitution-maker watching, the explosive charge a language clause could carry in a new state built out of Partition. Bengali entered the Eighth Schedule of the Indian Constitution alongside thirteen other languages, and so did Sindhi, a language with no territory inside India at all, carried into the republic by the Hindu Sindhi refugees who had lost their entire province to Pakistan. The presence of Sindhi in the Eighth Schedule is a small, hard piece of evidence for the determinant reading. A schedule of the languages of India has no structural reason to include the language of a province that is not in India. It has a Partition reason: the refugees were in India, their language was their portable homeland, and the founders wrote it into the text. Article 347's power for the President to direct recognition of a language spoken by a substantial proportion of a state's population, and the mother-tongue instruction protections that became Article 350A by amendment in 1956, extend the same logic: language inside the Indian Union was to be managed by concession and recognition, because the founders had watched language become a border question in Bengal and a sovereignty question in Punjab, and because the Dhaka protests of 1948 showed them where the refusal of recognition led.

The prehistory of the September 1949 debate explains its heat. R.V. Dhulekar had demanded, in the Assembly's first week in December 1946, that proceedings be conducted in Hindi and had declared, in the form the debate record preserves, that members who did not know Hindustani had no business in the house; Nehru and the chair contained the demand, but the Hindi movement inside the Congress, organised through figures such as Purushottam Das Tandon, treated the constitutional language clause as the movement's culminating battle. The numerals question split the difference in a way that reveals the formula's method: Article 343 adopted the international form of Indian numerals for Union purposes rather than the Devanagari numerals the Hindi movement wanted, a small surrender by the maximalists that purchased southern and Bengali assent to the script and language provisions. The Munshi-Ayyangar formula was therefore not a midpoint discovered by exhaustion. It was a portfolio of trades, language for the Hindi provinces, script shared, numerals international, English retained for fifteen years, the Eighth Schedule opened to fourteen languages, each trade priced against a constituency that Partition had made newly dangerous to offend. The southern members who accepted the formula, including T.T. Krishnamachari with his celebrated warning against Hindi imperialism, were buying insurance for their own provinces from the same settlement that insured Punjab's Sikhs and Bengal's refugees.

Two companion clauses complete the picture and both bear the same date-stamp of caution. Article 348 kept English as the language of the Supreme Court and the high courts, and of authoritative legislative texts, until Parliament by law provided otherwise, insulating the higher judiciary from the language transition entirely; the men who wrote that insulation had watched the Lahore and Calcutta high courts split between two dominions in August 1947, their benches and bars divided by the Radcliffe award, and they prized continuity of legal language as a form of state continuity. Article 351 directed the Union to promote the spread of Hindi and to develop it as a medium of expression for the composite culture of India, drawing on Hindustani and on the other languages of the Eighth Schedule. The promotion duty and the continuation of English sit side by side in the same Part, seventeen articles of truce between a language the founders wished to grow and a language they dared not dismiss. Only a founding conducted amid the debris of two broken provincial settlements writes both halves of that truce without embarrassment.

The deeper point about the language settlement is its temporality. Article 343 is a clause with a clock: fifteen years of English, then a decision. The founders built deferral into the text because Partition had taught them the price of forcing a cultural settlement on a frightened population at a moment of state formation. Where the Cabinet Mission generation might have imposed a formula, the post-Partition Assembly chose a formula plus a fuse, the Official Language Commission under Article 344 and the parliamentary committee that would follow it, machinery for postponing the explosion to a calmer decade. Deferral as a technique appears across the founding text, in the directive principles and in the amendment procedure, but in the language Part its motive is documented in the debate speeches of 12 to 14 September 1949, where member after member justified compromise by pointing at the condition of the country. The country they pointed at was a partitioned country.

## Dimension Four: Minority Protection for Those Who Remained

On 6 December 1948 the Constituent Assembly took up the religious-freedom clauses of the draft Constitution, and the debate that ran through 7 December 1948, recorded in Volume VII of the Constituent Assembly Debates, is the documentary heart of this article's fourth dimension. Draft Article 19, which became Article 25, guaranteed freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality and health. The propagation word was the battleground. Lokanath Misra argued that propagation was a cover for conversion by inducement and that a secular state had no business protecting it. Muslim members, among them B. Pocker Sahib Bahadur and Naziruddin Ahmad, answered from the exposed position of a community that had just watched a subcontinent divide on the question of whether Muslims could live as equal citizens in a Hindu-majority state. K.M. Munshi, closing the case for retaining propagation, grounded the clause explicitly in reassurance: he told the Assembly that the word had been deliberately kept, that the minorities had been given assurances, and that the retention of the propagation right formed part of the settlement on which Muslim confidence in the Indian Union was to be rebuilt after Partition. The brief for this article directs attention to Munshi's intervention for exactly this reason. The founder defending the clause named its beneficiary and its occasion. The beneficiary was the post-Partition Muslim who had remained in India, roughly thirty-five million people by the count the 1951 census would formalise, and the occasion was the insecurity of a community whose members had buried relatives in 1947, whose property was being vested in Custodians as evacuee property when they fled and returned, and whose loyalty the state publicly questioned while privately requiring their confidence.

One omission from the text belongs to this dimension, because the founders considered the label and refused it while granting the substance. In November 1948 K.T. Shah moved amendments to describe the Union in the Constitution's opening article as a secular, federal, socialist union of states. Ambedkar opposed the insertion, and the Assembly rejected it, on the reasoning the record preserves: that the organisation of the state on particular social and religious lines was a policy for future electorates to choose, not a creed for the constitutional text to impose. The episode is often recruited into arguments about whether the founding was secular. For this article its value is narrower and more useful. The Assembly that declined the word secular in 1948 adopted, in the same season, Articles 25 to 30 in their full protective form, and defended Article 25's propagation clause as a minority assurance. Confidence, the founders judged, would be built by enforceable rights a frightened community could invoke, not by an adjective in the Preamble. The adjective arrived only with the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976, a generation later, when the assurance problem the founders had faced had changed its nature entirely.

The surrounding clauses carry the same calibration. Article 26 protects the right of religious denominations to manage their own affairs and administer their property. Article 27 bars taxation for the promotion of any particular religion. Article 28 regulates religious instruction in state-funded institutions. Article 29 protects the language, script and culture of any section of citizens and bars denial of admission to state-aided educational institutions on grounds of religion, race, caste or language. Article 30 guarantees minorities based on religion or language the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice, and bars discrimination in granting aid. Read as a set, Articles 25 to 30 are a confidence architecture: each clause answers a specific fear that a minority community, in the winter of 1948, had documented reason to hold. The fear of forced assimilation is answered by propagation and denominational autonomy. The fear of cultural erasure is answered by Article 29. The fear of educational exclusion is answered by Articles 29(2) and 30. A confidence architecture presupposes a crisis of confidence, and the crisis has a date and a name.

The lineage the founders were dismantling deserves a paragraph, because the size of the May 1949 reversal is visible only against it. Separate electorates for Muslims dated from the Indian Councils Act of 1909, the Morley-Minto reforms, and had been extended by the Lucknow Pact of 1916, in which Congress itself accepted communal electorates with weightage as the price of a joint nationalist platform. The Communal Award of August 1932 extended separate electorates further, and the Poona Pact of September 1932 replaced them for the Depressed Classes with reserved seats in joint electorates, the model the founding generation knew best. For forty years, then, the default technology of minority protection in Indian constitutionalism had been political: separate rolls, reserved seats, weightage. The Advisory Committee's August 1947 report still belonged to that world. Its May 1949 report abandoned that world for religious minorities altogether. Technologies of that age are not abandoned because a committee changes its mind about political theory. They are abandoned when the political world that made them plausible collapses, and the collapse, in this case, was the creation of Pakistan on the explicit argument that Muslims required a separate political container. The founders read that argument as a refutation of the technology itself.

Article 30 carries one further innovation that Partition scholarship rarely isolates. Its protection runs to minorities based on religion or language, which made the linguistic minority a constitutional category in its own right for the first time in Indian law. The category mattered in 1949 because the internal map was already moving: linguistic provinces were coming, the Assembly knew it, and every future state would manufacture new linguistic minorities inside its borders, including minorities created by the Partition migrations themselves, such as the Bengali refugees of Assam and Tripura and the Sindhi merchants scattered across Bombay and Madhya Pradesh. A clause written for the minorities of 1949 was thus aimed, with unusual foresight, at minorities that the reorganisation of the Union would go on creating for decades. The foresight is itself a Partition habit: a generation that had watched a border manufacture minorities overnight wrote minority into the text as a permanent, recurring condition rather than a transitional problem.

The negative evidence is as strong as the positive. In August 1947 the Advisory Committee chaired by Patel, reporting on minorities, had still contemplated political safeguards of the older type: reserved seats in legislatures for religious minorities, weightage arrangements inherited from the pre-Partition negotiating tradition. By May 1949 the same committee, in a changed country, recommended the abolition of reservation for religious minorities altogether, retaining it only, and temporarily, for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and Anglo-Indians, and the Assembly accepted the change, with Patel presenting the May 1949 report in terms that framed the abandonment of separate political safeguards as a gift the minorities themselves should welcome in the interest of integration. The reversal is unintelligible as intellectual progress alone. Between the two reports lay Partition: separate electorates and communal reservation had become, in the founders' stated reasoning, the machinery that had produced Pakistan, and the machinery had to be dismantled in India even at the price of leaving minorities dependent on the cultural and educational protections of Articles 25 to 30 and on the general guarantee of equality. [The secularism debates that the post-Partition Muslim-insecurity context shaped](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/) and the minority clauses were redesigned together, in the same months, by the same logic: protection moved from the political register to the cultural register because the political register had been discredited by division.

Two members' interventions fix the emotional register of the settlement. H.C. Mookherjee, the Christian vice-president of the Assembly who chaired the Minorities Sub-Committee, repeatedly asked the majority community to consider how the clauses would read to a minority reader who had to decide whether the new state was safe to trust. Begum Aizaz Rasul, the most prominent Muslim woman in the chamber, argued against separate electorates from within the minority position, holding that reservation had harmed the community it claimed to protect and that the remaining Muslims of India should stake their future on the common citizenship the Constitution promised. Her speeches are the strongest internal evidence that the Articles 25 to 30 settlement was a negotiated confidence bargain and not a majoritarian donation: minority members traded political safeguards away and took cultural rights and common citizenship in exchange, and they said, on the record, that the exchange was the lesson of Partition. The determinant reading simply believes them.

## The Federal Redesign of August 1947 and B.N. Rau's Notes

The federal structure is where this article grants Austin his strongest ground, and the grant has to be made precisely so that the determinant claim survives it. A federal frame of some kind was overdetermined at the founding: the Government of India Act of 1935 had built a federation-shaped machine, the national movement had promised linguistic and regional accommodation since the Nehru Report of 1928, and a country of India's scale could not be governed as a unitary state by any plausible administration. The parliamentary executive, responsible to the lower house, came from the same inheritance. None of that is Partition's work, and the determinant claim does not need it to be. What Partition changed was the federation's centre of gravity, and the change is datable to a five-week window.

The Objectives Resolution, moved by Nehru on 13 December 1946 and adopted on 22 January 1947, promised a republic in which the territories would possess and retain the status of autonomous units, exercising all powers save those assigned to the Union, with residuary powers vested in the units. That was the Cabinet Mission federation: a weak centre holding defence, foreign affairs and communications, above grouped provinces. The Union Powers Committee's first report of April 1947 still worked inside the logic of that scheme. Then came the plan of 3 June 1947, and with it the disappearance of the federation's intended counterparty. The Union Powers Committee's second report, dated 5 July 1947, recommended the opposite architecture: a strong centre, residuary powers at the Union, and a federation designed for cohesion rather than for the reassurance of a departing partner. Rau's August 1947 notes track the Constitutional Adviser reworking the scheme on paper in the weeks after the June plan, striking the grouping machinery, reconsidering the provincial constitutions for a smaller union, and treating the centre's authority as the premise rather than the problem. The Drafting Committee's draft of February 1948 then embodied the July decisions in text, including the provision that became Article 248, residuary power to Parliament, and the emergency conversion of the federation into a unitary system under Part Eighteen.

Territory itself had to be constituted at the same speed. Article 1 declares that India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States, and the First Schedule listed the states of a territory assembled from the former British provinces and more than five hundred princely states whose accession Patel and V.P. Menon negotiated through the States Ministry from July 1947 onward. The accession instruments of 1947, beginning with the rush of signatures before 15 August and continuing through Junagadh, Hyderabad and Kashmir, made the Union's map a diplomatic artefact before it was a constitutional one. [The asymmetric federal design that the August 1947 reorganisation produced](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) bears the marks of that assembly process everywhere: Article 370's special arrangement for Jammu and Kashmir, born of an accession signed under invasion on 26 October 1947 and of the commitment to a reference to the people that accompanied it; the Sixth Schedule's autonomous district councils for the tribal areas of Assam, designed for a frontier province whose eastern boundary Partition had just redrawn through the Sylhet referendum; the financial and administrative asymmetries that let unequal states sit inside one Union. Each asymmetry is a receipt for a negotiation conducted in the shadow of division. A federation designed in tranquillity for a settled territory does not accumulate receipts of that kind. The redesign of August 1947 is the hinge of the whole founding, and Rau's notes are its minute book: they record the exact weeks in which the founders stopped designing a constitution for the India of the Cabinet Mission and started designing one for the India Partition had left them.

## Accession as Constitutional Fact: Junagadh, Hyderabad and Kashmir

Article 1's Union of States was not a description of a territory the founders possessed. It was a claim staked over a territory still being assembled while the drafting proceeded, and three accessions show the assembly writing itself into the text. Junagadh, a princely state in Kathiawar with a Muslim ruler and a predominantly Hindu population, acceded to Pakistan in September 1947, an accession Pakistan accepted; India refused to recognise the result, administered the state after its Nawab fled to Karachi, and confirmed the position by a referendum on 20 February 1948 in which the vote for India was overwhelming. The episode taught the States Ministry a rule the Constitution absorbed: accession was not complete when a ruler signed, only when the settlement held on the ground and, where contested, before the people. Hyderabad, the largest of the states, signed a standstill agreement in November 1947 and spent 1948 in armed ambiguity, its Razakar militia raiding across the borders of the surrounding provinces, until the Indian Army's police action of 13 to 17 September 1948 ended the Nizam's bid for independence and brought the state into the Union by force followed by accession. The Assembly debated its provincial constitutions in the autumn of 1948 with Hyderabad's smoke still in the newspapers, and no member needed to be told why the Union's emergency and direction powers had to reach inside the states.

Kashmir wrote the deepest clause. Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947, under invasion, accepting Union authority in defence, external affairs and communications, and Mountbatten's acceptance letter of 27 October 1947 recorded that the question of accession would be settled by a reference to the people when law and order were restored. India carried the complaint against Pakistan to the United Nations Security Council on 1 January 1948, and the ceasefire of 1 January 1949 left the state divided along a line that hardened into permanence. When N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, who had handled the Kashmir file as minister, moved the draft article that became Article 370 on 17 October 1949, he justified its special arrangement expressly from the circumstances: the war still formally unresolved, the state's own constituent process pending, Sheikh Abdullah and his colleagues sitting in the Assembly as representatives of a state whose relationship with the Union remained, in the mover's own framing, incomplete. Article 370 was placed in Part Twenty-One, the temporary and transitional provisions, because its authors understood it as the written form of an unfinished accession. The clause is the cleanest specimen of this article's whole argument: a provision whose text, location and stated rationale are each unintelligible without the events of October 1947, defended on the floor by the minister who had administered those events, in a chamber that included the acceding state's own leadership. Contexts do not draft clauses of that specificity. Determinants do.

Read the columns together and the matrix does the work the narrative sections argue. Every row joins a clause to a dated challenge and a named defender, and in every row the defender's stated reason, in debate or in committee, was the post-Partition condition rather than an abstract doctrine. The matrix also disciplines the claim's limits. It contains no row for the parliamentary executive, none for judicial review, none for universal franchise, because no honest trace runs from those provisions to the division of 1947. A determinant with edges is a finding. A determinant without edges would be a slogan.

The matrix has a second use, for the reader who arrives from the examination hall or the debating chamber rather than from the archive. Each row is a self-contained answer to a standard question, why the emergency provisions are unitary in design, why the citizenship articles carry dates, why the language Part contains a timetable, why minority protection is cultural rather than political, and each answer is stated with its evidence attached, the provision, the challenge and the founder, so that the answer can be checked against the Debates rather than merely repeated. Reference works earn their citations by being checkable at speed. The matrix is built to that specification deliberately, because the standard treatments it competes with, the encyclopaedia entries and the coaching notes, state the provisions without the challenges and the founders' reasons, and a reader who needs the reasons has nowhere in those treatments to go.

## The Counterfactual Test: The Founding That Did Not Happen

Counterfactual reasoning earns its place in this article only because the archive supplies the control case. The control case is the founding the Assembly was designing before June 1947, and its documents survive: the Cabinet Mission Plan of 16 May 1946, the Objectives Resolution of January 1947, the Union Powers Committee's first report of April 1947. Run the four dimensions against that control. Citizenship, in the control founding, required no Articles 6 or 7 at all: no mass migration between sovereign states had occurred or was planned, and a citizenship Part for an undivided India needed only birth, descent and residence, the Article 5 material, plus a parliamentary power for the future. The permit date exists in the text because the migration exists in the world. Language, in the control founding, still faced the Hindi question and the southern question, but it did not face a Punjab in which language choice had fused with Sikh survival, nor a Bengal amputated from half its speakers, nor a Sindhi refugee population requiring a Schedule entry for a lost province; the fifteen-year bridge and the Eighth Schedule's composition would have differed in ways the September 1949 debate lets a reader measure. Minority protection, in the control founding, ran through the political register the Advisory Committee was still using in August 1947: reserved seats, weightage, negotiated communal arithmetic inside a federation whose units included Muslim-majority provinces as constitutional facts. The May 1949 reversal, the move to cultural rights and common citizenship, is a decision that only a Partition generation takes, because only a Partition generation has watched the political register fail catastrophically. Emergency and federal power, in the control founding, were designed for reassurance of the units, residuary powers downward, a centre confined to three subjects; the adopted text reverses the polarity, and the reversal is dated 5 July 1947 in a committee report written four weeks after the June plan.

The control case also identifies, by contrast, the provisions where no determinant trace should be claimed, and naming them protects the argument from its own success. Universal adult franchise, adopted when the Assembly in 1949 confirmed one person one vote for a population of whom the great majority could not read, descends from the national movement's pledges, the Karachi Resolution lineage and the Motilal Nehru Report of 1928, and its adoption debates turn on democratic principle rather than on any need created by division. The independent judiciary, the Supreme Court's structure and the writ jurisdiction of Article 32, which Ambedkar called the very soul of the Constitution in the sitting of 9 December 1948, descend from the federal court tradition of the 1935 Act and from Rau's comparative consultations of 1947. Partition neither supplied those provisions nor visibly recalibrated them, and the determinant reading does not pretend otherwise. A method that can say where its cause does not operate is a method a reader can trust where it says the cause does.

One further control observation belongs to the test, because it concerns the chamber itself. After August 1947 the Assembly's Muslim membership, the constituency for whom the minority clauses mattered most, had shrunk to the members who had chosen India, fewer than thirty by the usual count, among them Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Rafi Ahmed Kidwai and Begum Aizaz Rasul. In the control founding of the Cabinet Mission scheme, the Assembly would have contained the Muslim League's full contingent, and its minority clauses would have been negotiated against a bloc able to walk out into a parallel state-making process, as the League in fact did. The clauses India adopted were negotiated instead with minority members who had already paid the price of staying and whose bargaining ask had narrowed to cultural protection and equal citizenship. The difference between those two negotiating tables is the difference between the two constitutions, and it was produced by a single event with a single name.

The complication the brief requires this article to face stands at the end of the test, and it should be stated at full strength. Some of what looks like Partition calibration has an older pedigree. Section 93 of the Government of India Act of 1935 let a governor take over a province, and the founders who borrowed the 1935 Act's skeleton borrowed emergency machinery with it. The strong-centre preference had pre-Partition advocates, Ambedkar's among them, who wanted central power for social reform against provincial caste oligarchies and would have wanted it in any India. The Objectives Resolution itself, for all its promise of autonomous units, was a Congress document, and Congress centralism was an organisational fact of the movement long before 1947. The determinant claim accommodates the complication by the distinction this article has maintained throughout: inheritances supplied the instruments; Partition supplied the settings. A governor's-rule clause in a colonial statute and Article 352 in a democratic constitution share a skeleton and differ in everything that makes them law: the holder of the power, the parliamentary control over it, the political memory that justifies it. What the founders did to the inheritance, the two-month approval, the rights suspensions graded between Articles 358 and 359, the conversion of the whole federation rather than one province's supersession, was done by men reasoning from the autumn of 1947. The counterfactual test cannot prove that an undivided India would have written a gentler emergency Part. It proves the narrower and sufficient point: the Assembly that wrote the actual Part Eighteen had before it a weak-centre draft tradition, reversed that tradition in the weeks after 3 June 1947, and stated its reasons on the record. Determinant is the accurate word for a cause documented at that resolution.

## What the Determinant Reading Changes

The test of a reading is what it makes visible that rival readings leave dark. The determinant reading lights up the subsequent career of the four provision families, because provisions calibrated under emergency conditions carry those conditions forward as a standing temptation and a standing defence. Article 352 was proclaimed during the war with China on 26 October 1962, during the war with Pakistan on 3 December 1971, and on the ground of internal disturbance on 25 June 1975, and the third proclamation's career, the Emergency of 1975 to 1977, is the event against which the Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978 rebuilt the clause: internal disturbance replaced by armed rebellion, the approval period tightened, written cabinet advice required. Read through Austin alone, the 1975 abuse is a betrayal of the founding text. Read through the determinant lens, the abuse is also a disclosure of the founding text: a clause calibrated by a generation that had administered emergency rule in Delhi in September 1947 was always going to be available, in a later crisis, to a government that preferred administration to politics. The determinant reading does not excuse the abuse. It explains the availability, and explanation is the beginning of repair.

The citizenship family shows the same forward logic. The Citizenship Act of 1955, enacted under Article 11, built the statutory house on the constitutional foundations of 1949, and its later amendments, in 1986 after the Assam Accord and in 2003 with the introduction of the illegal-migrant bar, replay the founding arguments in statutory form: which migrants the republic owns, which dates divide the deserving from the undeserving, what documents a displaced person must produce. The Assam movement of 1979 to 1985 turned on a cut-off date, 25 March 1971, contested with a passion that re-enacts, almost clause for clause, the Assembly's August 1949 argument over 1 March 1947 and 19 July 1948. A student who has read the founding debates as Partition debates recognises the Assam argument instantly. A student who has read them as abstract citizenship theory does not. That recognitional advantage is the practical yield of the determinant reading, and it extends to the classroom: a teacher preparing slides on the September 1949 language debate or the citizenship sittings of August 1949 can open the deck through [a browser-based presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and walk a class from the clause on the screen to the refugee column outside the hall in two sentences, because the clause and the column belong to the same week of the same founding.

The language family yields a parallel recognition, because its fuse burned on the schedule the founders had set. Article 343's fifteen years expired on 26 January 1965. Parliament had already acted through the Official Languages Act of 1963, continuing English as an associate official language, and Nehru had assured the Lok Sabha in 1959 that English would continue for as long as non-Hindi speakers required it. In January and February 1965, Madras State nevertheless erupted in the anti-Hindi agitations, student protests and self-immolations that told Delhi the deferral had purchased time without purchasing consent, and the Official Languages (Amendment) Act of 1967 entrenched the bilingual settlement indefinitely. The sequence is the determinant argument in its purest form: a clause timed in 1949 against the fears of divided provinces detonated on its own calendar date sixteen years later, and the republic answered with the same technique the founders had used, another deferral, because the underlying settlement had never been concluded. A reader who treats the language Part as a 1949 technicality cannot explain 1965. A reader who treats it as a Partition truce with a fuse explains 1965 in one step.

The minority-protection family yields the third recognition. The confidence architecture of Articles 25 to 30 has been litigated for seven decades, and the litigation keeps returning, explicitly or not, to the 1948 question of what the clauses were for. When the Supreme Court reads Article 25's propagation right or Article 30's institutional autonomy, the strongest arguments on every side reach back to the December 1948 debates and to the bargain those debates recorded: cultural protection in exchange for the surrender of separate political safeguards. Courts and commentators who treat the clauses as timeless liberal postulates miss the bargain and therefore misprice both the rights and their limits. The determinant reading restores the price. It also restores the dignity of the minority members who struck the bargain, Begum Aizaz Rasul arguing against the reservation her community had demanded for a generation, H.C. Mookherjee asking the majority to read its own clauses through minority eyes. Those speeches are not decoration on the settlement. They are the settlement, and a founding account that files Partition under context has no shelf on which to place them.

## Written Through Partition: The Claim Restated

### What did the Constituent Assembly do at its final sitting on 24 January 1950?

At its final sitting on 24 January 1950 the Assembly elected Rajendra Prasad first President of India, adopted Jana Gana Mana as the national anthem with Vande Mataram as national song of equal status, and two hundred and eighty-four members signed the Constitution.

The Indian Constitution was not written despite Partition; it was written through Partition. The sentence has been tested against the four families of provisions where the test can be run. The emergency architecture of Part Eighteen and the preventive-detention compromise of Article 22 were defended on the floor, in August and September 1949, by reference to the violence and administrative collapse of 1947 and 1948. The citizenship Part froze the migration's own administrative dates, 1 March 1947 and 19 July 1948, into Articles 6 and 7, and layered its universalism over migrant, returnee and diaspora strata that only the division explains. The language settlement of 14 September 1949 bought time with a fifteen-year English bridge while Punjab and Bengal, the two divided provinces, burned with language politics, and wrote a stateless refugee language, Sindhi, into the Eighth Schedule. The minority clauses of Articles 25 to 30 were defended by K.M. Munshi in December 1948 as reassurance to the Muslims who had remained, in the same season in which the Assembly dismantled the political safeguards of August 1947 and rebuilt protection on cultural ground. Behind all four families stands the federal redesign of the summer of 1947, when the Union Powers Committee reversed itself between April and July and B.N. Rau's notes recorded a constitution being re-planned for a smaller, frightened, governable India.

Granville Austin's founders remain the heroes of this account, and their craftsmanship is not diminished by the determinant reading; it is specified. Craftsmanship is always exercised on material, and the material of the Indian founding was a divided subcontinent, fifteen million displaced persons by the highest careful estimates, two new borders, a war in Kashmir and a capital full of refugees. Niraja Gopal Jayal's reading, defended here, locates the founding's meaning in that material without reducing the founders to its prisoners: at each calibrated clause, a named founder chose the setting, stated the reason, and carried the chamber. The Constitution that commenced on 26 January 1950 is therefore best read as the Partition generation's double achievement: a universal charter of rights and citizenship, and a security settlement for the state that charter had to survive inside. Flatten either half into context and the other half becomes unintelligible. Read the founding through Partition, and the whole text comes into focus at once.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Partition affect the Indian Constitution?

Partition affected the Constitution as a determinant rather than a backdrop. The Assembly lost its members from territories that became Pakistan in August 1947 and shrank from 389 to 299 members. Its federal blueprint reversed between April and July 1947, from autonomous units holding residuary powers to a strong centre. The citizenship clauses were written around the migration, with the permit date of 19 July 1948 in Article 6 and the cut-off of 1 March 1947 in Article 7. The emergency provisions were defended in 1949 by reference to the violence of 1947, and the minority clauses were framed as reassurance for the Muslims who remained in India.

### Q: What does Article 5 say about citizenship?

Article 5 confers citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution on every person who had a domicile in the territory of India and met one of three further conditions: the person was born in that territory, or either parent was born in that territory, or the person had been ordinarily resident there for not less than five years immediately before commencement. The layered alternatives of birth, descent and residence were deliberate. Partition had destroyed documents and scattered families, so a single strict test would have excluded displaced people the republic intended to include. Article 5 supplied the general rule to which Articles 6, 7 and 8 then added the migration-specific cases.

### Q: Why did the Constituent Assembly reorganise after August 1947?

The Assembly reorganised because Partition changed its membership, its legal mandate and its design problem at once. Members elected from territories assigned to Pakistan withdrew to form a separate Constituent Assembly in Karachi, reducing the Indian body to 299 members and forcing its committees to be reconstituted. The Indian Independence Act of 18 July 1947 made the Assembly sovereign and freed it from the Cabinet Mission Plan's three-tier federation with its grouping scheme. The federal scheme had to be redesigned for a smaller union, a task B.N. Rau's notes of August 1947 record in progress, and the Union Powers Committee had already reported for a strong centre on 5 July 1947.

### Q: How did Partition shape the emergency provisions?

The emergency provisions of Articles 352 to 360 were debated in August and September 1949 by members who had governed through the Punjab violence, the Delhi emergency administration of September 1947, the Kashmir war from October 1947 and the Hyderabad action of September 1948. That experience shaped the adopted architecture: a presidential proclamation for war, external aggression or internal disturbance, parliamentary approval within two months, and the conversion of the federation into a unitary system for the emergency's duration. Critics such as H.V. Kamath warned against concentrating such power, and Ambedkar defended the design as federal in normal times and unitary when survival required, a defence drawn directly from the founding years.

### Q: Did Partition affect the language settlement in the Constitution?

Partition affected the language settlement through the two provinces it divided. In East Punjab, Urdu's departure left Hindi and Punjabi facing each other, and the Sikh demand for a Punjabi-speaking province made the script and language questions matters of communal survival by 1949. In Bengal, division split the Bengali-speaking world, while East Pakistan's Urdu-only policy, proclaimed in 1948, showed how explosive a language clause could be. The Assembly's answer, adopted on 14 September 1949, made Hindi in Devanagari the official language under Article 343 while continuing English for fifteen years, buying time for every one of those fires, and the Eighth Schedule included Sindhi, the language of refugees whose province lay outside India.

### Q: What was the role of B.N. Rau in the Constituent Assembly?

B.N. Rau was the Constitutional Adviser to the Constituent Assembly, appointed before the Assembly first sat in December 1946. He prepared the initial draft of the Constitution in October 1947, travelled to the United States, Canada, Ireland and Britain in the autumn of 1947 to consult jurists on questions including judicial review and due process, and advised the Drafting Committee throughout its work. For this article his August 1947 notes are the key document: preserved in his papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, they record the reorganisation of the federal scheme that Partition forced, worked through in the weeks after the plan of 3 June 1947.

### Q: How did Partition shape Article 25 on religion?

Article 25 guarantees freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, and the propagation word survived because of Partition. When the Assembly debated the clause on 6 and 7 December 1948, recorded in Volume VII of the Debates, opponents argued that propagation licensed conversion by inducement. K.M. Munshi defended retaining the word as part of the assurances given to the minorities, so that the roughly thirty-five million Muslims who had remained in India could trust that the new state would protect the public life of their faith. The clause was therefore framed as a confidence measure for a community whose insecurity after 1947 was documented and recent.

### Q: What did Article 6 grant to people who migrated from Pakistan to India, and why does it name 19 July 1948?

Article 6 made a person who migrated to India from Pakistan a citizen if that person, or a parent or grandparent, was born in India as defined by the Government of India Act of 1935. Migrants who arrived before 19 July 1948 qualified on that basis alone. Migrants who arrived on or after that date also had to be registered, after six months of residence in India, by an officer appointed for the purpose. The date marks the introduction of the permit system controlling movement from West Pakistan in July 1948. The Constitution thereby froze an administrative instrument of the migration into the legal boundary between two classes of citizen.

### Q: What did Article 7 say about people who had migrated to Pakistan after 1 March 1947?

Article 7 provided that a person who had migrated from India to Pakistan after 1 March 1947 was not to be deemed a citizen of India, even if that person satisfied Article 5, with one exception. A migrant who returned to India under a permit for resettlement or permanent return could be registered as a citizen after six months of residence, on the Article 6 basis. The cut-off date marked the onset of the Punjab disturbances that preceded formal Partition. The clause governed the hard cases of 1948 and 1949: families, many of them Muslim, who had fled in fear and then sought to come home to houses and livelihoods in India.

### Q: What does Article 8 provide for persons of Indian origin living outside India?

Article 8 allows a person who, or whose parent or grandparent, was born in India as defined by the Government of India Act of 1935, and who is ordinarily residing in a country outside India, to be deemed a citizen of India if registered by an Indian diplomatic or consular representative in that country, on an application made in the prescribed form. The provision reached across the new borders and across the older diaspora at once. It recognised that Indian belonging in 1949 included people whom Partition and earlier emigration had placed outside the territory, and it made registration, rather than residence, the act that converted origin into citizenship for that class.

### Q: How did Articles 10 and 11 leave citizenship open to Parliament, and what did the Citizenship Act of 1955 do?

Article 10 continues every citizen's status subject to any law Parliament may make, and Article 11 gives Parliament plenary power to legislate on the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to it. The founders thereby made the constitutional settlement of Articles 5 to 9 a commencement settlement, not a permanent code. Parliament used the power in the Citizenship Act of 1955, which set out acquisition by birth, descent, registration and naturalisation and the modes of termination, and later amendments, including those of 1986 and 2003, narrowed citizenship by birth and addressed migration from neighbouring countries. The founding dates of 1947 and 1948 thus became the first entries in a statutory argument that has never closed.

### Q: What did the Indian Independence Act of 1947 do in law?

The Indian Independence Act, which received royal assent on 18 July 1947, created two independent dominions, India and Pakistan, from 15 August 1947. It ended the suzerainty of the British Crown over the princely states and terminated the treaties on which paramountcy rested, abolished the office of Secretary of State for India, and provided for the partition of Bengal and Punjab subject to the boundary awards. Section 8 vested each dominion's Constituent Assembly with full legislative power for that dominion, free of the reservation and disallowance controls of earlier constitutional law. The Act is therefore the legal hinge of the founding: the same statute that divided British India made the Assembly that remained in New Delhi sovereign.

### Q: Who drew the Radcliffe Line, and when was the boundary award published?

Cyril Radcliffe, a London barrister who had not previously visited India, chaired the boundary commissions for Bengal and Punjab in July and August 1947. Each commission contained judges nominated by Congress and by the Muslim League, who deadlocked on most disputed districts, leaving the line to Radcliffe's own decision. He signed the awards on 12 and 13 August 1947, and they were published on 17 August 1947, two days after independence, Mountbatten having held them back so that the award would not shadow the transfer celebrations. Radcliffe left India on the day of publication and never returned. Districts such as Gurdaspur and Ferozepore, whose allocation carried road access to Kashmir and canal headworks with it, remained disputed in public memory long after the legal question closed.

### Q: What did the Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947 decide, and how was it accepted?

The plan announced by Viceroy Mountbatten on 3 June 1947 proposed the partition of British India where the provincial legislatures of Bengal and Punjab so voted, referendums in the North-West Frontier Province and Sylhet, the continuation of the Constituent Assembly for the territories remaining in the Indian Union, and the transfer of power in August 1947 rather than June 1948. The All India Congress Committee accepted the plan by resolution on 14 and 15 June 1947, with Govind Ballabh Pant among its movers, on the stated ground that it offered the only route to a strong central government for the India that remained. The Muslim League Council also accepted it. Acceptance converted the Assembly from a body designing a united federation into one designing a divided country's constitution.

### Q: What do Articles 358 and 359 suspend during an emergency, and how do they differ?

Article 358 provides that, while a proclamation of emergency declaring that the security of India is threatened by war or external aggression is in operation, the freedoms of Article 19 are automatically restricted, so that the state may legislate and act notwithstanding them. Article 359 works differently: it empowers the President to suspend, by order, the right to move any court for the enforcement of such Fundamental Rights as the order specifies, for the emergency's duration. The distinction is between automatic restriction of one article's freedoms in a war emergency and a discretionary suspension of remedies for named rights in any emergency. The Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978 later placed Articles 20 and 21 beyond the reach of an Article 359 order.

### Q: How do Articles 29 and 30 protect minority culture and education beyond the religious clauses?

Article 29 protects any section of citizens residing in India with a distinct language, script or culture in its right to conserve it, and it bars denial of admission to state or state-aided educational institutions on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language. Article 30 gives all minorities, whether based on religion or language, the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice, and bars discrimination against minority-managed institutions in the granting of state aid. Together the clauses moved minority protection from the political register of reserved seats, which the Assembly abandoned for religious minorities in May 1949, to the cultural and educational register, where a community's language and schools could be defended as rights rather than negotiated as shares of power.

### Q: Why did India adopt a single citizenship instead of citizenship of each state?

The Constitution provides one citizenship for the whole Union and no citizenship of a state, although India is federal. The choice suited a founding in which people were moving across internal boundaries in millions and in which several hundred princely states had just been integrated into provinces and unions of states. A dual citizenship, on the American pattern, would have let each unit define its own members at the very moment when the republic needed belonging to be portable and uniform, so that a refugee registered in Delhi carried the same status in Bombay. The single citizenship also matched the strong-centre design the Union Powers Committee adopted in July 1947, and Article 11 left the whole subject adjustable by one Parliament.

### Q: What did the Nehru-Liaquat Pact of 8 April 1950 promise the minorities of India and Pakistan?

The pact, signed in Delhi by Jawaharlal Nehru and Pakistan's Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan after the Bengal crisis of early 1950, guaranteed minorities in each dominion complete equality of citizenship irrespective of religion, security of life, property and personal honour, freedom of movement, occupation, speech and worship, and a share in public appointments consistent with those guarantees. It provided for commissions of inquiry into the recent disturbances and for minority commissions to monitor implementation, and it pledged both governments to restore abducted women and looted property and to discourage propaganda against the other dominion. In India its constitutional mirror was the settlement of Articles 25 to 30. Shyama Prasad Mookerjee resigned from the cabinet over the pact, judging its promises unenforceable in East Bengal.

### Q: What did the Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act of 1949 do?

The Act, passed in December 1949, implemented the Inter-Dominion commitments to recover persons abducted during the Partition violence. It defined an abducted person broadly, covering women and girls taken from their families and children born in the aftermath, and it empowered police and designated officers to recover such persons, place them in camps, and restore them across the border, with tribunals deciding disputed cases. The recovery operation, in which social workers such as Mridula Sarabhai served in Punjab, restored thousands of women in both directions over several years. The Act remains a hard document for any account of the founding, because the state frequently decided a woman's belonging against her stated wish, treating the recovery of citizens as a duty owed to families and communities as much as to the woman herself.

### Q: What was the evacuee property regime, and how did it shape belonging after Partition?

When Muslims left India for Pakistan and Hindus and Sikhs left Pakistan for India, each side left houses, shops, factories and land behind. From 1949, ordinances and then the Administration of Evacuee Property Act of 1950 vested this abandoned property in a government Custodian, who could allot it to incoming refugees. The regime began as trusteeship in case owners returned, and hardened, as return became unlikely, into permanent redistribution that shaped the property map of cities such as Delhi, Lucknow and Amritsar. The regime mattered constitutionally because it decided the material content of belonging. A family allotted an evacuee house in Karol Bagh received, in bricks, the citizenship that Articles 5 to 11 conferred in words, while the departed owner's claim faded from a right of return into a file in the Custodian's office.
