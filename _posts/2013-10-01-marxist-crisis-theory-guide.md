Crisis theory is the one part of Marxist economics where the argument among Marxists themselves has never closed. Ask what Marx held about the value of a commodity, about exploitation, or about the historical role of the factory, and a reasonably settled answer can be assembled from finished texts he prepared for print. Ask why capitalism periodically breaks down into crisis, and the answer dissolves into a quarrel that has run for well over a century, because the founder left the materials for several answers and the verdict for none. His writings supply a theory of falling profitability, a theory of goods produced and never sold, a theory of industries growing out of proportion to one another, a theory of wages rising until profits buckle, and a theory of credit expanding until the paper structure collapses. Each theory has distinguished defenders. Several contradict one another outright. This guide maps the whole field, gives each family its strongest form, and then applies the one device that separates them cleanly.

![Marxist crisis theory, the five families of crisis explanation, profit production proportion and money - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-59.webp)

That device deserves a name at the outset, because everything in the pages ahead is organised around it. The device is the wage test. Take any argument that claims to explain capitalist crisis in Marxist terms and ask a single question of it: what does the argument predict will follow when real wages rise? The families answer differently, and two of them answer in flatly opposite directions. The profitability tradition reads a sustained rise in wages as a compression of the profit share that brings breakdown closer. The underconsumption tradition reads the same rise as a strengthening of working class demand that pushes breakdown further away. Both cannot be describing the same mechanism, and an exposition that blends them into one layered story, as many popular presentations do, has merged two rival engines and called the merger a theory. Learning to run the wage test on any crisis argument, including the ones in this guide, is the skill the guide exists to teach.

The scholarly situation behind the quarrel is worth grasping before any mechanism is examined. Marx planned a systematic treatment of political economy in six books, covering capital, landed property, wage labour, the state, foreign trade, and the world market with its crises. Only the first book was substantially begun, and even it was never completed to his satisfaction. Crisis therefore appears in his work the way weather appears in a diary: constantly, vividly, and always in the middle of something else. It is analysed inside a chapter on money, inside a set of reproduction schemas, inside a discussion of interest and banking, inside a long manuscript critique of Ricardo. Anyone who announces a single Marxist theory of crisis has performed an act of assembly, choosing some passages as load bearing and demoting others to decoration. Honest scholarship names the assembly it has chosen. That discipline, naming the reconstruction rather than pretending to quote a finished doctrine, is the thread this guide follows from its first section to its last.

A second preliminary distinction matters just as much. In this tradition, the word crisis does not mean a recession in the ordinary journalistic sense of two difficult quarters. It names a violent reconciliation: a moment when claims accumulated during the boom, debts contracted, factories built, inventories stocked, turn out to exceed what the underlying production of value can honour, and the excess is destroyed in a rush through bankruptcies, unemployment, idle capacity, and collapsing prices. Crisis, on this understanding, is not an interruption of the system's normal working but one of its working parts, the periodic purge by which disproportion and overexpansion are forcibly corrected. Whether that picture is right is a separate question, and this guide will give the rival pictures, Marxist and non-Marxist alike, their full hearing. But a reader who imports the mild everyday meaning of the word will misunderstand every family in the grid, because each of them is explaining the violent event, not the mild one.

## Crisis theory and the missing book

Begin with the documentary record, since the record explains the disagreement better than any doctrinal summary can. Marx referred to crisis constantly across forty years of writing. The Communist Manifesto of 1848 already speaks of commercial crises that periodically destroy not only existing products but large parts of the productive forces themselves, and it treats those crises as evidence that bourgeois relations have grown too narrow for the wealth they generate. His journalism of the eighteen fifties watches the crisis of 1857 unfold across continents and treats it as a confirmation that the phenomenon is systemic rather than local. The notebooks of 1857 and 1858, later published as the Grundrisse, return to breakdown repeatedly while sketching the architecture of a critique that was still taking shape. Yet when the mature work appeared, crisis had no chapter of its own. The first volume of Capital treats it mainly through the theory of money. The second volume, published by Engels after Marx died, embeds it in the analysis of reproduction. The third volume, assembled from manuscripts of the eighteen sixties, contains both the most famous crisis mechanism in the tradition and a long, unfinished engagement with banking and credit. The systematic volume on the world market and its crises, projected in letter after letter, was never written at all.

### Did Marx have a single theory of crisis?

No. His writings contain material for several distinct explanations, including falling profitability, disproportionality between sectors, realisation failure, and credit instability. He never assembled them into one account, which is why the tradition contains rival crisis theories, each claiming a different mechanism, rather than one.

The pattern will be familiar to readers of this series, because it recurs across Marx's intellectual estate. The chapter on classes that was to conclude the third volume of Capital breaks off after little more than a page, just as the definition of class is about to be attempted. The projected book on the state exists only as an entry in a publishing plan. Again and again, the point where the system most needs a finished statement is the point where the manuscripts fall silent, and the silence becomes a seedbed: every later school plants its own reconstruction in the gap and tends it as the authentic growth. Crisis theory is the largest of these seedbeds. The falling rate of profit school reads the third volume's third part as the buried masterpiece. The realisation school reads the second volume's schemas as the true centre. The credit school reads the third volume's fifth part, on interest bearing capital, as the prophetic text. Each reading is grounded in genuine pages. None can excommunicate the others on textual authority alone, because the author who might have ranked the mechanisms died with the ranking unwritten.

Understanding this changes how the literature should be read. A monograph announcing the Marxist theory of crisis is usually a brief for one family wearing the whole tradition's coat. Its citations will be accurate and its selection silent. The reader's defence is to ask, of every such book, which passages it has promoted and which it has quietly left in the archive, and whether the demoted passages might support a rival engine. That habit of auditing selections is worth more than memorising any single mechanism, because the mechanisms themselves are not especially difficult once they are separated. What is difficult, and what separates serious commentary from pamphleteering, is keeping the separation visible while each theory is being expounded with sympathy. Sympathy without separation produces the familiar blended account in which profits fall, goods pile up unsold, sectors mismatch, wages squeeze, and banks collapse, all at once, in a fog where everything causes everything and nothing could ever count as evidence against the story. This guide refuses the fog. Each family gets its own section, its own proponents, its own prediction, and its own stated conditions of failure.

One more feature of the documentary record deserves emphasis, because it disciplines everything that follows. Marx distinguished, with unusual care, between showing that crisis is possible within a money economy and explaining why crisis is necessary, why it must erupt at a particular point in the cycle. The first task he completed, in the manuscripts on theories of surplus value, as part of his demolition of the doctrine associated with Jean-Baptiste Say and adopted by Ricardo: the doctrine that production automatically generates the demand for its own output, so that general overproduction is impossible. Against that doctrine Marx showed that selling and buying are separate acts, divided in time and space once money mediates exchange, and that nothing guarantees their reunion. A gap can open. But a gap that can open is not a gap that must open, and Marx knew the difference perfectly well, because he mocked economists who mistook the abstract possibility of crisis for its explanation. The families in this guide are, every one of them, attempts to supply the missing necessity: to name the mechanism that forces the possible rupture into actual occurrence. Judged as a set, they are five different completions of one unfinished argument, and the wage test is the instrument that keeps the completions from being mistaken for each other.

## What counts as a crisis in the tradition

Before the families can be compared, the phenomenon itself needs a steadier definition than journalism provides, because rival theories sometimes disagree about what they are explaining. Strip the accounts to their shared core and a capitalist crisis, in the Marxist literature, has four marks. Production or its financing expands beyond the limits set by profitable sale. The excess is not gently absorbed but violently written off, in failures, defaults, and destroyed capacity. Employment contracts sharply as firms cut output and labour together. And the destruction itself restores the conditions for a new expansion, by cheapening assets, disciplining wages, and clearing inventories, so that the cycle can begin again from a lower base. The fourth mark is the distinctive one. Mainstream accounts typically treat recovery as the return of health after an illness. The Marxist families, for all their differences, tend to treat the crisis itself as the cure the system administers to its own accumulated imbalances, brutal in method and incomplete in result, since the conditions that produced the imbalance are reproduced by the recovery it makes possible.

### What separates a crisis from an ordinary downturn in this literature?

Severity and function. An ordinary downturn is a pause in growth; a crisis in this literature is a forcible liquidation in which debts are defaulted on, firms fail in waves, capacity stands idle, and the values of assets are written down until profitable production can resume on a cleared field.

The distinction carries a consequence for evidence. If crisis is defined by the liquidation function, then episodes differ in kind and not merely in depth, and a theory that predicts the mild episodes may still fail on the violent ones. The long controversy over whether the breakdowns of the nineteenth century, the catastrophe of the nineteen thirties, and the financial collapse examined later in this series are instances of one phenomenon or of several is partly a controversy about this definition. Writers in the profitability tradition see a single rhythm underneath: expansion strains the production of surplus value, the strain surfaces somewhere, and the liquidation follows. Writers in the credit tradition are more willing to treat each episode as stamped by its own financial architecture. The definitional question is therefore not pedantry. It decides what a theory must predict, and a theory held to the violent episodes faces a harder test than one allowed to claim every quarterly dip as a confirmation.

There is also a temporal point. The families disagree about where in the cycle the decisive strain builds. Profitability accounts locate the rot in the boom's later stages, as mechanisation raises the ratio of machinery to labour across the economy. Underconsumption accounts locate it much earlier, in the chronic gap between what workers can buy and what industry can produce, a gap the boom temporarily papers over with investment demand and credit. Disproportionality accounts locate it in the pattern of expansion itself, since uncoordinated investment decisions guarantee that some sectors overshoot. Credit accounts locate it in the financing layer, where obligations multiply faster than the earnings meant to service them. A single observed boom can thus be read four ways before it breaks, and the readings matter because they identify different leading indicators: the profit rate, the consumption share, the sectoral pattern, the debt structure. The grid later in this guide lines these indicators up side by side, and the worked examples further on show them being applied to the same historical episode with different results.

Finally, the tradition's own vocabulary needs one clarification. Breakdown, collapse, and crisis are not synonyms in the careful literature, however often they are merged in polemic. Crisis is the recurrent event. Breakdown, in the technical sense given to it during the controversy examined below, is a terminal event: a failure from which accumulation cannot restart because its own laws have made further profitable production impossible. Marx's texts support the recurrent event richly and the terminal event thinly, a point on which the scholarly consensus is unusually broad, extending to many writers who defend other parts of the crisis literature warmly. Confusing the two has damaged the tradition's credibility more than any external criticism, because predictions of imminent terminal breakdown have been issued repeatedly since the eighteen nineties and the system has repeatedly declined to oblige. This guide keeps the two words apart throughout, and the section on the breakdown controversy explains how the terminal doctrine was constructed, by whom, and why most of the tradition eventually let it go.

## The possibility of crisis inside a money economy

The oldest stratum of Marx's crisis thinking is also the most modest, and its modesty is the point. In a barter exchange, giving and receiving are one act: the shoemaker hands over shoes and takes corn in the same gesture, and a general failure of exchange is hard to picture because every sale is simultaneously a purchase. Money dissolves that unity. Once a commodity is sold for money, the seller holds a general claim on society's products and may exercise it later, elsewhere, or not at all. Sale and purchase drift apart in time and place. The circuit of commodities, which Marx writes as commodity to money to commodity, can snap at its middle link, with money hoarded, withheld, or simply not forthcoming because a previous link in somebody else's circuit has already failed. Add money's second monetary function, its role as means of payment, and the web tightens: producers sell against promises, promises chain firms together, and one default can travel along the chain converting private embarrassment into general stoppage. Crisis, at this level of analysis, is possible the moment exchange is mediated by money. No theory of profit, wages, or machinery has yet been needed.

### Why does the existence of money by itself make crisis possible?

Because money separates the act of selling from the act of buying. A producer can sell and then wait, hoard, or fail to receive payment owed from an earlier sale, so chains of transactions that barter kept fused can break at many points, and one break can propagate through payments owed between firms.

Marx developed this argument while dismantling the doctrine later summarised as Say's law, the claim that supply creates its own demand and that general gluts are therefore impossible, since producing a commodity is simultaneously creating the income with which other commodities are bought. The manuscripts posthumously published as Theories of Surplus Value attack that doctrine at its strongest point, Ricardo's version of it. Ricardo reasoned from barter, where the identity of sale and purchase holds, to a money economy, where it does not. Marx's counter was that the identity had been smuggled in: money is not a transparent veil over barter but a restructuring of exchange that introduces delay, hoarding, and the independent movement of prices and debts. His celebrated sneer at economists who defined away crisis by abstraction belongs to this stratum. Denying the possibility of general overproduction, he suggested, required treating a capitalist economy as if it were a Robinson Crusoe arrangement writ large, with the money form quietly deleted whenever it became inconvenient.

The distinction that emerges here, between the possibility of crisis and its necessity, is the single most useful precision in the entire cluster of articles around this one, and researchers treat it as the entry point to the serious literature for good reason. Possibility says the institutional form permits rupture. Necessity says some mechanism internal to accumulation compels rupture at intervals, so that crisis is not an accident that policy or luck might permanently prevent but a recurrent product of the system's own motion. Marx completed the first argument and deliberately refused to pretend he had completed the second merely by stating the first. He was scathing about the tautological comfort of explaining crises by scarcity of money or by the mere fact that sales sometimes fail, and in the same manuscripts he insisted that world market crises must be understood as the real concentration of all the contradictions of bourgeois production, a formulation that promises a unified account while deferring its delivery. The five families are rival deliveries of that promise. Each accepts the possibility established against Say. Each then nominates a different contradiction as the one that periodically forces its way to the surface.

Holding the distinction steady prevents two common errors. The first error upgrades possibility into proof: a writer shows that selling and buying can separate, declares crisis explained, and treats every downturn as a footnote. Marx himself blocked this move in advance, which is why citing the separation passages as his crisis theory misrepresents him. The second error runs the other way, demanding that any respectable theory derive the exact timing of the next collapse from first principles, a demand no rival school of cycle theory meets either. Between those errors sits the actual scholarly question, which is comparative: which proposed mechanism best matches the sequence of events in documented crises, the behaviour of profits, wages, output, and debt as expansion gives way to contraction. That comparative question is empirical, and it is the reason the families can be graded against evidence rather than merely recited. The wage test belongs to this comparative stage. It does not ask which family sounds most Marxist. It asks what each family commits its holder to expect, starting with the variable on which they most visibly disagree.

## Why possibility is not an explanation

A short section on method is warranted here, because the necessity question decides how every family should be judged. Consider what a mechanism must do to earn its place. It must identify a variable that deteriorates as the boom proceeds, deteriorates because the boom proceeds, and deteriorates far enough to flip expansion into contraction without help from outside shocks. Weather, war, and policy blunders can trigger individual downturns in any theory; a Marxist crisis mechanism is interesting only if it shows the boom manufacturing its own reversal. By that standard, the separation of sale and purchase fails as an explanation while succeeding as a precondition, since nothing in the mere existence of money makes the separation widen on a schedule. The standard is demanding, and several celebrated Marxist arguments fail it on inspection, including some versions of the realisation story that assume the demand gap they were meant to derive. Applying the standard impartially is the best antidote to the fog described earlier. Each family below is therefore presented in a fixed order: the variable it watches, the reason the boom degrades that variable, the form the rupture takes, and the evidence that would embarrass it.

The same standard clarifies what the families share, because beneath their quarrels they hold a common conviction that distinguishes them from most rival cycle theories. They treat the downturn as endogenous, generated by the expansion itself, rather than as a meteor strike from outside the economy. Shocks exist in these accounts, harvests fail and wars interrupt, but shocks explain timing at most, never the underlying fragility that a shock exposes. A system in rude health shrugs off a bad harvest; a system stretched by its own success converts a modest trigger into a general liquidation. This shared conviction is the tradition's real common core, more fundamental than any particular mechanism, and it explains why the families can quarrel so bitterly while remaining recognisably one tradition. Outsiders sometimes miss this and describe Marxist crisis theory as a prediction machine for dates. Insiders know it is better understood as a family of arguments about why capitalist expansions carry the seeds of their interruption, with the quarrel located in which seed matters most.

## The first family: falling profitability and overaccumulation

Pride of place in popular presentations belongs to the profitability family, and its textual home is the third part of the third volume of Capital, where Marx develops the law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall. The dedicated treatment of that mechanism, its mathematics, its counteracting influences, and the century of technical dispute around it, sits in the companion article on [the tendency of the rate of profit to fall](/2013/10/15/tendency-rate-of-profit-to-fall/), and the disambiguation matters: this pillar describes the family's role among its rivals and does not re-derive the law. The core intuition can nonetheless be stated plainly. Competition forces each firm to mechanise, substituting machinery and materials for living labour. Only living labour, on Marx's value theory, produces new value and hence surplus value. As the mass of capital grows faster than the labour it employs, the ratio of surplus value to total capital advanced is pressed downward, even while the total mass of profit may still be rising. The rate of profit trends down across the long development of the system, checked and interrupted by counteracting influences that Marx himself catalogued, from cheaper machinery to foreign trade to the depression of wages below their value.

How does a falling trend in profitability become an acute crisis rather than a gentle squeeze? The family's answer runs through overaccumulation. Capital accumulates in search of profit; as the rate declines, a growing share of the accumulated stock can find no employment at the customary rate of return. Marx's striking formulation in the third volume is that the real barrier of capitalist production is capital itself: production expands as if the only limit were the productive forces, while the conditions for valorising the product lag behind, until the discrepancy discharges itself in a crisis that idles factories and workers side by side. Idled factories beside idle workers is the family's signature image, because it captures the absurdity the theory claims to explain, a society stopping production not from scarcity but from an excess of capital relative to profitable outlets. Henryk Grossmann gave this line its most rigorous early development in his 1929 study of accumulation and breakdown, arguing that Otto Bauer's reproduction schemas, extended far enough, show surplus value eventually insufficient to valorise the accumulated stock while maintaining capitalist consumption, so that breakdown follows from the arithmetic of accumulation itself. Paul Mattick carried the argument into the postwar decades, and later writers in this line, among them Andrew Kliman and Anwar Shaikh in their different idioms, have continued to place profitability at the centre, Shaikh with particular emphasis on turbulent competition as the transmission belt between falling profitability and general disorder.

### Does a falling profit rate by itself explain the timing of a crisis?

Not without help. The law states a tendency operating over long periods, interrupted by counteracting influences, so profitability writers add an overaccumulation trigger: capital eventually exceeds what can be employed at the going rate, investment halts abruptly, and the halt converts a slow squeeze into a sudden general stoppage.

Apply the wage test and the family shows its profile at once. A rise in real wages, for this tradition, is not the root cause of anything, since the root cause sits in the composition of capital built up over decades. But a wage rise does compress the share of profit in new value, and where profitability is already strained, compression hastens the moment when investment ceases to pay. The family therefore predicts that crises gestate during periods of rising labour costs relative to productivity and that they frequently break shortly after profitability peaks, with the wage movement acting as an accelerant on a fire built by mechanisation. Note the distance from the profit squeeze family, examined below, for which the wage rise is the fire itself. The two families agree on the sign of the wage effect and disagree completely about its rank in the causal order, a nuance the wage test reveals only when its follow up question is asked: not merely what wages do, but how much of the story they carry.

The family's stated conditions of failure deserve equal clarity. If profitability could fall for generations while accumulation proceeded smoothly, with crises arriving only from external shocks, the mechanism would be refuted in practice whatever its textual pedigree. The theorem published by Nobuo Okishio in 1961 pressed a sharper internal challenge: if real wages stay constant, cost cutting technical changes that individual capitalists would actually adopt raise, rather than lower, the general rate of profit, so the law as popularly stated needs repair or restriction. Defenders have replied with alternative value frameworks, with the distinction between the rate on new investment and the average rate, and with long run statistical reconstructions, and the argument remains one of the most technically alive in Marxist economics. For the purposes of this pillar, what matters is the family's scientific posture: it is the most formally specified of the five, the most exposed to measurement, and correspondingly the one whose defenders and critics can most nearly agree about what the data would have to show. The companion article on the empirical record takes up that measurement dispute in full.

## The second family: realisation failure

Surplus value has to survive two journeys, and the first family watches the first of them, its production. The second family watches the second journey, its realisation: the sale of the finished commodities at prices that convert embodied surplus value into money profit. Production can proceed flawlessly while realisation fails. Warehouses fill with goods embodying value that nobody will purchase at remunerative prices, and the failure then travels backward, halting production lines whose technical operation was never in question. The textual home of this family is the second volume of Capital, with its analysis of the circulation of capital and its reproduction schemas dividing social production into a department making means of production and a department making articles of consumption. For commodities to be realised, each department's output must find buyers in the right proportions and at the right moment, and the purchasing power must actually be exercised. Anything that interrupts the monetary circuit, a credit contraction, a collapse of investment demand, a shortfall of consumption, leaves value stranded in commodity form, and stranded value is the seed of crisis in this family's telling.

### What exactly fails when writers speak of a realisation crisis?

The sale fails, not the making. Commodities leave the factory embodying surplus value, but buyers with money do not appear in sufficient number at prices covering costs plus the expected profit, so value stays locked in unsold stock, payments downstream are missed, and production is cut back in response.

Rosa Luxemburg gave the family its most famous and most contested development in The Accumulation of Capital of 1913. Studying the reproduction schemas, she asked who buys the portion of the social product that embodies accumulated surplus value, if workers can buy back only the equivalent of their wages and capitalists personally consume only part of the remainder. Her answer was that an expanding capitalism requires demand from outside itself, from non-capitalist strata and territories, and that imperialism is the political form of that requirement. When the outside is exhausted, realisation fails systemically. The argument drew heavyweight criticism from within the movement almost immediately: Nikolai Bukharin replied that the schemas themselves demonstrate expanded reproduction to be formally possible, since investment demand from the expanding capital goods department can absorb the surplus product, and that Luxemburg had confused a difficulty of exposition with an impossibility in the object. Otto Bauer's schema based reply made a similar point arithmetically. Most later scholars treat Luxemburg's specific solution as mistaken while crediting her question, where the effective demand for a growing output originates, as one of the permanent questions of macroeconomics, later given a different answer by Keynes. The episode matters for this guide because it shows a family being corrected by its own side on the evidence of the shared texts, an episode to remember when the unfalsifiability objection arrives below.

Realisation in its general form should be kept distinct from underconsumption, its best known variant, though the two are popularly merged. The general claim is that the sale of the social product at valorising prices is a fragile achievement requiring definite proportions and a definite flow of money demand. The failure can originate on the investment side, when firms stop ordering machinery, as easily as on the consumption side. Underconsumption narrows the claim: the binding constraint is specifically the restricted consumption of the mass of the population, whose wages are held below the value of the consumption goods the system can produce. On the narrow claim, capitalism chronically manufactures more consumption capacity than its own wage bill can clear, and crisis is the periodic surfacing of that chronic gap. The narrowing changes the politics and the predictions alike, because a theory blaming investment volatility and a theory blaming low wages prescribe opposite remedies and opposite readings of the same statistics. The grid in this guide therefore scores underconsumption as a distinct row inside the realisation family's territory: one family by descent, two engines by logic, with the wage test applying differently to each, as the next section shows. The vocabulary of the two neighbouring concepts receives its own treatment in the companion article on [overproduction and underconsumption](/2013/11/01/overproduction-and-underconsumption/), which untangles the terms as Marx and his critics used them.

## Underconsumption and the founder's awkward footnote

Underconsumption has ancestors older than Marxism: Sismondi argued at the start of the nineteenth century that production outrunning popular income produces gluts, and J. A. Hobson later gave the doctrine a liberal form linking underconsumption to imperialism. Within the Marxist tradition the variant drew strength from an obvious moral arithmetic. Workers produce the wealth; workers receive back a fraction of it as wages; the fraction cannot purchase the whole; therefore the whole cannot be sold. Paul Sweezy's The Theory of Capitalist Development of 1942, for decades the standard English language exposition of Marxist economics, assigned underconsumption a central place while handling it with care, and the later monopoly capital school built an entire stagnation theory upon the tendency of the surplus to rise faster than the outlets for absorbing it. That school's own account, and the way it converts chronic demand shortage into a theory of slow growth punctuated by speculative episodes, is treated in the companion article on [monopoly capital stagnation theory](/2014/02/01/monopoly-capital-stagnation-theory/), and is counted in this guide as the sixth account standing alongside the classic five.

The embarrassment for the simple version of the argument sits in Marx's own text, and honest exposition must put it on the table rather than step around it. In the twentieth chapter of the second volume, discussing the reproduction of the money material and the views of earlier economists, Marx directly attacks the explanation of crises by underconsumption of the masses. He calls it a pure tautology to say that crises arise from the scarcity of solvent consumers, and he observes that the system attempts to overcome that scarcity in ways the doctrine ignores. Worse for the simple doctrine, he notes the empirical sequence runs the wrong way: crises are regularly prepared by periods in which wages generally rise and the working class receives a relatively greater share of the consumption fund, exactly when, on the crude theory, demand shortage should be easing. If low consumption as such caused crises, the timing of booms and slumps would be inverted relative to what is observed. Defenders of the variant reply that the tautology passage targets a superficial moralising version, that a sophisticated underconsumptionism speaks of proportions between departments rather than absolute popular poverty, and that the rising wages before a crisis are themselves part of the boom whose investment structure is unsustainable. The reply has force, and it illustrates a recurring pattern in this field: a family survives criticism by migrating toward a neighbouring family, in this case toward disproportionality, and the migration quietly concedes the original engine was too weak to run alone.

### Why did Marx himself resist the underconsumption explanation?

He judged the simple version circular and contradicted by the cycle's timing. Explaining crises by a shortage of paying consumers restates the phenomenon instead of deriving it, and the observed sequence, in which breakdowns follow stretches of generally rising wages and fuller working class consumption, points the causal arrow elsewhere.

Run the wage test over this territory and the family's variants separate beautifully. General realisation theory is agnostic about wages: demand failure can strike from the investment side with wages high or low, and the wage movement is at most one channel among several. Strict underconsumption is not agnostic at all. It predicts that rising real wages relieve the system's core difficulty, expanding the consumption fund and postponing breakdown, while stagnant or falling wages deepen the glut. This is precisely the opposite sign to the profitability family's prediction, and it is why the two families cannot both be right about the same episode in their strong forms. An exposition that praises a wage rise for easing demand in one paragraph and blames it for squeezing profits in the next, without noticing the gear change, has switched families mid argument. The switch is common in journalism and in platform oratory, and the wage test exists to catch it. Later formal work in a broadly Marxian spirit, notably the model of Amit Bhaduri and Stephen Marglin distinguishing wage led from profit led growth regimes, gave this opposition an exact modern form: economies differ in whether higher wage shares stimulate or depress demand and accumulation, so the correct answer can vary by place and period, but varying is itself a finding that must be argued from evidence rather than assumed by blending.

## The third family: disproportionality

The third family locates the rupture neither in profitability nor in purchasing power but in pattern. Capitalist production is unplanned across firms while being technically interlocked within them: steel output presupposes definite quantities of coal, machinery, transport, and labour, and consumption goods output presupposes a definite relation to the wage bill and to replacement investment. The reproduction schemas of the second volume display the proportional relations that smooth reproduction would require. Disproportionality theory argues that the anarchy of decentralised investment guarantees systematic violation of those relations: sectors expand on independent expectations, fixed capital is lumpy and long lived, information arrives late, and by the time overshoot in one branch is visible, commitments elsewhere have multiplied it. Crisis is the violent reimposition of proportion, the market's brutal audit of a pattern nobody designed. Mikhail Tugan-Baranovsky, a legal Marxist in the Russian debates, built the earliest systematic version from the English crisis history, arguing that crises arise from the disproportionate distribution of productive forces and that a properly proportioned expansion could in principle proceed without a consumption foundation of the kind underconsumptionists demanded, since machines can for a time be produced to make more machines. Rudolf Hilferding's Finance Capital of 1910 incorporated disproportionality into a wider account of cartelised industry, and the young Lenin, polemicising against Russian populists who doubted capitalism could develop a home market, used proportionality reasoning to argue that the market grows precisely through the expansion of the capital goods department.

### Can wrong proportions between industries by themselves produce a general crisis?

Yes, on this family's account, because the branches are chained together by input requirements and payments. Overshoot in a leading sector strands complementary capacity elsewhere, orders vanish across the chain at once, and the correction takes the form of simultaneous idle plant in industries that each appeared healthy in isolation.

The family's great strength is its fit with the texture of actual downturns, which rarely present as a uniform failure of everything and typically begin in identifiable sectors, construction, capital goods, a fashionable technology, before generalising through orders, credit, and employment. Its great weakness is the mirror image of the underconsumption problem: a theory of wrong proportions must specify right proportions independently of the market process that supposedly violates them, and the specification is delicate, since proportions shift with technique, tastes, and the capital stock itself. Critics add that pure disproportion, without a monetary and credit mechanism, struggles to explain why local overshoot becomes general paralysis rather than a shuffling of resources between branches, painful but partial. Defenders answer that fixed capital cannot shuffle: a half built railway or a specialised mill embodies its proportion in concrete and steel, and its idling destroys value rather than relocating it. That answer, note, recruits the credit family as a transmission mechanism, which is legitimate provided the recruitment is acknowledged: disproportionality as trigger, credit collapse as amplifier, a division of labour between families that keeps each one's claims testable instead of dissolving them into a blend.

For the wage test, disproportionality is the quiet family. A general rise in real wages shifts demand toward consumption goods and away from the capital goods department's output, which can either relieve or aggravate an existing imbalance depending on where the overshoot sits. The family therefore issues no unconditional wage prediction, and that silence is itself diagnostic: an argument that treats sectoral mismatch as decisive will speak about wages only as one influence on the pattern of demand, never as the hinge of the story. Readers applying the wage test should treat a shrug as information too. It tells them the argument's centre of gravity lies in structure rather than distribution, and that its evidence will be found in industry level data, capacity utilisation by sector, and the sequence in which orders dried up, not in aggregate wage and profit shares.

## The fourth family: profit squeeze

The fourth family gives wages the starring role that the first family denies them. Its mechanism begins at the top of the boom, in prosperity itself. Expansion drains the reserve army of labour, the pool of unemployed and underemployed workers whose existence Marx analysed as a lever of accumulation. As the pool empties, the bargaining position of the employed strengthens: strikes succeed, wage settlements outrun productivity growth, and the share of profits in national income is squeezed from the side of labour. Firms respond by raising prices where they can, by resisting investment whose returns have thinned, and eventually by cutting production and employment, which recreates the reserve army and restores both profitability and workplace discipline from the employer's side. The Polish economist Michał Kalecki, working in a neighbouring tradition, had sketched the political version of this logic in 1943: full employment sustained for long would undermine the social position of the employer, so captains of industry would prefer a policy regime that periodically restores unemployment. The Marxist profit squeeze literature of the nineteen seventies gave the argument its empirical dress. Andrew Glyn and Bob Sutcliffe's study of British capitalism argued that a rising wage share, driven by organised labour's strength, had compressed profits to the point of crisis, and Raford Boddy and James Crotty developed a comparable account for the United States, modelling the cycle as the outcome of class conflict over distribution at high employment.

### How do rising wages turn a boom into a breakdown on the squeeze account?

Prosperity empties the pool of available labour, organised workers press claims that outpace productivity, and the profit share narrows until firms cut investment and hiring. The cuts recreate unemployment, the recreated unemployment weakens wage pressure, and profitability recovers on the far side of the slump the squeeze produced.

Textual critics sometimes object that this family owes more to the nineteen seventies than to Marx, and the objection should be answered rather than dismissed. Marx did treat the wage movement across the cycle as integral to accumulation: the chapter on the general law of capitalist accumulation in the first volume describes wages rising during expansion until they begin to encroach on accumulation, whereupon accumulation slackens, demand for labour falls, and the wage rise is reversed. The dynamics of the reserve army are his. What later writers added was the institutional machinery of collective bargaining, incomes policy, and the postwar welfare state, through which the squeeze operates in economies where unions negotiate national settlements. The family is thus a development rather than a quotation, and its defenders are generally candid about that, which strengthens rather than weakens its standing: it claims to extend the founder's cycle logic to institutions he did not live to see.

The wage test lights this family up like a beacon, since here the wage rise is neither accelerant nor remedy but cause. The prediction is exact: crises in this account are preceded by tight labour markets, accelerating wage settlements, and a measurable fall in the profit share, and they are followed by the restoration of profitability through unemployment. The nineteen seventies in Britain and the United States fit the template closely enough to make the family's reputation. Its difficulties begin where episodes do not fit. The great financial collapse treated later in this series arrived after decades of stagnant wages and a falling wage share in the United States, the opposite configuration, which is why commentators of the squeeze persuasion had little distinctive to say about that episode while credit and underconsumption writers had much. A family that explains one decade superbly and falls silent on the next has not been refuted, but its domain has been measured, and honest adherents now usually present profit squeeze as a theory of a particular regime of accumulation, the organised labour regime of the postwar decades, rather than as the universal engine of capitalist crisis. That retreat to a stated domain is intellectually honourable, and it is exactly the kind of move the unfalsifiability objection below claims the tradition never makes.

## The fifth family: credit and fictitious capital

The fifth family reads the third volume's fifth part, on the division of profit into interest and profit of enterprise, and on the credit system, as the prophetic core of Marx's crisis thinking. Its central category is fictitious capital: claims on future revenue, shares, bonds, and bank created credit, which circulate and appreciate as if they were wealth itself while representing only titles to value yet to be produced. Credit, in this account, is Janus faced. It is the great accelerator of accumulation, mobilising idle money, financing expansion beyond the limits of any firm's own savings, and equalising profit rates across branches. It is also the great concealer, allowing production to expand far beyond the limits of realisable demand while the paper claims generated along the way masquerade as proof that the expansion is sound. Marx observed that the credit system develops as a reaction against the older forms of hoarding and usury and simultaneously becomes an enormous mechanism of overproduction and overspeculation, and that in a crisis the demand for money as means of payment turns savage precisely because credit, acceptable everywhere the day before, is acceptable nowhere once confidence breaks. The crash, on this reading, is the moment when fictitious values are forcibly reconciled with the real accumulation that was supposed to underwrite them.

### Why does credit first postpone a crisis and then make it worse?

Credit lets firms produce and sell beyond the limits of existing purchasing power, bridging gaps that would otherwise stop expansion early. The bridge is built of promises, and when confidence falters the promises are called in together, so the adjustment arrives later, larger, and synchronised across firms that borrowed on the same expectations.

Later writers extended the account in several directions. Hilferding analysed the fusion of bank and industrial capital and the changed crisis pattern of the cartel era. Twentieth century financialisation literature, including work associated with the Monthly Review tradition, traced the migration of profit making into financial channels as production profitability faltered, making the fifth family a close cousin of the first: where profitability declines, capital seeks returns in speculation, and the credit superstructure swells relative to its base. Hyman Minsky, no Marxist but a fellow traveller on this terrain, formalised a kindred progression from hedge to speculative to Ponzi finance, and the family resemblance is often acknowledged on both sides. The distinguishing Marxist claim is the anchor: fictitious capital is fictitious relative to surplus value production, so the reconciliation, when it comes, is governed by how much real valorisation the underlying economy can deliver. That anchor separates the family from purely psychological accounts of bubbles, in which mania and panic float free of production altogether.

For the wage test, the credit family behaves like the disproportionality family, quietly. Wage rises matter to it mainly through their effect on borrowers' incomes and on the consumption loans and mortgages woven into modern credit structures. In an economy where household debt is large, wage stagnation can be the slow poison and wage growth a stabiliser, which aligns the credit account, in those conditions, with the underconsumption sign rather than the squeeze sign. But the alignment is conditional, not definitional, and the family's true variables are leverage, maturity mismatch, and the quality of collateral. A reader applying the wage test to a credit based argument will therefore extract predictions about debt ratios first and wage effects second, and an argument that leads with finance while quietly depending on a wage squeeze to do its causal work has mixed its families in a way the test is designed to expose. The detailed application of this family to a modern collapse belongs to the companion article on [Marxism and the financial crash](/2013/12/15/marxism-and-the-financial-crash/), which examines how each family in the grid was deployed after that episode and how their deployments fared.

## A sixth account: stagnation, monopoly, and the spatial fix

Alongside the five families from the classical literature stands a sixth account, developed from the early nineteen eighties and drawing on the monopoly capital tradition, which deserves separate registration because its time horizon differs from all the others. Where the five families explain the cycle, the periodic rupture, this account explains a condition: a mature capitalism chronically generating more surplus than it can absorb in consumption and productive investment, and therefore prone to long stagnation relieved by military spending, by the sales effort, by financial expansion, or by geographical displacement of the problem into new regions and new asset markets. David Harvey's presentation is the most influential. Reading the third volume alongside the Grundrisse, Harvey treats overaccumulation as a state in which capital and labour power stand idle side by side without a profitable way of combining them, and he traces the system's repertoire of fixes: temporal deferral through credit, spatial expansion into new territories and built environments, and the devaluations that periodically wipe the slate. The account borrows the first family's engine and the fifth family's instruments and stretches both across decades rather than cycles.

Registering this account as sixth, rather than dissolving it into the families it borrows from, does justice to its distinctive prediction. Stagnation theory expects long plateaus of mediocre growth and recurring speculative episodes rather than a neat rhythm of boom and liquidation, and it reads crises as moments when the fixes fail together. Its wage prediction is the underconsumption sign extended over the long term: a rising wage share enlarges the absorption capacity of the system and eases the chronic problem, while wage repression deepens the surplus disposal difficulty and pushes capital toward speculation and foreign outlets. Critics object that the account explains too many different outcomes, stagnation when growth is slow, crisis when growth breaks, boom when a fix succeeds, and that its flexibility approaches the unfalsifiability that the next sections of this guide take seriously as a charge against the whole field. Defenders reply that the theory specifies mechanisms and sequences, and that its account of urbanisation and the built environment as a sink for surplus capital has generated genuinely novel research rather than mere redescription. The dispute is left open here deliberately. A pillar article maps the field; it does not rig the verdicts.

## The five families grid

The argument so far can now be compressed into the artifact this guide is built around. The five families grid sets each family's mechanism, its leading proponents, its answer to the wage test, the evidence that would count against it, and the place in this series where the family receives extended treatment. Read across a row and you have one complete theory in miniature. Read down the wage column and you have the field's central disagreement at a glance, since the column contains both of the opposite signs and both of the diagnostic silences. The grid is a map, and like every map in this series it is drawn to be used: a reader meeting a new crisis argument in a book, a lecture, or a newspaper column should be able to place that argument on the grid within a paragraph or two of its opening moves, and to notice when an argument migrates between rows without signalling the move.

| Realisation failure | Output embodying surplus value cannot be sold at valorising prices, so value strands in commodity form and production halts behind the blockage | Luxemburg in her accumulation study, with Bukharin as internal critic | No fixed sign; the failure can originate on the investment side with wages high or low | Demand shortfalls shown to follow rather than precede output cuts in the decisive episodes | The overproduction and underconsumption article |

Two instructions govern the grid's honest use. First, the rows are rivals at the level of primary mechanism even where they cooperate at the level of transmission. Nothing prevents a real episode from featuring a profitability strain, a sectoral overshoot, and a credit collapse in sequence; everything prevents an analyst from claiming all three as the cause without ranking them, because the ranking decides what the theory predicted beforehand and therefore what the episode proves. Second, the evidence column is each row's signature on a contract. A family whose defenders refuse every entry in that column, explaining away profitability recoveries, wage sequences, and sectoral data alike, has withdrawn from the empirical conversation, and the reader is entitled to treat it thereafter as a vocabulary rather than a theory. Keeping the contract visible is the difference between the grid as an analytical instrument and the grid as a museum display.

## The wage test applied

The wage test can now be stated with the precision its repeated use in this guide requires. Given an argument about capitalist crisis, proceed in three steps. Identify the variable the argument treats as decisive: the profit rate, the demand flow, the sectoral pattern, the profit share, or the debt structure. Ask what the argument implies about a sustained rise in real wages, holding productivity growth in view. Then compare the implication with the grid. If the argument implies that rising wages bring breakdown closer, it belongs to the profitability or squeeze rows, and the follow up question, whether wages are accelerant or engine, decides between them. If it implies that rising wages push breakdown away, it belongs to the realisation or stagnation rows. If it shrugs, it belongs to the pattern or credit rows, and the shrug should be confirmed by the argument's evidence, which ought to be sectoral or financial rather than distributional. The test takes minutes, requires no mathematics, and works on texts of any period, including Marx's own. Run on the third volume, it returns a mixed verdict, profitability engine in one part, credit engine in another, which is exactly the finding the opening sections established by the slower road of documentary survey.

### How does the wage test expose which theory an argument is using?

Ask what the argument predicts from a sustained rise in real wages. Opposite answers fall out of the major families: squeeze and profitability accounts say breakdown nears, underconsumption accounts say it recedes, pattern and credit accounts deflect to their own variables. The exposed answer names the family in use.

The test's deeper value is disciplinary rather than taxonomic. Most popular crisis writing does not announce a family; it accumulates grievances. Profits are too low and wages are too low, speculation is rampant and demand is deficient, all in the same column, with the author apparently unaware that raising wages would, on half the listed causes, make things better and, on the other half, worse. The wage test forces a choice, and the forced choice improves the writing even when the writer dislikes the result, because a chosen mechanism can be argued with, evidenced, and taught, while an accumulation of grievances can only be applauded. Teachers in particular report that the single question transforms student essays on crisis, converting surveys of everything Marx ever said about breakdown into arguments that stake something. What is staked is a prediction about distribution, and predictions about distribution are checkable against the national accounts of any episode the writer cares to name. Bhaduri and Marglin's later distinction between wage led and profit led regimes, encountered earlier in this guide, shows the same opposition surviving translation into formal growth theory, evidence that the test identifies a real fork in the economics and not a verbal trick of this guide's devising.

Worked briefly on three famous episodes, the test shows its sorting power. The nineteen seventies in the advanced economies arrived with tight labour markets, militant wage bargaining, and falling profit shares: the squeeze row lights up, the underconsumption row darkens, and profitability writers must argue the longer mechanisation trend beneath the distributional surface, which some did with profit rate reconstructions. The nineteen thirties followed a decade of productivity growth outrunning wages in American manufacturing, with enormous sectoral overshoot in construction and automobiles: disproportionality and underconsumption readings contend, and the credit row claims the amplification through the banking collapse. The financial crisis examined later in this series followed long wage stagnation and a household debt explosion: squeeze is silent, credit and underconsumption speak first, and profitability writers divide between those who find a prior profit rate disturbance and those who treat the episode as belonging to another row altogether. Reasonable scholars dispute each sorting. That they can dispute it in these terms, row against row, evidence against evidence, is the test working as designed.

One refinement protects the test against a cheap misuse. The wage movement that matters is the movement of real wages relative to productivity, expressed in the wage share, and not the headline settlement figures that newspapers report. A large nominal settlement in an inflationary year may leave the share untouched or even reduced, and an argument graded on nominal figures has been graded on the wrong variable. Likewise the test should be run over the whole expansion rather than over its final quarters alone, since the squeeze family's claim concerns the cumulative tightening of the labour market and the profitability family's claim concerns a trend measured in decades. Critics who run the test on a single year's figures and announce a family's death are committing the timing error the section on the grid's limits warns against, and defenders are entitled to say so. Precision about the variable and the window is not pedantry. It is what separates an instrument from a slogan, and the test's inventors in the literature, from the participants in the nineteen seventies profit squeeze controversy onward, observed the precision instinctively because they were arguing over national accounts they all had open on the table. The reader who keeps those accounts open, share beside productivity beside profit rate, holds the seminar room's advantage in any company.

## Opposite predictions are not a synthesis

A natural peace making instinct arises at this point, and it should be resisted in its naive form. The instinct says the families are complementary layers of one rich theory: profitability the deep layer, realisation the middle, credit the surface, each true at its own level. Layering language of this kind decorates many textbooks. It fails for a logical reason that the wage column of the grid makes visible. Two accounts that issue opposite instructions about the same variable in the same episode cannot both be deep truths about that episode, because the episode went one way and not the other: wages rose or fell, the profit share narrowed or widened, and the sequel either matched a family's expectation or embarrassed it. Sophisticated syntheses exist, and they earn their status by specifying sequence and domain: this mechanism governs the long trend, that one the timing, a third the form of the collapse, with the boundaries stated before the evidence is consulted. Simon Clarke's critical surveys of the literature are instructive here, since Clarke argued that several families, whatever their differences, share a common defect in treating crisis as the product of a single barrier to accumulation, and that Marx's own most promising line ran through the contradictory unity of production and circulation rather than through any one family's variable. Whether or not that verdict stands, its form is the right one: a synthesis that argues, specifies, and accepts exposure.

### What would count as evidence against each family in practice?

Each row of the grid signs its own contract. Profitability falls if recoveries proceed without prior liquidation of capital values. Squeeze falls if breakdowns follow wage stagnation as often as wage militancy. Underconsumption falls if gluts clear while mass consumption shrinks. Credit falls if deep crises strike unleveraged economies with the same rhythm as leveraged ones.

The practical moral for readers is to distrust any exposition in which the families never lose. In the genuine literature they lose constantly, and their losses are documented by their rivals with relish and by their own adherents with reluctance. Grossmann's breakdown arithmetic was taken apart by reviewers who extended his schemas differently and found recovery where he found termination. The underconsumption reading of the nineteen thirties was battered by data showing consumption fell less than investment. The squeeze reading of the nineteen eighties was embarrassed by crises that followed its prescribed cure, wage discipline, rather than its prescribed cause. This record of losses is not a scandal. It is the normal metabolism of a research field, and it will matter when the strongest objection to the whole field is stated below, because the objection's strongest form depends on pretending the losses never happen.

## The breakdown controversy

No episode in the field's history illustrates its dynamics better than the controversy over breakdown, the Zusammenbruch debate that ran through German social democracy and its successors for four decades. In the eighteen nineties Eduard Bernstein, reviewing the movement's expectations against the evidence of cartels, expanding credit, a growing world market, and rising working class living standards, concluded that capitalism was developing adaptive capacities that made a terminal collapse increasingly improbable, and that socialist strategy should adjust accordingly. The orthodox reply, associated with Karl Kautsky and, in a different register, with Luxemburg's pamphlet on reform and revolution, defended the expectation of deepening contradiction while rarely specifying a mechanical terminus. What the controversy needed, and lacked, was a text of Marx that settled whether collapse was a theorem or a tendency talk. None existed, for the reasons this guide opened with. The breakdown doctrine was thus a construction of the tradition, assembled from the third volume's most dramatic passages and from the Manifesto's rhetoric of graves dug and gravediggers produced, and contested from its first assembly by socialists who read the same passages otherwise.

Grossmann's 1929 volume, The Law of Accumulation and Breakdown of the Capitalist System, attempted to convert the construction into a proof. Working from Bauer's four period reproduction schema, Grossmann extended the arithmetic across enough periods to show the surplus available for capitalists' consumption shrinking toward zero and beyond, at which point accumulation must break down from its own internal conditions, with no external shock required. The book appeared months before the Wall Street crash, a coincidence that gave its argument an aura no seminar could have conferred. Critics responded on several levels. The schema's assumptions, constant rates of surplus value and of accumulation among them, did the proving; relax them, allow the rate of surplus value to rise or accumulation to slow, and the terminus recedes indefinitely. Other critics accepted the arithmetic and denied the conclusion, since a system approaching the schema's limit would change its parameters under pressure from class struggle and policy long before the limit was reached, converting breakdown back into crisis. The distinction those critics enforced, between a terminal event derivable from a model with frozen parameters and the recurrent crises of a system whose parameters are fought over, has held. Terminal breakdown survives in the literature as a limiting case and a historical episode in the movement's self understanding. As a prediction it has the poor record this guide is obliged to state plainly: it has been confidently scheduled many times in a hundred and thirty years, and the system has survived every scheduled date, usually by means, adaptation, state intervention, spatial expansion, that the doctrine had classified as impossible or as postponements of itself.

### Did Marx predict a final collapse of capitalism?

The texts do not contain that prediction in derivable form. They contain recurrent crisis, a falling tendency in profitability, and dramatic rhetoric about the system's historical transience. The terminal doctrine was assembled later by followers, disputed from the start, and largely set aside by the tradition's own scholarship.

The controversy nonetheless left permanent assets. It forced the tradition to distinguish tendency from destiny, a distinction the careful literature now maintains automatically. It produced the first rigorous attempts to model accumulation dynamics over long horizons, ancestors of later growth theory inside and outside Marxism. And it stands as the field's clearest cautionary tale about the cost of overclaiming: a movement that promises a datable terminus and misses it pays for the miss with the credibility of everything else it says, including the parts, the analysis of recurrent crisis, that were defensible all along. Readers who meet breakdown rhetoric in polemical writing should apply the wage test and then a second question, the date question: does the argument depend on this crisis being the crisis? If it does, it has left the five families behind and entered the territory this section has surveyed, where the historical record is unkind.

## The mainstream of cycle theory, stated fairly

Neutrality in this guide is not a posture but a workload, and the workload here is to state the non-Marxist theories of the cycle at a strength their own adherents would accept. Those theories are numerous and mutually hostile, and several command formidable evidence. The monetary account associated with Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz reads the greatest modern contraction as a policy disaster: the American money stock was allowed to fall by a third between 1929 and 1933 through banking panics the central bank could have prevented, converting a serious recession into the Great Depression. On that reading the cycle's worst episodes are neither necessary nor systemic in origin; they are failures of identifiable institutions, correctable by better rules. The real business cycle school associated with Finn Kydland and Edward Prescott models fluctuations as the economy's efficient response to real shocks, above all shifts in technology, with output and employment moving together as households and firms adjust optimally; in its strong form it denies that the fluctuations are pathologies at all. New Keynesian work restores pathology with different machinery: sticky prices and wages, coordination failures, and financial accelerators in which falling collateral values force credit contraction, a mechanism developed by Ben Bernanke and collaborators that gives banking panics a rigorous modern form without any Marxist premise. The Austrian school of Mises and Hayek, heterodox but influential, blames artificially cheap credit for distorting the structure of production toward long projects that cannot be completed profitably, an account whose family resemblance to disproportionality Marxist writers have occasionally acknowledged through gritted teeth. Keynes himself stands behind much of this landscape as its presiding alternative: investment governed by volatile expectations, demand determining output, economies capable of settling below full employment without any tendency of the profit rate entering the story.

These rivals land real blows on the five families, and the blows should be catalogued rather than parried in bulk. The monetary account explains the depth of the nineteen thirties more parsimoniously than any demand deficiency story that cannot say why demand collapsed when it did, and it identifies a policy lever that demonstrably worked when finally used. Real business cycle modelling set a standard of formal explicitness that Marxist cycle writing, much of it verbal and diagrammatic, has rarely matched, and its insistence that a theory specify the shock it invokes exposes how often family based accounts invoke their mechanism without identifying its trigger in the episode at hand. The financial accelerator literature achieves, with mainstream tools, a version of the credit family's core insight, that balance sheet deterioration propagates contraction, which raises the fair question of what the Marxist version adds beyond the assertion that finance is ultimately anchored in production. Meanwhile the strongest empirical regularity in the whole field embarrasses everyone impartially: expansions do not die of old age in any simple rhythm, contractions vary enormously in depth and duration, and no school, Marxist, monetarist, or Keynesian, possesses a forecasting record on turning points that would survive comparison with its own rhetoric. Humility about prediction is the mainstream's legitimate reproach to breakdown talk, and it is accepted here in full.

What the Marxist families claim in reply, when they are being careful, is a difference of question rather than a monopoly of answers. The mainstream theories largely ask what propagates a disturbance once it occurs: money, stickiness, collateral, expectations. The families ask why the system periodically arrives at a condition in which a disturbance can do systemic damage, why fragility accumulates during good times as a byproduct of success itself. Minsky, who asked exactly that question from inside a broadly Keynesian frame, is the standing proof that the question is not owned by any school. A fair verdict on the contest is therefore comparative and open: the families offer the most developed accounts of endogenous fragility in the literature, the mainstream offers the most developed accounts of propagation and policy, and a reader who wants to understand a particular episode will usually need both shelves of the library. This pillar does not grade the contest further, because grading it is the work of the cluster's later articles, which take the families one at a time into the evidence.

## Marx and Keynes on the demand side

One comparison deserves a section of its own because students persistently blur it, and the blur conceals what is distinctive in each thinker. Keynes and the Marxist realisation tradition agree that demand is not automatic, that an economy can produce more than it can sell at expected prices, and that saving and investment are separate decisions whose mismatch generates instability. The agreement ends there. Keynes located the instability in the psychology of investment and the preference for liquidity, treated the marginal efficiency of capital as hostage to convention and mood, and prescribed demand management as the remedy of a system he wished to preserve. The Marxist families locate the instability in the structure of production for profit: demand fails, on their accounts, because valorisation fails, because the surplus embedded in output cannot be converted, or because the proportions of production have outrun any demand pattern that existing distribution can support. The difference surfaces in the wage test with unusual clarity. Keynesian policy welcomes wage supported consumption as stabiliser without theorising the profit share as the system's governor. Underconsumption Marxism agrees on the welcome and adds a theory of why distribution keeps failing. Profitability Marxism dissents from both, holding that demand support merely relocates the strain onto a profit rate already compromised by the capital stock's composition. The companion article on [Marx versus Keynes on crisis](/2014/01/15/marx-vs-keynes-on-crisis/) works this comparison through the texts of both writers, including Keynes's sparse and ambivalent engagements with Marx himself, and readers who suspect the two demand theories of being the same theory in different vocabularies will find the suspicion examined rather than assumed.

## The strongest objection: a framework that cannot lose

Every field has an objection it must survive, and for this field the objection is not that capitalism lacks crises, which nobody denies, nor that the mechanisms are individually unintelligible, which this guide has spent its length refuting. The objection is that the tradition, taken as a whole, has arranged itself so that it cannot be wrong. State it at full strength, as its best critics state it. A theory earns the name by risking itself on predictions that might fail. The Marxist crisis literature, the objection runs, risks nothing, because it keeps five incompatible explanations in stock and deals them out after the event. Profits fell before the crash? The profitability family is produced. Profits were healthy but banks failed? The credit family is produced. Wages squeezed, wages stagnant, sectors mismatched, demand deficient: whatever the configuration of the episode, some row of the grid fits it, and the fit is then advertised as confirmation of Marxist crisis theory as such. A framework compatible with every outcome is compatible with knowledge of none. Worse, the structure of the tradition rewards the manoeuvre: each family has its journals, its conferences, its founding texts, and the institutional incentive is to defend one's row rather than to grade the grid, so family switching at the level of the movement is matched by family entrenchment at the level of the schools. On this account, the field is not a science with rival theories but a repertoire of redescriptions, and its century of literature is the record of a vocabulary elaborating itself while claiming the authority of explanation.

The objection, so stated, is the clearest formulation of the unfalsifiability charge in Marxist economics, and any honest pillar must concede how much of it lands. It lands on popular writing almost completely: the newspaper column that finds its preferred family confirmed by every headline is a real and abundant artefact, and the previous sections of this guide have already condemned it in the objection's own terms. It lands on a certain academic defensiveness too, the kind that responds to every failed prediction by discovering that the true mechanism was another row's all along. Conceding that much, the reply can be exact rather than indignant, and it has three parts. First, the unit of falsification is the family, not the tradition. Individual families make the risky predictions catalogued in the grid's evidence column, and they have lost on them in public: breakdown arithmetic was dismantled, the simple underconsumption timing claim was battered by the wage sequence Marx himself flagged, the squeeze account's domain shrank when episodes arrived with the wrong distributional signature. A repertoire of redescriptions does not generate losses of that kind; research programmes do. Second, the wage test converts the objection into a procedure. Family switching is only invisible when the switcher is not asked the discriminating question in advance. Ask it, record the answer, and the switch, when it comes, is a datable change of theory rather than a seamless confirmation, which is all that falsification ever requires in the historical sciences. Third, the objection proves too much if pressed to its limit, since rival pluralism is the normal condition of immature sciences and of some mature ones: medicine keeps incompatible models of diseases it nonetheless treats, and seismology cannot predict the events it explains. The reasonable demand is not a single theory but honest bookkeeping, each family graded on its own predictions, switches acknowledged as switches. This guide has tried to model the bookkeeping. Whether the tradition's institutions reward it is a sociological question on which the objection's pessimism may yet be vindicated, and the concession is left standing rather than argued away.

## Where the crisis material actually sits in the texts

Students regularly ask where, in the physical corpus, Marx's crisis discussion is located, and the honest answer is an itinerary rather than an address. Begin in the first volume with the chapter on money, where the separation of sale and purchase and the means of payment function first establish the formal possibility of breakdown. Move to the manuscripts on theories of surplus value, above all the long chapter on Ricardo's theory of accumulation, where the critique of Say's law is worked out and the possibility and necessity distinction is drawn with the greatest care anywhere in the corpus. Proceed to the second volume: the reproduction schemas in its third part supply the raw material of both the disproportionality and realisation families, and the chapter on the reproduction of money material contains the attack on simple underconsumption, so that one volume houses a family and its sharpest internal critic within a few hundred pages of each other. End with the third volume, whose third part develops the falling tendency of the profit rate and whose fifth part, on interest and credit, contains the richest nineteenth century treatment of banking panic by any economist: the analysis of the money market in crisis, the demand for means of payment, and the fictitious capital categories all sit there, unfinished and interleaved with Engels's editorial brackets. The Grundrisse adds a fourth stratum, exploratory and brilliant, including the fragment on machines that later writers promoted beyond its place in the argument. Scholars who keep their reading organised in a structured notebook system, such as the study tool at [VaultBook Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), can attach each family to its textual home in exactly this itinerary form, so that the map of mechanisms and the map of manuscripts correct each other as the reading deepens.

The itinerary teaches a lesson the doctrinal summaries miss. Read in this order, the corpus shows a thinker whose crisis remarks thicken and change register as his system matures: monetary possibility first, reproduction structure second, profitability and credit last, each stratum presupposing the earlier ones without replacing them. The families' quarrel is therefore partly an argument about strata, about which layer of a developing analysis deserves priority when the layers point in different directions. Priority disputes of that kind are not settled by louder citation. They are settled, if at all, by the comparative evidence of episodes, which is why this guide keeps returning from texts to predictions. The texts tell the reader what each family claims. Only the record tells the reader what each family is worth.

## The unfinished work pattern, once more

It is worth pausing on the pattern this article shares with others in the series, because crisis theory is its purest instance. Three times in Marx's mature project the structure calls for a finishing statement that never arrives: the definition of class near the end of the third volume, the book on the state within the six book plan, and the treatment of the world market and its crises that was to crown the whole design. In each case the absence generates a tradition of competitive completion, and in each case the scholarly virtue is the same: to mark the join between what the founder wrote and what the completer supplied. Crisis scholarship that observes the marking can be argued with profitably even by its opponents, because both parties know which claims are textual and which are constructions. Scholarship that conceals the marking produces the counterfeit certainty this guide has been resisting from its first page, in which a reconstruction assembled in 1929 or 1972 is quoted as though it were a doctrine delivered in 1867. The wage test is, among its other uses, a marking device. It cannot be run on a blur. It obliges the writer to say which completion is in play, and in doing so it performs on contemporary arguments the same service that source criticism performs on the manuscripts.

## A short history of the sorting itself

The families were not always visible as families, and the history of their sorting clarifies their boundaries. The first generation of the debate, in the eighteen nineties and nineteen hundreds, argued in the vocabulary of breakdown and revision, with disproportionality and realisation present but unnamed as separate engines. Sweezy's 1942 synthesis performed the first widely read taxonomy in English, organising the theories he surveyed around the falling profit rate, underconsumption, and disproportionality, and awarding underconsumption a prominence his Marx chapters' evidence only partly supported, a tilt later scholarship corrected. The postwar decades professionalised the field: the Union for Radical Political Economics and journals such as the Review of Radical Political Economics gave the families institutional homes in the English speaking world, the nineteen seventies added profit squeeze as distribution data accumulated, and Erik Olin Wright's typologies of crisis theories in that decade sorted the mechanisms by their location in production, circulation, and class relations, a sorting still used in teaching. Anwar Shaikh's contributions pressed the profitability family's claims with historical statistics on the American economy, while Clarke's critical history argued, as noted earlier, that the families share deeper assumptions than their polemics admit. The sorting is therefore itself a historical artefact, revised as new episodes supply new configurations, and the grid in this guide is the present state of that artefact, offered with the expectation that the next major episode will embarrass at least one row and enrich another. Taxonomies in a living field are wagers on the future shape of the evidence, and this one is placed openly.

## Reading a downturn with the grid

Method, to be worth anything, must survive contact with a case, so consider how the grid disciplines the reading of one documented episode without delivering a rigged verdict: the downturn of the nineteen seventies in Britain and the United States, chosen because unusually rich distributional data survive for it. The diagnostic sequence runs through the rows in a fixed order and records hits and misses in the open. Profitability first: reconstructions of the American profit rate show a decline from the mid nineteen sixties, driven partly by rising capital intensity and partly by international competition, so the row registers a genuine hit on trend, though the timing of particular slumps within the decade does not track the trend's smooth descent. Squeeze second: wage shares rose into the early seventies, settlements outran productivity in Britain dramatically, and the row registers the strongest timing hit of any family for the first oil shock era recessions, before the distributional configuration reverses under the policy regimes of the following decade. Underconsumption third: mass consumption was buoyant until the recessions themselves, so the row misses on this episode, and its adherents' attempts to score it anyway remain the weakest pages of that decade's literature. Disproportionality fourth: the energy sector's transformation after the oil price rises gives the row a partial hit on pattern, though the initiating price shock was external to the mechanism the family describes. Credit fifth: the decade's banking strains were real but secondary, a modest hit on amplification only. A reader who performs this exercise honestly possesses something better than a verdict on the nineteen seventies. The reader possesses the habit of graded, row by row assessment, transferable to any episode, including the ones this series treats in its applied articles, and proof against the single family triumphalism that the unfalsifiability objection rightly scorns.

## Cause and trigger are different things

A distinction the families handle with varying grace, and which readers should police in every account they meet, separates the underlying condition from the precipitating event. The collapses that open famous crises are usually financial and specific: the failure of a great house, the suspension of a bank, the bursting of a railway or property speculation. Overend, Gurney and Company in 1866, the Baring crisis of 1890, the American panic of 1907: each has its immediate narrative of overextended credit and vanished confidence. No family in the grid claims its mechanism was the clerk who refused the fatal loan. The mechanisms claim something subtler and more defensible: that the condition of the system at that moment determined whether a specific failure remained a specific failure or generalised into a liquidation of everything. Fragility is the families' subject; the spark is biography and accident. This division of explanatory labour protects the theories from an easy refutation, since showing that a crisis was triggered by a harvest failure or a war indemnity does not touch the claim that only accumulated imbalance made the trigger catastrophic. But the protection must not become another unfalsifiable shelter. A family earns the fragility claim only by measuring the fragility independently of the crisis it is summoned to explain: profitability reconstructed from accounts compiled before the break, wage shares from settlements struck before the panic, leverage from balance sheets published before the run. Where the measurement is possible only in hindsight, coloured by knowledge of the ending, the account should be read as narrative rather than as test.

Marx's own period observations respected the distinction intuitively. Watching the crisis of 1857 spread from American railway shares to Hamburg merchants to English banks, he treated the sequence as the exposure of a single overextended commercial structure rather than as a chain of independent accidents, and his journalism of those months reads like a fragility audit conducted in real time. The first volume later generalised the point through the analysis of the credit system's role in equalising and accelerating, and the third volume's banking chapters return obsessively to the moment when the scramble for means of payment reveals which capitals were sound and which were fictitious. Modern fragility language, balance sheet recessions, debt deflation in Irving Fisher's classic account, belongs to the same intellectual gesture whatever the school, one more instance of the question of endogenous weakness being nobody's property.

## What ends a crisis, according to each family

A theory of crisis that cannot describe recovery is half a theory, and the families diverge instructively when asked what terminates the liquidation. Profitability accounts answer with devaluation: the crisis destroys capital values through bankruptcy, asset write downs, and the scrapping of plant, so that the survivors' capital is smaller relative to the labour it commands, the rate of profit recovers on the shrunken base, and accumulation can restart. Recovery, on this telling, is not despite the destruction but because of it, a logic that explains the tradition's grim insistence that crises are functional for the system even while ruinous for its participants. Realisation accounts answer with clearance: inventories are sold off at whatever prices clear them, excess capacity in consumption goods is retired, and demand and supply realign at a lower level from which proportionate growth can resume. Disproportionality answers similarly in the language of pattern: the audit completes itself when the sectoral stocks again stand in workable relations, usually after the oversized branches have shrunk hardest. Squeeze accounts answer with discipline: unemployment rebuilt by the slump restores the reserve army, wage claims moderate, the profit share widens, and investment returns on the basis of restored margins. Credit accounts answer with the writing down of fictitious capital to the level real earnings can service, assisted in the modern era by the state's lender of last resort operations, which socialise the reconciliation and thereby change its distribution without changing its necessity. Stagnation accounts, characteristically, answer that nothing fundamental is terminated: the slump clears the ground for another round of fixes, another credit expansion, another spatial displacement, and the underlying absorption problem resumes beneath the recovery's noise.

### Does a crisis repair the system that produced it?

On most family accounts, partially and temporarily. Liquidation devalues capital, clears inventories, rebuilds unemployment, and writes paper claims down toward real earnings, restoring the conditions for expansion. The repair reproduces the mechanisms that generated the fragility, which is why the tradition expects recurrence rather than resolution.

These rival recovery stories are not decorative. They generate further tests, because the character of documented recoveries differs: recoveries led by asset devaluation and profit rate restoration favour the first family; recoveries tracking wage share compression favour the fourth; recoveries that begin only after debt stocks are written down or inflated away favour the fifth. Historians of particular episodes can and do grade the families on recovery evidence as well as on onset evidence, and the double grading is one reason simplistic scorekeeping, this family won the twentieth century, collapses under scrutiny. Different families win different phases of the same cycle in different decades, a finding that would scandalise only those who expected the grid to behave like a league table rather than like a set of instruments.

## Money, gold, and the monetary constitution of Marx's century

Every mechanism in the grid operated, in Marx's lifetime, inside a monetary constitution radically unlike the fiat regimes of later eras, and forgetting that constitution distorts the reading of his crisis texts. The crises he watched unfolded under metallic money and, in England, under the Bank Charter Act of 1844, which tied the note issue to gold and split the Bank of England into departments in the hope of making paper behave like metal. Marx attacked the Act's theory mercilessly in the third volume, siding largely with the Banking School against the Currency School, and the historical rhythm gave his polemic its edge: in the crises of 1847, 1857, and 1866 the Act had to be suspended by government letter so the Bank could issue beyond the legal limit, whereupon the panics subsided, a sequence Marx read as experimental refutation of the doctrine that restriction creates stability. The suspension episodes matter for the families because they show a nineteenth century state already intervening at the monetary nerve of crisis, already acting as the reconciler of fictitious and real values a generation before lender of last resort doctrine was codified by Walter Bagehot. The credit family in Marx is thus not a theory of unregulated finance running to its doom. It is a theory of a system whose crises periodically force the state to suspend the system's own monetary rules, and that pattern, rules kept in booms and broken in panics, will be recognised by any student of later financial history.

The metallic setting also disciplines the underconsumption and realisation families' original forms. Under a gold standard, deficient demand expresses itself partly as hoarding of the universal equivalent, and the drainage of gold between nations transmits crises across borders with a mechanical vividness that fiat regimes reproduce only in translated forms: currency crises, sudden stops, reserve losses. When this guide's grid is applied to later episodes, the transmission column changes its vocabulary while the mechanisms keep their shapes, and readers performing the translation should say so explicitly rather than pretend the nineteenth century texts anticipated institutional details they could not have contained. That candour about translation is the same marking discipline commended earlier, applied to monetary history: the join between Marx's gold world and the reader's fiat world belongs in the open.

## The world market as the crisis's true stage

Marx remarked, in the manuscripts on surplus value, that world market crises must be regarded as the real concentration and forcible adjustment of all the contradictions of bourgeois economy. The sentence is programmatic rather than analytical, but it points at a feature of the phenomenon the families ignore at their peril: capitalist crises were international from early on. The crisis of 1825, often counted the first general crisis of overproduction, spread from English speculation in Latin American loans and mining shares to the Latin American economies themselves. The crisis of 1857 earned its place in Marx's imagination precisely because it was the first to circle the globe, striking the United States, Britain, the German states, and Scandinavia in connected sequence. Transmission ran through trade credit, commodity prices, and gold flows, and each national episode wore the colours of its local banking law while sharing the underlying configuration of overtrading. The families differ in how naturally they globalise. Disproportionality scales awkwardly, since proportions between departments blur when departments trade across frontiers. Realisation globalises eagerly, as Luxemburg's entire construction testifies. Credit globalises fastest of all, since claims cross borders more easily than factories, a ranking the subsequent century and a half of international financial crises has done nothing to disturb. A pillar article need not adjudicate the international form of each mechanism; the cluster's applied articles take up particular episodes. But the grid should be read with its original stage in view: the unit of analysis was never really the closed national economy of the textbooks, and tests run on national data alone inherit that simplification.

## Long waves and the temptation of rhythm

Adjacent to the families, and periodically fused with them, stands the idea that capitalist development moves in long waves of roughly half a century, ascending and descending phases governed by deep determinants. Nikolai Kondratiev assembled the statistical case in the nineteen twenties from price, interest, and production series. Joseph Schumpeter built the waves into his theory of economic development, tying them to clusters of innovation and to the creative destruction by which new industries annihilate old ones, a concept whose resemblance to the Marxist liquidation function is real but whose moral and theoretical frame, entrepreneurial and individualist, is Schumpeter's own. Ernest Mandel's Late Capitalism gave the waves a Marxist reconstruction, arguing that the downswing's mechanisms are endogenous to accumulation while the upswing's triggers involve extra economic shocks, an asymmetry designed to let the falling profit tendency govern the long pattern without mechanically dictating each turn. The long wave literature enriches the families with periodisation and tempts them with numerology in equal measure. Wave dating is elastic enough to absorb almost any chronology, and the elasticity should put readers on guard: a fifty year rhythm that can be stretched to fit every major episode explains the calendar the way the blended synthesis explains the mechanism, by accommodation. The sober use of the waves is heuristic, as a reminder that the families' mechanisms operate at different speeds in different eras, so that the squeeze family's decade and the profitability family's half century need not be rivals for the same stretch of time. With that caution entered, the wave literature can be left to its specialists, its presence in this guide recording the field's outer boundary rather than one of its engines.

## Crisis interpretation and political strategy

The stakes of the taxonomy were never only academic, and a history minded section should say plainly why generations of activists treated crisis theory as strategy in disguise. If breakdown is terminal and near, the rational posture is preparation for the succession. If crises are recurrent corrections within a durable system, the rational posture shifts toward reforms that alter the system's distribution and regulation between its crises. The Bernstein controversy was explosive precisely because both sides understood that a taxonomic judgement, capitalism adapts or capitalism terminates, carried an organisational conclusion inside it. Variants of the same inference recur wherever the families are debated: squeeze theory, assigning the crisis to labour's strength, has been used to argue both for and against militant wage strategy; underconsumption, assigning it to labour's weakness, underwrites redistributionist programmes; credit theory underwrites regulatory ones. A scholarly guide takes no side in those inferences, and Part Six discipline forbids dressing a preference as a finding. But readers deserve the warning that the grid's rows have political gravities, pulling arguments toward characteristic policies, and that a writer's row selection is therefore worth noticing in the way one notices a judge's prior rulings: not as disqualification, as orientation. The wage test remains the cleanest orientation device available, because it extracts the row from the rhetoric in public, where the extraction can be checked.

## Periodicity and the material basis of the cycle

Why should crises return at intervals of roughly a decade, as they conspicuously did through the middle of the nineteenth century, in 1825, 1836, 1847, 1857, and 1866? Marx's most original answer to the timing question sits in the second volume's analysis of the turnover of capital, where he suggests that the cycle of connected turnovers, spanning the working life of fixed capital, supplies a material basis for the periodic character of crises. Machinery is bought in waves because technical revolutions and competitive pressure bunch investment; machinery wears out on a schedule set by its physical and moral depreciation; the bunching of replacement years later echoes the bunching of installation, so that the economy's demand for the capital goods department rises and falls with a rhythm inscribed in its equipment. Engels's editorial notes and Marx's correspondence return to the idea with evident satisfaction, and later writers connected it to the observed correlation between investment cycles and the lifespan of plant. The suggestion is disproportionality given a clock: proportions between departments are not merely wrong in the abstract but wrong on a schedule, because the capital stock's age structure synchronises the error. Modern investment theory rediscovered related mechanisms in echo effects and replacement cycles, usually without awareness of the anticipation. For the grid, the episode is a reminder that the families are not sealed compartments in the founder's own handling either. The periodicity discussion recruits the reproduction analysis to the service of a timing mechanism, and it does so explicitly, with the join marked by the argument itself. Marked joins are this guide's recurring definition of legitimate combination, and the founder, at his careful best, observes it.

## The state enters the crisis

No family in the classical literature assigned the state a leading part, for the adequate reason that the nineteenth century state was fiscally small beside the economy it governed. Later writers in every family have had to revise that assignment, and the revisions form a natural experiment in the field's adaptability. A state that spends two fifths of national income, insures deposits, manages demand, and stands behind its banking system alters each mechanism's operation. Profitability writers argue that state borrowing and expenditure can postpone liquidation and thereby deepen the eventual devaluation, or alternatively that military and infrastructure spending constitute absorption outlets the stagnation school catalogued. Squeeze writers confront the fact that governments can legislate the wage bargain itself, through incomes policies and labour law, making the state an umpire inside their mechanism rather than a spectator of it. Credit writers watch the lender of last resort socialise losses and ask whether fictitious capital reconciled by public guarantee is reconciled at all, or merely transferred to the sovereign's balance sheet, a question the euro area's troubles later posed in an exact institutional form. Realisation writers note that welfare transfers directly supplement the consumption fund whose deficiency their theory indicts, so that a developed welfare state is, on their account, an institutionalised countertendency to underconsumption. These revisions share a pattern worth naming: none of the families collapsed on contact with the enlarged state, and each absorbed the state by extending its own variable, which believers cite as flexibility and critics as further evidence of the repertoire problem. The wage test adjudicates the local disputes as usual. A crisis account that credits state demand with postponing breakdown while blaming wage growth for causing it owes the reader an explanation of why one flow of purchasing power differs in kind from the other, and the explanation, when produced, invariably reveals the family: distribution governed accounts distinguish them by their effect on the profit share, realisation accounts cannot distinguish them at all and must drop the wage blame, credit accounts redirect attention to the state's guarantee structure. The test's power is that it keeps extracting such confessions from arguments that arrived dressed as common sense.

## Measuring profitability: the data behind the first family

Because the profitability family leans hardest on measurement, its evidence problems deserve a section of their own, distinct from the theoretical dispute the companion empirical article treats in full. A profit rate is a ratio, and every element of the ratio is contested. The numerator may be measured before or after tax, before or after interest, including or excluding the financial sector, and including or excluding capital gains on assets that a rising stock market has revalued. The denominator, the capital stock, may be valued at historical cost or at replacement cost, a choice that can reverse the trend in inflationary decades, since historical cost denominators shrink in real terms while replacement cost denominators swell. Capacity utilisation complicates the cycle's reading: a rate of profit measured on all installed capital dips mechanically in recessions because output falls faster than the stock, so defenders of the family must separate the trend from the utilisation effect to avoid claiming the crisis as evidence for its own cause. Scholarly reconstructions by writers such as Shaikh and, in a different idiom, by Robert Brenner in his account of postwar turbulence, or by Gérard Duménil and Dominique Lévy in their long run series for the American and French economies, disagree about levels while converging, in several cases, on a pattern of postwar decline into the nineteen seventies followed by partial recovery. That pattern's contested status is the honest summary: the data are hard enough to sustain a serious research programme and too soft to have ended one. Readers should therefore treat any flat assertion that the profit rate has or has not fallen across some period as a request to inspect the accounting conventions underneath, in exactly the spirit in which this guide has taught them to inspect family selections in texts.

## Crises of scarcity and crises of abundance

A comparative guard belongs in any complete guide, because it marks the boundary of the phenomenon the families claim as theirs. Pre-capitalist crises were typically crises of underproduction: harvest failure, plague, and war destroyed the means of subsistence directly, and prices rose on scarcity. The capitalist crisis inverts the signature: goods exist in excess, factories stand capable, workers stand willing, and production halts amid plenty because the plenty cannot be sold at valorising prices. Marx savoured the inversion and treated it as diagnostic of the mode of production, since only a system organised by exchange value could starve in a warehouse. The guard matters for method because it warns against annexing every historical catastrophe to the grid: famines from crop failure, however politically mediated, are not realisation crises, and wartime destruction is not devaluation in the family's sense. It matters equally in the other direction. The command economies of the twentieth century generated their own characteristic disruptions, shortage, hoarding, and investment cycles driven by plan bargaining, documented by economists such as János Kornai, and apologists who cite those disruptions against the grid misunderstand its scope: the families claim to explain the breakdowns of a system of generalised commodity production, not to monopolise the concept of economic disorder. Scope discipline of this kind is unfashionable in polemic and indispensable in scholarship, and its observance here is deliberate. A theory that knows its territory can be graded within it; a theory that annexes all misfortune has announced, once again, that it cannot lose.

## One episode, five readings: the nineteen thirties as a grading exercise

The worked method shown earlier on the nineteen seventies deserves a harder application, and the nineteen thirties supply it, because that catastrophe has been claimed by every row and by several mainstream rivals besides. Begin with the configuration before the break. American profitability in the nineteen twenties is contested ground: some reconstructions find manufacturing profit rates healthy into 1929, buoyed by rapid productivity growth, while others detect a rising capital output ratio pressing on margins. The profitability row accordingly opens with a disputed measurement rather than a trumpet blast. Distribution is clearer: productivity in American manufacturing outran real wages across the decade, the wage share lagged, and farm incomes collapsed early, which gives underconsumption its favourite configuration and explains the staying power of demand deficiency readings of the Depression among both Marxists and Keynesians. Disproportionality points to the grotesque overshoot in construction and automobile capacity and to the agricultural sector's decade long depression inside the general boom, a pattern hit of the first order. Credit claims the episode's amplification entire: the leveraged investment trusts, the call loan market, and above all the banking panics of 1930 to 1933, which the monetary historians independently identify as the mechanism converting recession into Great Depression. Squeeze, finally, is silent where it should be, since the decade before the crash was one of weakened unions and lagging wages, and the family scores only in the later thirties, when the sit down strikes and rising wage costs amid recovery fed a vigorous contemporary debate about the recession of 1937 and 1938. Tally the grades and no row wins the episode outright. Underconsumption and disproportionality read the prelude best, credit and its monetarist cousin read the catastrophe's depth best, profitability argues the background, and squeeze explains a later act. This distribution of honours is, for this guide, the mature form of the field's claim: not a master key but a set of instruments whose relative performance varies measurably by phase and configuration, and whose joint use, with the joins marked and the grades recorded, outperforms any single family's claim to the whole. Scholars who find that conclusion deflating are invited to compare it with the forecasting and explanatory record of any rival school across the same century. Deflation of pretension, distributed impartially, is the beginning of method in this subject.

## The reproduction schemas at walking pace

Because two of the families draw their charters from the second volume's schemas, readers who have never watched the schemas move deserve one slow pass through the machinery, in prose and with the founder's own illustrative proportions kept simple. Divide the whole social production into two great departments. Department One produces means of production: machines, raw materials, fuel, the things other producers consume while producing. Department Two produces articles of consumption: food, clothing, housing services, the things households consume while living. Each department's output can be resolved, on Marx's value analysis, into three components: the value of the means of production used up, called constant capital; the value of the labour power employed, called variable capital; and the surplus value created beyond both. For the whole machine to reproduce itself on the same scale year after year, the departments must exchange with each other in exact proportion. Department One's workers and capitalists need consumption goods from Department Two; Department Two needs replacement machinery and materials from Department One; and the value magnitudes must match, so that Department One's variable capital plus surplus value equals Department Two's constant capital. Marx's own numerical illustration runs Department One at four thousand constant, one thousand variable, one thousand surplus, and Department Two at two thousand constant, five hundred variable, five hundred surplus, and the equality holds: one thousand plus one thousand equals two thousand, and the system can, on paper, repeat itself forever.

Everything interesting happens when the paper machine is disturbed. Let Department One accumulate, investing part of its surplus in additional machinery and labour rather than consuming it, and the equality must be re-established at a new level through a choreography of exchanges that Marx traces with painful patience, because each department's accumulation rate constrains the other's market. Let the accumulation rates diverge, or let money hoarding interrupt the exchanges, or let Department Two's output mix drift away from the wage goods and capitalist consumption goods its partner department's incomes demand, and the paper machine jams. The jams are the schemas' gift to crisis theory. Disproportionality reads them as the normal condition of an unplanned economy, bound to jam periodically and violently. Luxemburg read them and asked her famous question about the source of the money demand that realises the growing surplus. Bukharin read them and concluded the jams were expositional, soluble within the model's own terms. Three families' worth of dispute grow from a few pages of arithmetic, which is why this guide insists that the schemas be read slowly rather than cited quickly: the same figures support rival conclusions depending on which exchanges the reader treats as fragile, and that dependence, visible on the page, is the most honest introduction to the field's condition that a beginner can receive.

## Engels the editor, and the making of the crisis canon

A fact too often skipped in the doctrinal literature is that the two volumes on which most families rest reached print through another man's editorial hands. Marx died in 1883 leaving the second and third volumes in manuscript states ranging from nearly finished drafts of the eighteen sixties to notebooks of excerpts and fragments. Engels spent the remaining twelve years of his life, alongside his own work, preparing the texts: the second volume appeared in 1885, the third in 1894. His prefaces describe the labour with characteristic plainness, the deciphering of handwriting, the selection among parallel drafts, the marking of insertions where he had supplied connective material. Modern scholarship, armed with the complete historical critical edition, has been able to compare the printed volumes with the manuscripts line by line, and its verdict is double. Engels's editing was faithful in substance and transformative in presentation: he imposed chapter structures, smoothed transitions, and occasionally chose the more systematic of two draft formulations, so that the printed third volume reads as a treatise where the manuscripts read as a workshop. The consequence for crisis theory is concrete. The architecture that invites readers to treat the falling rate of profit as the centrepiece of a system is partly an editorial architecture, since the law's part stands third in a volume whose order Engels settled from Marx's plans and drafts. The credit part's famous disorder, by contrast, is the workshop showing through, and Engels himself warned readers that this section of the manuscript was the least finished. Families built on the treatise like parts of the volume and families built on the workshop parts therefore inherit different kinds of textual authority, a nuance the priority disputes rarely acknowledge. Knowing which stratum one is standing on, printed system or edited workshop, is part of the marking discipline this guide commends, and the manuscripts' progressive publication has made the knowledge available to any reader willing to consult the editorial scholarship.

## A panic in the age of gold, watched closely

Abstract mechanisms gain credibility when they can narrate a single dated episode without strain, so let the credit family demonstrate on the panic of 1866, the crisis Marx and Engels watched from Manchester and London as it broke. The house of Overend, Gurney and Company, the greatest discount house in the City, had drifted from bill broking into illiquid long term lending to railways and finance companies, funding the position with money repayable on demand. When confidence cracked in the spring of 1866, the house suspended payment on the eleventh of May, a Friday remembered as Black Friday, owing sums contemporaries estimated in the millions of pounds against assets that could not be realised at any speed. The Bank of England's rate was driven to ten per cent, the government issued the letter suspending the Bank Charter Act's restriction on the note issue, and the panic, denied its monetary fuel, burned down to a severe but bounded commercial crisis. Marx's correspondence of those weeks tracks the event with a connoisseur's attention, noting which houses were sound, which were rotten, and how the scramble for means of payment sorted them. Every element of the fifth family's template is present in the narrative: credit bridging the gap between illiquid commitments and liquid obligations, fictitious values carried at face until the moment of testing, the state's emergency suspension of its own monetary rules, and the subsequent liquidation distinguishing capitals by their real earnings rather than by their paper. The episode also shows the template's limit with equal clarity. The underlying trade cycle of the eighteen sixties, the cotton famine's aftermath, the railway investment wave, supplied the combustible material the discount house's imprudence ignited, and a purely financial telling of 1866 that ignored the industrial configuration would commit exactly the trigger and cause confusion this guide warned against. Narration, like taxonomy, is a discipline of marked joins.

## The long postwar boom as a problem for everyone

Any field confident of its mechanisms must explain not only the crises that occurred but the long crisis that did not. Between the late nineteen forties and the early nineteen seventies the advanced capitalist economies experienced their fastest sustained growth, full employment across decades, and no financial collapse remotely comparable to the nineteen thirties. For traditions expecting periodic liquidation, a quarter century of near continuous expansion is an anomaly requiring work, and the families' responses constitute a revealing stress test. Profitability writers pointed to the special conditions of the postwar settlement: a capital stock devalued by depression and war, cheap energy, a backlog of technologies from the nineteen thirties awaiting commercialisation, and American hegemony stabilising the world market, conditions whose gradual exhaustion should restore the tendency's bite, as several argued it visibly did after the mid nineteen sixties. The stagnation school, writing from inside the boom itself, held that the boom was the anomaly's explanation: Baran and Sweezy argued that military expenditure, the sales effort, and suburbanisation were absorbing a surplus that would otherwise have stagnated the system, so the golden age was the fix working, not the problem absent. Squeeze writers noted that the boom's later years displayed exactly their mechanism assembling, wage militancy rising as unemployment vanished, awaiting only the distributional confrontation of the nineteen seventies. Keynesian and mainstream accounts credited demand management and Bretton Woods, claiming the anomaly as their own vindication. The episode disciplines all parties impartially. A theory of crisis that required an auxiliary story for twenty five years of contrary evidence has spent some of its credibility, and the families' auxiliary stories differ in candour: those that specified the special conditions in advance of their exhaustion earned more than those that discovered the conditions afterwards. The pattern is the unfalsifiability problem in its temporal form, and the honest literature, to its credit, argues about the golden age in exactly those terms.

## Counteracting influences as the first family's stress test

Return, with the field now mapped, to the first family's most delicate internal feature, because it decides how that family may legitimately be defended. Marx's own presentation of the falling tendency lists the counteracting influences in the same breath as the law: increasing intensity of exploitation, depression of wages below their value, cheapening of the elements of constant capital, relative overpopulation, foreign trade, and the growth of stock capital. The law is therefore a tendency fighting its own antibodies from birth, and its net direction in any period is an empirical outcome, not a deduction. This structure creates a standing temptation. When profitability falls, the law is confirmed; when profitability rises, the counteracting influences are invoked; and a critic will ask, reasonably, what observation the theory has excluded. The family's serious defenders accept the question and answer it with specification. They commit to periods long enough for the antibodies' own limits to bind, since machinery can be cheapened only as fast as productivity in the capital goods sector grows; they commit to the mechanism's signature, a rising ratio of capital to labour preceding the strain; and they point to the counteracting influences' internal contradictions, foreign trade cheapening inputs while expanding the scale on which the tendency operates, as reasons the net outcome is biased downward over long horizons. Whether those commitments suffice is the subject of the technical literature and of this cluster's article on the tendency itself. What the pillar can settle is the form a legitimate defence must take: names, periods, and signatures stated before the data are consulted, the same bookkeeping standard the guide has applied to every family without favour.

## What the families presuppose about value

Beneath the taxonomy sits a shared foundation that a complete guide should expose rather than assume, since the families divide over mechanisms while standing on common ground. All five presuppose some version of the proposition that profit originates in surplus labour, that the value newly created in production divides between the reproduction of labour power and a surplus appropriated by the owners of capital, and that the system's quantities, profit rates, wage shares, departmental proportions, are usefully analysed as distributions of that newly created value. A reader who rejects the labour theory of value outright will therefore find the families' internal quarrels moot at the root, however instructive their descriptions of financial fragility or distributional conflict remain. The presupposition has its own famous internal difficulty, the transformation of values into prices of production, on which a century of technical literature has failed to converge and which blurs, in principle, the exact measurement of any value magnitude in price data. The families handle the difficulty with different degrees of candour. Profitability writers confront it head on, since their central ratio is the contested magnitude, and the schools within that family, temporal single system interpreters, simultaneous equation readers, and others, are defined largely by their transformation commitments. Realisation and credit writers can proceed further on monetary magnitudes alone, which partly explains their appeal to readers impatient with value theory's unresolved core. A pillar article is not the place to adjudicate the transformation problem, and this guide does not pretend otherwise. It insists only that the presupposition be visible, because a reader who knows which parts of an argument rest on contested foundations can weigh the argument's superstructure correctly, crediting the descriptions that survive translation into other frameworks and discounting the deductions that do not.

## Overcapacity across borders: the border dispute inside the profitability camp

Late twentieth century scholarship produced one influential account that resists tidy placement on the grid, and its resistance is instructive enough to earn it a section. Robert Brenner's explanation of the long downturn after 1973, set out in his 1998 study of the world economy in turmoil, locates the trouble neither in the aggregate capital labour ratio of a single economy nor in wages as such, but in uneven development between national manufacturing systems. New entrants, first German and Japanese, later East Asian producers, built modern capacity in industries where established American and British producers were already committed, and because the established producers' fixed capital and labour arrangements could not be abandoned cheaply, the result was chronic international overcapacity and overproduction within manufacturing: too much capacity chasing the same world markets, prices and profit rates in the traded goods sector pressed down for decades, and no single nation's liquidation able to clear a glut that was global in structure. Partisans of the falling rate family claim the account as a specification of their mechanism, since profitability is plainly the variable in distress. Disproportionality writers claim it with equal justice, since the disproportion in question runs between national industrial systems rather than between departments. Brenner's own framing, centred on the horizontal competition of capitals rather than on the vertical composition of each capital, declines both adoptions. The scholarly moral matches this guide's recurring one. Border disputes between families are not defects in the taxonomy; they are the taxonomy working, forcing an important argument to specify precisely which variable it watches and through what structure the variable deteriorates, and exposing that even sophisticated writers sometimes hold a mechanism at a level their chosen family's vocabulary cannot quite express.

## The Asian crisis as a late century grading exercise

The financial crises that struck East and Southeast Asia in 1997 and 1998 offer a second late century exercise in grading, valuable because the mainstream literature itself split along lines that shadow the grid. One mainstream reading, associated with Paul Krugman's earlier work on crony capitalism and moral hazard, held that implicit state guarantees had driven overinvestment by banks and conglomerates whose projects could not earn their cost of capital: an overaccumulation story in local dress, with profitability quietly doing the work. A rival reading, pressed by Steven Radelet and Jeffrey Sachs among others, held that the economies' fundamentals were broadly serviceable and that the catastrophe was a creditor panic, a self fulfilling withdrawal of short term foreign lending that turned illiquidity into insolvency: the credit family's pure panic form, arrived at from inside orthodox institutions. Marxist commentators of the period divided along the same seam, some stressing the region's integration into a stagnating world profitability structure, others the fictitious character of the property and equity booms the panic deflated. Distributional data sorted the families as usual: the squeeze family had nothing to say, since the crisis economies combined weak labour movements with, in several cases, wage growth dependent on the very capital inflows that reversed; underconsumption spoke mainly to the aftermath, when collapsed import demand deepened the regional slump. The exercise's lesson for readers is the portability of the instruments. The grid was drawn from nineteenth century texts and twentieth century controversies, yet it sorts a late century emerging market crisis without forcing, because the mechanisms it catalogues are configurations of production, distribution, proportion, and credit that recur wherever those four elements are combined under private ownership and market coordination. Portability is not proof. It is, however, the quality that separates a taxonomy of mechanisms from a chronicle of one tradition's opinions about its own century.

## Recognising the families by their vocabulary

Practised readers eventually sort arguments by ear before they sort them by logic, because each family has developed a lexical signature over its century of use. Profitability writing speaks of valorisation difficulties, of the rising mass of capital set in motion by each worker, of counteracting influences and of productive labour shrinking as a share of employment. Realisation writing speaks of outlets, of markets that must be found, of surplus that cannot be absorbed and of demand that fails to materialise at the point of sale. Disproportionality writing speaks of balance, of departments and branches, of bottlenecks beside gluts and of the anarchy that prevents their coordination. Squeeze writing speaks of shares, of settlements, of the reserve army's depth and of discipline in the workplace. Credit writing speaks of leverage, of fictitious values, of paper wealth and of the moment when claims are presented for payment in money that does not exist in sufficient quantity. Stagnation writing speaks of maturity, of surplus disposal problems, of waste as a functional necessity and of fixes that defer. Learning the signatures accelerates reading across the whole literature, including the journalism that borrows the vocabularies without the arguments. The acceleration carries one risk, which the wage test exists to neutralise: a writer may borrow a family's words while running another family's logic, speaking of absorption while reasoning about profit shares, or of fictitious capital while reasoning about consumption shortfalls. Trust the predictions, not the perfume. Ask what the argument expects from wages, from profitability's trajectory, from the sectoral pattern and the debt stock, and the family in actual use will identify itself whatever costume the vocabulary has provided.

## The political cycle of full employment

One descendant of the squeeze family grew influential enough to require separate notice, because it converts the family's economics into a theory of policy rhythm. Kalecki's 1943 essay on the political aspects of full employment argued that lasting full employment under capitalism would face resistance from business leadership on social rather than narrowly economic grounds: the sack would cease to discipline, workplace authority would erode, and the confidence on which investment was said to depend would be withheld until policy restored a margin of unemployment. The stop go cycles of postwar British economic management were subsequently read through this lens by many commentators, with governments alternating between expansion that pleased electorates and restriction that reassured boardrooms, and the political business cycle literature in mainstream political science developed a neighbouring formalisation in which incumbent governments time expansions for electoral advantage. The Kaleckian version remains the sharper instrument for this guide's purposes because it is falsifiable in the family's own currency: it predicts that sustained full employment will generate organised political pressure for restrictive policy even while profits remain adequate in accounting terms, pressure whose stated rationale will invoke confidence and discipline rather than margins. Readers can grade that prediction against the documentary record of employers' organisations in the full employment decades, and the record, memoranda and all, is unusually quotable. The episode also demonstrates the tradition at its methodological best, a mechanism stated in a handful of pages, exposed to archival evidence, and still argued about on terms its author would recognise. Whatever verdict a reader reaches on its truth, its form is the standard to which this guide has held every family: a named variable, a directional prediction, and an evidence trail that others can audit.

## Who buys the surplus: the accumulation debate in full

The realisation family's founding controversy rewards a fuller hearing than the summary given earlier, because its afterlife reaches far beyond the tradition that hosted it. Luxemburg's question was disarmingly simple. In expanded reproduction, part of the surplus value is not consumed but accumulated, converted into additional machinery and additional labour power. The commodities embodying that part must nonetheless be sold before the conversion can occur. Workers cannot buy them, since wages equal only the variable capital already advanced. Capitalists as consumers take only the portion of surplus they devote to revenue. Who, then, buys the accumulating portion, year after year, in a closed capitalist system? Luxemburg examined the schemas' arithmetic, concluded that the exchanges it depicts presuppose the very demand they were meant to explain, and located the missing buyers outside the system, in the peasants, artisans, and colonies of the non-capitalist world, whose progressive absorption into the market both fed accumulation and foretold its eventual starvation. Her opponents replied that the question contains its answer: the accumulating portion is bought by the departments themselves in the act of expanding, Department One purchasing means of production from itself and Department Two in proportions the schemas can be made to display, so that investment demand is not a deus ex machina but the system's own largest customer. Bauer's four year numerical model was constructed to exhibit exactly that self sustaining choreography, and Bukharin's polemic supplied its doctrine: a capitalism of pure producers and workers is an abstraction, but the abstraction is coherent, and its coherence refutes the claim of logical impossibility.

Posterity distributed the honours in a way neither camp foresaw. Kalecki, arriving at macroeconomic dynamics independently through Rosa Luxemburg's question as much as through Marx's schemas, compressed the resolution into his celebrated profit identity: capitalists as a class earn what they spend, since their investment and consumption expenditure returns to them as the realised profit of the system, while workers spend what they earn. The identity vindicates the coherence claim against Luxemburg's impossibility charge while vindicating her instinct that demand, including demand originating outside current production, decides what gets realised. Joan Robinson, introducing the English translation of Luxemburg's accumulation study, treated its author as a forerunner of effective demand theory who had asked Keynes's question a generation early with Marxist tools. The scholarly consensus that emerged is a model of the grading this guide recommends: the specific impossibility thesis failed, the imperialism corollary drawn from it lost its foundation, and the question itself, where aggregate demand for a growing surplus originates and what sustains it, migrated into the permanent agenda of economics. Families are rarely refuted whole. They are refuted in layers, and what survives the refutation is often the most valuable stratum, the question that organised the inquiry. Remembering that pattern will protect readers from the symmetrical errors of declaring a family dead because its strongest claim failed, and of declaring it vindicated because its question still matters.

## Joint stock, credit, and the system's self transcendence

One further stratum of the credit chapters deserves extraction, because later writers of several families have mined it and because it displays Marx thinking about crisis and about the system's historical character in the same movement. Analysing the joint stock company in the third volume's chapter on the role of credit, Marx describes the transformation of the functioning capitalist into a manager of other people's capital and of the owner into a mere recipient of dividends, a development he characterises, in a daring formulation, as the abolition of capital as private property within the confines of the capitalist mode of production itself. Production on an enormous scale becomes possible without the personal fortune any individual could assemble; speculation receives its institutional charter at the same moment, since other people's money invites a recklessness that one's own discourages; and the credit system that equalises profit rates across the economy simultaneously centralises the command over social savings in a financial layer increasingly remote from the production it finances. For the credit family, this analysis supplies the structural reason crises grow more financial as capitalism matures: the larger the share of accumulation routed through joint stock and banking channels, the larger the fictitious superstructure relative to the earnings beneath it, and the more the reconciliation, when it comes, takes the form of collapsing paper rather than of glutted warehouses. For the stagnation family, the same chapters feed the surplus absorption story, since a financial sector of growing relative size is itself an outlet, employing labour and absorbing revenue while producing no goods against which its claims can be checked. For critics of both families, the passage is a warning against treating Marx's credit analysis as a moral denunciation of speculation: the system's self transcendence and its instability are, in his presentation, one process with two faces, the socialisation of production advancing inside the very forms that make breakdown periodic. A reader who grasps that doubleness has grasped something about the founder's method that no taxonomy conveys, and the families' best modern writing, on financialisation above all, is best understood as an attempt to recover it with contemporary evidence.

## How the one theory myth was manufactured

The misreading this guide most labours against, the belief that Marx delivered a single crisis doctrine, has a traceable history, and tracing it acquits the founder while indicting his transmitters. The mass parties of the Second International required teachable doctrine: pamphlets, party schools, and popular expositions had to compress a sprawling, unfinished economics into catechisms a working class readership could master in evening classes. Karl Kautsky's popular exposition of Marx's economic doctrines, the era's most successful compression, presented the crisis material in the idiom of its decade's debates, with the emphasis falling where the Bernstein controversy had placed it, on the system's deepening contradictions and eventual supersession. Compression is not falsification, but it has a physics of its own: mechanisms that fit in a pamphlet chapter survive, distinctions that require the second volume's arithmetic drop away, and the layered separation this guide has restored, possibility here, necessity there, five rival completions of the necessity claim, flattens into the single assertion that capitalism produces crises by its nature. Twentieth century textbook lineages inherited the flattening from both directions at once, since hostile summaries preferred one doctrine to refute and friendly ones preferred one doctrine to teach. The scholarly recovery of the plurality is comparatively late work, driven by the complete edition's manuscripts, by the translation of the Grundrisse, and by historians of economic thought who read the crisis texts in their documentary order rather than in their catechism order. Readers who wonder why a century of intelligent people misdescribed the field's shape will find the answer in that institutional history rather than in any individual's carelessness: doctrines are simplified by the machinery that distributes them, and the wage test, requiring as it does a writer to stake a prediction in public, is best understood as a counter machine, a small device for reintroducing into popular exposition the distinctions that distribution had ground away.

## The monetary thread that runs through every family

Step back from the taxonomy once more and a connective tissue becomes visible that the row by row treatment necessarily obscured. Every family's mechanism, whatever its home territory, must at the decisive moment express itself in money: in payments that cannot be met, in credit that cannot be rolled over, in prices that cannot be realised. Profitability deteriorates for years in the quiet of cost accounts before it erupts as a monetary stoppage when firms can no longer borrow against expected valorisation. Disproportion accumulates in physical stocks and order books before it announces itself as a chain of dishonoured bills. Even the squeeze operates through the monetary hinge, since the wage advances it blames are claims on money revenue that firms must validate in sales. This is why the first volume's apparently modest analysis of money's functions carries such weight in the architecture of the whole: the separation of sale and purchase is not one mechanism among others but the universal medium in which every mechanism must travel to reach the surface of events. Marx signalled the point in his programmatic remark that the world market crisis is the forcible adjustment of all the system's contradictions at once, since the adjustment is forcible precisely because it is monetary, a general demand for payment in the universal equivalent that no private promise can counterfeit. The observation reframes the families' rivalry in a final, clarifying way. They are not competing descriptions of different events. They are competing accounts of which contradiction, among the several a maturing expansion develops, is the one that finally presents itself for payment, and the wage test works because distribution is where several of the candidate contradictions leave their earliest and most legible traces. A reader who holds both truths together, the plurality of mechanisms and the unity of their monetary stage, possesses the field in its mature form, which is all that a pillar article can honestly promise to deliver.

## Interest at the peak of the panic

One of the third volume's most quietly original observations ties the credit family to the profitability family with a knot neither can cut. In ordinary times, Marx argues, the rate of interest and the rate of profit move in loose relation, interest being a portion of profit ceded to the money capitalist. In the crisis the relation inverts in appearance: interest climbs to its maximum precisely when profit of enterprise collapses, because the demand in a panic is not for capital to invest but for money to settle, for means of payment with which maturing obligations can be discharged at any price. Firms that would not borrow at five per cent to expand will borrow at ten to survive the week. The observation matters beyond its elegance. It explains why the monetary surface of a crisis is such a treacherous guide to its depths: the variable that screams loudest at the peak, the interest rate, is an effect of the scramble for liquidity and not a reading of the underlying profitability that conditioned the scramble. Policy traditions that treat crisis as fundamentally a money market event, curable by supplying the missing liquidity, rest on the surface reading. Marx's counter claim is not that liquidity provision fails, the suspension episodes showed it working, but that it answers the panic's question rather than the cycle's: it stops the payments chain from snapping while leaving the devaluation of unsound capitals to proceed by slower means. Every subsequent debate about lender of last resort policy, from Bagehot's rule to the interventions of the twenty first century, replays that distinction in new institutional dress, and the families divide predictably over it, credit writers emphasising how much of the damage is the panic itself, profitability writers insisting that liquidity merely changes the timetable of the reckoning. The wage test does not apply here; a different instrument does, the question of what problem the proposed remedy solves, and readers armed with both questions can interrogate nearly any crisis commentary in print.

## The human register of the cycle

A guide organised by mechanisms risks a bloodlessness the founder's own writings never display, and one corrective section belongs in the pillar for the record. The young Engels's study of the English working class in 1844 and 1845 was built on close observation of what the trade cycle did to households: employment that vanished with the mill's order book, wages that rose briefly in the boom only to be clawed back in the slump, families cycling between the workshop, the pawnshop, and the workhouse on a rhythm set by markets they could not see. Marx's mature analysis preserves the register inside the theory. The reserve army chapter of the first volume is, among other things, an account of what periodic crisis means for the people periodically expelled from production, and the case for the tradition's enduring appeal cannot be understood without it: the families promise not merely to explain an aggregate fluctuation but to explain why insecurity arrives in waves for those least able to bear it. Rival cycle theories are not indifferent to unemployment, and the best of them measure their success by its reduction. The difference is one of theoretical location. Where mainstream accounts treat joblessness in a slump as a quantity to be minimised by better stabilisation, the Marxist families treat it as a mechanism of the system itself, the means by which labour is redistributed, wages are disciplined, and the conditions of the next expansion are prepared. A reader need not accept that location to recognise its consequence for the evidence: the tradition will always read a crisis's unemployment figures as data about the system's functioning and not only about its failure, and its critics will always suspect that reading of converting suffering into confirmation. Both the reading and the suspicion belong in an honest guide, and they are entered here without a verdict, because the verdict depends on the comparative performance of the whole framework, which this article has taught the reader to grade rather than assumed the right to pronounce.

## Two questions the grid cannot answer

Intellectual honesty about an instrument includes a statement of its limits, and the grid has two that readers should carry alongside its uses. The first is magnitude. The families explain why expansions generate their interruptions; none of them, in classical form, predicts how deep a given interruption will cut. Depth depends on the stock of malinvestment and debt accumulated, on the banking system's capital, on the state's willingness to intervene, on the world market's simultaneous condition, variables the mechanisms constrain but do not quantify. A profitability strain of identical size can precede a sharp short recession in one configuration of balance sheets and a decade long depression in another, and the difference lies in the credit and policy strata rather than in the originating strain. Readers who demand depth forecasts from the grid are asking a taxonomy to do a model's work, and the disappointment that follows is a category error this guide would rather prevent than share. The second limit is timing. The mechanisms specify sequences, profitability strains before investment halts, wage shares peak before squeezes bite, leverage builds before panics, but they specify no clock. Between the completed configuration and the break there stretches a variable interval governed by confidence, by refinancing calendars, by accident and policy, the very trigger territory this guide separated from causes at the outset. Marx's own periodicity suggestion, the fixed capital replacement cycle, was an attempt to supply the missing clock from within the theory, and its partial failure, investment rhythms being looser than machinery lifetimes, stands as the field's reminder that timing may be irreducibly partly exogenous. A grid that knows it cannot tell you when or how deep is a more trustworthy guide to why and by what mechanism than any apparatus that promises the whole, and the families' century of embarrassment can be read, in large part, as the record of partisans forgetting one or the other of these limits at the moment of maximum confidence.

## Falling prices and the arithmetic of debt

A feature of the crises Marx observed, so constant that his texts take it for granted, has faded from later experience and needs recovering for the mechanisms to be read correctly: prices fell, hard, in nineteenth century contractions. Under metallic money and without deposit insurance or discretionary central banking of the modern kind, the liquidation expressed itself as a general scramble for a shrinking stock of acceptable means of payment, and the prices of commodities and assets dropped together. Falling prices transform the arithmetic of every family. Debts are contracted in nominal terms, so deflation raises the real burden of every obligation precisely when earnings collapse, converting illiquidity into insolvency along the whole chain of credit, the dynamic Irving Fisher formalised in 1933 as debt deflation and illustrated from the American catastrophe then unfolding. Capacity that might have survived a recession at stable prices is destroyed when its output must be sold into a falling market against fixed charges. Wages, sticky even in that flexible century, rise in real terms for those who keep employment while the employed total shrinks, which complicates the squeeze family's bookkeeping for the gold standard era: the distributional movement inside a deflationary crisis is not the movement the family predicts for the boom that preceded it, and its writers learned to specify that their claims concern the expansion's end, not the contraction's depths. Modern regimes, committed by doctrine and by institutional design to preventing general deflation, force the liquidation into other channels, asset prices, exchange rates, sovereign spreads, so that the families' mechanisms reappear with altered price signatures. Readers grading old texts against new episodes should perform that translation consciously, asking not whether prices fell as the nineteenth century template expects, but where the devaluation the template requires was obliged to occur under a monetary constitution designed to refuse it at the commodity level.

## A note on the standing of the claims in this guide

Scholarly hygiene requires one closing clarification before the misreadings are catalogued, concerning the confidence grades of what has been asserted. The documentary claims, the locations of arguments in the corpus, the publication history of the volumes and of the major interventions from Luxemburg to Grossmann to Sweezy, and the distributional configurations of the episodes used as worked examples, represent the settled record of the scholarship as this guide's authors understand it, and dissent on details would not disturb the map. The interpretive claims, the five family taxonomy itself, the wage test as the field's discriminating instrument, and the relative grading of families against episodes, are this guide's argued constructions, continuous with taxonomies in the literature from Sweezy and Wright onward but not copied from any one of them, and they are offered for use and correction rather than as doctrine. The evaluative claims, above all the concessions entered against the unfalsifiability objection and the plain statement that terminal breakdown predictions have a poor record, follow Part Six discipline: verdicts are given where the record warrants them, attributed where scholars divide, and withheld where the evidence remains genuinely open. Readers who keep those three grades separate while using the guide will extract from it exactly the kind of value it was built to deliver, a map whose roads can be trusted at different speeds.

## Misreadings this guide exists to correct

Five errors recur in the coverage of this subject with a persistence that suggests structural causes, and each can now be corrected in a sentence or two. The first error presents the falling rate of profit as Marx's theory of crisis, full stop, ignoring the reproduction, realisation, and credit materials that occupy more pages of the corpus than the law itself. The second error presents the families as compatible layers of one doctrine, dissolving their opposite wage predictions into a general atmosphere of contradiction. The third error attributes to Marx a prediction of final collapse, when the terminal doctrine was a later construction contested from its birth and largely abandoned by the tradition's scholarship, as the breakdown section documented. The fourth error equates crisis in this literature with recession in the ordinary sense, missing the liquidation function that defines the phenomenon and makes the theories' claims specific enough to test. The fifth error cites the possibility of crisis, the separation of sale and purchase in a money economy, as though establishing possibility were the same as explaining necessity, a confusion Marx himself ridiculed in the economists of his day. Behind all five errors sits a single convenience: they spare the writer the labour of distinguishing. The distinctions are the subject. A reader who has absorbed them owns the field's map, and a writer who evades them, however sonorous the prose, is selling atmosphere in place of analysis.

## How to choose: a discipline for readers

Pillars map fields; they do not conscript readers. Yet a guide that refused all guidance on choosing among the families would shirk at the final fence, so the following discipline is offered as method rather than as verdict. Choose by episode, not by allegiance: reconstruct the configuration before a breakdown that interests you, the profit rate's trajectory, the wage share's movement, the sectoral pattern, the credit structure, and let the configuration nominate the rows that speak to it, in the graded manner practised on the nineteen thirties above. Choose by exposure: prefer, other things equal, the family that risks the most specific prediction about the variable you can measure, because its successes will teach you more and its failures will teach you sooner. Choose provisionally and in writing: record the row and the expectation before consulting the sequel, the elementary bookkeeping that converts hindsight into knowledge. And hold the mainstream shelf within reach throughout, since the monetary and financial accelerator accounts of propagation will often explain the phase of an episode that the families' onset mechanisms leave dark. None of this requires the reader to abandon judgement about which family has performed best across the long record, and this guide's own weighting can be stated without breaching its neutrality, since it is a judgement about scholarship rather than about parties. The profitability and credit families have generated the deepest research programmes, the most explicit measurement, and the most productive controversies, and they carry correspondingly great weight in the cluster that follows. The realisation family, corrected by its critics, survives as the indispensable question about demand's origin. Disproportionality persists as the pattern discipline every account needs and none can run on alone. Squeeze owns one era's evidence outright and furnishes the wage test's clearest pole. Stagnation frames the longest horizon and the loosest predictions, valuable as orientation, hazardous as explanation. A reader who follows that discipline will end, as most serious scholars of the subject have ended, with a weighted pluralism: some rows trusted more than others, all rows distrusted in their triumphalist moods, and the grid itself retained as the field's irreplaceable contribution, a map of the ways capitalist expansion can undermine its own conditions, drawn by a tradition that argued about the map for a century and improved it by arguing.

The wider field into which this pillar fits, its concepts of value, exploitation, accumulation, and class presupposed throughout, is surveyed in the companion article presenting [Marxist economics as a complete guide](/2016/04/01/marxist-economics-complete-guide/), and readers who arrived here from the news rather than from the library are advised to let that survey supply the foundations this article has had to assume. The pillar's own foundations are now laid: five families, one later account, one test that separates them, and one objection that keeps them honest. The cluster that follows takes each load bearing element in turn, the profit tendency, the empirical record, the demand concepts, the applied case, and the comparison with Keynes, and submits this introduction's map to the evidence in detail. Maps earn their authority by surviving such submissions. This one is offered in that spirit.

A final word belongs to the readers who use the map in anger, in the middle of some unfolding contraction, when every commentator within earshot is dealing families like cards. Slow down. Establish the configuration before reaching for the mechanism, insist on the wage question before accepting the vocabulary, and remember that the scholars who built this field at its best, whatever their family, shared a patience the news cycle punishes: they let an episode finish before they graded it. The grid rewards that patience and exposes its absence, in others and, on honest days, in oneself.

## For students and exam candidates

The mark carrying move in any examination treatment of this topic is plurality with separation. An answer that names one mechanism, however elegantly, reads to an examiner as a summary of a summary; an answer that names the families, assigns each its textual home and its leading proponents, and then applies the wage test to show that the families are rivals rather than layers will outscore it on every published marking criterion this guide's authors have inspected. Structure your answer as the grid is structured. Open with the documentary fact that no finished crisis theory exists and that the material sits scattered across the volumes, since that fact explains the plurality you are about to display. Give each family one tight paragraph: variable watched, reason the boom degrades it, form of the rupture, one named proponent. Then turn the test on the set, stating the opposite predictions that follow from a rise in real wages and noting which families fall silent on the question, because the silences discriminate as surely as the signs. Close with the unfalsifiability objection and the bookkeeping reply, which demonstrates the evaluative judgement examiners reward most. Candidates who can also place the possibility and necessity distinction accurately, crediting the manuscripts against Say's law for the first and the families for the competing versions of the second, hold the topic's full range in one answer.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This topic teaches best as a controversy with an instrument attached. Set the primary extracts in the itinerary order used in this guide, money chapter first and credit chapters last, so that students watch the strata accumulate and can argue about priority from evidence rather than from allegiance. Then introduce the wage test early and make it the seminar's standing rule: no contribution may praise or blame a mechanism without stating its wage prediction, a rule that transforms the usual survey discussion, in which every theory receives polite assent, into a sequence of forced choices students remember. The breakdown controversy supplies the ideal case study in the sociology of theory, since it shows a tradition constructing a doctrine its founder never stated, defending it institutionally, and then quietly retiring it, and a single session on Grossmann's schema, with students extending the arithmetic under varied assumptions, teaches more about model dependence than a lecture on method. Assessment design follows naturally: give candidates an unfamiliar episode with distributional and financial data attached, and mark the grading of families row by row, rewarding recorded misses as highly as recorded hits. The pedagogical aim throughout is the habit this guide has modelled, sympathy in exposition, separation in judgement, and bookkeeping at the end.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For working researchers the field's live frontier is measurement under disagreement. The profitability family's fate turns on capital stock valuation and on the treatment of financial profits, and new archival and statistical work still moves the long run series enough to matter, so the empirical cluster articles should be read as progress reports rather than as settlements. Historians will find the richest underworked seam in the transmission mechanisms: the gold standard era's crisis propagation through trade credit and bullion flows is documented in banking archives that the theoretical literature cites far more often than it opens. Journalists and analysts covering a fresh downturn can use the grid as a checklist against deadline rhetoric. Establish which family a quoted expert's argument belongs to by asking the wage question, and print the answer's implication alongside the quotation, so that readers can see what the analysis commits its holder to expect next. Resist, in copy, the two deadline temptations this guide has catalogued: the blended account in which every mechanism fires at once, and the terminal framing in which the present episode is silently promoted to breakdown status. Both temptations sell. Both misinform. The discipline of naming the family, the prediction, and the evidence that would embarrass it fits in a single paragraph, and it is the paragraph that distinguishes analysis from commentary in coverage of any crisis, in any decade.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Marxist theory of crisis?

There is no single theory bearing that name, and the honest answer begins there. The phrase covers a family of arguments sharing one conviction, that capitalist expansions generate the conditions of their own interruption, while disagreeing about the operative mechanism: falling profitability, unsold output, sectoral disproportion, wage driven profit squeeze, or credit collapse. Marx's writings contain material for all of them, assembled in different proportions by different successors. Any presentation offering one mechanism as the theory has silently selected from the corpus, and the first scholarly question to ask of it is which passages the selection promoted and which it set aside.

### Q: Did Marx have a single theory of crisis?

He did not. Crisis material is scattered through his work: the possibility of breakdown in the analysis of money, reproduction and its proportions in the second volume of Capital, the falling tendency of the profit rate and the credit system in the third, and the assault on Say's law in the manuscripts on surplus value. The systematic volume on the world market and its crises, projected in his six book plan, was never written. Later writers assembled the fragments into rival theories, each able to cite genuine texts, none able to claim the founder's own ranking of the mechanisms.

### Q: Why does capitalism have crises at all?

Each family in the field answers through its own mechanism, but the answers share a root: production is undertaken for profit by independent firms whose decisions are coordinated only after the fact by markets, so expansion systematically overshoots the conditions of its own continuation. Investment outruns profitable outlets, output outruns solvent demand, sectors outrun their proportions, wages outrun the profit share, or credit outruns the earnings meant to service it. Crisis is the name for the forcible correction that follows. Rival schools attribute the overshoot to policy error or external shock; the Marxist families are united in treating the overshoot as generated inside the expansion itself.

### Q: What is the difference between the possibility and the necessity of crisis?

Possibility is established by the form of exchange itself. Once money mediates transactions, selling and buying separate in time and place, payment chains link firms together, and rupture becomes conceivable without any deeper cause. Marx established that much against the doctrine that supply automatically creates its own demand. Necessity is the harder claim that some mechanism inside accumulation forces rupture at intervals, so crisis recurs as a product of the system's motion rather than as an accident. Each of the five families is a candidate mechanism of necessity, and the scholarly argument between them is an argument about which compulsion the evidence supports.

### Q: What are the main families of Marxist crisis theory?

Five families dominate the literature, with a sixth standing alongside. Falling profitability and overaccumulation locate the strain in the rising mass of capital relative to the labour that produces surplus value. Realisation failure locates it in the sale of output at valorising prices, with underconsumption as its best known variant blaming restricted mass purchasing power. Disproportionality blames the unplanned pattern of investment between sectors. Profit squeeze blames the wage advances that full employment makes possible. Credit accounts blame the expansion of fictitious capital beyond real earnings. The sixth, stagnation theory, extends the time horizon to chronic surplus absorption problems.

### Q: Do Marxist crisis theories contradict each other?

In several cases, directly. The clearest signature is the effect of rising real wages. Profitability and profit squeeze accounts predict that higher wages compress the profit share and bring breakdown closer; underconsumption accounts predict that higher wages strengthen demand and push breakdown away. Both predictions cannot hold for the same episode in their strong forms. Other pairs coexist more peacefully, since a sectoral overshoot can trigger a credit collapse in sequence, provided the analyst ranks the mechanisms and marks the join. Unranked blending, in which every mechanism is invoked at once, is the habit serious scholarship in the field criticises most.

### Q: Does Marx predict that capitalism will collapse?

Not in any form that survives textual inspection. His works predict recurrent crises, describe a falling tendency in the rate of profit checked by counteracting influences, and treat capitalism as a historically transient order. A dated or mechanically necessary terminal collapse appears nowhere as a derivation. The breakdown doctrine was constructed by followers, above all in the German social democratic debates and in Grossmann's 1929 attempt to prove termination from reproduction arithmetic, and it was contested from the beginning by other Marxists. The tradition's mainstream scholarship has since set the terminal claim aside while retaining the analysis of recurrent crisis.

### Q: What was the breakdown controversy?

It was the long dispute, running from the eighteen nineties through the nineteen thirties, over whether capitalism would terminate through its own economic laws. Eduard Bernstein opened it by arguing that cartels, credit, and expanding markets showed a system adapting rather than collapsing, and that strategy should follow the evidence. Orthodox replies defended deepening contradiction without specifying a mechanism of termination. Henryk Grossmann then attempted the specification in 1929, extending reproduction schemas until surplus value appeared insufficient for continued accumulation. Critics showed the terminus depended on freezing parameters that struggle and policy would move, and the controversy closed with a durable distinction between recurrent crisis, which the texts support, and terminal breakdown, which they do not.

### Q: Where does Marx discuss crisis in Capital?

Everywhere except in a chapter of its own. The first volume's treatment of money introduces the separation of sale and purchase and the payment chains through which failure spreads. The second volume's reproduction schemas ground the disproportionality and realisation families, and its chapter on money material reproduction attacks simple underconsumption directly. The third volume's third part develops the falling tendency of the profit rate, while its fifth part contains the extended analysis of credit, banking panic, and fictitious capital. The sharpest statement of the possibility argument sits outside Capital altogether, in the manuscripts on theories of surplus value directed against Ricardo.

### Q: Which crisis theory do most Marxists hold today?

No census of the tradition exists, and any answer naming proportions would invent data. What can be said from the literature is that single mechanism allegiance has weakened: most scholarly writing in the field works with a ranked combination, profitability or credit usually carrying the greatest weight, and the terminal breakdown claim commands only a small minority. Textbook presentations still overweight the falling rate of profit because it is the most formally developed mechanism. The defensible summary is that the tradition's centre of gravity lies with plural, evidence graded accounts, while its popular presentations remain more monotheistic than its scholarship.

### Q: How does the five families grid separate a profit-rate crisis from a realisation crisis?

By the location of the failure and the evidence each location predicts. In a profit rate crisis the difficulty begins in production: the surplus extracted per unit of capital advanced has fallen, investment loses its rationale before goods pile up, and the signature evidence is a deteriorating profitability series preceding the break. In a realisation crisis production may be healthy while the sale fails: inventories accumulate, order books thin, and commodities embodying surplus value cannot convert into money at expected prices. The grid forces the analyst to say which failure came first, because sequence is evidence, and an account that will not commit to a sequence has not yet chosen a family.

### Q: What should a student check before calling a downturn a crisis of accumulation?

Four checks discipline the label. Establish that the episode involved forcible liquidation, failures, defaults, idle capacity, and asset write downs, rather than a mild pause in growth. Reconstruct the configuration beforehand: the profit rate's direction, the wage share's movement, the sectoral pattern, and the credit structure. Identify which family that configuration nominates and state what the family would expect next. Finally, record what would count against the reading, so the analysis can lose honestly if the sequel disagrees. A downturn surviving those checks may be called a crisis of accumulation with a clear conscience; one that fails them is a recession wearing borrowed grandeur.

### Q: Why did Marx never publish a finished crisis theory?

Because crisis belonged to the unwritten outer volumes of his design. His six book plan reserved the systematic treatment for the books on foreign trade and the world market, which stood last in the sequence and were never reached. The volumes he did prepare treat crisis where their subjects touch it, in money, reproduction, profit, and credit, and illness, financial pressure, and the sheer growth of the project's middle volumes consumed the decades in which the outer books might have been drafted. What survives is therefore richer than a single theory and less orderly: a set of powerful partial analyses whose ranking he never performed, which is precisely why the tradition after him is a field of rivals.

### Q: How does the wage test separate the families in a concrete downturn?

Reconstruct the wage movement first: whether real wages and the wage share rose, stagnated, or fell across the expansion preceding the break. Then read the families off the configuration. A downturn following rising wage share and tight labour markets fits profit squeeze, with profitability arguing the longer trend beneath. A downturn following wage stagnation and lagging consumption fits underconsumption or realisation readings. A downturn in which distribution barely moved directs attention to the sectoral pattern and the debt structure. The test separates because the families staked opposite or silent positions on that variable in advance, so the observed movement grades them without any party being able to adjust its story after the fact.

### Q: What evidence would count against a disproportionality explanation?

Three findings would damage it seriously. Evidence that the proportions between the capital goods and consumption goods sectors, and among major branches, remained within their customary ranges through the expansion preceding the break. Evidence that the downturn began simultaneously across sectors with no identifiable leading overshoot, since the family expects a pattern of origin before generalisation. And evidence that idle capacity after the break was no more concentrated in the previously oversized branches than elsewhere, since the family's correction story requires the audit to fall hardest where the disproportion grew. A family defender may recruit credit collapse as the generalising mechanism, which is legitimate, but the recruitment must be marked, and the sectoral evidence must still be produced.
