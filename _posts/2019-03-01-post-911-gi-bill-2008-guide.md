The operative question behind the Post-9/11 GI Bill is precise: what education has a service member earned by serving on active duty after September 11, 2001, and in what form should the federal government deliver that earned education when service ends? Congress legislated against a concrete failure. A volunteer force had fought two long wars through repeated deployments, while the principal education benefit available at separation paid a flat monthly sum that had fallen far behind actual tuition charges, required a payroll contribution from junior enlisted pay, and offered nothing toward housing during enrollment. The statute enacted in 2008 answered the question by rebuilding the benefit around actual charges and actual local costs, rather than around a single national payment detached from both.

![Student veterans on a university campus studying under the Post-9/11 GI Bill and the article angle on a benefit priced by place - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-49.webp)

The problem had a history. The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 had paid tuition toward an approved course of study and had added a subsistence allowance during enrollment, treating education as readjustment earned by wartime service. The Montgomery GI Bill of 1984 rested on a different premise. Designed for a peacetime all volunteer force, the Montgomery program operated as a recruiting inducement: a service member contributed 1,200 dollars through pay reductions and received a fixed monthly amount at separation, bearing personally the risk that tuition would rise faster than Congress adjusted the rate. By the 2007 to 2008 academic year that amount reached 1,101 dollars per month for a full time student, for education and living expenses combined, regardless of the charges at the institution attended. Veterans of the Iraq and Afghanistan wars therefore faced a benefit that discounted the price of college without covering the price, at the moment when years spent in uniform had placed degree completion years behind civilian peers.

The 2008 answer carries a consequence that defines the program and that a reader must grasp before any detail makes sense. The Post-9/11 GI Bill is a benefit whose value depends on the veteran's zip code, because the housing component tracks local military housing rates and the tuition component tracks public rates, so identical service yields materially different amounts by location. Two veterans with indistinguishable records, discharged on the same day at the same percentage tier, receive different tuition payments where state public charges differ and different housing payments where school zip codes differ. Geography sits inside the formulas by design. Congress tied assistance to the real local cost of tuition and of shelter, accepting unequal dollar totals for equal service as the price of paying real costs rather than an averaged fiction.

The design has three payments. Tuition and fees move directly from the Department of Veterans Affairs to the institution, measured against actual charges and bounded by a ceiling. A monthly housing allowance moves to the student, measured against the Basic Allowance for Housing for a service member in pay grade E-5 with dependents in the zip code of the institution, and payable only for enrollment above half time. A books and supplies stipend moves to the student as well, measured by credit hours at a flat national rate. A service scale then multiplies every component, rising from 40 percent after 90 aggregate days of qualifying service to 100 percent after 36 months, with the full measure granted after a service connected disability discharge following 30 continuous days. Entitlement totals 36 months, the equivalent of four academic years of full time study.

Around that core, the statute built contested machinery. Transferability allowed a service member, after six years of service and an agreement to serve four additional years, to assign unused entitlement to a spouse or children, converting a separation benefit into a retention instrument. The Yellow Ribbon Program invited institutions to share the cost of charges above the ceiling, with the department matching the institutional contribution for beneficiaries at the maximum tier. A revenue counting rule inside a separate statute, the Higher Education Act, treated veterans benefits as nonfederal revenue for proprietary institutions, giving each enrolled veteran a compliance value far beyond tuition paid. Amendments in 2010 and in 2017 widened the training covered, replaced a state based tuition ceiling for private institutions with a national cap, restored entitlement lost to school closures, and removed the deadline for using benefits for a defined generation of veterans.

The sections that follow take the statute in order: origins and the passage bargain of 2008, the architecture of the three components and the service scale, transferability and the Yellow Ribbon Program, the counting rule and the recruitment economy that the rule created, the amendment history through the Colmery Act, and administration, measured effects, and limits. The record assembled across those sections supports neither unqualified celebration nor unqualified indictment. Completion evidence showed a majority of tracked student veterans finishing a credential. Payment incentives, at the same time, rewarded enrollment at institutions whose students finished at low rates. A complete guide holds both findings in view, because the statute produced both.

## Origins and Passage

The statute enacted in 2008, formally the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008, presented itself from the first draft as a restoration. Senator Jim Webb of Virginia, the sponsor who wrote and guided the measure to passage, framed the ambition against a precedent set more than six decades earlier: a government that had asked a citizen force to fight a long war owed that force an education benefit generous enough to change the direction of a life. The comparison was structural. The 2008 law borrowed the architecture of the Second World War program, tuition paid toward an actual course of study, support for living costs during enrollment, and entitlement measured in months of schooling, and the borrowing carried an argument about fairness between generations. If the generation that fought from 1941 to 1945 had received a benefit of that scale, the generation that volunteered after September 11, 2001, and deployed repeatedly to Afghanistan and Iraq had a claim to treatment measured against the same standard. Every controversy that followed, over cost, over retention, and over transferability, unfolded inside that framing. Opponents rarely disputed that veterans of the post-2001 wars deserved more than the predecessor program provided; the dispute concerned how much more, how quickly eligibility should vest, and whether generosity at separation would weaken a force dependent on experienced volunteers.

The lineage began with the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 22, 1944, sixteen days after the Normandy landings. Harry W. Colmery, a Kansas lawyer, a veteran of the Army Air Service in the First World War, and a former national commander of the American Legion, drafted the proposal in longhand during the Christmas and New Year holidays spanning 1943 and 1944. Colmery understood, from the experience of veterans after 1918, that mass demobilization handled badly produced unemployment and wasted capacity. The American Legion carried the draft into Congress as a comprehensive readjustment program rather than a narrow education grant. Education formed one part of a larger settlement that also included unemployment assistance, loan guarantees, and health care and employment provisions. The design of the education title mattered for every later debate. The federal government paid tuition and fees for an approved course of study, within a statutory ceiling, and paid the veteran a subsistence allowance during enrollment. The benefit was not a flat sum detached from the price of the institution chosen; the program responded to actual charges. That responsiveness explained much of the program's reach, as veterans entered institutions that family income would otherwise have placed beyond reach. A Congressional Record accounting credited 7.8 million veterans with using the education and training provisions. Defenders of reform in 2007 and 2008 returned repeatedly to one point drawn from that record: the 1944 act had treated education as readjustment earned by service, not as a personnel incentive calibrated against recruiting targets. The distinction between readjustment and incentive became the central fault line of the 2008 debate, because the predecessor program then in force rested on the opposite premise.

### Why did veterans of the Iraq and Afghanistan wars need a new GI Bill?

A new benefit was needed because the Montgomery GI Bill paid a flat monthly sum that had fallen behind actual tuition charges, required a $1,200 contribution, and was designed for peacetime service. Repeated wartime deployments after September 11, 2001 made that limited, contributory design inadequate for the force bearing those deployments.

The predecessor was the Montgomery GI Bill, enacted in 1984 as the Veterans' Educational Assistance Act and codified as Chapter 30 of Title 38 of the United States Code. The program took its name from Representative G. V. Montgomery of Mississippi, known as Sonny Montgomery, the chairman of the House Committee on Veterans' Affairs who championed the measure. Congress designed the 1984 program for a different problem from the problem of 1944. The armed forces of the nineteen eighties were an all-volunteer force in peacetime, needing a benefit that would attract recruits without the open-ended tuition commitment of the wartime model. The Montgomery GI Bill therefore operated as a contributory arrangement attached to enlistment. A service member who enrolled accepted a reduction in basic pay of $100 per month during the first twelve months of service, a total contribution of $1,200, and in exchange earned a fixed monthly payment during a later course of study, paid to the individual rather than to the institution. The student then paid tuition, fees, books, and living costs from that amount and from any other resources available. The structure placed the risk of price variation on the veteran: if tuition rose faster than Congress adjusted the monthly rate, the gap belonged to the student. A full account of that contributory design, its payment rates, and its Selected Reserve counterpart appears in [the complete guide to the Montgomery GI Bill and the 1984 settlement](/2019/03/15/montgomery-gi-bill-1984-guide/), which traces the program that the 2008 statute supplemented but did not repeal.

The gap widened across the two decades after 1984. College tuition charges rose persistently faster than general consumer prices, while adjustments to the monthly rate depended on congressional action and arrived intermittently. The Department of Veterans Affairs acknowledged the arithmetic in advocacy for increases. In a 2000 statement, Secretary of Veterans Affairs Togo D. West Jr. observed that the $300 monthly benefit of 1985 had covered 70 percent of average college tuition, while the $536 rate in effect in 2000 covered 58 percent. By the 2007 to 2008 academic year, according to a National Center for Education Statistics profile of veterans in higher education, the Montgomery GI Bill paid a full-time student up to $1,101 per month for education and living expenses combined, for a maximum of $9,909 across a nine-month academic year, regardless of tuition and fee charges at the institution attended. Against the published charges of a four-year public university, that amount left a substantial balance to be met from savings, employment, or borrowing. Senator Webb summarized the defect in a phrase quoted by Inside Higher Education during a Senate Committee on Veterans' Affairs hearing in May 2007, calling the Montgomery GI Bill a peacetime GI Bill. A contributory program calibrated for peacetime recruiting, paying a flat amount unaffected by the actual price of attendance, had been asked to serve as the principal readjustment benefit for a force at war.

The wars after September 11, 2001 changed the population the benefit served and the terms on which that population had served. The United States fought in Afghanistan from October 2001 and in Iraq from March 2003 with a volunteer force and without a draft, which meant that the burden of repeated deployment fell on a comparatively small share of the population, concentrated among those who had enlisted and among members of the National Guard and the Reserves ordered to active duty. The statutory findings at the head of the Webb bill stated the premise plainly, in language later carried into Public Law 110-252: terrorists had attacked the United States on September 11, 2001, members of the armed forces had been called to the defense of the nation, and service on active duty had been especially arduous since that date. Arduous service altered the equity calculation in three ways. First, many service members completed multiple combat tours, so a formula that treated a post-2001 enlistment as equivalent to a peacetime enlistment ignored the distinguishing fact of the service. Second, Guard and Reserve members left civilian employment, civilian education, and family households for activations that resembled regular active duty in risk and duration, while the educational programs available to reservists paid considerably less than the active duty program. The Congressional Research Service later identified provision of benefits to reservists equivalent to those provided to members of the regular armed forces for equivalent active duty service as the first of the enacted program's four main objectives. Third, the opportunity cost of service had risen. A volunteer who spent three or four years in uniform, including deployments, returned to competition with peers who had completed degrees during those same years. A benefit covering only part of tuition and nothing toward housing merely discounted the price of beginning again. Iraq and Afghanistan Veterans of America made the shortfall a central legislative priority, documenting in January 2007 that almost 300,000 Iraq and Afghanistan veterans were relying on education benefits that did not cover the cost of college.

Webb brought a biography that opponents found difficult to dismiss as uninformed about military institutions. A Naval Academy graduate, Webb had served as a Marine infantry officer in Vietnam, received the Navy Cross, and later served as Secretary of the Navy under President Ronald Reagan. Webb entered the Senate from Virginia in January 2007 and introduced the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act as S. 22 at the opening of the 110th Congress; Congress.gov records the introduction date as January 4, 2007, and Webb repeatedly described the introduction as fulfillment of a pledge made on the first day in office. The principal co-sponsors gave the bill a bipartisan and veteran character from the start. Senator Chuck Hagel of Nebraska, a Republican and a decorated Army veteran of Vietnam, joined as the leading Republican co-sponsor. Senator John Warner of Virginia, a Republican and former Secretary of the Navy, and Senator Frank Lautenberg of New Jersey, a Democrat and Second World War veteran, stood among the principal sponsors. Webb and Hagel set out the case jointly in a 2007 article in The New York Times, arguing that a GI Bill for those who had given so much to the country, often including repeated combat tours, should be viewed as an obligation, and that first-class service to the country deserved first-class appreciation. Webb contended that the country had understood the obligation correctly in 1944 and had then allowed the understanding to lapse, replacing a readjustment right with a recruiting inducement whose value inflation had quietly reduced. Restoring the 1944 standard, in the Webb formulation, required three elements the Montgomery GI Bill lacked: tuition support tied to actual charges at public institutions, a living allowance during enrollment, and eligibility earned by wartime service without a payroll contribution from junior enlisted pay. The American Legion, AMVETS, the Veterans of Foreign Wars, Disabled American Veterans, Paralyzed Veterans of America, and Student Veterans of America supported the measure before passage, a breadth of endorsement that Webb cited in May 2008 in observing that the measure had received the full support of every major veterans' organization.

The retention objection deserves statement at full strength, because the legislators who advanced the objection spoke from military experience and identified a genuine tension in personnel policy. Senator John McCain of Arizona led the opposition to the Webb formula. McCain, a naval aviator who had survived more than five years as a prisoner of war in North Vietnam after capture in 1967, argued that generosity at an early point in a career inverted the incentives on which an all-volunteer force depends. The armed forces invest heavily in training during a first enlistment, and the return arrives in the second and third enlistments, when a service member has become a squad leader, a maintainer, or an instructor. A benefit worth the full cost of a public university education, available after a single term, gives capable junior members a funded reason to leave at the point when the services can least afford the loss. McCain presented the alternative as calibration rather than opposition to generosity. Alongside Senator Lindsey Graham of South Carolina and Senator Richard Burr of North Carolina, McCain introduced S. 2938, the Enhancement of Recruitment, Retention, and Readjustment Through Education Act, which would have raised Montgomery GI Bill benefits substantially while calibrating the increase to time in service, so that greater service earned greater benefit, and which included transferability to spouses and children from the outset. A White House statement issued by the Press Secretary on May 26, 2008 endorsed that approach in explicit terms, stating that President George W. Bush specifically supported the Graham, Burr, and McCain legislation because the proposal allowed transferability and calibrated an increase in education benefits to time in service, while the Department of Defense held specific concerns about the Webb legislation because the Webb bill lacked transferability and could negatively affect military retention. Secretary of Defense Robert M. Gates pressed a related version through Pentagon Press Secretary Geoff Morrell, who told reporters on May 21, 2008 that Gates supported an enhanced GI Bill and considered the generosity deserved, but feared that benefits vesting after as little as two years of service would encourage premature departure. Gates proposed that enhanced benefits become available after six years of service, rewarding at least one reenlistment, and Morrell warned against troops with three years of service leaving because the benefit was so enticing, at a moment when the force needed to hold battle-tested personnel. Education Weekly, summarizing the McCain alternative during the floor debate, reported that the proposal would have raised the monthly benefit from $1,100 to $1,500, with benefits of up to $2,000 per month tied to longer service; the McCain language was never brought to a vote. A Congressional Budget Office estimate supplied the empirical core of the objection and was cited by both camps. Opponents cited the estimate for a projected decline in retention of 16 percent, reasoning that a measurable share of first-term members who would otherwise have reenlisted would instead separate to use the benefit. The logic was internally consistent. Education benefits usable only after separation operate as a separation incentive by construction, and a force fighting two campaigns had no ready reserve of trained replacements.

Webb answered the objection on empirical ground and on purpose. Webb noted that the same Congressional Budget Office analysis that projected a 16 percent decline in retention also projected a 16 percent increase in recruitment attributable to the new benefit, so that the force would gain accessions at the entry point even as separation rose. Webb also cited the baseline behavior of the enlisted force: about 70 percent of enlisted members separated at or before the end of an initial enlistment, without any enhanced benefit to induce departure. A policy designed around the minority who reenlisted, Webb argued, would deny an adequate readjustment benefit to the majority whose service consisted of a single wartime enlistment and whose need for education on return was the precise need the 1944 act had addressed. The deeper answer concerned purpose. If the benefit existed primarily as a personnel instrument, calibration to retention targets followed logically. If the benefit existed as readjustment earned by arduous service, conditioning adequacy on additional years of service confused the instrument with the obligation. Webb held the second position without qualification, describing the cost of the program on the Senate floor on May 20, 2008, in words reported by Education Weekly, as emphatically a cost of war. Contemporary estimates placed the ten-year cost of the enhanced benefits at about $52 billion. The House of Representatives initially paired passage with a financing mechanism, a surcharge of 0.47 percent on incomes above $500,000 for individuals and $1 million for couples, adopted at the urging of fiscally conservative House Democrats. The Senate declined to include the surcharge, and the enacted law funded the benefits through the appropriations structure of the supplemental measure itself, a sequence examined in [the longer record of federal veterans legislation and the programs that sequence produced](/2019/07/15/us-veterans-legislation-history/). President Bush added a further complication. The White House threatened a veto of the supplemental appropriations measures in May 2008, citing excessive domestic spending in a statement from the Office of Management and Budget dated May 20, 2008, and the administration objected to attaching domestic provisions to troop funding. The veto threat forced supporters to assemble majorities large enough to make a veto untenable and to negotiate the one substantive change that the President and the Secretary of Defense had identified as a condition of support.

The legislative path ran through the war funding bill because that vehicle guaranteed consideration. S. 22 accumulated co-sponsors through 2007 and received a Senate Committee on Veterans' Affairs hearing on May 7, 2008, recorded by Congress.gov as the bill's last committee action, but a freestanding bill remained exposed to delay and to a veto without a must-pass measure attached. Attachment to the Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2008, designated H.R. 2642, solved the procedural problem and created the bargain that produced enactment. The House approved an amendment adding the expanded benefits to the supplemental measure on May 15, 2008, according to a contemporaneous account by the National Association of Independent Colleges and Universities. The Senate acted on May 22, 2008, approving the amendment containing veterans' education benefits and other domestic provisions by a vote of 75 to 22, a margin beyond the two-thirds threshold required to override a veto, and approving the war funding amendment in the same package by 70 to 26. Senator McCain, campaigning as the presumptive Republican nominee for President, did not attend the May 22 vote, a fact reported by Government Executive in contemporaneous coverage. Negotiation between the chambers, the White House, and the Pentagon then produced the compromise text in June. On June 19, 2008, the House agreed to the Senate amendment with the final compromise language by a recorded vote of 416 to 12, recorded as Roll Number 432 in the House action history for H.R. 2642. On June 26, 2008, the Senate agreed to the House amendments by a vote of 92 to 6, recorded as Record Vote Number 162. President George W. Bush signed H.R. 2642 into law on June 30, 2008, creating Public Law 110-252. The Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008 became Title V of that public law and added a new Chapter 33 to Title 38 of the United States Code, as the Congressional Research Service summarized in a brief overview issued on July 28, 2008. At the signing, President Bush thanked the members who had worked on the expansion, naming Senators Webb and Warner alongside Senators Graham, Burr, and McCain. The Congressional Research Service recorded the program's effective date as August 1, 2009, thirteen months after enactment, allowing time to build payment systems and transfer election machinery. Eligibility reached back to service on or after September 11, 2001, and individuals received fifteen years from discharge in which to use the entitlement, according to the same overview.

Transferability entered the statute as the passage bargain that answered the retention objection. President Bush had called on Congress in the State of the Union address in January 2008 to allow service members to transfer unused benefits to spouses and children, a proposal that Secretary Gates had advanced after hearing the request from military spouses, including at Fort Hood, Texas, according to American Forces Press Service reporting in June 2008. The White House statement of May 26, 2008 treated the absence of transferability in the Webb bill as a stated ground of objection alongside the retention concern, and the compromise text adopted in June 2008 added transferability to the Webb benefit structure rather than substituting the alternative bill's graduated benefit scale. On June 19, 2008, after the provision had been added, President Bush indicated that the President would sign such a bill. The Congressional Research Service stated the purpose directly: transferability was included primarily at the behest of the Department of Defense, to promote retention and to offset any potential decline in retention resulting from the more generous benefits. Deputy Under Secretary of Defense Bill Carr made the same point at implementation in July 2009, calling transferability the most requested initiative received from service members. The full mechanics of election, service thresholds, and family use rules appear in the transferability section of this guide. Enactment on those terms closed the argument that had opened in January 2007, leaving a readjustment benefit modeled on 1944 joined to a retention instrument built into that benefit at the insistence of officials who had feared generosity without condition.

## The Architecture of the Benefit: Three Payments, Three Measures, and a Service Scale

The statute at the center of this guide carries a formal name that fixes both its subject and its moment. Enacted law knows the measure as the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008. The opening words tie entitlement to a date, September 11, 2001, and to the service that followed that date, so the benefit belongs by definition to a particular generation of service members rather than to veterans in general. The middle words, educational assistance, place the measure inside a long statutory family of readjustment benefits administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs, a family that reaches back through the Montgomery GI Bill to the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944. The closing words, Act of 2008, record the year of enactment, even though no payment under the new program reached any student until the following year. The Congressional Research Service uses the formal name when precision matters, while the shorter label, the Post-9/11 GI Bill, does the work of public identification.

That instrument did not travel through Congress as a freestanding bill. The Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008 was enacted as Title V of the Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2008, a large appropriations measure that supplied emergency funding for the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan alongside a set of domestic provisions. The combined measure became Public Law 110-252, and the President signed it on June 30, 2008. Placement inside a war funding bill gave the education benefit the momentum of that bill, but Title V did not appropriate a sum for a single fiscal year and lapse. Title V wrote a new, enduring entitlement program into the permanent code of federal law. The Congressional Research Service, in its primer on the program, describes the 2008 Act in exactly those terms: enacted as Title V of the Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2008, Public Law 110-252, on June 30, 2008, and effective on August 1, 2009.

Codification followed the subject. The new program took its place principally in chapter 33 of title 38 of the United States Code, the title that gathers the law of veterans' benefits. Chapter 33 runs from section 3301 to section 3325, and its internal organization mirrors the logic of the benefit itself. A subchapter of general provisions supplies definitions, including the definition of qualifying active duty service. A second subchapter states the educational assistance entitlement and how payments are computed. A third subchapter collects administrative provisions, including the rule that fixes the period within which entitlement must be used. Within that structure, section 3311 describes the individuals entitled to assistance, section 3312 fixes duration, section 3313 states the amounts payable and the percentages that scale those amounts to length of service, and section 3321 states the time limitation on use.

The gap between signature and effect was deliberate. Although Public Law 110-252 became law on June 30, 2008, the new program went into effect on August 1, 2009, and payments under chapter 33 attach only to training pursued on or after that date; the Department of Veterans Affairs pamphlet literature states that no payment may be made for training pursued before that date. The thirteen month interval gave the Department time to build payment systems and to compute the state by state tuition ceilings that the statute required. The Act of 2008 is therefore a program of 2009 in every practical sense.

Beneath the formal identity sits the structural decision that defines the program. The Post-9/11 GI Bill benefit is not one payment. The benefit is three separate payments, computed on three different bases, paid through two different channels, and governed by three different relationships to cost. Tuition and fees are measured against the charges of an institution and capped by a ceiling drawn from other institutions in the same state. The housing allowance is measured against a military pay table keyed to the ZIP code of the school. The books and supplies stipend is measured against credit hours enrolled, at a flat national rate that takes no account of place at all. Because the three computations start from three different measures, identical service produces materially different benefits depending on where a veteran enrolls. Two veterans with indistinguishable service records, discharged on the same day with the same percentage of entitlement, receive different tuition payments if their states set different ceilings, and different housing payments if their schools sit in different ZIP codes. The benefit, in short, is a benefit whose value depends on the veteran's ZIP code, and that dependence is not an accident of administration. Congress wrote geography into two of the three formulas on purpose, tying educational assistance to the real local costs of tuition and of shelter rather than to a single national sum. The contrast with the Montgomery GI Bill, housed in chapter 30, sharpens the point: that older program paid a monthly amount that turned on category of service and rate of training rather than on institutional price or local rent.

The first component, and the largest in most cases, is the payment for tuition and fees. Chapter 33 directs that payment away from the student entirely. The Department of Veterans Affairs pays tuition and fees directly to the educational institution on the beneficiary's behalf, for each academic term, up to the amount the statute allows. The institution certifies enrollment, the Department pays the institution, and the beneficiary never handles the money and never owes the Department an accounting for funds that passed straight from the government to the school. Within the allowed amount, the payment covers the actual charges for tuition and fees that the institution assesses the student for the program of education. The operative question for the first component is therefore how the institution's actual charges compare with the ceiling that the statute imposes, because the ceiling, rather than the charge, decides the payment whenever charges run high.

As enacted in 2008, the ceiling had a distinctive and consequential design. The statute capped the tuition and fees payment at the highest amount of established charges for tuition and fees that a public institution of higher learning in the state charged for a full time undergraduate program. The Department of Veterans Affairs pamphlet of October 2008 phrases the cap as the most expensive in state undergraduate tuition and fees at a public institution of higher education in the state of enrollment. Administration divided the ceiling into two parts, because tuition and fees behave differently. The tuition element was computed per credit hour, taking the highest per credit hour undergraduate tuition charged by any public institution in the state and multiplying that rate by the credit hours in which the beneficiary enrolled. The fees element was computed per term, taking the highest total fees charged by a public institution in the state for a term. The two parts combined to produce the maximum payment for the term. A beneficiary whose institution charged less received the actual charges. A beneficiary whose institution charged more received the ceiling amount, and the difference remained the beneficiary's responsibility unless another provision of law, such as the Yellow Ribbon Program treated elsewhere in this guide, contributed toward it.

A ceiling built from the most expensive public institution in each state produced a map of maximum benefits with no two states alike. Contemporary analysis of the original design emphasized the spread: a 2018 study of the program, reported in Inside Higher Ed, observed that under the original formula the maximum payment in Delaware stood as low as 665 dollars while the maximum in Colorado reached 43,035 dollars, figures that reflected the structure of the original ceilings rather than any difference in the service of Delaware and Colorado veterans. The ceiling measured the price of public education inside one state's borders, so the federal benefit inherited every peculiarity of fifty separate state pricing decisions. For a beneficiary enrolled at a public institution at in state rates, actual charges ordinarily fell at or below the ceiling, so the first component ordinarily covered the full assessed tuition and fees, subject to the service percentage described later in this section. For a beneficiary enrolled at a private institution, or at charges above the ceiling, the payment stopped at the ceiling; the statute made no separate private institution allowance in its original form, and the public maximum governed every enrollment in the state.

The second component reverses the direction of the first. Where tuition money flows to the institution, the monthly housing allowance flows to the beneficiary, in arrears for each month of training. The allowance exists because the statute treats shelter during education as a cost of education, and because a student who must pay rent from wages must divide the week between employment and study. Nothing about the beneficiary's own housing history enters the computation. The statute does not ask what rent the beneficiary pays, whether the beneficiary lives with family, owns a home, or rents a room. The statute asks a single geographic question, where the institution sits, and takes the answer from a table built for an entirely different purpose.

### How is the monthly housing allowance calculated?

The monthly housing allowance equals the Basic Allowance for Housing for a service member of pay grade E-5 with dependents in the ZIP code of the institution where training occurs, multiplied by the percentage tied to length of qualifying service, and it is payable only when enrollment exceeds half time.

The borrowed table is the Basic Allowance for Housing, the allowance that the Department of Defense pays to service members who live in civilian housing, differentiated by pay grade, dependency status, and the housing costs of each military housing area. Section 3313 of title 38 fixes the reference point with precision: the monthly housing allowance for a beneficiary pursuing a program of education in residence equals the monthly Basic Allowance for Housing payable to a member of the Armed Forces with a pay grade of E-5, the fifth enlisted grade, with dependents, for the ZIP code area in which the institution of higher learning offering the program is located. The with dependents rate, higher than the rate for a member without dependents, applies to every beneficiary regardless of actual family status, because the statute fixes a reference household rather than inquiring into households. The ZIP code element imports the Department of Defense survey of local housing costs, so a school in a high cost metropolitan area generates a high allowance and a school in a low cost rural area generates a low one. The Congressional Research Service primer describes the component in the same terms, as an amount equal to the E-5 with dependents Basic Allowance for Housing where the beneficiary pursues the program.

Eligibility for the allowance turns on enrollment intensity, and the threshold is strict. The allowance is payable only to a beneficiary enrolled on more than a half time basis. Enrollment at exactly half time produces no housing payment at all, and enrollment below half time produces none: individuals enrolled half time or less are ineligible to receive a housing allowance. The measure of intensity is the rate of pursuit, the proportion that the beneficiary's credit hours bear to the credit hours that the institution regards as a full time load. In the standard undergraduate example used in Department of Veterans Affairs guidance, twelve credits constitute a full load, six credits constitute exactly half time and therefore fail the threshold, and seven credits qualify. As enacted in 2008, a beneficiary who crossed the threshold received the allowance at the full local rate for the beneficiary's service percentage, without proration for the number of credits carried beyond the threshold. The Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Improvements Act of 2010, Public Law 111-377, changed that feature, and from August 1, 2011 the allowance was prorated in proportion to the rate of pursuit, rounded to the nearest tenth, so that enrollment intensity scaled the payment continuously rather than merely switching the payment on. One further exclusion belonged to the original design and survived amendment. A beneficiary serving on active duty receives no monthly housing allowance for the period of that service, because the armed forces already provide that individual with housing or with the Basic Allowance for Housing itself, and the statute declines to pay the same cost twice. The Congressional Research Service states the exclusion directly: individuals are ineligible to receive a housing allowance for the period during which they are serving on active duty.

Distance learning exposed the geographic logic of the allowance to its hardest test, and the treatment of distance learning changed in a dated and identifiable step. The housing formula presupposes a place of study: the ZIP code of the institution supplies the rate. A program pursued entirely through distance learning has no necessary connection to the institution's ZIP code, since the student may live anywhere. As enacted in 2008, the statute resolved the difficulty by exclusion. A beneficiary whose entire enrollment consisted of distance or online learning received no monthly housing allowance, while a beneficiary who took at least one course in residence received the allowance tied to the institution's location. Congress answered the resulting criticism with the Improvements Act of 2010. For enrollment periods beginning on October 1, 2011, a beneficiary pursuing a program solely through distance learning on a more than half time basis became entitled to a monthly housing allowance equal to one half of the national average of the Basic Allowance for Housing amounts payable for the relevant class of member, a figure that the Department of Veterans Affairs stated as 673 dollars and 50 cents per month at the full time rate for a beneficiary at the 100 percent service tier in 2011.

The third component is the smallest, the simplest, and the only one computed without reference to geography or to institutional charges. The statute provides an annual stipend for books and required supplies, paid to the beneficiary, with a maximum of 1,000 dollars per academic year. The Congressional Research Service primer states the structure exactly: individuals receive a maximum stipend of 1,000 dollars per year for books and required educational expenses, paid on the basis of the credit hours in which the individual is enrolled each term, with each credit hour worth 41 dollars and 67 cents. The arithmetic closes neatly. At 41 dollars and 67 cents per credit hour, a full time load of twelve credit hours in a term yields 500 dollars for that term, and two such terms exhaust the 1,000 dollar annual maximum at twenty four credit hours for the year. A beneficiary enrolled for fewer credit hours receives the per credit amount for the hours taken, scaled by the service percentage, and the cap binds the year rather than the term. Department of Veterans Affairs descriptions of the stipend add that the money is paid directly to the student, at the beginning of the term, without any requirement of receipts. Administration of the stipend expanded in one respect under the 2010 Improvements Act, which from October 1, 2011 extended the stipend to students on active duty, a group the original payment structure had left outside that component.

Across all three components runs a single scaling mechanism, and the mechanism is what converts service into money. Entitlement under chapter 33 is proportional. The statute first computes each component at its full measure: the tuition ceiling for the state, the housing rate for the ZIP code, the stipend maximum for the year. The statute then multiplies each full measure by a percentage drawn from a schedule in section 3313, and the percentage depends on one variable alone, the aggregate length of the beneficiary's active duty service on or after September 11, 2001. A beneficiary at a given percentage receives that percentage of the tuition amount otherwise payable, that percentage of the housing allowance, and that percentage of the books and supplies stipend, so the schedule governs the whole benefit rather than any single part of it. The Department of Veterans Affairs fact sheet literature from the program's first years illustrates the multiplication with a beneficiary who has five months of qualifying service: that individual receives 40 percent of the tuition benefit, 40 percent of the monthly housing allowance, and a maximum of 400 dollars in the books and supplies stipend, the last figure being 40 percent of the 1,000 dollar annual maximum. Service history sets the fraction, geography and enrollment set the base amounts, and the payment is the product of the two.

The schedule begins at the threshold of eligibility itself. A minimum of 90 aggregate days of active duty service on or after September 11, 2001 opens the program, and service of at least 90 days but less than six months places the beneficiary at 40 percent of the maximum amounts. The percentage then climbs in steps as aggregate service lengthens. Service of at least six months but less than twelve months yields 50 percent. Service of at least twelve months but less than eighteen months yields 60 percent. Service of at least eighteen months but less than twenty four months yields 70 percent. The steps in the lower half of the schedule are spaced six months apart, and each step adds ten percentage points, so the schedule rewards additional service at a steady rate through the first two years. Aggregate service, as the word aggregate signals, may be accumulated across multiple periods of active duty; the statute counts the sum of qualifying service rather than demanding a single unbroken tour, a feature of particular consequence for members of the reserve components.

The upper half of the schedule continues the same progression to its ceiling. Service of at least twenty four months but less than thirty months yields 80 percent. Service of at least thirty months but less than thirty six months yields 90 percent. Service of at least thirty six months yields the full measure, 100 percent of every component. One refinement governs the counting of service in the lower tiers, and the Department of Veterans Affairs fact sheets state the refinement as a note to the schedule itself. Periods of entry level and skill training, the initial training through which new service members pass at the start of service, do not count toward aggregate service for beneficiaries below the twenty four month line; service of 90 days but less than twenty four months is measured excluding that training time. At twenty four months and above, entry level and skill training time counts.

One class of beneficiary bypasses the climb entirely. An individual who serves at least 30 continuous days on active duty on or after September 11, 2001 and is discharged or released because of a service connected disability receives entitlement at the 100 percent level, without regard to the total length of service performed. The Department of Veterans Affairs presentations of the schedule place that category beside the thirty six month category, as a second route to the full measure. The schedule prices entitlement as a return on time given; the disability provision recognizes that a service member whose career the service itself cut short, through injury or illness connected to that service, gave a measure of service that a calendar understates. Thirty days of continuous service followed by a disability discharge therefore outweighs, in the statutory accounting, thirty five months of service followed by an ordinary separation at the 90 percent tier.

Duration supplies the second temporal boundary of the benefit, distinct from the service schedule that sets its rate. Section 3312 fixes the total quantity of entitlement at 36 months, the equivalent of four academic years of full time study, and the Department of Veterans Affairs pamphlet literature states the figure in the same general terms: a beneficiary may generally receive up to 36 months of entitlement under the Post-9/11 GI Bill. Entitlement is consumed as training is pursued, with a month of full time training consuming a month of entitlement and part time training consuming entitlement in proportion to the rate of pursuit, so the 36 month account measures training received rather than calendar time elapsed.

The third temporal boundary closes the period during which the account may be spent. As enacted in 2008, section 3321 imposed a delimiting period of 15 years: entitlement had to be used within the 15 year period beginning on the date of the beneficiary's last discharge or release from active duty, and entitlement expired at the end of that period. The Department of Veterans Affairs pamphlet of October 2008 states the rule as eligibility for 15 years from the last period of active duty of at least 90 consecutive days, with the same period extended to the disability category measured from the corresponding discharge. The 15 year span was itself a design decision, since the Montgomery GI Bill had allowed 10 years, and the longer chapter 33 period acknowledged that beneficiaries might delay education for further service or for family obligations. Section 3321 as enacted borrowed its machinery for interruptions and extensions from the corresponding provisions governing the older program, applying the running rules of section 3031 of title 38 to the new 15 year period. Later legislation revisited the delimiting period for later cohorts of beneficiaries, and that development belongs to the amendment history treated elsewhere in this guide.

Read together, the elements of the design explain why no single dollar figure can describe the benefit. Length of qualifying service fixes the percentage, through a schedule that rises from 40 percent at 90 days to 100 percent at thirty six months and that grants the full measure immediately after a service connected disability discharge following 30 continuous days. The institution's state fixes the tuition ceiling, through a benchmark drawn from the most expensive public undergraduate charges inside that state's borders. The institution's ZIP code fixes the housing allowance, through a military housing table for an E-5 with dependents, subject to the more than half time threshold and, after the amendment effective in 2011, subject to proration by rate of pursuit and to a half national average substitute for distance enrollment. Credit hours fix the books and supplies stipend, through a flat rate of 41 dollars and 67 cents per credit hour up to 1,000 dollars in a year. A benefit built in that fashion cannot be summarized as a sum, because the sum changes with the map. The statutory answer to the cost of education after military service was to refuse a single national price and to build, instead, a set of measures that follow the beneficiary to the place of study and price the education there.

## Transferability and the Yellow Ribbon Program

The Post-9/11 GI Bill was built as a personal benefit, earned by an individual through service and ordinarily consumed by that individual after separation. Two provisions of chapter 33 of title 38, United States Code, qualify that personal design. Section 3319 permits a service member, under tightly drawn conditions, to give unused educational entitlement to a spouse or to one or more children. Section 3317, which establishes the Yellow Ribbon G.I. Education Enhancement Program, permits a college or university to share with the Department of Veterans Affairs the cost of charges that exceed what the basic benefit pays.

The most persistent error in accounts of the Post-9/11 GI Bill concerns transferability. Transfer is never automatic. Entitlement does not flow to a spouse or child by operation of marriage, birth, or the mere passage of time in service. A transfer happens only when an eligible service member makes an affirmative election, designates the dependent or dependents who will receive entitlement, specifies the number of months assigned to each, obtains approval through the service concerned, and accepts an additional service obligation as the price of the election. A veteran who never made that election during service holds a benefit that belongs to the veteran alone, and no application filed after separation can manufacture a transfer that service never approved. Section 3319 states the purpose of the transfer authority in direct terms: the authority exists to promote recruitment and retention in the uniformed services. That stated purpose explains every restriction that follows, because a benefit designed as a retention instrument must be exchanged for service, and an exchange must be completed while the member remains in a position to give the service promised.

### Can GI Bill benefits be transferred to a spouse or child?

A service member with at least six years of service who agrees to serve four additional years may elect to transfer unused Post-9/11 GI Bill entitlement to a spouse, to one or more children, or to a combination of both, in designated months, provided the election occurs during service and receives service approval.

The mechanics of section 3319 begin with the pool of transferable entitlement. A member may transfer only unused entitlement, and the total number of months transferred may not exceed thirty six months, the full measure of chapter 33 entitlement for a member who has consumed none of it. A member who has already used a portion of the entitlement may transfer the unused remainder, divided in whatever allocation the member chooses among a spouse and children. The statute requires the member to designate the dependent or dependents receiving the transfer and to designate the number of months transferred to each dependent. Eligible dependents are drawn from the categories of dependents defined in section 1072(2) of title 10, and the eligible recipients under section 3319 are the member's spouse and the member's children. The Secretary of Defense prescribes regulations governing the manner of authorizing transfers and the eligibility criteria, and each service administers applications, verifies service, records the additional service agreement, and approves or disapproves requests under those regulations. Approval is a service decision, and statutory eligibility does not compel approval in an individual case.

The eligibility gate has two parts, and both parts must be satisfied at the time the transfer request is approved. The standard path under section 3319 requires completion of at least six years of service in the Armed Forces together with an agreement to serve at least four more years as a member of the uniformed services. Service counted toward the six year threshold includes active duty service and Selected Reserve service. The four additional years run from the transfer election, and the obligation attaches regardless of the number of months transferred, so a member who transfers a single month to place a dependent inside the system accepts the same four year commitment as a member who transfers the full thirty six months. A second path, administered through regulations prescribed under section 3319, covers members whose careers cannot accommodate a full four year extension. A member who has completed at least ten years of service and who is precluded by policy or by statute from serving four additional years, for example through a mandatory retirement date or a high year tenure limit, may transfer entitlement upon agreement to serve the maximum period that the applicable policy or statute permits. The ten year alternative preserves the exchange logic for members near the end of a career, who give all of the additional service the service will accept.

Timing rules give the retention design its force. An individual approved to transfer entitlement may execute the transfer only while serving as a member of the Armed Forces when the transfer is executed. A member who has retired or separated has, with narrow statutory exceptions enacted for cases involving death, lost the power to designate a new transferee. A retiree with a full unused entitlement and a child entering college has no transfer to give, because the election window closed at separation. Families therefore confront the transfer decision as a mid-career decision, made years before any child reaches college age and often before the family can predict which dependent would benefit most. The allocation among designated dependents can be adjusted later, but a dependent never designated during the member's service cannot be added afterward as an original transferee.

The service agreement carries a financial sanction that completes the exchange. If a member fails to complete the additional service agreed to under section 3319, the amount of any transferred entitlement that a dependent has used by the date of the failure is treated as an overpayment of educational assistance, and the member alone bears liability for repayment to the United States. The statute excuses liability where the failure results from the death of the member, and a parallel exception covers failures attributable to reasons specified in section 3311(c)(4), the provision addressing separations for disability and comparable involuntary circumstances. Department of Defense policy likewise softens the obligation for involuntary separations outside the member's control, including honorable discharge in a reduction in force. A voluntary departure before the obligation matures, by contrast, exposes the member to repayment of every dollar a dependent consumed.

Revocation and modification operate on a different principle, because control over unused entitlement never leaves the member's hands. Section 3319 permits the transferring individual to modify or revoke at any time the transfer of any unused portion of the entitlement. Modification and revocation are accomplished by written notice submitted to both the Secretary concerned and the Secretary of Veterans Affairs. Months that a dependent has already consumed cannot be recovered, because consumed entitlement has been charged against the member's account month for month at the time of use, but every unconsumed month remains subject to reallocation among designated dependents or to outright revocation that returns the months to the member. The statute reinforces that continuing control by providing that transferred entitlement may not be treated as marital property or as an asset of a marital estate subject to division in a divorce or other civil proceeding. A divorce does not itself terminate a spouse's eligibility to use transferred entitlement, as Department of Veterans Affairs guidance confirms, yet the member may revoke the unused portion after the divorce becomes final. The death of the transferring individual does not disturb the arrangement: section 3319 provides that the death of the member does not affect the use of entitlement by the dependent to whom the entitlement was transferred.

Use rules divide sharply between spouses and children, and the division reflects the ages and circumstances Congress expected each class of transferee to occupy. A spouse may begin using transferred entitlement once the transfer stands approved, because the eligibility threshold itself guarantees that the member has completed at least six years of service, the commencement condition that section 3319 sets for spouses. The spouse may use the entitlement while the member remains in service or after separation. A spouse receives educational assistance in the same manner as the individual from whom the entitlement was transferred, with one concrete consequence: a spouse receives no monthly housing allowance during a period in which the member serves on active duty, because chapter 33 pays no housing allowance in respect of a member in that status. The spouse's window for use is governed by the delimiting framework of section 3321. As enacted in 2008, section 3321 closed the period for using entitlement at the end of fifteen years beginning on the date of the member's last discharge or release from active duty, and Department of Veterans Affairs guidance under that framework gave a spouse fifteen years after the member's last separation from active duty to consume transferred entitlement. The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, often called the Forever GI Bill, amended section 3321 to provide that entitlement does not expire for an individual whose last discharge or release from active duty occurred on or after January 1, 2013, and a spouse using transferred entitlement from such a member likewise faces no expiration date.

A child transferee operates under a later and narrower window. Section 3319 permits a child to commence use only when two conditions coincide: the member making the transfer has completed at least ten years of service in the Armed Forces, and the child has either completed the requirements of a secondary school diploma or an equivalency certificate or attained eighteen years of age. A child may use the entitlement while the member remains in service or after separation, and the statute treats a child, for rate purposes, as if the member were not on active duty. That treatment carries the reverse of the spouse's housing consequence: a child may receive the monthly housing allowance even during a period in which the member serves on active duty, as Department of Veterans Affairs guidance confirms. The child's use is freed from the fifteen year delimiting date that section 3321 otherwise imposes, because section 3319 expressly permits a child to use transferred benefits without regard to that date, but an absolute age boundary replaces the calendar boundary. A child may not use transferred entitlement after attaining twenty six years of age. The window therefore runs from the later of the two starting conditions through the eve of the child's twenty sixth birthday.

One further rule unifies spouse and child use: transferred entitlement carries the member's tier percentage. Chapter 33 pays educational assistance at percentage rates scaled to the length of qualifying active duty service. Section 3319 sets the monthly rate payable to a dependent at the same rate at which the entitlement would be payable to the member making the transfer, with a child computed as if the member were not on active duty. A member entitled at a rate below the maximum therefore transfers a benefit of proportionally reduced value, and a family calculating the worth of a transfer must apply the member's percentage to tuition and fee payments, to the housing allowance where payable, and to the books and supplies stipend alike. Each month of entitlement that a dependent consumes is charged against the member's entitlement at the rate of one month for each month used, so a transfer divides a finite account rather than multiplying the thirty six months the member earned alone.

The architecture of gates, obligations, and windows answers a political problem that nearly defeated the Post-9/11 GI Bill during enactment, and the legislative history explains why transferability took the form it did. Senator Jim Webb of Virginia, the lead sponsor of the legislation, proposed a benefit modeled on the educational assistance extended to veterans of the Second World War. The generosity of the proposal drew an objection grounded in personnel policy: a benefit valuable enough to transform a veteran's prospects after only a few years of service would give experienced service members a powerful reason to leave at the first opportunity, draining the force of the noncommissioned officers and mid-grade officers on whom the services depended during two prolonged wars. The Department of Defense pressed that retention objection, and President George W. Bush and Senator John McCain of Arizona, the Republican nominee for President in 2008, voiced parallel concerns about the effect of the proposal on retention. Transferability entered the legislation as the mechanism that answered the objection. The Congressional Research Service, in its analyses of the program, reports that transferability was included primarily at the behest of the Department of Defense and in response to requests from service members, as a means of promoting retention, and that the Department considered transferability an incentive to offset any potential decline in retention resulting from the more generous benefits payable to veterans.

The logic of the compromise rewards attention, because the design converts the very feature that threatened retention into an instrument of retention. A separation benefit pays a member to leave; a transferable benefit conditioned on additional service pays a member to remain. The six year threshold places the election beyond the first enlistment, at the career stage where the services compete hardest to retain experienced personnel, and the four year agreement extends the member's commitment to a full decade. The ten year threshold for a child's use extends the incentive deeper into a career, since a member who transfers entitlement with a young child in view gains the full family value of the transfer only by remaining in service until the child can use the entitlement during the college years. The benefit also matches the life cycle of the career force, since a mid-career member with a spouse whose education was deferred and children approaching adolescence may value education for the family more highly than education for personal use. By permitting the entitlement to follow the family's needs rather than the member's alone, Congress made continued service more valuable to precisely the experienced, family anchored personnel whose departure the services feared. The Post-9/11 GI Bill accordingly became, as the Congressional Research Service observes, the first GI Bill to allow qualifying service members on a permanent basis to transfer entitlement to dependents, a structural departure from the personal and nontransferable character of every predecessor program.

Transferability solves the allocation of entitlement inside a family. A separate problem arises from the relationship between the benefit ceiling and the price of education, and that problem falls hardest on families drawn to private institutions. The tuition and fee component of chapter 33 tracks public sector prices: as enacted, the benefit measured tuition coverage against the most expensive public in-state undergraduate program in the state where the student enrolled, and the statutory design anchors the ceiling to public in-state rates under every later formulation. A private college or university sets charges without reference to that anchor, and private charges routinely exceed the public in-state benchmark by a wide margin. The arithmetic produces a shortfall: the Department of Veterans Affairs pays tuition and fees up to the ceiling, the institution charges the full price, and the difference remains as an obligation of the student. The same structural gap appears in two related settings. A student attending a public institution as an out-of-state student faces charges set for nonresidents, while the basic benefit answers only to the in-state rate, and a graduate student at a public institution faces graduate charges that exceed the undergraduate in-state figures against which the original ceiling was measured. A veteran or transferee confronting the gap must cover the shortfall through personal funds, loans, or institutional aid unrelated to chapter 33.

The Yellow Ribbon Program, established by section 3317 under the formal name Yellow Ribbon G.I. Education Enhancement Program, is the statutory answer to that shortfall, and the answer is deliberately built on voluntariness and shared cost rather than on an increase in the federal ceiling. Section 3317 directs the Secretary of Veterans Affairs to carry out a program under which colleges and universities may enter into agreements with the Secretary to cover a portion of tuition and fee charges that the basic educational assistance does not cover, with the institution's contributions matched by equivalent contributions from the Secretary. Under section 3317, the Secretary provides up to fifty percent of the remaining costs for tuition and mandatory fees when the college or university voluntarily agrees to match an equal percentage of the remaining costs. An institution willing to contribute half of the unmet charges therefore completes the price in combination with the federal match. The combined contributions may not exceed the full cost of the charges, and Department of Veterans Affairs payments under the program are made directly to the institution. Yellow Ribbon contributions carry no additional charge against the student's entitlement account.

Eligibility for Yellow Ribbon assistance is narrower than eligibility for chapter 33 itself. Regulation and Department of Veterans Affairs guidance confine the program to individuals entitled to educational assistance at the one hundred percent rate, the maximum tier, together with designated dependents using entitlement transferred from a member at that rate. A beneficiary entitled at a reduced percentage cannot receive Yellow Ribbon funds, and a transferee whose member earned a reduced tier is excluded on the same ground. Agreements may support students at private institutions whose charges exceed the ceiling, students at public institutions charged at out-of-state rates whose basic benefit covers only the in-state figure, and students in graduate programs whose charges exceed the amounts the basic benefit pays. Department of Veterans Affairs guidance on individual agreements confirms that a school may structure contributions by degree level, distinguishing undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral students, and by division, distinguishing students enrolled in a specified college or professional school within a university.

Every other term of Yellow Ribbon assistance belongs to the institution, and the program's benefits therefore vary across institutions in ways that no federal schedule can predict. Participation is the institution's choice: no college or university must enter a Yellow Ribbon agreement, and an institution that declines leaves beneficiaries to the basic ceiling and the full shortfall. Each agreement is made between the Secretary and the individual institution, and section 3317 requires the agreement to specify the manner of the institution's contributions, whether by direct grant, scholarship, or otherwise, the maximum amount of the contribution to be made for any particular individual in any given academic year, and the maximum number of individuals for whom the institution will make contributions in any given academic year. Because the agreement fixes amounts and numbers by academic year, participation is in substance negotiated school by school and year by year, and an institution may enlarge, reduce, or withdraw its commitment in a later agreement. An institution may cap its contribution at a modest fixed sum for a small number of students, or commit to half of the unmet charges for every eligible student admitted, and both arrangements satisfy the statute. Institutions select recipients on a first come, first served basis among eligible applicants, as the governing regulation requires, and the Secretary publishes the list of participating institutions together with information on each agreement.

Read together, sections 3319 and 3317 describe a benefit that is generous in amount and exacting in condition. Entitlement can cross from a member to a spouse or child, but only through an election made in service, purchased with years of additional service, bounded by commencement rules and age limits, and valued at the member's own percentage rate. Charges above the public in-state ceiling can be met, but only where an institution volunteers to share the cost and only for beneficiaries at the maximum rate, in amounts and numbers that the institution itself fixes each academic year. Families that understand the conditions early, during the years when elections remain open and agreements can be compared, can arrange service, education, and finance around the statute as written.

## The Revenue Counting Rule and the Recruitment Problem It Created

The Post-9/11 GI Bill paid tuition and fees directly to institutions, and that payment method gave the benefit a second function beyond financing an education. At proprietary institutions, meaning the for-profit colleges and trade schools that participate in the federal student aid programs, a dollar of veterans benefits also performed accounting work. The dollar helped the institution satisfy a federal revenue test that had nothing to do with veterans policy on its face, and failure of that test could end the institution's access to the far larger stream of federal student loans and Pell Grants on which the sector depended. Understanding the veteran enrollment patterns of the decade after 2008 therefore requires understanding the test itself: what Congress wrote, which dollars Congress counted on each side of the ratio, and what a rational admissions office concluded from the arithmetic. Congress defined the federal side of the ratio by naming one title of one statute, and every federal education dollar that arrived under a different title fell, by default, onto the private side of the ledger.

The rule sat inside the Higher Education Act of 1965, the statute that authorizes the federal student aid programs collectively called Title IV aid after the title of the act in which they appear. Section 487(a)(24) of the act, codified at section 1094(a)(24) of title 20 of the United States Code, required a proprietary institution, as a condition written into its program participation agreement with the Department of Education, to derive no more than 90 percent of its revenues from Title IV sources. Section 487(d) of the act, codified at section 1094(d) of title 20, supplied the calculation, specifying which receipts counted as Title IV revenue and which receipts counted as revenue from other sources. The definition of a proprietary institution appeared separately in section 102(b) of the act, codified at section 1002(b) of title 20, and it limited the rule's application to institutions that were neither public nor private nonprofit. Public universities and nonprofit colleges faced no equivalent revenue ratio, whatever share of their budgets arrived from federal sources. A full account of that statutory framework appears in [the complete guide to the Higher Education Act](/2014/09/15/higher-education-act-1965-guide/), because the incentive that shaped veteran recruitment was manufactured entirely inside the Higher Education Act and merely applied to money that the Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense supplied.

Congress designed the test as a market check. The reasoning, repeated in committee reports and in analyses by the Congressional Research Service, held that an education worth buying would attract some buyers other than the federal student aid programs. An institution that persuaded employers, state governments, scholarship funds, or students paying from earnings and savings to cover at least a tenth of its revenue had demonstrated that someone with a choice valued the training at something close to the charged price. An institution that persuaded nobody, and that collected every dollar from Title IV loans and grants, had demonstrated only an ability to process federal paperwork. The ratio therefore operated as a crude proxy for quality and price discipline in a sector where the customer and the payer were different parties, where the payer was the federal government, and where the usual signals of a failing product, such as customers refusing to pay, arrived in the muted form of loan defaults years after enrollment. The Congressional Research Service recorded arguments on both sides of the proxy's value across successive reauthorizations, but the proxy had one virtue that more refined quality measures lacked: revenue is countable, auditable, and difficult to dispute.

The ratio began stricter than it ended. The Higher Education Amendments of 1992, Public Law 102-325, imposed an 85/15 rule under which a proprietary institution could derive no more than 85 percent of revenue from Title IV sources and had to obtain at least 15 percent from other sources. The 1992 legislation formed part of a broader congressional response to fraud and abuse in the proprietary sector, a response that also restricted incentive compensation for recruiters and limited eligibility for institutions enrolling most of their students at a distance. A 1997 study by the General Accounting Office, the agency renamed the Government Accountability Office in 2004, found that proprietary institutions relying more heavily on Title IV funds tended to record poorer completion and placement outcomes and higher loan default rates than institutions with more diversified revenue, and the study informed the reauthorization debate that followed. The Higher Education Amendments of 1998, Public Law 105-244, loosened the ratio rather than tightening it, reducing the required non-Title IV share from 15 percent to 10 percent and converting the 85/15 rule into the 90/10 rule. The Higher Education Opportunity Act of 2008, Public Law 110-315, then restructured the consequences of failure and specified in greater detail which revenue sources could count toward the 10 percent side, including revenue from education and training programs that were not themselves eligible for Title IV aid.

Enforcement followed a graduated design that the 2008 act formalized. An institution that failed the ratio in a single institutional fiscal year received provisional certification to participate in the Title IV programs for up to two institutional fiscal years, a status that allowed the Secretary of Education to attach additional conditions, such as extra reporting, to the institution's program participation agreement. An institution that failed in two consecutive institutional fiscal years lost eligibility to participate in the Title IV programs for at least two institutional fiscal years, and regaining eligibility required a demonstration of compliance with the act's eligibility and certification requirements across a further two year period. The Department of Education also had to disclose publicly, through the College Navigator website, the identity of each proprietary institution that failed the requirement and the extent of the failure. For an institution financed overwhelmingly by Title IV aid, the two year loss of eligibility amounted to a corporate death sentence administered in stages, and the provisional year functioned as a warning period during which an institution near the line had every reason to change the composition of its revenue quickly. The fastest lawful way to change that composition, after the Post-9/11 GI Bill took effect in August 2009, was to enroll students whose tuition arrived from the Department of Veterans Affairs or the Department of Defense.

### How did the counting rule make a veteran worth nine times as much?

The counting rule treated Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense education money as if it were private revenue, even though Congress appropriated every dollar. Each dollar from those sources filled one tenth of the nonfederal share a proprietary institution had to show, and each dollar therefore supported nine dollars of Title IV aid.

The asymmetry rested on a drafting choice so plain that later debate often described it as a loophole, a word that captures its effect while understating its textual clarity. Section 487(a)(24) capped revenue from the programs authorized by Title IV of the Higher Education Act. The Post-9/11 GI Bill, by contrast, was authorized by chapter 33 of title 38 of the United States Code, the title governing veterans benefits, and it was administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs. Military tuition assistance for service members on active duty rested on Department of Defense authority, principally section 2007 of title 10 of the United States Code, and each armed service ran a tuition assistance program under that authority. Neither stream passed through Title IV, neither stream appeared in the statutory list of federal student aid programs that the ratio counted, and the Department of Education's implementing regulation at section 668.28 of title 34 of the Code of Federal Regulations accordingly placed both streams on the non-Title IV side of the calculation. Federal money therefore counted as nonfederal money for the single purpose that determined whether an institution kept access to federal student aid. A school could describe the resulting figure to its auditors as revenue from sources other than federal student aid, and the description was accurate as a matter of regulatory classification and false as a matter of economic substance.

The arithmetic deserves slow treatment, because the incentive followed from the arithmetic and from nothing else. Consider a proprietary institution with total counted revenue of one million dollars in a fiscal year. Compliance required at least one hundred thousand dollars from non-Title IV sources and permitted at most nine hundred thousand dollars from Title IV sources. Suppose the institution's students, paying from the ordinary mixture of Pell Grants and federal loans, generated the full nine hundred thousand dollars of Title IV revenue and no private revenue at all. Without a non-Title IV dollar, the institution failed the ratio completely. Each veteran enrolled under the Post-9/11 GI Bill changed the denominator and the numerator at once. Tuition paid by the Department of Veterans Affairs counted toward the one hundred thousand dollar requirement, so ten thousand dollars of GI Bill tuition performed the same compliance work as ten thousand dollars of tuition paid by an employer or from a student's savings. Because the requirement was one tenth of revenue, ten thousand dollars on the 10 percent side supported ninety thousand dollars on the 90 percent side, and the ratio of support was exactly nine to one. One dollar of veterans benefits unlocked nine dollars of Title IV aid; ten dollars unlocked ninety; a cohort of veterans paying one hundred thousand dollars in aggregate tuition unlocked nine hundred thousand dollars of loans and grants drawn by the rest of the student body. Veterans Education Success, a nonprofit organization providing legal services to student veterans, stated the ratio in exactly those terms in a 2017 report on the rule, and a later analysis prepared for the Postsecondary Equity and Economics Research project used the same formulation: a dollar of benefit allowed an institution to accept nine dollars of additional federal funding through the aid programs.

Two consequences followed from the structure of the entitlement itself. First, GI Bill revenue arrived without the default risk that attached to loan revenue. A student who borrowed and defaulted damaged the institution's cohort default rate and, in aggregate, invited regulatory sanction, whereas a veteran using an earned entitlement generated no loan, no default, and no repayment obligation tied to the institution's outcomes. Second, the Post-9/11 GI Bill paid generously and predictably, covering the full in-state tuition and fees at public institutions and a substantial capped amount at private institutions, amounts that for-profit tuition schedules frequently matched or approached. The counting rule converted those virtues into a compliance asset. A veteran was worth the tuition paid, worth the absence of default risk, and worth nine times the tuition in preserved access to Title IV revenue, and the third component of that value existed only because Congress had defined the federal side of a ratio by the location of a program in the statute books rather than by the source of the money in the Treasury.

The recruitment record of the following decade is best read as institutions responding to that valuation. The Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions, chaired by Senator Tom Harkin of Iowa, documented the response in a sequence of reports built from data that the committee compelled thirty proprietary education companies to produce. A December 2010 committee report, titled Benefitting Whom, addressed military education benefits directly and found that the companies were expanding veteran enrollment rapidly as Post-9/11 GI Bill spending grew, with eight of the ten largest recipients of Post-9/11 GI Bill tuition payments operating as for-profit institutions. The committee's analysis showed the thirty companies deriving an average of 86 percent of revenue from Department of Education student aid in 2009, a figure close enough to the 90 percent ceiling to make revenue counted on the 10 percent side strategically precious, and Senator Harkin stated in releasing committee findings on military recruiting that benefits counted on the 10 percent side could mean the difference between compliance and severe penalties for a school near the threshold. The committee's culminating report of July 30, 2012, titled For Profit Higher Education: The Failure to Safeguard the Federal Investment and Ensure Student Success, recorded that taxpayers supplied more than thirty billion dollars a year to the companies operating for-profit colleges, including about 25 percent of Department of Education student aid funds, 37 percent of Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits, and 50 percent of Department of Defense tuition assistance funds, with the named federal sources together accounting for 86 percent of revenues at the fifteen publicly traded companies in the study. A follow-up committee report in July 2014, addressing whether the new GI Bill was working as Congress intended, returned to the same structure and treated the counting rule as a central explanation for the enrollment patterns under review.

The committee reports also assembled the internal evidence of method. Drawing on company documents, interviews with former employees, and undercover recordings, the 2012 report found that many companies trained recruiters in tactics of emotional exploitation directed at prospective students, and that some companies misled prospective students about program cost, completion time, completion rates, graduate employment, credit transferability, and accreditation. The committee stated the connection to veterans in a single finding: the 90/10 rule had made veterans and service members prime targets for aggressive recruiting tactics. Holly Petraeus, then serving as Assistant Director for Service Member Affairs at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, supplied the formulation that subsequent debate quoted most often, writing in 2011 that the counting asymmetry gave for-profit colleges an incentive to view service members as dollar signs in uniform and to use aggressive marketing to draw them in. The committee record supported the drier version of the same claim: recruiters worked under enrollment quotas, and military students carried benefits that satisfied a regulatory ratio as well as a tuition bill.

The Government Accountability Office supplied independent corroboration of recruitment method, though the office addressed the sector as a whole rather than military recruitment in isolation. In August 2010 the office reported undercover testing at fifteen for-profit colleges, conducted by investigators posing as applicants. Personnel at four of the fifteen colleges encouraged fraudulent practices, including falsifying financial aid forms, and personnel at all fifteen colleges made deceptive or otherwise questionable statements to the undercover applicants, including misrepresentations of likely earnings after graduation and unclear information about program duration, cost, and graduation rates. Read together, the accountability office reports and the Senate committee reports describe a system under consistent pressure: revenue concentrated near a statutory ceiling, a category of student whose enrollment relieved that pressure at a nine to one rate, and recruitment organizations that the documentary record shows pressing hard on exactly the students the arithmetic rewarded.

Enforcement actions by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Federal Trade Commission during the same period exposed a related maneuver on the 10 percent side of the ratio, the manufacture of non-Title IV revenue from the students themselves. Private student loans counted as non-Title IV revenue, and tuition priced above the maximum available Title IV aid forced students into private borrowing that simultaneously financed the institution and improved its ratio. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau sued ITT Educational Services in February 2014, in the bureau's first action against a for-profit college company, alleging that the company pressured students into high interest, high risk private loans. The bureau sued Corinthian Colleges in September 2014, alleging that the company drew tens of thousands of students into private loans, marketed under the Genesis name, through false claims about job prospects and career services, and then used unlawful debt collection tactics against students enrolled at the time; a federal court entered a default judgment against Corinthian in October 2015 fixing liability at more than 530 million dollars, by which point the company had entered bankruptcy and closed. The Federal Trade Commission's action against DeVry University addressed the advertising end of the same recruitment economy. The commission alleged that DeVry deceived prospective students by claiming that 90 percent of graduates actively seeking employment obtained jobs in their field within six months of graduation, and by claiming that bachelor's degree graduates earned incomes averaging 15 percent higher than graduates of all other colleges and universities. DeVry settled the action in December 2016 for one hundred million dollars, divided between 49.4 million dollars in cash payments to harmed students and 50.6 million dollars in debt relief. None of the three actions charged a violation of the counting rule itself, and that absence is instructive. The counting rule was lawful to exploit. The violations that enforcement could reach lay in the methods that the ratio rewarded: the inflated earnings claims that filled seats, and the private loan machinery that converted federal dependence into apparent market revenue.

Analyses that recalculated the ratio with veterans and Defense Department money counted as federal revenue showed how much of the sector's compliance depended on the classification. An internal Department of Education analysis, reported by Inside Higher Ed in October 2014 from data first reported by the Center for Investigative Reporting, found that 133 additional colleges would have failed the ratio in the 2013 data release if GI Bill benefits and Pentagon tuition assistance had counted toward the 90 percent side, against 29 colleges that failed under the governing definition, and that a further 292 colleges would have landed within five percentage points of the line. Veterans Education Success reached a comparable conclusion from Department of Education data in its 2017 report on the rule, and the Century Foundation placed the dispute in a longer institutional history, chronicling how the Department of Veterans Affairs had adopted an enrollment based 85/15 safeguard under the Korean War era GI Bill of 1952 in response to repeated targeting of veterans by proprietary schools using aggressive and deceptive recruiting. Defenders of the governing definition answered on grounds that the Congressional Research Service recorded without endorsing: counting military benefits as federal revenue might lead some institutions to discourage GI Bill and tuition assistance students from enrolling, might close institutions that some veterans chose for flexible schedules and program formats, and might waste federal investment when closures forced students to restart programs elsewhere. Career Education Colleges and Universities, the trade association for the sector, opposed the statutory change on access grounds when Congress took the question up in 2021.

Congress resolved the classification question in the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021, Public Law 117-2, enacted in March 2021. Section 2013 of that act amended the Higher Education Act to replace the Title IV boundary with a federal boundary, providing that a proprietary institution could derive no more than 90 percent of its revenue from federal education assistance, defined as federal funds disbursed or delivered to or on behalf of a student for use in attending the institution. GI Bill benefits and Defense Department tuition assistance fell inside that definition by its terms, and the 10 percent share had thereafter to come from sources that were nonfederal in substance as well as in classification. Congress delayed the amendment's bite: the revised calculation applied to institutional fiscal years beginning on or after January 1, 2023, giving institutions a transition measured in fiscal years rather than in months. The delay closed the episode on the same terms on which the episode had opened. A definition written into a revenue ratio in 1992, loosened in 1998, and left untouched while a new veterans entitlement flowed through it after 2009 had assigned a regulatory price to a veteran's enrollment, and institutions had organized recruitment around that price for as long as the definition lasted. Section 2013 removed the premium by moving the money to the side of the ratio where its source said it belonged, and the recruitment problem that the Senate committee, the accountability office, and the enforcement agencies had documented lost the accounting foundation on which the documented conduct had rested.

## Amendment and Evolution

The Post-9/11 GI Bill that took effect on August 1, 2009 was not treated by Congress as a finished design. The first years of operation exposed gaps between the structure enacted in 2008 and the ways veterans actually studied. A benefit built around degree study fitted awkwardly around apprenticeships and employer based training. A housing allowance built around physical attendance fitted awkwardly around distance learning. A tuition ceiling built around public charges in each state produced payments that varied by geography rather than by the cost of the education purchased. Two major amending statutes addressed those defects in turn. The Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Improvements Act of 2010, signed on January 4, 2011 as Public Law 111-377, widened the kinds of training the benefit could buy and replaced the state based tuition ceiling for private institutions with a single national cap. The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, signed on August 16, 2017 as Public Law 115-48 and widely called the Forever GI Bill, removed the time limit on use for a defined generation of veterans, restored entitlement lost to school closures, recalculated the housing allowance, and added a further extension of entitlement for study in science, technology, engineering and mathematics. Between those two statutes, section 702 of the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014, Public Law 113-146, tightened the treatment of resident tuition at public institutions.

The Department of Veterans Affairs fact sheet for Public Law 111-377 stressed that its provisions did not share a single commencement date. Some took effect on enactment on January 4, 2011, others on August 1, 2011, and the largest group on October 1, 2011. The act also adjusted eligibility itself. Full time National Guard service performed under Title 32 orders for organizing, administering, recruiting, instructing or training the Guard, and full time Guard service authorized by the president or the secretary of defense in response to a national emergency supported by federal funds, was brought within the definition of qualifying service, with the Congressional Research Service recording the change as retroactive to August 1, 2009 and payable after September 30, 2011. Guard members whose service had resembled active service in daily terms, yet had fallen outside the original definition, could therefore build entitlement from that service rather than losing credit for the period.

The most visible widening concerned the kinds of education and training that counted. From October 1, 2011, the benefit reached noncollege degree programs offered by institutions that did not grant degrees, on the job training, apprenticeship training, flight training and correspondence training, alongside degree programs. The Department of Veterans Affairs described the October changes as allowing payment for programs at schools that did not grant degrees, and reporting by the American Forces Press Service quoted Keith Wilson, director of the education service at the department, calling the expansion significant. Noncollege degree study was paid by reference to actual net cost, subject to the national cap described below for private and foreign institutions, with a books and supplies allowance of up to 83 dollars per month. On the job and apprenticeship training was paid as a monthly benefit prorated according to time spent in the program, again with a books and supplies allowance of up to 83 dollars per month. Flight training carried a separate annual limit of 10,000 dollars and correspondence training a separate annual limit of 8,500 dollars, in each case measured against actual net cost for the academic year. Vocational direction was therefore written into the payment structure with rates and ceilings of its own.

Distance learning received a housing settlement of a deliberately intermediate kind. As the architecture section of this guide records, the original design paid no housing allowance for enrollment pursued solely at a distance. The change itself took effect on October 1, 2011: a student other than a student on active duty who was enrolled solely in distance learning became eligible for a housing allowance equal to one half of the national average of the basic allowance for housing payable to a member in pay grade E-5 with dependents, recorded at a full time rate of 673 dollars and 50 cents for 2011 at the 100 percent tier. Congress accepted that a distance learner incurred housing costs, while declining to pay the locality based rate attached to a campus the student never attended.

Proration supplied the second half of the housing reform, and it cut in the opposite direction for campus based students. Before the change, a student enrolled more than half time could receive the full housing allowance regardless of how far above the half time threshold the enrollment stood. The department explained the new rule by stating that the housing allowance had become tied to training time, so that a full allowance required full time study. From 2011 the allowance was therefore prorated according to the rate of pursuit. A related change ended interval payments, the practice of continuing the housing allowance through breaks between terms when no classes were in session. Ending break payments reduced monthly continuity, yet it also preserved entitlement, because months in which no training occurred no longer consumed the benefit in the same way. The same act extended the books and supplies stipend to students on active duty, allowed reimbursement for more than one licensing or certification test, and allowed reimbursement of fees for national admission tests such as the SAT, the ACT, the GMAT and the LSAT, as the Army summary of the act recorded at signing.

### How did the tuition ceiling change from a state maximum to a national cap?

The tuition ceiling reform of the 2010 act replaced the state based measure described in the architecture section of this guide with a national one for private and foreign institutions. From August 1, 2011, tuition and fees at private and foreign institutions were capped at 17,500 dollars per academic year.

At public institutions, the department paid the actual net cost of in state tuition and fees, after waivers, reductions, scholarships and other designated aid. The cap was prorated according to the benefit tier of the student, so a student eligible at the 80 percent tier faced a correspondingly reduced ceiling, and amounts above the cap at a participating institution could be addressed through the Yellow Ribbon Program, under which an institution agreed to contribute toward the uncovered charges and the department matched that contribution. Federal Register notices announcing later annual rates described sections 3313, 3315 and 3315A of title 38 as directing an increase in the maximum payments for each academic year, and the Congressional Research Service described the adjustment by reference to the average increase in undergraduate tuition in the United States as determined by the Department of Education. The figure of 17,500 dollars therefore functioned as the starting point for an indexed national maximum rather than as a frozen sum.

The reasoning behind the change was administrative as much as financial. A ceiling derived from the highest public charge in each state tied federal payment for a private education to an unrelated public price. A national cap gave students, institutions and the department one published figure for an academic year, against which net cost could be measured in the same way in every state. At a public institution, the federal payment tracked the actual net in state cost charged to the student. At a private or foreign institution, the payment tracked that same net cost up to the national maximum, leaving the gap to the student, to institutional aid or to a Yellow Ribbon agreement. Transitional protection softened the shift, because the department recorded that certain students attending private schools in select states could continue to receive benefits at the rate payable during the previous academic year.

Section 702 of the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014 addressed a different weakness in the public side of that settlement, because a veteran who moved to a state for study after discharge could face nonresident charges despite having had no practical opportunity to establish residence before enrollment. Section 702 directed the secretary of veterans affairs to disapprove a program at a public institution of higher learning for GI Bill payment if the institution charged covered individuals tuition and fees above the resident rate, for terms beginning after July 1, 2015. A covered individual included a veteran who lived in the state where the institution was located and enrolled within three years of discharge from a period of active duty service of 90 days or more, together with individuals using transferred benefits and individuals using the Marine Gunnery Sergeant John David Fry Scholarship who met the corresponding conditions. The sanction gave the rule force, because an institution that refused the resident rate risked approval for the program as a whole.

The Colmery Act of 2017 operated at a larger scale. The Congressional Research Service report on Public Law 115-48 counted more than 30 amendments to the Post-9/11 GI Bill and to other programs administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs, with most provisions effective either on enactment or on August 1, 2018. Harry W. Colmery, a former national commander of the American Legion, was credited with drafting the original text that became the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, work recorded in the Congressional Record as drafted in December 1943 and signed into law by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 22, 1944. Naming the 2017 act for Colmery linked the revision to the drafter of the Second World War readjustment statute, and the popular name of Forever GI Bill linked the act to its best known provision, the removal of the deadline by which benefits had to be used.

### What did the Forever GI Bill change about the time limit for using benefits?

The Colmery Act removed the fifteen year delimiting date for a veteran whose last discharge or release from active duty fell on or after January 1, 2013. The same removal applied to a spouse using transferred benefits from such a veteran and to Fry Scholarship recipients first eligible on or after that date.

For veterans outside that cohort, the fifteen year period continued to govern, so the act drew a bright line at the start of 2013 rather than abolishing the delimiting date for every beneficiary at once. The Congressional Research Service summary stated the rule in terms of last discharge or release, and department guidance applied the same date to Fry Scholarship recipients who first became eligible on or after January 1, 2013. A veteran discharged after the line held 36 months of entitlement without an expiry date, and could sequence education around later events, including a first degree taken long after service or a change of occupation in middle life. The months of entitlement remained finite and continued to be charged according to rate of pursuit and period of enrollment.

Two eligibility extensions accompanied the removal of the deadline, each effective for training beginning on August 1, 2018. A servicemember or honorably discharged veteran awarded the Purple Heart on or after September 11, 2001 became entitled to Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits at the 100 percent benefit level for up to 36 months, regardless of whether aggregate service alone would have produced that tier. The provision answered a gap under which a member whose service had been cut short by wounds could fall short of the thresholds for full benefits as a direct consequence of that injury. The second extension concerned the Yellow Ribbon Program. Before the Colmery Act, Fry Scholarship recipients were excluded from Yellow Ribbon payments. Sections 103 and 104 of the act, as summarized by the Congressional Research Service, extended Yellow Ribbon eligibility to Fry Scholarship recipients and to Purple Heart recipients with effect from August 1, 2018, while eligibility for servicemembers on active duty at the 100 percent level and for spouses using benefits transferred from such members was set by the statute to follow on August 1, 2022. Yellow Ribbon contributions, matched by the department, addressed the charges remaining after the national cap.

Restoration of entitlement for students caught by a school closure formed the most remedial part of the act. Section 109 applied to closures and certain program disapprovals occurring after January 1, 2015, a reach back that the Congressional Research Service connected to the collapse of large proprietary chains, naming institutions owned by Corinthian Colleges, including Heald College, and institutions owned by ITT Educational Services, including ITT Technical Institutes. Corinthian closed in April 2015 and ITT closed in September 2016, leaving students with entitlement already charged for courses that produced no transferable credit and no completed credential. Under the earlier rules, entitlement consumed at an institution that later closed was not restored, so the loss fell on the student twice. The Colmery Act allowed restoration for an incomplete course or program where the student could not receive credit or lost training time because of the closure, with the Government Accountability Office recording that restoration took effect on November 14, 2017 and that students affected by closures between January 1, 2015 and August 16, 2017 could qualify where no credits had been transferred to another college. Reporting in Military Times added that, for later closures, the act provided restoration measured against the term in progress together with a period of continued housing payment, described at passage as up to four months of housing stipend.

The housing allowance was recalculated in two distinct ways, and the two changes are easily confused because both concerned the basic allowance for housing paid to a member in pay grade E-5 with dependents. The first change concerned the level of the rate. Before the Colmery Act, the GI Bill housing allowance had been insulated from a reduction mechanism that the Department of Defense applied to its own housing allowance, under which the member absorbed a small share of housing cost. Department summaries placed removal of that exemption for new users on January 1, 2018. A student who first used the Post-9/11 GI Bill on or after January 1, 2018 therefore received a monthly housing allowance based on the Department of Defense rate itself, which the Air Force Times described at implementation as approximately 1 percent lower than the rate paid under the earlier method. Students who had used the benefit before that date retained protection against a decrease in the allowance where the underlying housing rate fell. The second change concerned the place to which the rate was attached. The statute required the allowance to be calculated from the ZIP code of the campus where the student physically attended the majority of classes, rather than from the location of the institution in which the student was enrolled, a distinction that mattered for branch campuses and satellite sites. Department material listed August 1, 2018 as the effective date for that campus based calculation.

Implementation of the campus based rule illustrated the distance between a statutory date and a payment date. The department encountered serious difficulty in rebuilding its payment systems to identify the campus of majority attendance, and housing payments during the fall term of 2018 were delayed and in many cases calculated on the older basis. In November 2018, Secretary of Veterans Affairs Robert Wilkie announced a reset, reported by the Air Force Times as pushing the corrected calculation to December 1, 2019 so that the technology and the formula could be completed first. The Colmery housing reforms therefore had two faces, a rate basis that had changed for new users from January 1, 2018, and a location basis enacted for August 1, 2018 whose full application in payment systems had been formally deferred to a named date in December 2019.

The Edith Nourse Rogers STEM Scholarship added time rather than changing a rate. Many undergraduate degrees in science, technology, engineering and mathematics require more credit hours than the standard structure that 36 months of entitlement was designed to cover, and a student could exhaust entitlement shortly before finishing. The Colmery Act authorized the department to provide up to nine additional months of Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits, to a maximum of 30,000 dollars, to an eligible student pursuing a qualifying undergraduate program in a STEM field, with the additional benefits scheduled to become available on August 1, 2019. Department and institutional guidance described eligibility in terms of a student who had exhausted entitlement or would exhaust entitlement within six months, who had completed at least 60 semester credit hours or 90 quarter credit hours toward the degree, and who was enrolled in a program requiring at least 120 semester credit hours or 180 quarter credit hours for completion. A second route covered a student who had earned a postsecondary degree in a STEM field and was enrolled in a program leading to a teaching certification. The scholarship could not be transferred to a dependent, and Yellow Ribbon payments could not be matched against it.

Smaller provisions widened access at the margins, and several took effect on enactment on August 16, 2017. Independent study, including online study, became available at institutions that were not institutions of higher learning, provided the institution was an accredited area career and technical education school offering postsecondary education or a postsecondary vocational institution, a change that the department listed among the provisions effective immediately and that the Congressional Research Service noted did not extend to Dependents' Educational Assistance beneficiaries. Work study was placed on a permanent footing by section 201. Three qualifying activities had been authorized on a temporary basis beginning with the Veterans Education and Benefits Expansion Act of 2001 and had been extended repeatedly by later statutes. The Colmery Act removed the expiration date and made those activities permanently eligible, namely outreach services furnished by employees of a state approving agency, hospital and domiciliary care and medical treatment for veterans at facilities of the department and at state homes, and activities related to the administration of a national cemetery or a state veterans cemetery. From August 1, 2018, the entitlement charge for a licensing or certification test and for a national test was prorated according to the actual fee charged, rather than consuming entitlement in larger fixed increments, and approved preparatory courses for licensing and certification tests sat within that framework as payable preparation for an approved test.

Reserve component service received a careful expansion of the service that counted toward eligibility. The Congressional Research Service primer recorded that Public Law 115-48 added three categories of orders to qualifying active duty service, with payment effective August 1, 2018. Service under section 12301(h) of title 10, covering orders to receive authorized medical care, to be medically evaluated for disability or to complete a Department of Defense health care study, counted where the service occurred on or after September 11, 2001. Service under section 12304a of title 10, covering response to a major disaster or emergency for a period of up to 120 days, and service under section 12304b of title 10, covering support of a combatant command on a preplanned mission, counted where the service occurred on or after June 30, 2008. Department guidance summarized the same change as an expansion for orders under sections 12304, 12304a and 12304b on or after that date. A related provision allowed reservists who had established eligibility under the Reserve Educational Assistance Program before November 25, 2015 and had lost that eligibility because of the sunset of that program to elect to have the service credited toward the Post-9/11 GI Bill. The tier structure was scheduled for consolidation on August 1, 2020. Under that scheduled change, the 40 percent tier would be eliminated, a student with aggregate qualifying service of at least 90 days but less than six months would qualify at the 50 percent level, and a student with at least six months but less than eighteen months would qualify at the 60 percent level.

Sequencing was therefore written into the substance of the Colmery Act, and a reader of the statute at March 1, 2019 faced a program in three states at once. One group belonged to enactment in August 2017. A second group belonged to 2018, with the Department of Defense based housing rate for new users from January 1, 2018 and the large August 1, 2018 group described in the preceding paragraphs, followed by an increase in Survivors' and Dependents' Educational Assistance monthly amounts from October 1, 2018 alongside a reduction of new entitlement under that program from 45 months to 36 months from August 1, 2018. A third group belonged to scheduled dates after March 2019, with the Rogers STEM Scholarship from August 1, 2019, the deferred campus based housing calculation from December 1, 2019, tier consolidation from August 1, 2020, and Yellow Ribbon eligibility for members on active duty and their spouses from August 1, 2022. Amendment of a benefit paid monthly proceeded by cohort and by academic year rather than by a single switch, and the date of enactment, the effective date in the statute and the date on which a payment system applied a rule could be three different dates for one provision.

## Administration, Measured Effects, and Honest Limits

Administration of chapter 33 rested with the Department of Veterans Affairs, acting through the Education Service within the Veterans Benefits Administration. That placement mattered. The benefit was an entitlement earned by service, adjudicated under title 38 of the United States Code, rather than a grant competed for through a financial aid office. The department determined eligibility, set the percentage tier tied to aggregate active duty service after September 10, 2001, issued payment, and answered for error. A college could certify enrollment and receive tuition money, but a college could not confer eligibility. A state could approve a program, but a state could not enlarge the federal entitlement. In practice the tidy statutory division created a chain with several hands on each claim, and a student experience often depended on whether every link performed a narrow task on time.

The route into the benefit began with an application to the Department of Veterans Affairs, ordinarily on the education benefits application form, and ended in the first instance with a certificate of eligibility. The certificate stated whether the applicant qualified, the percentage of the maximum benefit payable, the months of entitlement available, and the delimiting date by which entitlement expired under the rules then in force. It was a determination letter rather than a payment. The student presented it to the chosen institution, whose certifying official used it to connect an enrollment record to a verified entitlement. The sequence protected the Treasury against payment on an unexamined claim and protected a student against enrolling on an assumption, but it introduced delay at the point where a term was about to begin, because aggregate service, prior use of other programs, and the irrevocable choice between chapter 33 and an older program all had to be resolved first.

Payment then split along lines drawn by the statute. Tuition and fee payments flowed from the department directly to the institution, on the basis of certified charges and subject to the applicable public, private, or foreign school limits. The monthly housing allowance and the books and supplies stipend flowed directly to the student. Money for charges belonged with the bursar, where an invoice could be checked against a catalog. Money for rent and books belonged with the household, where the obligation fell due whether or not a registrar had finished a file. The split also split the consequences of error. A tuition overpayment created a debt entangled with the institution and its refund policy. A housing overpayment created a debt owed by the student personally, in cash, for money that had in many cases already been spent on rent for a month that had passed.

Between the department and the classroom stood the state approving agencies. Each state designated an agency, funded through a contract with the Department of Veterans Affairs, to review, approve, supervise, and withdraw approval from programs of education and training offered in that state for use of GI Bill benefits. The agencies examined catalogs, facilities, and records, assessed compliance with state and federal criteria, conducted supervisory visits, and transmitted approval decisions to the department. The Congressional Research Service, in a 2017 account of the agencies, described the role as one intended to secure access to a range of quality programs, with the department overseeing the approval process and the agencies applying standards in the local setting. Approval was therefore a gate of a particular kind. It certified that a program met approval criteria and could receive benefit payment. It did not certify that a typical student finished, that credits transferred, or that earnings after completion justified the charges, because the approval system was built around eligibility and compliance rather than published outcome thresholds.

The term by term work fell to schools. A school certifying official reported each enrollment to the department, stating the program, the beginning and ending dates, the credit or clock hours, and the tuition and fee charges, and then amended that certification when enrollment changed. Certification was the trigger for payment and the record against which payment was later audited. Late certification delayed tuition and housing payment alike, and an unreported reduction in hours left the department paying at the wrong rate until the amendment arrived. The Government Accountability Office, in a February 2011 report on education benefits, found the department dependent on exactly this machinery, relying on state approving agencies to approve schools and on schools to report enrollment status, and it recommended a more systematic review of oversight of both. A benefit defined by enrollment could only be as accurate as the enrollment reporting underneath it.

Withdrawal exposed the hardest edge of that machinery. When a student dropped a course, reduced hours, or left a program after payment had issued, the certified record no longer matched the service purchased, and an overpayment arose. The department recalculated the award and established a debt, collected through its Debt Management Center. Mitigating circumstances, meaning events beyond the control of the student such as illness, an unavoidable change in employment conditions, or a family emergency, could limit the amount treated as recoverable for a course not completed. Absent accepted mitigation, a student who withdrew early could owe housing money for the period and face repayment connected to tuition paid to the school, while the school refund might not match the federal debt dollar for dollar. Debt therefore fell most heavily at the moment of failure or crisis that had caused the withdrawal, and it fell on the individual. The entitlement was generous on entry, and exact on exit.

The first test of the system arrived before any routine had formed. Benefits became payable on August 1, 2009, and the autumn term brought a surge of first claims, transfers from the Montgomery GI Bill and the Reserve Educational Assistance Program, and certificates sought by veterans who intended to enroll in a later term. In testimony to Congress in the autumn of 2009, the Department of Veterans Affairs reported that education claims had risen by 180,000 over the corresponding period of the prior year, that an additional 230 staff had been hired since May 2009, and that mandatory overtime and reassignment of more than 70 staff to claims work had not prevented untimely service in every case. Processing relied on interim systems, and amended awards and overlapping terms proved especially difficult. Housing money, the portion tied to rent, was the portion most visibly late. Secretary Eric K. Shinseki answered the backlog with an emergency measure. On October 2, 2009, the department began issuing advance payments of up to 3,000 dollars to enrolled students who had not received benefit payment, against housing and book amounts already earned and subject to deduction from later payments. A student presented photo identification, a course schedule, and a certificate of eligibility at a regional benefits office, and staff also traveled to campuses with large veteran enrollments. In the first week, according to the department statement to Congress, advances went to more than 41,000 students, totaling approximately 120 million dollars. The episode was not evidence that the entitlement had failed, because the advance was an acceleration of an honored obligation rather than a new benefit. It was evidence that a statute can be fully funded on paper and partly unusable in a rental office if adjudication cannot keep pace with a term calendar.

### How many people used the Post-9/11 GI Bill in its first decade?

Department of Veterans Affairs figures reported about 365,640 participants and 5.5 billion dollars in obligations in fiscal year 2010, rising to about 790,000 participants in fiscal years 2014, 2015, and 2016, before falling to 708,069 participants and 10.7 billion dollars in fiscal year 2018.

The trajectory behind those figures explained the political weight of the program by the end of the decade. Fiscal year 2009 captured only August and September of operation and recorded 34,393 participants. Participation then climbed through 555,329 in fiscal year 2011 and 754,529 in fiscal year 2013, before settling on a plateau of roughly 790,000 in each of fiscal years 2014, 2015, and 2016. Obligations followed enrollment upward, from about 5.5 billion dollars in fiscal year 2010 to a peak of about 11.6 billion dollars in fiscal year 2016, then declined to about 10.7 billion dollars in fiscal year 2018 even as the average benefit per participant in that year reached about 15,074 dollars. The figures counted veterans, service members, and dependents, because transferred entitlement and survivor benefits formed part of chapter 33 from enactment, and participants could appear in more than one year. Scale of that order meant that administrative choices on matters as dry as certification timing and debt collection affected a population comparable to the enrollment of a large state university system.

The Congressional Budget Office supplied an independent frame for the same expansion. Its January 2019 baseline for the program projected about 710,000 beneficiaries for fiscal year 2019, an average annual benefit of about 16,002 dollars, and total payments of about 11.4 billion dollars. Projections are not outcomes, but the estimate confirmed the order of magnitude established by the department series. Within a decade the benefit had moved from a new entitlement with a two month first year to a recurring federal commitment of roughly eleven billion dollars a year, buying tuition and a housing support that older GI Bill programs had folded into a single monthly payment at far lower levels.

Enrollment response received separate treatment from Liang Zhang of New York University, in research using American Community Survey data for the years from 2005 to 2015 and a sample of approximately 200,000 post-9/11 era veterans. Zhang estimated that the program raised overall college enrollment by about three percentage points against the period before adoption, with a larger effect of about four percentage points in the early period and a smaller effect of about two percentage points in later years. The pattern fitted the structure of eligibility. A retroactive entitlement reached a backlog of veterans who had separated years earlier and deferred enrollment under the less generous Montgomery program, producing an early surge that could not repeat once that backlog had been absorbed. Even the smaller later effect represented a real change at the margin, in a population with older average age, family obligations, and employment histories unlike those of a conventional entering class.

Evidence on completion required a different kind of record, because payment records alone could not say whether a credential had been earned. The National Veteran Education Success Tracker, published in 2017 by Student Veterans of America with the Department of Veterans Affairs and the National Student Clearinghouse, matched benefit records to enrollment and completion records held by the Clearinghouse. For a subset of 822,327 records with enrollment data, the project found 440,441 postsecondary completions, a completion rate of 53.6 percent, and a further 18 percent enrolled in a term during the matching window in 2015, producing a combined success rate of 71.6 percent for student veterans who had completed or were progressing in a program during the period from August 2009 to December 2013. A fuller discussion of [the completion and earnings record assembled for student veterans](/2019/06/01/gi-bill-education-outcomes/) belongs with the outcomes literature rather than with payment totals. The result cut in two directions. A completion rate above half, achieved by students older on average and more likely to carry family and work obligations, refuted any blanket claim that benefit users enrolled without finishing. A rate near half also meant that a large minority of entitlement was spent on enrollment that ended, within the observation period, without a credential, and the tracker counted completion of the program pursued rather than completion within a standard time to degree.

The retention prediction from the passage debate, stated in full in the origins section of this guide, rested on a Congressional Budget Office estimate by Director Peter R. Orszag in a letter of May 8, 2008. Orszag estimated that raising the present value of education benefits at the first reenlistment point from about 40,000 dollars to more than 90,000 dollars implied a decline in the reenlistment rate from about 42 percent to about 36 percent, that selective reenlistment bonuses costing an estimated 6.7 billion dollars for the years from 2009 to 2013 would be needed to hold force size if the prediction held, and that enlistment would rise by about 16 percent on the strength of the richer benefit. Later assessment faced a difficulty that the estimate, framed as a price effect holding other terms constant, could not escape: other terms refused to hold constant.

Transferability, whose election mechanics and service thresholds appear in full in an earlier section of this guide, was the largest of the moving terms, and it moved by design. Because eligibility to transfer arose only inside service and after substantial service, the cohort whose separation decision the generosity prediction concerned most directly acquired a reason to remain that existed only in service. The compromise therefore confounded the natural experiment. If retention held up, the cause might be transferability, wartime bonuses, a weak civilian labor market after the financial crisis of 2008, or some combination in unknown proportions. If retention fell, the fall might understate the underlying pull of the benefit, because transferability had been working against it. The statute had answered an objection by building the counterweight into the treatment itself, and honest evaluation had to begin from that entanglement.

Later work reflected exactly that ambiguity. Jennie W. Wenger and coauthors, in a 2017 RAND Corporation study titled Are Current Military Education Benefits Efficient and Effective for the Services, concluded that the chapter 33 benefit carried a small negative association with retention relative to the Montgomery GI Bill, with the effect mitigated in part by transferability, and found higher retention associated with transferability among service members with dependents than among service members without dependents. Benjamin L. Castleman of the University of Virginia, Francis X. Murphy, and William L. Skimmyhorn, in an EdPolicyWorks working paper updated in October 2017, modeled transfer as deferred compensation exchanged for continued Army service and found a modest stabilizing effect on Army retention, with a preferred estimate of about two percentage points, alongside takeup highest among senior members with higher pay and education already completed. Read together, the two lines of work supported a narrow conclusion. Transferability appears to have retained some members, particularly members with dependents and members deep into a career. Neither isolated the net effect of the whole statute, because bonuses, deployments, unemployment, and eligibility cohorts changed across the same years. A verdict that the retention objection was refuted, or a verdict that the objection was vindicated, goes beyond the evidence.

Geography imposed a limit that no completion figure could cure. The housing allowance was keyed to the Basic Allowance for Housing for a service member in pay grade E-5 with dependents in the zip code of the institution, so identical service and identical entitlement produced materially different monthly support depending on where a chosen program sat. A veteran at a program in a high cost metropolitan zip code received support reflecting that market. A veteran at an equivalent program in a low cost town received far less, even where rent consumed a comparable share of a household budget, and a veteran who chose a program near family, work, or a medical facility absorbed the difference personally. The logic was defensible, because a national flat rate would overpay in one market and underpay in another. The formula also made place a component of the value of an entitlement earned by identical service, and no adjustment inside the statute equalized that component. The zip code was the mechanism, and its unevenness was the price of tying support to local housing markets.

Program quality imposed a second limit. The statute paid benefits at approved programs, and approval, certification, and payment could all be regular while a program delivered weak instruction, credits that other institutions refused, or completion rates far below sector norms. Nothing in the benefit formula as enacted reduced payment to an institution because its students finished at low rates, and nothing rewarded an institution because its students finished at high rates. Oversight proceeded through eligibility, compliance review, and, in serious cases, suspension or withdrawal of approval, blunt instruments aimed at disqualification rather than graduated instruments aimed at performance. The Government Accountability Office recommendation of February 2011 pointed at the gap. A payment system can establish that a student was enrolled in an approved course and that charges matched a published schedule. A payment system of that design cannot, without additional measures and additional authority, establish that the course was worth the entitlement month it consumed.

Housing support also depended on intensity and on the academic calendar in ways that left predictable gaps. Eligibility for the housing allowance required enrollment at more than half time, and payment rose with the rate of pursuit rather than attaching in full to any enrollment at all. The Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Improvements Act of 2010 then ended interval or break pay, effective August 1, 2011, so that housing payment stopped during periods between terms and resumed with the next certified enrollment. Entitlement was not consumed during the break, which preserved months for later study, but rent and food costs did not observe the academic calendar. A student with a December to January gap or a long summer interval faced an income interruption built into the amended design, and part time enrollment could reduce or eliminate the housing component entirely. The benefit was most complete for a full time student in continuous enrollment. Each departure from that pattern reduced the practical value of the entitlement.

The entitlement clock divided cohorts as sharply as geography divided places. As enacted, chapter 33 entitlement generally expired fifteen years after the last discharge or release from active duty, a delimiting rule that pressed hardest on veterans who spent the first years after service in employment, caregiving, or treatment and came to education late. The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, signed on August 16, 2017 and known as the Forever GI Bill, removed the delimiting date for individuals whose last discharge fell on or after January 1, 2013, and for specified dependents, while leaving the fifteen year limit in place for the earlier cohort. Two veterans with comparable service could therefore face entirely different clocks because one had separated in December 2012 and the other in January 2013. The reform was substantial and its boundary was arbitrary in the way that effective dates are arbitrary. Cohorts separated by a single date lived under different rules for the same benefit.

Two contested readings of the record deserve statement in strong form, each on the authority of a named source. The strongest affirmative reading came from Student Veterans of America through the National Veteran Education Success Tracker of 2017. On that reading, a benefit that paid actual tuition charges and added housing support produced completion of 53.6 percent and progress or completion of 71.6 percent in the tracked population, rates presented by the organization as comparable to those of students generally and better than those of adult learners, achieved by a population in which first generation college students formed a large share and in which business, health, and science, technology, engineering, and mathematics fields accounted for a substantial portion of degrees. The conclusion drawn was that readjustment assistance worked when it removed the price barrier and the living cost barrier at the same time.

The strongest critical reading came from Hollister K. Petraeus, Assistant Director for Servicemember Affairs at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, in 2011, and from the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions under Chairman Tom Harkin, in the committee investigation reported in 2012 and in a follow up analysis of 2014. Petraeus identified the counting rule at the center of the complaint. Under the 90/10 rule of the Higher Education Act, a for profit institution had to draw at least ten percent of revenue from sources other than federal student aid under title IV, yet GI Bill and Defense Department tuition assistance money counted toward that ten percent even though the money was federal. An enrolled veteran therefore satisfied a market test that the rule was supposed to impose, and Petraeus warned that the arrangement gave some for profit colleges an incentive to regard service members as dollar signs in uniform. The Harkin committee reported that eight of the ten largest recipients of program tuition payments in the period examined were for profit institutions and that benefit spending at a for profit institution cost roughly twice the spending at a public institution for a comparable year. On that reading, a formula neutral on its face subsidized low completion institutions because payment followed enrollment, because entitlement months were spent before outcomes were known, and because the counting rule made each veteran unusually valuable to the institutions with the weakest claim on public money. The two readings were not measuring the same thing. One measured what the tracked population achieved. The other measured what the payment incentives rewarded. Both belonged in an honest account of the statute.

## Assessment: A Benefit Priced by Place and Service

Assessment of the Post-9/11 GI Bill begins with the design decision that every later controversy inherited. Congress in 2008 refused a single national price for education after wartime service and built instead a benefit priced by place and by service: tuition measured against public charges, housing measured against a military housing table for the zip code of study, and every component multiplied by a percentage earned through aggregate active duty. The decision made the benefit adequate in a way that the Montgomery GI Bill, paying a flat monthly sum, had ceased to be. The same decision made the benefit unequal in dollar terms among veterans whose service was identical, and no amendment removed that inequality, because the inequality was the mechanism by which adequacy was achieved.

The passage bargain supplied the second permanent feature. Transferability answered the retention objection by paying a career member, in family education, for additional years of service, and the condition structure of section 3319 kept that exchange explicit: election during service, designation of dependents, a four year obligation after six years completed, and repayment liability where the obligation went unfulfilled without excuse. The Yellow Ribbon Program supplied a narrower answer to a narrower problem, meeting charges above the public ceiling only where an institution volunteered to share the cost and only for beneficiaries at the maximum tier. Both provisions rewarded planning inside service and inside family life. Both also meant that the practical value of the statute depended on decisions taken years before enrollment, under information that many families received late or never received in usable form.

Measured effects resist a single verdict. Participation reached roughly 790,000 in each of fiscal years 2014, 2015, and 2016, and federal obligations peaked near 11.6 billion dollars in fiscal year 2016, a scale that made administrative performance a policy question in itself. The completion record assembled through the National Veteran Education Success Tracker showed 53.6 percent of tracked student veterans completing a credential and 71.6 percent completing or progressing, evidence that tuition and housing support together removed barriers that a tuition discount alone had left standing. Retention evidence remained genuinely ambiguous, with transferability retaining some members, particularly members with dependents, while bonuses, deployments, and labor market conditions moved across the same years and defeated clean attribution. The counting rule episode stood apart as the clearest design failure connected to the statute, because a classification inside the Higher Education Act assigned each veteran a nine to one compliance value to proprietary institutions until Congress rewrote the boundary in 2021.

A reader choosing between programs faces a different question from a reader studying the statute, and the choice deserves separate treatment: [a direct comparison of the Montgomery and Post-9/11 programs](/2019/07/01/montgomery-vs-post-911-gi-bill/) sets the two benefits side by side on payment method, contribution, and value by place of study. The specialist treatments connected to this guide cover the ground that a pillar account can only summarize: the Montgomery settlement that the 2008 statute supplemented, the longer sequence of federal veterans legislation, the Higher Education Act framework that produced the counting rule, and the completion and earnings record for student veterans. Each of those treatments appears at the point in the preceding sections where the subject arises, and a reader following a single thread, whether transfer elections, Yellow Ribbon agreements, or delimiting dates, gains most by moving to the specialist account at that point. For organizing the statute itself, with its tiers, dates, and cross references held together across a long course of study, a reader can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html).

The statute that emerges from the full record is neither the restoration of 1944 that supporters invoked nor the retention hazard that opponents predicted. The benefit restored the structure of 1944, payment toward actual charges plus support for living costs, inside a personnel system that 1944 never faced, an all volunteer force dependent on reenlistment by experienced members. Congress legislated both purposes into one chapter of title 38 and accepted the friction between readjustment and retention as a permanent condition of the program. Administration, amendment, and oversight after 2009 managed that friction without dissolving the underlying bargain: generous assistance, earned by service, priced by place, transferable only at the price of more service, and bounded by approval and counting rules written in other statutes for other purposes. Understanding the Post-9/11 GI Bill means holding that bargain whole.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Post-9/11 GI Bill pay for?

The program pays three distinct amounts for an approved program of education. Tuition and fees are paid directly to the institution for actual charges, up to the ceiling that applies to the institution type and academic year. A monthly housing allowance is paid to the student for enrollment above half time, based on a military housing rate for the zip code of study. A books and supplies stipend is paid to the student per credit hour, up to 1,000 dollars per academic year. Each amount is multiplied by the percentage tier earned through qualifying service, and total entitlement is 36 months of training.

### Q: Can you transfer Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits to family?

Transfer is possible only through an election made during service. A service member who has completed at least six years of service and who agrees to serve four additional years may elect to transfer unused entitlement to a spouse, to one or more children, or to a combination of both, designating the months assigned to each dependent and obtaining approval through the service concerned. A spouse may use transferred entitlement after approval, while a child must wait until the member has completed ten years of service and the child has finished secondary school or reached eighteen years of age. A child may not use transferred entitlement after age twenty six.

### Q: How is the Post-9/11 GI Bill housing allowance calculated?

The allowance equals the Basic Allowance for Housing payable to a service member in pay grade E-5 with dependents for the zip code area of the institution or campus of study, multiplied by the percentage tier tied to length of qualifying service. The with dependents rate applies to every beneficiary regardless of actual family status, because the statute fixes a reference household. Payment requires enrollment on more than a half time basis, and after the amendment effective in 2011 the amount is prorated by the rate of pursuit. Enrollment pursued solely through distance learning draws half the national average rate, and no allowance is payable during active duty service.

### Q: How much service earns full Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits?

Full benefits at the 100 percent tier require aggregate active duty service of at least 36 months on or after September 11, 2001. The schedule in section 3313 rises in steps from 40 percent at 90 aggregate days through 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 percent as service lengthens, and each step multiplies the tuition payment, the housing allowance, and the books stipend alike. A second route reaches the same tier without the full calendar measure: an individual who serves at least 30 continuous days and is discharged or released because of a service connected disability receives entitlement at the 100 percent level.

### Q: What is the Yellow Ribbon Program in the Post-9/11 GI Bill?

The Yellow Ribbon Program, established by section 3317, addresses charges that remain after the basic tuition and fees payment reaches the applicable ceiling, a gap that arises most often at private institutions, at public institutions charging out of state rates, and in graduate programs. A college or university may volunteer an agreement with the Department of Veterans Affairs to contribute toward the unmet charges, and the department matches the institutional contribution, with the federal share reaching up to half of the remaining cost. Eligibility is confined to beneficiaries entitled at the 100 percent tier and to designated dependents using entitlement transferred from a member at that tier.

### Q: Did the Post-9/11 GI Bill hurt military retention?

The evidence assembled after enactment supports a narrow answer rather than a verdict. The Congressional Budget Office had projected a decline in retention of about 16 percent, offset by a projected increase in recruitment of about 16 percent, reasoning that a benefit usable after separation rewards departure. Later research complicated that prediction. A 2017 RAND Corporation study found a small negative association between the chapter 33 benefit and retention relative to the Montgomery GI Bill, mitigated in part by transferability, while research on Army transfer elections found a modest stabilizing effect of about two percentage points. Bonuses, deployments, and labor market conditions changed across the same years, defeating clean attribution.

### Q: What did the Forever GI Bill change in the Post-9/11 GI Bill?

The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, called the Forever GI Bill, removed the fifteen year delimiting date for individuals whose last discharge or release fell on or after January 1, 2013. The act granted entitlement at the 100 percent tier to Purple Heart recipients, extended Yellow Ribbon eligibility to Fry Scholarship recipients and Purple Heart recipients from August 1, 2018, and restored entitlement lost when a school closed or a program lost approval. The act also recalculated the housing allowance on the Department of Defense rate for new users from January 1, 2018, tied the rate to the campus of attendance, and authorized additional months for qualifying science, technology, engineering, and mathematics study.

### Q: Why did for-profit colleges target Post-9/11 GI Bill students?

The incentive came from the 90/10 revenue rule in the Higher Education Act, under which a proprietary institution had to draw at least ten percent of revenue from sources other than federal student aid under Title IV. Benefits paid by the Department of Veterans Affairs and tuition assistance paid by the Department of Defense counted toward that ten percent, even though both streams were federal money. Each dollar of veterans benefits therefore supported nine dollars of Title IV aid, and GI Bill revenue carried no loan default risk. Senate committee findings and Hollister Petraeus of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau documented aggressive recruiting built on that arithmetic, which Congress ended by statute in 2021.

### Q: Who is eligible for the Post-9/11 GI Bill?

Eligibility rests on qualifying active duty service on or after September 11, 2001, with a minimum of 90 aggregate days opening the program and longer service raising the percentage tier. Aggregate service may accumulate across multiple periods of duty, a feature of particular consequence for members of the National Guard and the Reserves. Later statutes widened the categories of service that count: the Improvements Act of 2010 brought specified full time National Guard service under Title 32 orders inside the definition, and the Colmery Act added categories of reserve orders covering medical care, disaster response, and support of a combatant command. A disability discharge after 30 continuous days qualifies at the full tier.

### Q: How many months of training does the Post-9/11 GI Bill provide?

Section 3312 fixes total entitlement at 36 months, the equivalent of four academic years of full time study. Entitlement is consumed as training is pursued rather than as calendar time passes. A month of full time training consumes a month of entitlement, and part time training consumes entitlement in proportion to the rate of pursuit, so a beneficiary enrolled at half time draws down the account at half the full time rate for each month of enrollment. The 36 month account is shared rather than multiplied where entitlement is transferred: each month a dependent uses is charged against the member account, month for month, at the time of use.

### Q: When does Post-9/11 GI Bill entitlement expire?

As enacted in 2008, section 3321 required entitlement to be used within fifteen years beginning on the date of the last discharge or release from active duty, after which unused entitlement expired. The Colmery Act of 2017 removed that delimiting date for an individual whose last discharge or release fell on or after January 1, 2013, and the removal extended to a spouse using transferred entitlement from such an individual and to Fry Scholarship recipients first eligible on or after that date. For the earlier cohort, the fifteen year period continued to govern. A child using transferred entitlement was freed from the delimiting date by section 3319 but remained bound by the age twenty six limit.

### Q: How does the Post-9/11 GI Bill differ from the Montgomery GI Bill in payment method?

The two programs move money in opposite directions. The Montgomery GI Bill required a contribution of 1,200 dollars through reductions in basic pay during the first twelve months of service and then paid a fixed monthly amount to the individual during study, leaving tuition, fees, books, and living costs to be met from that amount. Chapter 33 requires no contribution. Tuition and fees are paid directly to the institution against actual charges up to a ceiling, the housing allowance and the books stipend are paid to the student under separate formulas, and the total varies with the place of study and the percentage tier rather than following a single monthly rate.

### Q: How is a Post-9/11 GI Bill certificate of eligibility obtained?

The route into the benefit begins with an application to the Department of Veterans Affairs, ordinarily on the education benefits application form. The department adjudicates the claim under title 38, resolving aggregate qualifying service, prior use of other education programs, and the choice between chapter 33 and an older program, and then issues a certificate of eligibility. The certificate states whether the applicant qualifies, the percentage of the maximum benefit payable, the months of entitlement available, and the delimiting date under the rules in force. The student presents the certificate to the chosen institution, whose certifying official connects the enrollment record to the verified entitlement before payment follows.

### Q: How does a school become approved for Post-9/11 GI Bill payment?

Approval runs through the state approving agencies. Each state designates an agency, funded through a contract with the Department of Veterans Affairs, to review, approve, supervise, and withdraw approval from programs of education and training offered in that state for GI Bill use. The agencies examine catalogs, facilities, and records, assess compliance with state and federal criteria, and conduct supervisory visits, transmitting approval decisions to the department. Approval certifies that a program meets the approval criteria and may receive benefit payment. Approval does not certify completion rates, credit transfer outcomes, or earnings after completion, because the system was built around eligibility and compliance rather than published outcome thresholds.

### Q: What happens after a Post-9/11 GI Bill overpayment?

An overpayment arises when the certified record stops matching the training purchased, most often after a student drops a course, reduces hours, or leaves a program after payment has issued. The department recalculates the award and establishes a debt, collected through the Debt Management Center. A tuition overpayment becomes entangled with the institution and the institutional refund policy, while a housing overpayment is a personal debt for money often already spent on rent. Mitigating circumstances, meaning events beyond the control of the student such as illness, an unavoidable change in employment conditions, or a family emergency, can limit the amount treated as recoverable for a course not completed.

### Q: Does the Post-9/11 GI Bill cover graduate study?

Graduate study at an approved program falls inside the benefit, and the same three components apply: tuition and fees paid to the institution against actual charges, a housing allowance tied to the zip code of study for enrollment above half time, and the books and supplies stipend. The price structure of graduate education exposed a gap under the original design, because the ceiling was measured against undergraduate public charges while graduate charges ran higher. After the 2010 reforms, private and foreign enrollment drew against a national cap, and Yellow Ribbon agreements could address charges above the amount paid, with institutions permitted to structure contributions separately for graduate and doctoral students.

### Q: How are out-of-state charges treated under the Post-9/11 GI Bill?

At a public institution, the basic benefit answers to the resident rate, so a student charged as a nonresident faced a shortfall between the payment and the assessed charges. Section 702 of the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014 narrowed that gap by directing the department to disapprove a program at a public institution that charged covered individuals above the resident rate, for terms beginning after July 1, 2015. Covered individuals included a veteran who lived in the state of the institution and enrolled within three years of discharge from active duty service of 90 days or more, together with specified transferees and Fry Scholarship recipients. Yellow Ribbon agreements offered a separate route at participating institutions.

### Q: Does reserve service count toward Post-9/11 GI Bill eligibility?

Qualifying service is counted in aggregate, so periods of active duty performed in separate activations accumulate toward the thresholds of the percentage schedule rather than requiring a single unbroken tour. That aggregation carries particular weight for the reserve components. The Improvements Act of 2010 counted specified full time National Guard service under Title 32 orders, retroactive to August 1, 2009, and the Colmery Act added further categories of orders, including service to receive medical care or evaluation, service in response to a major disaster or emergency, and service supporting a combatant command on a preplanned mission. A related Colmery provision allowed certain reservists who lost eligibility under the Reserve Educational Assistance Program at the sunset of that program to elect credit toward chapter 33.

### Q: What is the Fry Scholarship within the Post-9/11 GI Bill framework?

The Marine Gunnery Sergeant John David Fry Scholarship extends education benefits to eligible spouses and children of service members who died in the line of duty, and the benefit is administered alongside chapter 33 rather than through a transfer election by the member. Fry recipients appear across the later amendments as a protected class of beneficiaries. Section 702 of the 2014 Choice Act counted Fry recipients among the covered individuals entitled to resident rate treatment at public institutions. The Colmery Act removed the delimiting date for recipients first eligible on or after January 1, 2013 and extended Yellow Ribbon eligibility to Fry recipients from August 1, 2018, closing an exclusion that had left survivor beneficiaries outside the cost sharing program.

### Q: Can the Post-9/11 GI Bill pay for licensing tests and test preparation?

Reimbursement for testing formed part of the benefit and widened under amendment. The Improvements Act of 2010 allowed reimbursement for more than one licensing or certification test and allowed reimbursement of fees for national admission tests such as the SAT, the ACT, the GMAT, and the LSAT. The Colmery Act then changed the accounting: from August 1, 2018, the entitlement charge for a licensing or certification test and for a national test was prorated according to the actual fee charged, rather than consuming entitlement in larger fixed increments. Approved preparatory courses for licensing and certification tests sat within that framework as payable preparation for an approved test.
