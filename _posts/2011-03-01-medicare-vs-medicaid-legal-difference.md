## The Two Titles Everyone Confuses

Ask a room of thoughtful adults to name the difference between Medicare and Medicaid, and the answer arrives within seconds: the first is for old people, the second is for poor people. The formula is tidy, memorable, and wrong in every direction that matters. It is not that the two arrangements never line up with age and income; they often do. It is that the formula teaches the wrong mental move. It trains a person to sort by label. The law sorts by statute, and those two habits point at different answers often enough that the tidy formula costs people real money, real coverage, and real time. The rest of this guide is an attempt to replace the label habit with the statute habit, one axis at a time.

Here is the claim the whole comparison rests on: most confusion about the two titles is not confusion about the programs at all. Readers know there are two of them, and they can usually recite the old-versus-poor line on demand. What they cannot do, and what the shorthand actively prevents them from doing, is determine which title of the Social Security Act answers the question sitting on their desk. A 68-year-old wondering who pays the part of a hospital bill that her hospital insurance left behind is not really asking which program she belongs to; she is asking whether Title XVIII or Title XIX governs the next dollar of that bill. The names Medicare and Medicaid are nicknames for two legal architectures, one codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395 and the sections that follow, the other at 42 U.S.C. 1396 and the sections that follow. The architectures answer different questions, and the skill this guide teaches is to ask the statute's question instead of the nickname's.

Take a concrete case. A daughter is told her father's nursing home costs $8,000 a month and asks, "Is this Medicare or Medicaid?" The label habit answers with a guess: he is 78, so Medicare. The statute habit asks better questions: does any title cover long-term custodial care as a general benefit, which of his resources count toward the assistance title's financial test, and what did his payroll tax record buy him? The first habit produces a confident wrong answer; the second produces the right set of questions. Most of the expensive mistakes people make with these two titles follow exactly that pattern: the wrong question, asked confidently, answered wrongly.

Start with the people the formula misplaces in the first direction. The shorthand says the federal title belongs to the old, yet hospital insurance under Title XVIII covers a substantial population that has not reached 65. Workers who draw Social Security disability insurance become entitled to that hospital insurance after 24 months on the disability rolls, under a rule written into 42 U.S.C. 426(b) and examined at length under Axis 1. People with end-stage renal disease enter through a separate statutory door that asks nothing about age. None of these beneficiaries is old in the sense the formula assumes, and every one of them holds the card the formula reserves for the old. A shorthand that cannot place a 45-year-old dialysis patient, or a 58-year-old former warehouse worker two years into disability benefits, is not a simplification. It is a misdirection, and it misdirects exactly the people who can least afford a wrong turn.

The formula misplaces people in the other direction just as reliably. Title XIX covers millions of people over 65, because beneficiaries of the federal title often qualify for the federal-state title as well; in that dual arrangement, the second title pays premiums and cost-sharing that the first title leaves behind. And the spend-down doorway admits people the word "poor" never described: when nursing home bills run to five figures a month, households that began comfortably middle class can qualify for the medical assistance title once their countable resources are exhausted. Axis 5 walks through that doorway in full. Neither exception is exotic, and neither is a loophole. Both are ordinary features of the statutes, operating exactly as written, and the shorthand cannot survive either one.

The shorthand fails on financing, too. It invites the impression that the federal title is free and the assistance title is charity. Neither is true. Hospital insurance was purchased in advance through payroll taxes, and the medical insurance half of the federal title carries a monthly premium for every enrollee. The assistance title, once a person qualifies, creates an enforceable claim to covered services under an approved state plan, not a discretionary handout. Free-versus-charity is as misleading as old-versus-poor, and it misleads in the same direction: it replaces the statute's actual terms with a story about who deserves what.

Underneath the misplaced people sits a deeper failure. Old-versus-poor describes who, and quietly pretends the how is the same for both titles. It is not. The federal title is uniform across the country and attaches to the individual; the federal-state title is administered by the states within federal guardrails, and the same household can qualify in one state and not in another. Eligibility under the first title never depends on a governor's budget or a legislature's mood; eligibility under the second sometimes does, through the optional groups a state may or may not cover. Consider what that means for a family choosing where an aging parent will live: the hospital insurance travels with the parent, while the assistance coverage may have to be re-established under a different state's rules. A comparison that stops at demographics misses the institutional difference that decides real cases, and it is the institutional difference, not the demographic one, that this guide keeps returning to.

This guide compares the two titles along six axes, each one a different way of asking which statute answers your question. The first axis is entitlement structure: what kind of legal claim each title creates, and against whom the claim runs. The second is financing and federalism: who pays for the promise, and who gets to define it. The third is eligibility mechanics: how a person actually qualifies, step by step. The fourth is benefit design and cost-sharing: what is covered, and what the beneficiary still owes. The fifth is long-term care: the doorway through which the non-poor enter the assistance title. The sixth is the decision framework itself, a set of plain questions that routes any real case to the right title. Six comparisons, and one habit to carry through all of them: ask the statute's question.

### Why does the old-versus-poor shorthand keep failing in real cases?

If you keep asking which program you belong to, you are asking the wrong question. Ask instead which statute decides your issue: Title XVIII turns on age, disability, or kidney failure plus work history, while Title XIX turns on category and income under your state's plan. The program answer follows the statute, never the shorthand.

## One Signature, Two Statutes

On July 30, 1965, in Independence, Missouri, Lyndon B. Johnson signed H.R. 6675 into law as Public Law 89-97, the Social Security Amendments of 1965, with former President Harry Truman seated beside him. One bill, one signature, two new titles inside the Social Security Act: Title XVIII, the hospital insurance program for the aged that took the name Medicare, and Title XIX, the grants-to-states program for medical assistance that took the name Medicaid. Readers who want the legislative history in full can start with [The Social Security Amendments of 1965 that created both programs](/2010/11/01/social-security-amendments-1965-medicare/). The act's own short title was the Health Insurance for the Aged Act, which records where the center of gravity sat in 1965; the assistance title entered as its companion, and the pairing has shaped every comparison since.

Congress wrote two titles rather than one because it was solving two different problems with two different theories of government action. Older Americans faced a market failure: commercial insurers either refused to cover the aged or priced coverage beyond their reach, so only a broad risk pool created by law could make hospital insurance affordable. The answer was social insurance on the Social Security model, with workers paying payroll taxes during their earning years and drawing hospital coverage as an earned right. Low-income families faced a different failure inside the existing welfare system: state public assistance programs covered cash needs unevenly and medical needs barely at all. The answer there was a federal offer rather than a federal program, matching funds for any state willing to build a medical assistance program within federal rules. Different diagnoses, different prescriptions, one signing ceremony.

The two titles also had two different ancestries, which is why they look so unlike each other. When Franklin Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act in 1935, medical benefits were left out of the bill; the committee studying the legislation feared that adding health insurance would sink the entire measure. The insurance title of 1965 therefore grew from the payroll-tax tradition that the 1935 act established, extended at last to medical care. The assistance title grew from a different root: the Kerr-Mills Act of 1960, which offered federal matching funds to states providing medical help to older persons whose medical expenses exceeded their incomes. Kerr-Mills gave states wide leeway and produced uneven results across the country, but it was the working template for Title XIX, and the template's fingerprints, state administration, federal matching, means testing, are all over the Medicaid statute.

A final note on names, because this guide will use them precisely. "Medicare" and "Medicaid" are the popular names; the law knows Title XVIII and Title XIX of the Social Security Act. The distinction is not pedantry. Popular names invite the label habit, while title numbers point at the actual statutory text where rights are defined. Whenever this guide says "the federal title," it means Title XVIII; "the assistance title" or "the federal-state title" means Title XIX. Keeping the two vocabularies separate is the first discipline of the statute habit.

The mechanisms still carry those theories, and they are worth reading as theories rather than as accidents. Under Title XVIII, coverage is earned through the payroll tax record and defined by federal law; the beneficiary's claim does not depend on any state's choices, and no state official administers it. Under Title XIX, coverage is financed jointly, with Washington reimbursing a share of each participating state's spending, and the program's shape reflects both federal requirements and state elections. One model centralizes the promise, the other distributes it. One treats the benefit as a return on contributions, the other as assistance conditioned on need. The confusion between the two titles begins here, in the mistaken impression that a shared birthday means a shared design.

The legislative path to that birthday was swift by the standards of major social legislation. The bill passed the House in April of 1965 and the Senate that July, went through a conference committee that reconciled the two versions, and reached the President's desk before the month was out. The speed reflected a rare alignment: a large congressional majority, a President who had made the measure central to his domestic agenda, and a public debate that had been running, in one form or another, since Truman's own failed health insurance proposals. Truman's presence at the signing was not ceremonial decoration; it was an acknowledgment that the 1965 act completed an argument two decades old.

The shared birthday matters because it explains the one thing the titles genuinely have in common: both are answers to the same 1965 judgment that private markets and existing welfare programs were leaving medical need unmet. Everything after that judgment diverges: mechanism, financing, administration, and the legal character of the beneficiary's claim. The rest of this guide maps the divergence. It begins, in Axis 1, with the divergence that has the sharpest legal consequences: what each title actually entitles a person to, and what has to be true before the entitlement attaches.

## Axis 1: Entitlement Structure

Of the six axes, entitlement structure carries the sharpest legal consequences, because it decides who owes whom what, and what happens when the money runs short. An entitlement, in the lawyer's sense, is a claim the beneficiary can enforce against the government: meet the statutory conditions, and the benefit is yours by law, not by grace of the annual budget cycle. The hospital insurance title creates that kind of claim for the people it covers. The medical assistance title creates a more conditional kind of claim, real once established, but bounded by state choices the federal title never allows. Everything else in the comparison, financing, administration, even the politics that surround the two titles, flows from this difference. Start here and the other axes arrange themselves; start anywhere else and they blur.

Start with the earned side. Hospital insurance under Part A of Title XVIII is financed by payroll taxes collected during a worker's earning years, and eligibility at 65 turns on the worker's record of coverage: forty quarters of covered work, roughly ten years, buys Part A without a premium. A quarter of coverage is a three-month calendar period in which the worker earned enough to be credited, with a maximum of four quarters creditable in any year, and the Social Security Administration keeps the lifetime tally that the Medicare statute then reads. The right attaches to the individual, or to a spouse through the worker's record, and it follows the beneficiary across state lines without any new eligibility test. Income does not reduce it, assets do not disqualify it, and no state legislature can narrow it. The claim is written into federal statute, which means only an act of Congress can change its terms, and the annual appropriations process cannot shrink it by neglect.

The payroll tax mechanics are worth stating plainly, because they are the consideration in this earned bargain. Under the statute in force in 2011, employees and employers each paid 1.45 percent of wages toward hospital insurance, for a combined 2.9 percent, with the self-employed paying the combined rate on their earnings. Those taxes do not sit in a personal account with the worker's name on it; they finance the system that pays for the current generation's hospital care, while the worker's own future claim rests on the quarters-of-coverage record the taxes create. The design pairs pay-as-you-go financing with an individual earnings record, which is why the entitlement can be both collective in funding and personal in right.

The earned character has edges worth noting, because they keep the comparison honest. Workers short of forty quarters may still enroll in Part A by paying a monthly premium that scales with their record, with a lower premium for those in the thirty-to-thirty-nine-quarter band than for those below thirty quarters. And the premium-free promise belongs to Part A alone: Part B, the supplementary medical insurance that covers physician and outpatient services, has always been voluntary and has always carried a monthly premium, even for beneficiaries whose Part A costs them nothing. The distinction matters whenever someone describes the federal title as free. Hospital insurance is earned and, for most, premium-free; the medical insurance half of the title is a subsidized purchase, and the subsidy is set by federal law rather than by any state's generosity.

The payoff of the earned design is uniformity. A worker who paid payroll taxes in one state and retires in another faces no second application, no income test, no waiting for a state agency's determination. The entitlement travels with the person because the statute defines it nationally and funds it nationally. There is one set of rules, one payer, and one card, good everywhere. When people describe the federal title as simpler than its sibling, this is usually what they mean, whether they know the statutory reason or not: the design leaves nothing for a state to decide, so there is nothing for a beneficiary to re-litigate at a state line.

The assistance title works nothing like that, because it was built to answer a different question. Eligibility under Title XIX is categorical and financial at once: a person must fit one of the groups the statute covers and must meet the income and resource standards the state sets for that group. Under the statute in force in 2011, the covered groups included the aged, the blind, people with disabilities, pregnant women, children, and parents of dependent children. Either lock without the other leaves the door closed. A person with no income but no covered category generally did not qualify, which is why a non-disabled adult without dependent children stood outside the program in most states no matter how little that adult earned. A person in a covered category whose income sat above the state's line did not qualify either. Need alone was never the test, and category alone was never the test; the statute demands both, and the state sets the financial thresholds within federal limits.

The categories themselves are an inheritance. They descend from the welfare law tradition that sorted the needy into recognized groups, the aged, the blind, people with disabilities, dependent children and their caretakers, rather than treating need as a single undifferentiated condition. That inheritance is why the assistance title can seem arbitrary to a newcomer: two neighbors with identical poverty can face opposite answers because only one fits a recognized category. The federal title never asks the category question at all. It asks about age, disability status, kidney failure, and the work record, and the answers come from federal records rather than from a caseworker's judgment about which group a person belongs to.

The second structural feature is the state election. Federal law, at 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(10), divides the eligible groups into those every participating state must cover and those a state may cover at its option. The mandatory groups form the national floor: no state plan is approved without them. The optional groups are a state's choice, and they include the medically needy, people whose incomes exceed the standard limits but whose medical expenses bring them within reach of eligibility. The result is a map of the assistance title that changes at state lines in a way the insurance title never does. Two households with identical incomes and identical diagnoses can face different answers in neighboring states, because one legislature elected an optional group years earlier and the other did not. The beneficiary's coverage can therefore depend on a political decision made long before the illness arrived, which is a sentence that can never be written about the federal title.

Once a person fits a covered group and the financial test, the state's obligation is real: the approved state plan must furnish the covered services to eligible individuals, and the federal match follows. But the boundaries of that obligation are drawn partly in state capitals, and states may narrow optional coverage when budgets strain, within the federal rules that constrain them. Compare that with the hospital insurance claim, which no governor and no state budget can touch. This is the axis's central contrast, stated without ornament: an individual, federally defined, contribution-based entitlement on one side, and a conditional, jointly defined, need-based entitlement on the other. The first asks what you paid in; the second asks what group you fit and what you have left.

Now the two exceptions that break the old-versus-poor formula from the inside, stated plainly as the task requires. The first belongs to the federal title. Section 426(b) of Title 42 provides that a person under 65 who has been entitled to disability insurance benefits, or to certain related benefits such as disabled child's or disabled widow's benefits, for 24 calendar months becomes entitled to hospital insurance. Age never appears in the test; status and duration do. The clock starts with entitlement to the disability benefit, and the hospital insurance follows only once the 24 months have run, which is why newly disabled workers face a coverage gap the statute simply accepts as part of the design. The second door stands even wider open: 42 U.S.C. 426-1 creates a separate entitlement for anyone medically determined to have end-stage renal disease, conditioned on insured status under the individual's own work record or a spouse's or parent's record, plus a filed application, with its own rules for when the entitlement period begins and ends. A teenager on dialysis can hold the same hospital insurance card as a 70-year-old retiree. The shorthand has no sentence for that case, and the statute has a whole section.

The 24-month clock deserves a closer look, because it is the provision's real teeth. The months are counted from entitlement to the disability benefit, meaning the date the benefit became payable, not the date the application was approved. Months covered by retroactive back pay count toward the 24, so a worker whose claim took two years to decide may find the hospital insurance arriving close behind the approval. The statute also handles the edges: related benefits such as disabled child's and disabled widow's benefits feed the same clock, and railroad retirement disability beneficiaries have their own parallel rule. The waiting period is a deliberate cost-control device built into the entitlement, not an administrative backlog, and it applies with the same force in every state.

The kidney-failure door has its own timing rules, spelled out in the same section. Entitlement begins with the third month after the month a regular course of dialysis starts, or with the month of a kidney transplant, and it ends twelve months after dialysis is no longer required or thirty-six months after a transplant. The entitlement is therefore conditional in a way the age-based entitlement is not: it lasts as long as the medical condition that created it, and no longer. A beneficiary who recovers kidney function loses the ESRD-based coverage on the statute's schedule, while keeping any coverage earned through age or disability. Even inside the federal title, different doors have different hinges.

The second exception belongs to the assistance title, and it runs in the opposite direction. When long-term-care bills reach five figures a month, the financial tests of Title XIX measure what is left after medical need has done its work, not what the household held before the illness. People who began as comfortably middle class, with savings and a paid-off house, can become eligible once the cost of care has spent those resources down to the state's limits. The law counts the remainder, not the starting point, and the spend-down rules are the mechanism that performs the subtraction. Axis 5 follows this doorway in full, through the nursing home cases where it matters most. For now, note the reversal it performs on the shorthand: the title associated with poverty regularly covers people nobody would have called poor at the outset, because poverty in this statute is measured after the illness rather than before it.

One population lives on both sides of this axis at once, and it is worth naming here: the dually eligible. These are beneficiaries who hold the federal hospital insurance by virtue of age or disability and also qualify for the assistance title by virtue of category and finances. For them, the assistance title typically pays the premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance that the federal title leaves behind, functioning as a supplement built from an entirely separate statute. Dual eligibility is the clearest proof that the two titles are not rival brands competing for the same people. They are separate legal answers that can both be true of one person at the same time, each paying for what the other does not.

Put the axis to work as a decision tool. When a question arrives, ask the two entitlement questions before anything else. First, did the person, or the person's spouse, pay into the hospital insurance system long enough to own a claim on it? If yes, the federal title is in play and no state can take it away. Second, does the person fit one of the assistance title's covered groups and sit within the state's financial limits for that group? If yes, the federal-state title is in play, and the state's choices about optional groups may expand or narrow what follows. Most real cases resolve at this axis. The remaining axes explain what each title then does with the person it has admitted.

### Why does the federal insurance title cover people under 65 at all?

Because Congress wrote disability coverage into 42 U.S.C. 426(b); age was never the rule. A worker under 65 who has drawn disability insurance benefits for 24 months becomes entitled to hospital insurance, and kidney failure opens a path under 42 U.S.C. 426-1. The statute sorts by status and contribution, not by the calendar alone.

## Axis 2: Administration: Washington Runs One Program, the States Run the Other

The sharpest structural divide between the two titles is administrative. Medicare is run from Washington under a single national chain of command, while Medicaid is run from the fifty state capitals under plans that Washington approves. That one fact determines who writes the operating rules, who pays the people who process claims, where a dissatisfied beneficiary takes a complaint, and which government stands as defendant when a coverage dispute reaches court. A reader who confuses the two designs will aim every inquiry at the wrong office. The distinction also explains why the programs feel so different in ordinary use even though Congress created both in the same 1965 enactment, Public Law 89-97. Any comparison of Medicare vs Medicaid that skips this axis will mislead, because eligibility rules and benefit packages only make sense once the reader knows which government operates the program in question.

Title XVIII vests administration of Medicare in the Secretary of Health and Human Services. Congress gave the Secretary broad authority to carry out the title's provisions, and the Secretary exercises that authority through the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, the agency that serves as the title's operating arm. That agency began in 1977 as the Health Care Financing Administration, a consolidation of the offices that had managed Medicare and Medicaid separately since 1965. It took its current name in 2001, when Secretary Tommy Thompson announced on June 14 of that year that the Health Care Financing Administration would become the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, a change meant to make the agency's identity match the two programs it administered. Inside the Department, the agency writes national regulations, issues the manuals that interpret them, sets payment policy, and oversees the contractors that do the daily work.

The federal character of Medicare does not mean that federal employees process every claim. From the beginning, the statute directed the Secretary to contract with private organizations for day-to-day operations. Hospitals dealt with fiscal intermediaries; physicians and other suppliers dealt with carriers. Those contractors applied uniform national coverage rules, and they answered to Washington rather than to any governor. The Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003 later consolidated the intermediary and carrier functions into Medicare Administrative Contractors, regional private firms that process claims, enroll providers, educate suppliers, and make initial coverage decisions under contracts the agency supervises. The line of accountability therefore runs straight upward: contractor to agency, agency to Secretary, Secretary to Congress.

Coverage policy in Medicare is also made nationally, though with an important local layer. The Secretary may issue national coverage determinations that bind every contractor in the country on whether an item or service is reasonable and necessary, and the agency develops those determinations through a public process with an opportunity for comment. Where no national determination exists, the individual Medicare Administrative Contractors issue local coverage determinations for their regions, which means coverage of a new technology can vary by region until Washington resolves the question. Both layers sit beneath notice-and-comment rulemaking: the agency's major payment and policy changes appear as proposed and final rules in the Federal Register, and providers, plans, and beneficiary advocates participate as commenters. One legacy division of labor survives from 1965: the Social Security Administration still enrolls beneficiaries in Medicare and collects Part B and Part D premiums, a reminder that the program grew out of the Social Security system even though its administration moved to the health agency decades ago.

A Medicare beneficiary who disagrees with a coverage decision follows a single national appeals ladder, and the statute at 42 U.S.C. 1395ff spells out each rung. The claim first returns to the contractor for redetermination. If the denial stands, a Qualified Independent Contractor reconsiders it. The next step is a hearing before an administrative law judge in the Department's Office of Medicare Hearings and Appeals, then review by the Medicare Appeals Council, and finally judicial review in federal district court. Courts generally require beneficiaries to exhaust this chain before suing, a doctrine that channels thousands of disputes into the administrative process and keeps coverage law uniform nationwide. The legal materials that govern these disputes are the agency's own manuals, the Medicare Claims Processing Manual and the Medicare Benefit Policy Manual among them. Those manuals are subregulatory guidance: they bind the contractors and the adjudicators, and while courts give them respectful attention, the statute and the Code of Federal Regulations remain the controlling law.

Medicaid turns the model inside out. Title XIX does not create a federal operating agency for the program's daily work; it invites each state to submit a plan describing how it will run its own program, and federal money flows only after the Secretary approves that plan. The mandatory contents of the plan sit at 42 U.S.C. 1396a, a long catalog of promises the state must make about eligibility, covered services, provider participation, payment methods, and beneficiary protections. Once approved, the state is the operator: it enrolls beneficiaries, contracts with or pays providers, sets payment rates within federal bounds, and processes claims through its own Medicaid agency or its own fiscal agents. Medicaid's structure [the state plan requirements and waiver authorities](/2011/02/01/medicaid-statute-structure-explained/) shows how the plan document functions as both a contract with Washington and an operating license. The federal role is supervisory. CMS reviews state plan amendments, approves or rejects them, and works through its Center for Medicaid and State Operations, the component charged with overseeing programs that states administer. When a state wants to depart from the statutory template, it seeks a waiver, most notably a demonstration waiver under section 1115 of the Social Security Act, 42 U.S.C. 1315, or a home and community based services waiver under section 1915(c), and the Secretary's approval of that waiver becomes part of the legal terms under which federal matching dollars flow.

The complaint path for a Medicaid beneficiary looks nothing like Medicare's ladder. The statute guarantees every applicant and enrollee an opportunity for a fair hearing before the state agency when a claim for assistance is denied or delayed, 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(3), and that hearing takes place in the state administrative system, not in a federal tribunal. Beyond the fair hearing, beneficiaries have sued state officials in federal court under 42 U.S.C. 1983 to enforce provisions of Title XIX that create individual rights, and the Supreme Court's decisions in this line have turned on whether particular subsections of section 1396a speak in rights-creating terms. State courts also hear challenges to state agency action under state administrative procedure acts. Meanwhile the federal government's principal enforcement tool against a noncompliant state is not a beneficiary's lawsuit but the compliance process: after notice and a hearing, the Secretary may withhold federal payments until the state plan comes back into compliance, 42 U.S.C. 1396c. The sanction is used sparingly, which leaves negotiation, plan amendments, and waiver conditions as the ordinary instruments of federal oversight.

The state plan is not a one-time filing. States amend it constantly as legislatures change benefits, adjust payment rates, or reorganize agencies, and each amendment requires federal approval before the new terms can draw matching funds. When CMS disapproves an amendment, the statute gives the state a right to reconsideration and ultimately to judicial review, so the approval process is itself a small administrative law system with the state Medicaid director on one side and the agency's regional office on the other. Waivers add a negotiated layer on top. A section 1115 demonstration is approved for a fixed term with special terms and conditions, and its renewal becomes a recurring negotiation over budget neutrality, the longstanding requirement that the demonstration not cost the federal government more than the program would have cost without it. In this way Medicaid's administration is continuous bargaining between sovereigns, where Medicare's is hierarchical direction from one.

Both programs also route large numbers of beneficiaries through private health plans, but the contracting authority sits in different hands. Under Medicare Part C, the Secretary contracts directly with Medicare Advantage organizations under 42 U.S.C. 1395w-21 and the sections that follow; the plans are federal contractors paid under national rules, and their enrollees keep the federal appeals protections with only modest variation. Under Medicaid, the state is the party that contracts with managed care organizations under 42 U.S.C. 1396b(m) and related provisions; the state sets capitation rates, monitors plan performance, and enforces contract terms, while the federal role is confined to approving the state's contracts and payment arrangements. A dispute with a Medicare Advantage plan is ultimately a dispute with a federal contractor; a dispute with a Medicaid managed care plan is ultimately a dispute with the state's chosen agent, heard first under the state's grievance and hearing rules.

Providers experience the administrative divide as two different enrollment and oversight regimes. A hospital that wants to bill Medicare must satisfy the conditions of participation set by federal regulation and enforced through survey and certification, a national quality gate that applies whether the hospital sits in Texas or Vermont. The same hospital's Medicaid participation is governed by the state plan and state provider agreements, which incorporate the federal participation requirements but add state-specific terms on payment, prior authorization, and managed care network membership. A physician excluded from one program faces exclusion from the other as well, because the statute requires states to exclude providers that the federal government has excluded, which makes the two administrative systems interlock at the point of program integrity even as they diverge everywhere else.

The practical lesson is that the two programs answer to different sovereigns, and a dispute aimed at the wrong one goes nowhere. A Medicare beneficiary who writes to a governor about a denied claim has contacted an office with no authority over the decision. A Medicaid enrollee who demands that Washington order a state to cover a service discovers that the federal government can only enforce the terms of the approved state plan, not rewrite them on the spot. The governing documents differ too. Medicare disputes are fought over the meaning of national manuals and regulations that apply identically from Maine to Hawaii. Medicaid disputes are fought over the state plan, state regulations, and the terms of any approved waiver, which means the same medical service can be covered in one state and excluded in another without either state violating federal law, so long as each stays within its approved plan and above the statutory floor.

Administration is therefore the axis that turns a benefits question into a venue question. Medicare's chain runs from the Secretary through CMS and its contractors to a federal appeals ladder; Medicaid's runs from the Secretary through plan approval and waivers to a state agency and its fair hearing system. Once that geometry is clear, the second axis becomes easier to grasp, because each program's money follows the same channels as its authority. The reader who keeps both chains in mind will rarely ask the wrong office for help, and will understand why identical medical needs can produce different answers under the two titles.

### Where does a coverage dispute begin in each program?

A Medicare dispute begins with the contractor that denied the claim, through redetermination and the federal appeals ladder in 42 U.S.C. 1395ff. A Medicaid dispute begins with the state agency's fair hearing process guaranteed by 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(3). Filing in the other system wastes months, because each forum can only apply its own program's governing law.

## Axis 3: Financing: Dedicated Trust Funds Against an Open-Ended Match

The second axis is financing, and it mirrors the first. Medicare raises and holds its own money in dedicated trust funds under federal control; Medicaid spends state money first and then collects a federal reimbursement fixed by statute. Readers sometimes treat the two programs as two windows into one federal health budget. They are not. Each has its own revenue base, its own accounting, and its own legal definition of what happens when the money runs short, and those definitions shape every policy argument made about either program's future. The legal consequence is that each program's budget pressure travels through different machinery. Medicare absorbs fiscal stress through its payment rules, while Medicaid absorbs it through the match rate and the rules about which state spending qualifies for the match.

Medicare's Hospital Insurance program, Part A, is financed through the Federal Hospital Insurance Trust Fund established at 42 U.S.C. 1395i. The fund's principal revenue is the payroll tax on wages and self-employment income, a dedicated levy whose proceeds the Treasury credits to the trust fund rather than to the general fund. The statute confines the fund's spending to the authorized purposes of Part A: inpatient hospital care, skilled nursing facility care, hospice, and certain home health services, plus the costs of administering those benefits. Trust fund accounting gives these restrictions teeth. The fund's balances are held as special Treasury securities that earn interest, the interest is credited back to the fund, and disbursements can be made only for the purposes Congress specified. When analysts speak of the trust fund's solvency, they mean the point at which its accumulated assets and incoming dedicated revenue can no longer cover full benefit payments on schedule. Under the statute in force in 2011, that measurement, and the annual report in which the programs' trustees publish it, supplies the legal vocabulary in which every debate about Part A's fiscal future is conducted.

The payroll tax that feeds the Hospital Insurance fund differs from the Social Security payroll tax in one structural respect: it applies to all covered wages and self-employment income with no annual cap. High earners therefore contribute on every dollar, while the benefit they can draw is limited by the program's coverage rules rather than by their contributions. The tax rate itself is fixed by statute, which means the fund's income side can only change when Congress amends the law. That rigidity is deliberate. Dedicated financing was designed to keep Part A self-supporting from its own revenue stream, and the trust fund's accounting is the legal mechanism that enforces the design: money in, benefits out, with the balance carried forward in securities.

The Federal Supplementary Medical Insurance Trust Fund at 42 U.S.C. 1395t finances Parts B and D on a different model. Its revenue comes from two streams: monthly premiums paid by beneficiaries and contributions from the federal government's general revenue. The statute sets the standard Part B premium each year by a formula tied to the program's projected per-beneficiary cost, so that premiums finance a defined share of expected spending and general revenue automatically supplies the remainder. Part D premiums follow a comparable structure through plan bids. Because general revenue fills whatever gap premiums leave, the Supplementary Medical Insurance fund cannot fail in the way the Hospital Insurance fund can; shortfalls are absorbed by larger automatic transfers from the Treasury. That design makes Parts B and D fiscally open-ended from the Treasury's perspective even though each year's premium is fixed by formula.

The premium side of the Supplementary Medical Insurance fund carries its own refinements. Beneficiaries who enroll late face a permanent premium surcharge, a statutory incentive to join when first eligible rather than waiting until care is needed. Higher-income beneficiaries pay an income-related monthly adjustment amount on top of the standard Part B premium, and a parallel adjustment applies to Part D, so that the premium finances a larger share of program cost for those enrollees. These adjustments keep the basic bargain intact, a uniform benefit package financed by a mix of enrollee contributions and general revenue, while varying the enrollee's share with income and enrollment timing.

The Board of Trustees, whose members include the Secretaries of the Treasury, Labor, and Health and Human Services and the Commissioner of Social Security alongside public trustees, reports to Congress each year on the financial condition of both funds. The report projects income and outgo over short and long horizons and measures the Hospital Insurance fund's projected depletion date under intermediate assumptions. The 2003 amendments added a further trigger: when the trustees project that general revenue will finance more than 45 percent of total Medicare outlays within the reporting window, the statute requires them to issue a Medicare funding warning, a formal finding that Congress and the President are expected to address under existing procedures. The warning changes no payment by itself, but it creates a recurring legislative moment in which Medicare's financing, rather than its benefits, becomes the subject of debate.

A word is in order about what the trust funds actually hold. Their assets are special-issue Treasury securities, instruments available only to the funds themselves, which represent the government's promise to make the money available when benefits come due. When the Treasury redeems those securities to pay hospital claims, it must raise the cash from current taxes, borrowing, or other revenue, just as it does for any other obligation. The trust fund structure therefore does not set money aside in a vault; it creates a legal claim on future government resources and a set of accounting rules that determine when that claim may be exercised. Understanding this is what separates the legal meaning of solvency, the date the fund can no longer pay full benefits on schedule, from looser talk about the program going bankrupt.

The legal consequence of dedicated financing is that Congress manages Medicare's cost principally through payment regulation rather than through appropriations battles. Because benefits are an entitlement funded outside the annual appropriations process, the pressure point is the set of rules that translate services into dollars: the prospective payment systems that fix hospital payment by diagnosis group, the fee schedules that price physician services, the benchmarks that set payments to Medicare Advantage plans, and the bidding system that prices Part D plans. The physician fee schedule illustrates the mechanism. The statute's sustainable growth rate formula set annual updates for physician payment, and when the formula called for reductions, Congress faced the choice of letting the cuts take effect or passing legislation to override them. The fight therefore took place inside the payment rules themselves, not in a debate over whether to fund the program at all. The original move in this direction came in 1983, when Congress replaced cost-based reimbursement for inpatient hospital care with diagnosis-related groups, fixed prices per admission that shifted the financial risk of long stays from the trust fund to the hospital. Every subsequent payment system has repeated the pattern: define the unit of payment by law or regulation, and let fiscal policy operate through the price.

Medicaid has no trust fund, no dedicated tax, and no capped appropriation. Instead, the statute promises the federal government will reimburse a fixed share of every qualifying dollar a state spends. The share is the Federal Medical Assistance Percentage, defined at 42 U.S.C. 1396d(b), and the payment obligation sits at 42 U.S.C. 1396b. The FMAP formula starts at 100 percent and subtracts 45 percent multiplied by the square of the ratio of the state's per capita income to the national per capita income, using three-year averages of Bureau of Economic Analysis data. Because the ratio is squared, poorer states draw a match well above the simple income ratio, and the statute then clamps the result between a floor of 50 percent and a ceiling of 83 percent, so that even the wealthiest state receives at least half of its qualifying costs from Washington and no state receives more than 83 percent. In practice the federal share has ranged from the 50 percent floor in the highest-income states to the mid-70s in the lowest-income states. Administrative costs are generally matched at 50 percent regardless of the FMAP, with higher statutory rates for specified functions such as mechanized claims systems and family planning services.

States assemble their share of the spending from their own treasuries, and the statute polices the sources they may use. General revenues are the ordinary base, but states may also rely on provider assessments and transfers from local governments, subject to federal rules that bar arrangements guaranteeing providers they will be held harmless for the tax. These financing rules matter because they define the real state contribution: the match rewards only genuine state spending, not money that circles back to the providers who paid it. Medicaid's financing is also countercyclical by construction. Enrollment climbs when the economy falters and state revenues fall, so the same downturn that swells program costs shrinks the treasuries that must meet them. Congress has responded during recessions by temporarily raising the FMAP, giving states a larger federal share precisely when their own resources are thinnest, and each such increase has required its own legislation with its own expiration date.

The open-ended match creates a distinctive legal obligation: the federal government must pay its FMAP share of every expenditure that qualifies under the approved state plan, with no statutory ceiling on the total. A state that expands its program within the plan's terms expands the federal liability automatically; a state that trims optional benefits or provider rates reduces it. The lever is therefore not a dollar appropriation but the definition of qualifying spending. Congress controls federal Medicaid cost by changing what counts: tightening or loosening eligibility categories, adding or removing mandatory services, adjusting the FMAP itself, or attaching conditions to waiver approvals. The state controls its own share of cost by designing benefits above the statutory floor, setting provider payment rates, and choosing how much of the optional territory in the statute to occupy.

When fiscal pressure mounts, the two programs therefore transmit it through different channels, and the legal vocabulary of the fight differs accordingly. Medicare battles are fought in the language of payment policy: hospital update factors, physician fee schedule conversion factors, the sustainable growth rate formula's scheduled reductions, Medicare Advantage benchmarks, and Part D premium bids. The trustees' annual report supplies the scoreboard, and the Medicare funding warning supplies the procedural prod. Medicaid battles are fought in the language of the match: the FMAP formula itself, temporary adjustments to the match enacted during economic downturns, for example in the 2009 stimulus legislation that raised FMAP rates for a limited period, and the continuing debate over whether eligibility should be broadened or narrowed. States, for their part, enter these fights with an instrument that has no Medicare counterpart: because they administer their own programs, states can reshape benefits and payment rates within federal bounds and thereby change both sides of the match at once, a power no Medicare contractor holds.

One last structural contrast belongs here. Medicare's administrative costs, the agency's payroll, the contractors' fees, the cost of running the appeals system, are paid out of the trust funds themselves, which keeps the program's operating budget inside its dedicated financing. Medicaid's federal share, by contrast, comes from general revenue through an appropriation that rises automatically with qualifying state spending; Congress does not vote a fixed Medicaid budget each year and then ration it among the states. The appropriation follows the entitlement rather than preceding it. That is why a Medicaid budget fight is about the rules that decide which claims the appropriation must cover, not about whether the appropriation will stretch to cover them.

Financing thus reinforces administration. Medicare's federal operation is funded by federal trust funds fed by dedicated taxes, premiums, and general revenue, and its fiscal politics play out in national payment rules. Medicaid's state operation is funded by state appropriations that draw an uncapped federal match, and its fiscal politics play out in the match rate and the definition of qualifying spending. With administration and financing mapped, the comparison can turn to the beneficiary's own question: which program's door opens for which person, and what follows when the answer turns out to be both. The financing axis also explains why the programs' political debates never quite merge. Proposals to change Medicare are argued in terms of premiums, payroll taxes, and payment rates; proposals to change Medicaid are argued in terms of the federal share, state flexibility, and the scope of the entitlement. Each debate is conducted in the vocabulary its financing created.

### Why does a federal budget fight look different in each program?

Medicare's fight runs through payment rules, because dedicated trust funds and entitlement benefits leave the payment formulas as the adjustable part. Medicaid's fight runs through the match rate and the definition of qualifying spending, because the federal obligation follows automatically from state outlays. Different machinery, different vocabulary, same fiscal pressure.

## Axis 4: Uniformity

Medicare is one program. Title XVIII establishes a single national benefit package for Parts A and B, and federal statute and regulation fix the eligibility rules, the covered services, the deductibles and coinsurance, and the payment methods. Those rules apply identically in every state, the District of Columbia, and the territories. A beneficiary entitled to hospital insurance under Part A carries the same entitlement in Florida and in Oregon, and a physician service covered under Part B in Maine is covered under Part B in Arizona. The contractors that process claims may differ by region, but the coverage rule they apply does not.

Eligibility itself is national and formula-driven. A person qualifies for premium-free Part A at age sixty-five on the basis of sufficient quarters of covered work, or earlier through twenty-four months of Social Security disability benefits or end-stage renal disease. Enrollment is automatic for those already receiving Social Security. No state official exercises discretion over these thresholds, and no state legislature can narrow or widen them. The door to Medicare opens the same way everywhere because Congress, not the states, holds the key.

Payment follows the same national logic. Congress replaced cost-based reimbursement with prospective payment systems and fee schedules set by federal formula: the inpatient prospective payment system for hospitals, the resource-based relative value scale for physicians, and comparable national schedules across service lines. Local inputs such as wage indices adjust the arithmetic, but the methodology is federal and uniform. A hospital in Texas and a hospital in Vermont are paid under the same statutory formula, and a coverage dispute about whether a service was reasonable and necessary is decided under the same national standard.

National coverage determinations bind every contractor in the country, and where no national determination exists, local coverage determinations fill the gap inside the federal framework rather than rewriting it. CMS manuals and rulings interpret the statute; they do not create fifty versions of it. The practical result is portability. A beneficiary who moves across state lines changes addresses and perhaps contractors, but the benefit package travels intact. Nothing in the move requires a new eligibility determination or a new coverage decision.

For lawyers, uniformity shortens the research path and stabilizes the answer. The operative rule for a Medicare question lives in the Social Security Act, the Code of Federal Regulations, and agency guidance, and two attorneys in different states who read the same provision reach the same conclusion. Advice given in one jurisdiction holds in another because the law being interpreted is the same law. A coverage memorandum written for a client in Ohio can be reused for a client in Nevada with only the facts changed.

Medicaid inverts this picture. Title XIX creates a federal-state cooperative program: the federal statute sets mandatory floors, including the groups a state must cover, the services it must furnish to the categorically needy, and the federal matching formula, and each state builds its program on top. States choose optional eligibility groups and optional services, set provider payment rates within federal limits, decide whether to adopt the medically needy program under 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(10)(C), and design delivery systems that range from fee-for-service to extensive managed care. Most states adopted the medically needy option, but not all, and the income levels, resource limits, and covered services attached to it differ where it exists.

The variation reaches eligibility itself. In 2011, a nondisabled adult without dependent children qualified for Medicaid in only a few states, while parents, pregnant women, children, the elderly, and people with disabilities faced income thresholds that differed state by state. Section 209(b) of the 1972 amendments even lets a state use eligibility criteria more restrictive than the federal Supplemental Security Income standard, so two people with identical disabilities and identical finances can face different Medicaid outcomes on opposite sides of a state line. Benefit packages diverge just as sharply: dental care, eyeglasses, personal care services, and prescription drug coverage are optional benefits that some states furnish generously and others barely at all.

States also reshape their programs without Congress. A state plan amendment lets a state change eligibility, services, or payment methods with federal approval, and waivers let it go further: section 1915(c) waivers permit home and community-based services while waiving comparability and statewideness, and section 1115 demonstration waivers let the Secretary waive provisions of section 1902 itself. Two neighboring states can therefore operate programs that share a name and a parent statute yet differ in who qualifies, what is covered, and what providers are paid. The federal statute is the frame; the state documents are the picture.

Portability is the casualty. Medicaid eligibility does not travel. A beneficiary who moves must apply anew, and the new state's plan, waivers, income methodologies, and optional choices decide the outcome. Coverage that was secure in one state can disappear in another even though the federal statute never changed, because the program that changed was the state's. Managed care penetration compounds the effect: a beneficiary accustomed to a mature managed care network in one state may land in a fee-for-service system in the next, with different participating providers and different prior authorization rules.

Predictability changes with it. The research path for a Medicaid question begins with the words "which state," because the dispositive rule usually lives in the approved state plan, a waiver's special terms and conditions, or state regulation. Federal law answers threshold questions, such as whether a state may do something at all. State documents answer the questions clients actually ask, such as whether this person qualifies and this service is covered. Competent Medicaid advice therefore starts with locating the right jurisdiction's documents, a step Medicare practice never requires.

Uniformity has one ragged edge worth noting. Medicare Advantage plans, private plans that contract with CMS to deliver Part A and B benefits, vary by county service area in their networks, cost-sharing, and supplemental benefits. A beneficiary who moves must enroll in a plan serving the new county. But even here the variation is plan choice inside a federal framework, not fifty sovereign programs: every plan must cover everything Parts A and B cover, bids and benefits are federally approved, and the underlying entitlement never depends on a statehouse.

Providers feel the axis in compliance costs. A hospital chain or equipment supplier faces one set of Medicare conditions of participation, one claims system, and one coverage manual nationwide, but fifty Medicaid provider agreements, fifty fee schedules, and fifty prior authorization regimes. The administrative burden of Medicaid practice scales with the number of jurisdictions served, while Medicare practice scales hardly at all.

Beneficiaries experience the same divide. Medicare publishes one national handbook, operates one national helpline, and funds State Health Insurance Assistance Programs that all answer from the same federal rulebook. Medicaid produces fifty handbooks describing fifty programs, and counseling organizations must master the local plan before they can advise. A volunteer trained in Illinois cannot reliably counsel a caller who just moved to Indiana, because the program changed at the border.

Uniformity also shapes strategy beyond individual cases. A single change to the Medicare statute or a single national coverage determination helps beneficiaries in every state at once, which is why national advocacy organizations and national provider groups concentrate their efforts in Washington. Medicaid advocacy must be fought state by state, in fifty legislatures and fifty waiver negotiations, because a victory in one capital does not travel. The axis that begins as a research convenience ends as a theory of where power sits.

This division was a design choice, not an accident. Congress modeled Medicare on social insurance administered nationally and modeled Medicaid on the earlier Kerr-Mills program of federal grants supporting state medical assistance, preserving state administration while attaching federal money and federal minimums. The consequence for practice is the axis in one line: a Medicare question asks what the federal rule says, while a Medicaid question asks which state's rule applies.

The bargain behind the divide is worth stating plainly. States accepted variation as the price of federal matching dollars and programmatic control, and the familiar laboratories metaphor is nowhere more concrete than in Medicaid waivers: an experiment approved in one state becomes the model its neighbors copy. The cost falls on practitioners and beneficiaries alike. Medicaid expertise is jurisdiction-specific in a way Medicare expertise is not; a specialist who relocates must relearn the program, and a family that moves must requalify for it. Uniformity is not just a research convenience. It determines whose knowledge transfers and whose coverage survives a change of address.

### Why do lawyers answer a Medicaid question with another question?

Because Medicaid operates as more than fifty state programs under one federal statute, the rule that decides eligibility, covered services, or payment almost always lives in a state plan, a waiver, or state regulation. Naming the state identifies the body of law that actually governs the case, and without it no reliable answer is possible.

## Axis 5: Long-Term Care

The sharpest practical difference between the two programs appears at the nursing home door, and it is written into the Medicare statute. Section 1862(a)(9) of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395y(a)(9), provides that no payment may be made under Part A or Part B for expenses incurred for custodial care, with an exception only for hospice care as otherwise permitted. Medicare's coverage rules [the statutory text of Medicare Parts A and B](/2010/12/01/medicare-parts-a-b-statute-explained/) draw a hard legal line between skilled care and custodial care, and only the skilled side is covered. Custodial care means help with the activities of daily living, such as bathing, dressing, eating, and supervision, that does not require the skills of medical or technical personnel. When the need is for that kind of help, Medicare does not pay, however real the need or heavy the cost.

What Medicare does pay for is narrow and conditional. It covers post-hospital extended care in a skilled nursing facility only after a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least three days, only when the beneficiary requires skilled nursing or skilled rehabilitation for a condition treated during that hospital stay, and only for up to one hundred days per benefit period, with beneficiary cost-sharing after the twentieth day. Coverage ends when the skilled need ends, when the services are no longer reasonable and necessary, or when the day limit is reached. A benefit period itself is a statutory construct: it begins with hospital admission and ends after sixty consecutive days without inpatient hospital or skilled nursing facility care, at which point a new benefit period, and a new one-hundred-day clock, can begin.

Home health coverage is similarly fenced. Medicare pays only when the beneficiary is confined to the home, needs intermittent skilled nursing care or physical, speech, or occupational therapy, and receives services under a plan of care established and periodically reviewed by a physician. Custodial home aides without a skilled component do not qualify. Hospice is the one setting where custodial-type services remain covered, because the statute expressly permits it: a beneficiary who elects hospice and is certified as terminally ill receives palliative care, including aide and homemaker services and respite care, that would otherwise fall under the exclusion.

The gap this creates is large. Dementia, frailty, and chronic dependence on help with daily activities are custodial needs as the statute defines them, and they are also the most common reasons older people enter nursing facilities for long stays. Families often discover the exclusion at hospital discharge, when a parent who can no longer live safely at home is found to need the very kind of care Medicare will not buy. A short skilled stay may be covered; the months and years that follow are not. Discharge planners deliver this news routinely, and elder law practices are built around the shock it produces.

Medicaid is the program that pays for those months and years. Nursing facility services are a mandatory benefit for the categorically needy under Title XIX, and states cover intermediate care facilities and, through state options and section 1915(c) waivers, home and community-based services that keep people out of institutions. Section 1915(c) waivers let states target services to specific populations, cap enrollment, and maintain waiting lists, which is why waiver slots are prized and scarce in many states. Measured by dollars and by bed-days, Medicaid is the principal payer of long-term care in the United States. That fact reorders the planning conversation: every question about how a family affords an extended nursing home stay, protects a spouse who remains at home, or qualifies a parent with modest resources is a Medicaid question, because Medicaid is the program whose rules decide the answer.

Those rules begin with financial eligibility, and here the statute surprises people who assume Medicaid serves only the poor. Under the medically needy option at 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(10)(C), a state may cover individuals whose income or resources exceed the ordinary limits once their incurred medical expenses are subtracted, a process known as spend-down. The mechanics are arithmetic, not moral: the state sets a protected income level, the applicant's countable income above that level is measured against incurred medical bills, and once the bills absorb the excess, eligibility follows. A person of comfortable means facing nursing facility bills can therefore become Medicaid-eligible by spending income and resources down to the state's protected levels. Stated plainly, Medicaid covers many people who are not poor, because in medically needy states eligibility turns on countable income and resources after medical costs, not on poverty as such.

Congress paired that doorway with gates. Transfers of assets for less than fair market value before applying trigger a penalty period of ineligibility for long-term care services, computed by dividing the transferred amount by the state's average monthly private-pay nursing facility rate. The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 lengthened the look-back period for such transfers to sixty months, moved the start of the penalty period to the date the individual would otherwise be eligible for Medicaid, and tightened the treatment of annuities, promissory notes, and life estates. Section 1917(b) of the Act, 42 U.S.C. 1396p(b), separately requires states to recover the cost of certain Medicaid benefits, including nursing facility services, from the estates of deceased beneficiaries, with hardship waivers as the limited safety valve. Section 1917(a) even permits pre-death liens in narrow circumstances. Planning around these provisions, lawful and common, belongs entirely to Medicaid practice.

The trust rules complete the picture. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 added section 1917(d), 42 U.S.C. 1396p(d), which generally counts trust assets as available resources, then carves out precise exceptions that elder law attorneys use daily. A special needs trust under subsection (d)(4)(A), established for a disabled individual under sixty-five by a parent, grandparent, guardian, or court and containing a payback to the state on the beneficiary's death, shelters assets without destroying eligibility. A pooled trust under subsection (d)(4)(C), managed by a nonprofit association for disabled beneficiaries, does the same with a modified payback. In states that cap income eligibility, a qualified income trust under subsection (d)(4)(B), the so-called Miller trust, channels excess income into Medicaid eligibility for nursing facility care. Every one of these instruments is a creature of Title XIX. No counterpart exists in Medicare, because Medicare has no asset test for its benefits and therefore no use for asset planning.

The community side of long-term care has its own Medicaid architecture. In Olmstead v. L.C., decided in 1999, the Supreme Court held that unjustified institutional isolation of people with disabilities can violate Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act, pushing states to rebalance their programs from nursing facilities toward home and community-based services. Congress reinforced the shift in the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 by adding section 1915(i), a state plan option for home and community-based services that requires no waiver, though states may cap enrollment, and by funding the Money Follows the Person demonstration to help institutionalized beneficiaries return to the community. The result is a Medicaid long-term care system with two doors, institutional and community-based, each governed by state choices layered over federal options.

The spousal protections belong there too. Section 1924 of the Social Security Act, 42 U.S.C. 1396r-5, added by section 303 of the Medicare Catastrophic Coverage Act of 1988, stops the institutionalization of one spouse from impoverishing the spouse who remains in the community. When one spouse enters a nursing facility, the statute attributes income by the name-on-the-check rule, sets aside a protected share of the couple's countable resources for the community spouse within a federally set floor and ceiling, and guarantees the community spouse a minimum monthly maintenance needs allowance, with a shelter component for high housing costs, drawn in part from the institutionalized spouse's income when the community spouse's own income falls short. A fair hearing can raise the allowance where exceptional circumstances demand it, and the Supreme Court in Wisconsin Department of Health and Family Services v. Blumer sustained the income-first method many states use to meet it. The institutionalized spouse's eligibility is then determined without counting the protected amounts. None of this machinery exists in Medicare, because Medicare never faced the problem: a program that does not pay for custodial long-term care needs no rules for dividing a couple's resources to pay for it.

Dual eligibility illustrates the boundary in a single beneficiary. Many nursing facility residents qualify for both programs at once, and the division of labor follows the axes exactly. Medicare pays first for the days it covers, the limited skilled nursing facility benefit after a qualifying hospital stay, while Medicaid pays for the custodial days Medicare excludes and wraps around Medicare's cost-sharing. The two cards in one wallet do not merge the programs; they mark the line between them.

In practice the line is drawn claim by claim. A physician's order sending a patient to a nursing facility does not make the stay a Medicare stay; contractors review the medical record for documented skilled need, and the physician's certification of that need is necessary but not sufficient. When contractors deny payment on the ground that the care was custodial, beneficiaries appeal through the Medicare administrative process, and administrative law judges decide, on records full of nursing notes and therapy logs, whether skilled personnel were actually required. Families lose these disputes with regularity, because the legal question is not whether the patient needed help but whether the help needed had to be skilled. That distinction, technical and unforgiving, is the daily working form of section 1395y(a)(9).

For families the axes converge into a decision tree. First, determine whether a skilled need exists that Medicare will cover and for how long, because those days are paid without regard to assets. Second, when the need turns custodial, turn to Medicaid: establish eligibility through spend-down or the applicable income pathway, examine the timing of any asset transfers against the sixty-month look-back, and invoke the spousal impoverishment protections before the community spouse's resources are committed. Third, consider the trust instruments Congress provided for disabled beneficiaries and the estate recovery rules that will follow. Each step belongs to a different title of the same Act, and mistaking which title governs is the most expensive error a family can make.

### Does Medicare pay for a long-term nursing home stay?

No. Section 1395y(a)(9) bars payment under Parts A and B for custodial care, so Medicare covers skilled nursing facility care only after a qualifying hospital stay, for a limited number of days, and while skilled need continues. Once the need becomes custodial, Medicare coverage ends and Medicaid, the principal payer of long-term care, is the program that pays.

## The Six-Axis Comparison Table

The axes above reduce to a single working method: identify the program, then apply its logic. Medicare rewards federal research because the federal rule decides the case; Medicaid rewards state research because the state plan, the waiver's special terms, or state regulation decides it. A practitioner who internalizes that distinction will look in the right place first, which is most of the battle in benefits practice. The table collects all six axes, including the litigation route summarized in the final row, so the full comparison can be consulted at a glance whenever a new fact pattern raises the threshold question of which program's logic governs. Used as a triage tool, it tells the practitioner where to look first: the federal statute and its regulations for Medicare, the state plan and its waivers for Medicaid, and the calendar for everything long-term care touches. It is the article in miniature: two programs born in the same 1965 Act, six places where their designs diverge, and one habit, naming the program before applying its logic, that prevents the most common mistakes in this field.

## Axis 6: Litigation Posture

A denied Medicare claim does not begin in court. The challenger enters an administrative appeal ladder built into the statute itself. First comes redetermination by the Medicare contractor that issued the initial decision, a paper review of the same file by the same organization. Next comes reconsideration by a qualified independent contractor, a fresh look by a different decision maker with no stake in the first outcome. Then a hearing before an administrative law judge, the first point at which the dispute resembles a trial, with testimony, evidence, and a reasoned decision. Then review by the Medicare Appeals Council, which may grant or decline a further look. Only after that sequence has run its course does judicial review in federal court become available. Each rung carries its own filing deadline, and missing one usually ends the climb. The order is compulsory rather than advisory. A beneficiary or provider who files in federal court before exhausting the ladder will be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, not heard on the merits. That rigidity is deliberate. Congress funneled these disputes through agency hands first so that specialized adjudicators, rather than generalist judges, would assemble the record and correct routine errors before the courts were ever involved.

The jurisdictional fence around the ladder comes from 42 U.S.C. 405(h), a Social Security Act provision that bars actions brought under the federal-question statute, 28 U.S.C. 1331, to recover on any claim arising under the Act. Congress carried that provision into Medicare by incorporating it through 42 U.S.C. 1395ii. In Weinberger v. Salfi, decided in 1975, the Supreme Court read 405(h) to strip federal-question jurisdiction over claims arising under the statute, holding that the provision did more than codify exhaustion of remedies: it withdrew the 1331 jurisdictional grant itself, so that even constitutional attacks had to travel through the Act's own review channels. The claim in Salfi arose under Title II, but the channeling principle the Court announced became the template for Medicare disputes. In Heckler v. Ringer, decided in 1984, the Court applied the template directly. The Ringer plaintiffs challenged the Secretary's policy on bilateral carotid body resection alongside their individual benefit denials, hoping that a procedural label would open the courthouse door. The Court held that all aspects of the challenge arose under the Medicare Act, that attacks on the Secretary's procedures were inextricably intertwined with claims for benefits, and that exhaustion was not futile merely because the agency had announced its position in advance. Section 405(g), with its demand that a claim first be presented to the Secretary and that administrative remedies then be exhausted, stood as the only avenue for judicial review. In Shalala v. Illinois Council on Long Term Care, decided in 2000, the Court confirmed the point once more, holding that 405(h), as incorporated by 1395ii, barred a federal-question suit challenging Medicare nursing-home enforcement regulations.

The doctrine that emerges is functional rather than formal. A claim arises under the Medicare Act when its standing and its substantive basis come from the Act, and courts refuse to reward artful pleading that dresses a benefits dispute in different jurisdictional clothing. The Ringer opinion drew a further distinction that every litigator must respect: presenting the claim to the Secretary is a nonwaivable prerequisite that only the agency process can satisfy, while exhausting the remaining administrative steps is enforced as a firm rule in the ordinary case, with only vanishingly narrow departures recognized. The safe assumption for any challenger is that the ladder must be climbed to its top. Even denials that begin with a contractor applying a national coverage determination travel the Medicare appeals process, [how CMS turns Medicare law into rules and denials](/2011/02/15/cms-medicare-rulemaking-guide/), rung by rung, before any judge sees them. When review finally arrives, it arrives on the administrative record the agency built, not as a fresh trial. The defendant throughout is the United States, sued through the Secretary. The forum is fixed, the clock is the agency's calendar of appeal deadlines and amount-in-controversy thresholds set by regulation, and relief takes the form of a corrected agency decision and an order to pay. The design reflects a legislative judgment that uniformity matters more than speed: one national program, one national set of coverage rules, and one national pipeline for correcting mistakes.

Medicaid disputes enter through a different door entirely, and the difference flows from the program's architecture. Because Medicaid is a federal-state cooperative program administered by the states, the typical defendant is not the United States but a state official: the state Medicaid director, the administering agency, or its contractors. Two doctrinal paths carry these cases into court. The first is the Ex parte Young action, named for the 1908 decision that permits suits against state officers in their official capacities seeking prospective injunctive relief to halt an ongoing violation of federal law. The device threads a constitutional needle: the state itself cannot be sued for damages without its consent, but its officers can be ordered to conform their future conduct to federal requirements. The second path is 42 U.S.C. 1983, the Reconstruction-era statute that authorizes damages and equitable relief against anyone who, acting under color of state law, deprives another of rights secured by the Constitution and laws of the United States. In Maine v. Thiboutot, decided in 1980, the Supreme Court held that the phrase and laws in 1983 means what it says. The statute reaches purely statutory violations of federal law, including rights created by the Social Security Act, and a family deprived of welfare benefits by a state's mistaken reading of federal law could sue the responsible state officials. Prevailing plaintiffs can also recover attorney's fees, which made 1983 the engine of private enforcement for cooperative-program statutes. Medicaid litigation has traveled that route ever since, because the federal government's ultimate sanction, the withholding of matching funds, is a blunt instrument that punishes beneficiaries along with the state and is therefore deployed sparingly.

The route, however, is not open to every grievance, and the boundary between enforceable and unenforceable provisions has been heavily litigated. Section 1983 protects rights, not every federal command, and the threshold question in each case is whether the provision invoked creates an individual right at all. In Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, decided in 1990, the Court held that the Boren Amendment, which required states to pay hospitals rates the state found reasonable and adequate to meet the costs of efficiently operated facilities, created a right enforceable by providers in a 1983 action for declaratory and injunctive relief. The Court also rejected the argument that available state administrative procedures for payment disputes foreclosed resort to 1983, since those procedures did not allow providers to challenge the overall rate-setting method and Congress had not built a comprehensive enforcement scheme incompatible with private suits. Twelve years later, Gonzaga University v. Doe narrowed the lens. The Court held that only a statutory provision containing rights-creating language that unambiguously confers an individual right, phrased in terms of the persons benefited, is presumptively enforceable through 1983. Under the framework the cases together establish, a court asks whether Congress intended the provision to benefit the plaintiff as an individual, whether the asserted right is stated in terms specific enough for a court to enforce, and whether the obligation is couched in mandatory rather than aspirational language. A provision that speaks in aggregate or systemwide terms, directing how a state plan must be structured rather than what an individual must receive, may fail the test. The practical consequence for Medicaid litigators is a threshold inquiry that Medicare litigators never face: identify the precise provision, ask whether its language focuses on individuals, and only then decide whether a private plaintiff may sue or must instead rely on the federal government's own enforcement tools.

The forum differences produce different clocks. A Medicaid beneficiary whose claim is denied or not acted upon with reasonable promptness is entitled under 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(3) to an opportunity for a fair hearing inside the state system, an administrative safeguard that resembles the lower rungs of the Medicare ladder. But the resemblance ends there. The beneficiary is not confined to the state process the way a Medicare claimant is confined to the federal one. The 1983 and Ex parte Young routes permit direct access to federal court once the claim is framed as a violation of an enforceable federal right, and they can run alongside the fair-hearing track. Providers denied proper reimbursement face the same fork: Wilder-style suits proceed in federal court while administrative payment disputes move through state channels. The strategic consequence is that Medicaid disputes are fought on two fronts at once, in state administrative hearings and in federal court, while Medicare disputes march in single file through the agency. Under the law in force in 2011, that asymmetry was settled doctrine on both sides of the line.

The breadth of the channeling rule deserves emphasis, because it surprises even experienced litigators. In Ringer, the plaintiffs did not merely ask for payment of their surgical bills; they asked the court to invalidate the Secretary's instruction barring payment for the procedure, a challenge that looked like ordinary administrative law. The Court treated the label as irrelevant. Where the standing to sue and the substance of the grievance both derive from the Medicare Act, the claim arises under that Act, and 405(h) closes the federal-question door. The consequence is that constitutional arguments, rulemaking challenges, and coverage-policy attacks all queue in the same line as individual benefit denials. The line moves slowly, and providers with large dollar amounts at stake sometimes find the wait costly, but the uniformity Congress purchased is real: the same coverage question receives the same answer in every state, because every answer passes through the same agency.

Put side by side, the two postures are mirror images. The Medicare litigant sues the United States, climbs a single federal ladder, and reaches court only at the end, on the agency's record and the agency's timetable. The Medicaid litigant sues state officials, may choose between a state fair hearing and a federal courthouse, and reaches court at the beginning, provided the provision invoked carries the rights-creating language that Gonzaga demands. The first system prizes uniformity and agency expertise; the second prizes direct judicial enforcement against many separate administrators. For the student comparing the statutes, the litigation axis thus restates the structural axis in procedural terms: one national program produces one courthouse door, while a federal-state partnership produces many.

### If a Medicare claim and a Medicaid claim are both denied, do they reach a judge by the same route?

No. A Medicare dispute must exhaust the appeal ladder before any federal court may hear it, because 42 U.S.C. 405(h) channels every claim arising under the Medicare Act through 42 U.S.C. 405(g). A Medicaid dispute can go directly to court as a suit against state officials under 42 U.S.C. 1983 or for prospective relief under Ex parte Young.

## Dual Eligibility: Where the Two Statutes Overlap

The comparison so far has treated Medicare and Medicaid as parallel tracks, and for most beneficiaries that picture holds. But a large population rides both tracks at once. Dual eligibles are individuals who qualify for Medicare by age or disability and simultaneously qualify for Medicaid by low income and limited resources. Some hold full Medicaid benefits on top of Medicare. Others qualify only for the Medicare Savings Programs, the limited-benefit categories that pay Medicare costs without conferring full Medicaid coverage. Either way, the overlap forces the two statutes to interact, and the interaction follows a fixed order of operations.

The first rule of the overlap is that Medicare pays first. For any service that Medicare covers, the provider bills Medicare, Medicare applies its own coverage rules and cost sharing, and only then does Medicaid enter the picture. Medicaid acts as a supplement rather than a substitute: it pays the Medicare premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance that the beneficiary would otherwise owe, within the limits of what the state plan allows. This wraparound function is why the programs can coexist without duplicating payment. The federal government funds the acute-care coverage through Medicare, and the joint federal-state program absorbs the cost sharing that would otherwise fall on people with the least ability to pay.

The second rule is that Medicaid fills the gaps Medicare leaves by design. The most consequential gap is long-term care. Medicare pays only for short, skilled spells of nursing-facility care following a hospital stay, and it pays nothing at all for the custodial nursing-home stays that stretch across months and years. Medicaid, by contrast, is the principal public payer for nursing-home care in the United States, covering the long custodial stays that Medicare excludes. For a dual eligible who needs months of nursing-home care, the practical sequence is therefore Medicare for the hospital and any covered skilled spell, then Medicaid for the long stay that follows. The same gap-filling logic extends to services such as dental care, eyeglasses, and hearing aids, which Medicare largely omits and which state Medicaid plans may cover. Dual eligibility is, in this sense, the place where the two statutes complete each other: Medicare supplies the acute-care foundation, and Medicaid supplies the long-term and supplemental coverage that the foundation does not reach.

Congress built a dedicated bridge between the programs for beneficiaries whose incomes are too high for full Medicaid but too low to absorb Medicare's premiums and cost sharing. The Medicare Savings Programs are mandatory Medicaid eligibility groups, administered by the states under Title XIX, that buy low-income Medicare beneficiaries into fuller protection. Three categories carry most of the weight, and each has its own statutory home. The Qualified Medicare Beneficiary group, established at 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(10)(E)(i), covers individuals with incomes at or below the federal poverty level and pays their Medicare premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance. Congress first created QMB as an optional state benefit in the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1986 and then made it a mandatory Medicaid category through the Medicare Catastrophic Coverage Act of 1988, effective at the start of 1989. The Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary group, at 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(10)(E)(iii), covers individuals with incomes above the poverty level but at or below 120 percent of it, and pays only the Part B premium. Congress added SLMB in the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990, with coverage effective at the start of 1993. The Qualifying Individual group, at 42 U.S.C. 1396a(a)(10)(E)(iv), covers individuals with incomes above 120 percent but below 135 percent of the poverty level, and likewise pays the Part B premium. Congress added QI in the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, with coverage effective in 1998. The definition of what these groups receive, the term Medicare cost-sharing, sits at 42 U.S.C. 1396d(p) and encompasses premiums, deductibles, coinsurance, and related charges.

The three categories differ in more than income bands. QMB is the most protective: because the state pays the beneficiary's cost sharing, federal law bars providers from billing the beneficiary for the balance, so a QMB enrollee faces no legal liability for Medicare deductibles or coinsurance on covered services. SLMB and QI are narrower by design, paying the Part B premium and nothing else, which leaves the beneficiary responsible for deductibles and coinsurance out of pocket. QI carries a further structural quirk: Congress funded it through a capped federal allotment rather than open-ended matching funds, so states distribute QI on a first-come, first-served basis and the category is unavailable to anyone who already receives other Medicaid benefits. These distinctions matter because they determine what dual eligibility actually feels like for the person holding the card. A full-benefit dual eligible with QMB status experiences Medicare with almost no out-of-pocket cost; a QI enrollee experiences Medicare with the premium handled and the rest left to the household budget.

Prescription drugs illustrate the same division of labor in a third setting. Dual eligibles receive their drug coverage through Medicare Part D rather than through Medicaid, and the low-income subsidy pays their Part D premiums and holds their drug cost sharing to nominal levels. Here again the statutes split the job instead of duplicating it: Title XVIII supplies the drug benefit, and the subsidy mechanism, funded outside the state plans, keeps it affordable for beneficiaries the states have identified as eligible. The pattern repeats everywhere the two programs touch. Medicare defines the benefit and pays first; Medicaid, through the savings programs and its own wraparound coverage, absorbs the costs that would otherwise keep low-income beneficiaries from using the benefit at all.

For the comparison this article draws, the dual-eligible population is the proof that the wrong question misleads. Asking whether Medicare or Medicaid is the better program misses the beneficiaries for whom the answer is both, in a fixed sequence, under two different statutes, enforced through two different litigation postures. The Medicare Savings Programs show Congress legislating the overlap on purpose: rather than merging the titles, it used Title XIX dollars to reinforce Title XVIII coverage, preserving each program's separate rules, separate administrators, and separate courthouse doors while stitching them together at the point of payment.

The bridge operates through a mechanism known as buy-in. Under agreements between the states and the Secretary, the state Medicaid agency identifies residents who qualify for a Medicare Savings Program and pays their Part B premiums directly to the federal government, maintaining their Part B enrollment without requiring the beneficiary to handle the premium. The beneficiary applies through the state Medicaid agency, not through the Social Security Administration, and the state applies income and resource counting rules modeled on the Supplemental Security Income program. This administrative detail carries a substantive lesson: the Medicare Savings Programs are creatures of Title XIX even though every dollar they spend purchases Title XVIII coverage. The state determines eligibility, the state pays, and the federal Medicare program receives the enrollee.

The programs are also a study in the difference between entitlement on paper and coverage in practice. Because the Medicare Savings Programs require an application through the state Medicaid agency, and because many eligible beneficiaries do not know the programs exist, enrollment has long lagged behind eligibility, a gap the Government Accountability Office flagged when it called for further outreach and administrative simplification. Beneficiaries who qualify for full Medicaid are typically evaluated for the savings programs as part of their Medicaid determination, but beneficiaries who qualify only for QMB, SLMB, or QI must apply and be found eligible on that separate track. The result is a persistent distance between the population Congress made eligible and the population actually receiving the benefit.

### Does Medicaid replace Medicare for people who qualify for both?

No. Medicare remains the primary payer for the services it covers, and providers must bill Medicare before Medicaid. Medicaid acts as a supplement: it pays Medicare premiums and cost sharing for qualifying dual eligibles and covers services Medicare largely omits, most notably long-term nursing-home care.

## The Verdict: Which Program to Learn First

Six axes of comparison now sit on the table: the statutory homes in Title XVIII and Title XIX, the federal and federal-state structures, the eligibility logics of age and disability versus need, the benefit designs of defined insurance versus comprehensive assistance, the administrative machinery of one national contractor system versus fifty state plans, and the litigation postures of a single federal ladder versus many courthouse doors. The question that remains is the practical one with which this article began: which program should a reader learn first. Both orders of study can be defended, and each defense is worth stating at full strength before a verdict is rendered.

### The Case for Learning Medicare First

The argument for Medicare first rests on simplicity and personal relevance. Medicare runs on one national rulebook. Its eligibility turns on work history, age, or disability rather than on income tests that vary by household composition. Its benefits are defined in federal statute and regulation, its contractors operate under national instructions, and its disputes follow a single appeal ladder to a single defendant. A student who learns Medicare first acquires a complete, self-contained system with no state-by-state footnotes. There is also the matter of personal encounter. Nearly every reader of this series will deal with Medicare directly, whether through their own enrollment, a parent's coverage decisions, or an employer's coordination of benefits. Learning first the program one will personally navigate is a defensible allocation of study time. And because Medicare's structure is the simpler of the two, it supplies the vocabulary, acute, hospital insurance, supplementary medical insurance, cost sharing, coverage determination, that makes the more complex program legible when the student turns to it.

### The Case for Learning Medicaid First

The argument for Medicaid first rests on complexity and payoff. Medicaid is structurally the harder program: a federal floor with state variation above it, eligibility categories that multiply across mandatory and optional groups, benefits that differ by state plan, and litigation that can begin in a state fair hearing or a federal courthouse. A student who masters Medicaid first has done the harder intellectual work upfront, and Medicare's single rulebook then feels straightforward by comparison. There is also the matter of where the hardest questions live. Medicaid is the principal public payer for long-term care, the question families ask most urgently and the area where Medicare's silence is loudest. State variation means that learning Medicaid pays off across a wider range of questions, because each new question requires checking the state plan rather than reciting a national rule. For a reader whose work touches poverty law, disability advocacy, or elder care, Medicaid is the program that answers the questions clients actually bring through the door.

### The Deciding Factor: The Statutory Home of the Question

Both cases are strong, which is why the verdict does not declare a universal winner. The deciding factor is narrower and more useful: the statutory home of the question. Learn first the program that answers the question you actually have. If the question concerns a federal payment rule, a coverage determination, or a dispute with a Medicare contractor, that question lives in Title XVIII, and Medicare should be learned first, because its single ladder and single rulebook will resolve it. If the question concerns state coverage of a service, eligibility for long-term care, or the interaction of low income with health coverage, that question lives in Title XIX, and Medicaid should be learned first, because only the state plan and its federal floor contain the answer. If the question concerns the overlap itself, premiums a low-income beneficiary cannot afford or nursing-home care after the skilled spell ends, then the dual-eligibility bridge is the place to start, and the order of study follows the order of payment: Medicare first as primary payer, Medicaid second as supplement. This factor decides without ranking the programs against each other. Neither statute is better designed than the other; they are designed for different jobs, and the right first step depends on which job the reader needs done.

Two examples show the factor at work. Suppose a reader's father receives a notice that his Medicare contractor has denied coverage for a course of physical therapy. The dispute concerns a federal coverage rule applied by a federal contractor, the defendant would be the Secretary, and the remedy runs through the administrative ladder. The statutory home of that question is Title XVIII, so Medicare comes first, and the appeals material in this series becomes the next stop. Suppose instead that a reader's mother, after a stroke, needs nursing-home care that will last beyond any skilled spell, and the family wants to know what the state will cover and what it will count as income. That question lives in the state plan under Title XIX, so Medicaid comes first, and the fair-hearing and state-plan material takes priority. Neither reader needs the full six-axis table memorized before starting; each needs the title that holds the answer, and the table then serves as the map for everything learned after.

For the reader who arrives with no specific question at all, the factor still points somewhere. Most readers will meet Medicare first in life, at enrollment or through a parent's coverage decisions, while Medicaid tends to arrive through a crisis, a disability determination, or a nursing-home admission. Starting with the program likeliest to appear first in one's own life is a reasonable default, and the six-axis table keeps the other program in view from the beginning.

One sequencing point follows whichever order the factor recommends: study the overlap last. The dual-eligibility bridge, the buy-in mechanism, and the savings programs only make sense once both foundations are understood, because every rule in the overlap borrows its meaning from the program it modifies. A reader who learns Medicare first will grasp the savings programs as modifications to cost sharing; a reader who learns Medicaid first will grasp them as eligibility groups. Either route arrives at the same understanding, provided the bridge is crossed after both banks are mapped.

For readers deciding what to study first, [a structured path through the series](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/) lays out the suggested order across all the articles. Readers who want to work the comparison actively can [organize notes, citations, and the six-axis table on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) and [drill the comparison material on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html).

The takeaway restates the diagnosis with which this article opened. Medicare versus Medicaid is the wrong question when it is asked as a contest, because the two programs were never built to compete. They were built by the same Congress, in the same public law, to do different work under different titles, through different administrators, answerable in different courts. The right question is always the specific one: whose coverage, for what service, under which title, in which state, enforced through which door. Answer those, and the comparison answers itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Medicare and Medicaid?

Medicare is a federally administered health insurance program whose Part A hospital coverage is an earned entitlement: workers and their families qualify through payroll tax contributions, and everyone enrolled faces the same national rules regardless of where they live. Medicaid is a joint federal-state program under an approved state plan, where eligibility is categorical and financial, so income, assets, and the state of residence all matter. The statutes sit in different titles of the Social Security Act for exactly this reason: Title XVIII creates one uniform federal program, while Title XIX creates more than fifty distinct state programs sharing federal standards and federal money. Financing differs too, since Medicare draws on dedicated trust funds while Medicaid draws on an open-ended federal match of state spending.

### Q: Can you have both Medicare and Medicaid?

Yes, and the population that does is called dual eligibles. Roughly nine million Americans qualify for both programs at once, usually because they are elderly or disabled with limited income and resources. Medicare remains the primary payer for covered services, which means it pays first and Medicaid fills the gaps. For these beneficiaries Medicaid commonly pays the Medicare Part B premium, deductibles, and coinsurance, and it covers long-term services that Medicare excludes. Special eligibility categories exist precisely for this coordination: Qualified Medicare Beneficiaries, Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiaries, and Qualifying Individuals receive Medicaid help with Medicare cost sharing even when they do not qualify for full Medicaid benefits. Dual eligibility is the main bridge between the two statutes.

### Q: Is Medicaid means-tested and Medicare not?

Broadly yes, with one important refinement. Medicaid eligibility depends on financial need measured against categorical and income tests, so it is means-tested in the ordinary sense. Medicare Part A is not means-tested at all: eligibility flows from work history and payroll tax contributions, and a wealthy retiree qualifies on the same terms as a poor one. The refinement is that Medicare's other parts do respond to income in narrower ways. Part B premiums rise with income through income-related monthly adjustment amounts, Part D includes low-income subsidies tied to financial need, and beneficiaries with limited means can qualify for the Medicaid-administered Medicare Savings Programs. So the shorthand holds for the core programs but overstates the case if applied to every Medicare premium.

### Q: Who runs Medicare and who runs Medicaid?

Medicare is run by the federal government. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services sets national rules, and private Medicare Administrative Contractors process claims and handle day-to-day payment under federal contracts, so a beneficiary in Florida faces the same coverage rules as one in Oregon. Medicaid is run by the states under federal oversight. Each state operates its program through a single state agency under a state plan approved by CMS, and that plan defines eligibility groups, covered benefits, and payment rates within federal floors and ceilings. CMS approves the plan and enforces federal requirements, but the state chooses benefit packages beyond the mandatory floor and sets provider rates. One national program versus fifty-plus state variations is the administrative core of the difference.

### Q: Why are Medicare and Medicaid in different statutory titles?

Congress wrote them as two different answers to two different problems. Title XVIII of the Social Security Act, added by Public Law 89-97 in 1965, created a uniform federal health insurance program for the aged modeled on Social Security itself, with benefits financed through dedicated payroll contributions and identical rules nationwide. Title XIX, added by the same law, created a cooperative federal-state program extending the earlier Kerr-Mills medical assistance approach, with states designing their own programs inside federal requirements and receiving an open-ended federal match. The titles reflect constitutional and fiscal architecture: a direct federal entitlement needs different statutory machinery than a conditional grant to states. Codification follows the same split, at 42 U.S.C. 1395 and following versus 42 U.S.C. 1396 and following.

### Q: Is Medicaid an entitlement like Medicare?

It depends which Medicare part supplies the comparison. Medicare Part A is an individual earned entitlement: once the work-history conditions are met, the benefit is a matter of right and Congress cannot withhold it from a qualifying person. Medicaid is an entitlement in a different legal sense. The statute entitles eligible individuals to the benefits described in the state plan, and states must serve everyone who meets the eligibility criteria, which courts have treated as creating enforceable rights in at least some provisions. What Medicaid is not is an earned entitlement: eligibility depends on categorical and financial tests set by each state within federal bounds, and a person can be ineligible in one state and eligible in another. One program's entitlement is national and earned; the other's is conditional and state-shaped.

### Q: Which is bigger, Medicare or Medicaid?

The answer depends on the measure, and the two programs split the crown. Measured by people covered, Medicaid was larger: in 2010 Medicaid enrolled about 54 million Americans while Medicare enrolled about 47 million, with the recession pushing Medicaid rolls upward as incomes fell. Measured by dollars spent, Medicare was larger: 2010 spending reached roughly $520 billion for Medicare against roughly $397 billion for Medicaid, because Medicare's per-enrollee cost runs higher given the age and disability profile of its population. Both figures flow directly from the statutes' designs. Medicaid covers more people because its means-tested eligibility reaches across ages and family situations, while Medicare spends more per person because it concentrates on the elderly and the long-term disabled. Ask which is bigger only after naming the metric.

### Q: Do Medicare and Medicaid cover nursing homes the same way?

No, and the difference is one of the sharpest in American health law. Medicare covers skilled nursing facility care only after a qualifying hospital stay, only for skilled services, and only for a limited number of days with rising coinsurance; it never covers custodial long-term care, which the statute expressly excludes. Medicaid is the nation's principal payer for long-term care. Nursing facility services are a mandatory Medicaid benefit for the categorically needy, and states can cover home and community-based services through waivers. Because of this split, middle-class families planning for a parent's nursing-home costs deal almost entirely with Medicaid rules: spend-down of countable assets, the five-year look-back for transfers, and spousal impoverishment protections. Medicare's nursing-home question is narrow and temporary; Medicaid's is broad and permanent.

### Q: How does dual eligibility coordinate Medicare and Medicaid for the same person?

Coordination follows a payment hierarchy written into both statutes. For any service both programs cover, Medicare pays first as the primary payer and Medicaid pays second, covering the Medicare deductibles and coinsurance that would otherwise fall on the beneficiary. Where only Medicaid covers the service, most notably long-term custodial care, Medicaid pays as primary. For dual eligibles in the Medicare Savings Programs, Medicaid also pays the Part B premium directly, which protects the beneficiary's Social Security check. States may require providers to bill Medicare first and accept the Medicare payment as payment in full, limiting balance billing. The arrangement means the beneficiary's practical experience depends on which statute answers each service, with Medicaid acting as the wrap-around that Medicare was never designed to be.

### Q: What is Medicaid spend-down and does Medicare have anything like it?

Spend-down is a Medicaid mechanism for people whose income sits above the eligibility line but who face large medical bills. Under the medically needy option, a state subtracts incurred medical expenses from countable income, and once the remaining income falls below the state's medically needy income level, the person qualifies for Medicaid. It functions like a deductible measured in medical bills rather than dollars paid. Medicare has no counterpart because Medicare is not means-tested: Parts A and B eligibility never turns on income, so there is no income threshold to spend down toward. Medicare does have its own cost exposure through deductibles, coinsurance, and the Part D coverage gap, but those are fixed cost-sharing features of an earned entitlement, not a path to eligibility. Spend-down is a Medicaid-only concept by statutory design.

### Q: Why must Medicare claimants exhaust administrative appeals before going to court?

Because Congress channeled Medicare disputes through a mandatory administrative ladder. Section 405(h) of the Social Security Act, made applicable to Medicare, bars federal courts from hearing claims arising under the Medicare statute until the claimant has completed the agency's review process: redetermination, reconsideration, an administrative law judge hearing, and the Medicare Appeals Council, with amount-in-controversy thresholds at the later stages. In Shalala v. Illinois Council on Long Term Care (2000), the Supreme Court confirmed that this channeling requirement applies broadly and displaces ordinary federal-question jurisdiction. The policy is expertise and efficiency: the agency gets the first and fullest look at coverage and payment disputes. Only after the administrative remedies are exhausted does judicial review in federal district court become available.

### Q: Why does Medicare exclude custodial care while Medicaid pays for it?

The exclusion is statutory and dates to the programs' different missions. Section 1862(a)(9) of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395y(a)(9), bars Medicare payment for custodial care, which Congress understood as help with daily living rather than the acute medical treatment Medicare was built to insure. Medicare was designed as hospital and physician insurance for the aged, so long-term supportive care fell outside its model. Medicaid was built on the welfare-law tradition of meeting basic needs, and Congress made nursing facility services a mandatory benefit for the categorically needy. The policy consequence is enormous: Medicaid became the default financier of American long-term care, which is why estate planning, asset transfers, and spousal protections are Medicaid topics. Changing either side of this split would require amending the statute, not reinterpreting it.

### Q: How does Medicaid's FMAP financing differ from Medicare's trust-fund financing?

FMAP, the Federal Medical Assistance Percentage, is the matching rate at which the federal government reimburses state Medicaid spending. It ranges from 50 percent to 83 percent, set by a formula comparing each state's per capita income to the national average, so poorer states receive a higher federal share. The match is open-ended: for every qualifying dollar a state spends, the federal government pays its FMAP share, with no cap. Medicare financing works nothing like this. Part A runs through the Hospital Insurance trust fund fed by payroll taxes, and Parts B and D run through the Supplementary Medical Insurance trust fund fed by premiums and general revenues. Money is credited to trust funds and spent from them, with annual trustees' reports measuring solvency. One system matches state spending; the other spends dedicated federal revenues.

### Q: What is a Medicaid state plan, and why does Medicare have no equivalent?

The state plan is the legal instrument through which a state operates Medicaid. Federal law requires a single state agency to administer the program under a written plan, submitted to and approved by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, describing which eligibility groups the state covers, which benefits it offers beyond the mandatory floor, how providers are paid, and how the program is administered. Amendments need federal approval, and the plan functions like the contract between the state and the federal government. Medicare has no equivalent because it is not a state program: one national statute, one set of coverage rules, and federal contractors handle administration directly. There is nothing for a state to propose or for CMS to approve, so the entire state-plan apparatus simply has no place in Title XVIII.

### Q: Can people sue state officials to enforce Medicaid rights under section 1983?

Sometimes, and the answer turns on the specific provision. Section 1983 of the Civil Rights Act of 1871 permits suits against state officials acting under color of state law for deprivation of federal rights. In Maine v. Thiboutot (1980) the Supreme Court held that section 1983 reaches violations of the Social Security Act, which opened the door to Medicaid enforcement suits. But Gonzaga University v. Doe (2001) later required that the federal provision contain clear rights-creating language before a private right of action exists. Since then, federal courts have divided over which Medicaid provisions qualify: requirements like reasonable promptness of assistance have supported suits in some circuits, while broad provisions such as the equal-access requirement have been held unenforceable by private plaintiffs in others. The litigation posture of Medicaid is therefore genuinely uncertain provision by provision.

### Q: How can someone under 65 qualify for Medicare through disability or kidney failure?

Medicare is not only for the elderly. People under 65 qualify through two distinct statutory pathways. First, individuals receiving Social Security Disability Insurance generally become entitled to Medicare after a 24-month waiting period from the onset of disability entitlement. Second, individuals with end-stage renal disease qualify for Medicare regardless of age and without the usual work-history requirements, with coverage tied to the start of dialysis or a kidney transplant. Congress added the disability pathway in 1972 and the kidney-disease pathway the same year, deliberately extending Medicare beyond its original aged population. These exceptions are why the old-versus-poor shorthand misleads: a forty-year-old on dialysis is a Medicare beneficiary, and the disability pathway makes Medicare one of the largest insurers of working-age disabled Americans.

### Q: What does Qualified Medicare Beneficiary coverage pay for?

Qualified Medicare Beneficiary, or QMB, is a Medicaid eligibility category that pays Medicare cost sharing for low-income beneficiaries. It covers the Part B premium, the Part A premium for the small number of beneficiaries who owe one, and Medicare deductibles and coinsurance, effectively shielding the beneficiary from most out-of-pocket Medicare costs. Eligibility is limited to people with income at or below 100 percent of the federal poverty level and limited resources, and it is administered through state Medicaid programs. Two sibling categories reach slightly higher up the income scale: Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiaries, between 100 and 120 percent of poverty, receive help with the Part B premium, and Qualifying Individuals, between 120 and 135 percent, receive Part B premium assistance subject to capped federal funding. QMB is the fullest of the three.

### Q: What do Medicaid's spousal impoverishment rules protect?

They protect the spouse who stays at home when the other spouse enters a nursing home on Medicaid. Before Congress acted, Medicaid's asset and income tests could force a couple to spend nearly everything before the institutionalized spouse qualified, leaving the community spouse impoverished. The Medicare Catastrophic Coverage Act of 1988, whose spousal provisions survived that law's repeal, created two protections: the community spouse may keep a share of the couple's countable resources, the community spouse resource allowance, and may receive a minimum monthly maintenance needs allowance from the institutionalized spouse's income. States set these amounts within federal floors and ceilings. The rules acknowledge that long-term care is often a decades-long expense and that the law should not require one spouse's destitution as the price of the other's care.

### Q: Why does Medicare use trust funds while Medicaid uses none?

The difference follows from how each program raises and spends money. Medicare Part A is financed primarily by a dedicated payroll tax, and Parts B and D by premiums plus general revenues, so Congress created the Hospital Insurance and Supplementary Medical Insurance trust funds as accounting vessels: earmarked revenues flow in, benefit payments flow out, and trustees report annually on solvency. The trust-fund structure makes the programs' finances legible and gives Congress an early-warning mechanism. Medicaid raises no dedicated revenue at all. It is a cooperative grant program: states spend their own funds on covered services and the federal government reimburses its FMAP share from annual appropriations. With no earmarked federal revenue stream to segregate, there is nothing for a trust fund to hold, so Medicaid operates entirely through the appropriations process.

### Q: Which statute controls when Medicare and Medicaid point to different nursing-home answers?

Whichever statute the particular question arises under, which is why the first step is classifying the question. A dispute over whether Medicare covers a specific skilled nursing facility stay is a Title XVIII question governed by the coverage rules and the custodial-care exclusion at 42 U.S.C. 1395y(a)(9), appealable through the Medicare administrative ladder. A dispute over eligibility for long-term care coverage, asset transfers, or the level of nursing facility payment is a Title XIX question governed by the state plan and federal Medicaid requirements, litigated against state officials. Where a dual eligible faces both, the payment hierarchy decides: Medicare pays first for services it covers, Medicaid covers what Medicare does not. The wrong-question diagnosis applies here in full: most nursing-home confusion is uncertainty about which statute is even being asked about.
