## Cold Open

![Nightjar: The Badge, the Burn and the Locked Stair - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-11.webp)

Vaun Mell came to the building because no other door in Carrowmere would have let him in without a question, and this one let him in with none at all.

He had walked since morning, not far in distance and not fast in pace, through streets that stayed wet even when the rain had stopped, past bonded warehouses with their shutters down and their yards empty, past the locks where the water moved slow and brown under the bridges, and he had held his right hand close against his coat the whole way, not because the cold found it first, though the cold did, but because the hand had learned a habit in the infirmary and had not unlearned it on the road. The scars crossed the palm in bands and crossed the back in bands, and where the bands met at the edges of the hand the skin pulled tight when he closed his fingers, so he did not close them. He carried nothing. A man who has carried crates for pay learns to walk empty with a particular plainness, shoulders level, arms loose, nothing swinging, and Mell walked empty in that way all the way from the wharf quarter to the records and requisitions building, as if an invisible load still set his balance.

The badge was in his left pocket. He had put it there before he left the room he had taken after the infirmary, and he had taken it out twice on the walk to look at it, once under an awning where a carter was mending a strap, and once in the lee of a warehouse wall where nobody stood. It was a plain thing, heavier than it looked, an Accord badge of the kind issued at a stores window to men whose work took them through doors that stayed shut to the public, and it sat in his palm with the comfortable weight of a tool that fits a known task. He knew what it said about him on paper. He knew the name that stood against it in the issue book, and it was not his name, and he had known that from the first day he carried it, and it had never once stopped a door for him. Doors had opened. Men at counters had nodded. Clerks had waved him through corridors with a crate on his shoulder and had not looked at his face at all, because the badge did the looking for them. That was what a badge was for, as it had been explained to him. You showed it. You carried. You were paid. The explanation had come from a badged mouth, in an ordinary room, with paper on the table, and he had believed it in the way a man believes the shape of his own work, because the work itself had never contradicted it.

He was not a man who lied easily, though he was a man who had learned to say less than he knew and to say it in words that had been given to him. When the infirmary clerks had asked him about his trade he had answered with the words he had been given, and he had heard himself answer wrong, not in the facts of carrying, which he knew in his back and in his shoulders and in the ruined skin of his hand, but in the words around the facts, the names for things, the order in which a carrying business was arranged, who engaged whom and under what form of paper. A carrier knows those things the way he knows a stair in the dark. Mell did not, and the gap had embarrassed him in the infirmary in a quiet way that stayed with him, because he was an educated man in his speech, a man who read what was put in front of him and remembered it, and he did not like discovering that his own life had passages in it he could only recite.

The infirmary had kept him eleven days, on a narrow bed in a room that smelled of boiled cloth and lamp oil, with the hand dressed and redressed and the pain arriving in waves that the dressings did nothing to shorten. He had borne it, without pride, because pride would have required an audience and there had been none he trusted. What Mell knew, and had not said to any of them, was that the pain had never once frightened him. It had hurt him without pause, kept him from sleep and from food and from any thought longer than the next breath, and still it had not frightened him, and that absence in himself puzzled him more than the pain had. A man expects fear to keep company with that much hurting. His had not come. He had lain there and hurt and waited, the waiting clean of fear as a swept floor is clean of dust, and wondered what kind of errand puts a man in that state, hurt to the edge of his strength and yet unafraid, as if the fear had been taken out of the matter earlier, somewhere upstream of him, by hands he had never seen.

When his record was secured, when the paper about him was complete and copied and put where paper of that kind is put, he had discharged himself within the hour. Nobody had argued. He had signed where he was shown to sign, with his left hand, slowly, and walked out into the wharf air with his right hand held close, and nobody had followed him, or if anybody had, they had done it so well that a man who watches warehouse yards for a living had seen nothing.

Now the building stood in front of him across a wet square, grey stone, tall windows, an ordinary records and requisitions building in the Accord quarter, the kind of place whose name nobody used because its function was its name. Mell crossed the square without hurry, took the badge from his pocket at the steps, held it low in his left hand at the angle at which porters present badges, and went in. The hall smelled of damp stone and ink. The keeper behind his desk looked at the badge and, if Mell was honest about it, at nothing else at all, not at the scarred hand held close, not at the damp coat, not at the face of a man who had slept badly for eleven nights. The badge was enough. The keeper made a note in the ledger and waved him toward the stair with the ease of a man waving a regular through his own place of work.

Mell climbed. The stairwell was stone, with a rail of dark iron and treads worn in their centres by generations of clerks carrying paper between floors. He climbed past the first landing, where a woman copying figures into a book did not look up, and past the second, where the windows gave onto the canal. His scarred hand he kept off the rail. The other touched it lightly, counting the climb the way carriers count, by landings and by breath. Paper moved through this place the way water moved through the locks outside, in measured amounts, under control, from level to level, and Mell moved with it, one more badged body in the flow, unremarkable, unrecorded beyond a keeper's note, exactly as he had been on every carrying run he had ever made.

The door he had come for was on the upper floor, at the end of a short corridor off the landing, and it was shut. It was the door of a requisition records room. He knew that from the words painted on its panel in the sober lettering the Accord used for rooms that held paper of consequence, and he knew it also from instruction, from a badged mouth, in an ordinary room, a long while back, in the days when instructions still arrived as if they were weather, expected and unquestioned. Check the door. That was the shape of it, when it came. Not open it. Not force it. Not wait beside it. Check it, and know, and carry the knowing back into the ordinary run of his days until he was asked. He had never been asked. That was a thing he had begun, lately, in the infirmary bed, to turn over in his mind the way he turned the badge over in his pocket. He had checked doors and carried crates and been paid through paper he had never read, and the asking had never come, and a man can live inside a shape like that for a long time before he notices that the shape has no door of its own on the inside.

He stood in the corridor and looked at the door. It was a good door, solid, painted the colour of all the doors in the building, with a handle worn bright where hands took it and a lock plate that showed the fine scratches of many keys. Mell reached out with his left hand, because the right would not close properly around anything, and he took the handle and tried it once, gently, in the manner of a man confirming what he already expects. It did not move. The lock held. The door stayed shut, and the corridor stayed empty, and nothing in the building changed its note. He stood there a moment longer with his hand on the handle, not pushing, not rattling, not doing any of the things a man does when he means to get through a door. He was doing the thing he had been told to do. He was checking. And underneath the obedience of the gesture, like a draught under the door itself, ran a thought he had not been given by anybody, in his own words at last. If he was never asked, then the checking was not an errand. It was a leash. And a man who finds a leash in his own hand has to decide, sooner or later, whether to keep holding it.

He let go of the handle and went back to the landing. He could go down and walk out past the keeper and be back in the wharf quarter by dark, the day unmarked by anything but a checked door. He could also stay. Staying was a thing he knew how to do. He had stayed eleven days in a bed that hurt him. He could stay on a landing.

Evening came into the building the way it came into all buildings in Carrowmere, by degrees and by routine. Clerks put on coats. Files were tied and carried to shelves. The woman in the room of shelves shut her book and her door. Somewhere below, keys turned in offices. Mell listened to the building empty itself around his stillness, and he did not move from the landing, and nobody came up to ask him to move, because a badged man standing on a landing in a records building as the light fails is furniture until somebody decides otherwise. Then, at the foot of the stairwell, an ordinary sound, unhurried and final. A key in a lock. The keeper locking the stairwell door for the night, as the keeper locked it every night, securing the upper floors in their order. The sound came up the stone well clearly, the turn, the bolt, the small ring of the key withdrawn.

Mell sat down on the landing, with his back against the wall and his legs drawn in clear of the treads, in the place where the rail turns. He put the badge back in his left pocket. He laid his scarred hand open on his knee, palm up, bands across it, and he looked at it in the grey light as if it were a paper somebody else had written on. He did not call out. He did not go down to the locked door and ask to be let out, though the keeper would have let him out with an apology and a note in the ledger and no harm done to anybody. He sat, and the building settled into its night quiet around him, and the decision he had been making since the infirmary, without knowing he was making it, finished making itself. He would stay where the building had put him, on the landing, above a locked door, below a checked one, until somebody came up who was willing to hear the small whole of what he knew. However long that took. He had carried heavier things than time.


## Tasking

The message reached Mira before the building had finished admitting what it held.

It came by the ordinary route, a runner from the records and requisitions building with a sealed note and a keeper's statement folded inside it, and Leyden read both standing at the window of his room with the light behind him, so that Mira, watching from the chair she had not been invited to take, saw his expression only when he turned. He turned with the particular stillness that meant the day had changed shape.

A badged man, Leyden said, was sitting on a landing in the requisitions building and would not come down.

Mira waited. She had learned, across a season of taskings, that Leyden's first sentence was the frame and never the picture, and that a question asked too early bought a shorter answer than patience did.

The building locked itself last night in its ordinary order, he said. The keeper locked the stairwell at the foot, as the keeper locks it every night, and found in the morning that the upper floors were occupied. A man on the landing above the second floor. He had been offered the door, a note in the ledger, and an apology for the inconvenience, in that order. He declined all three. He asked, politely, by the keeper's account, whether the Ninth Office still kept rooms in the building. Then he sat down again.

Rask, standing by the door with his coat still on, made the sound he made when a case arrived already refusing to be simple. Reni Vosk, at the narrow desk with the file open in front of her and her pen uncapped, looked up with the bright attention of a junior officer who believes, as she believed most things about the Accord, that a procedure exists for this and that the procedure will be followed.

Has he given a name, Mira said.

He has given nothing, Leyden said. He has shown a badge. That is the beginning and the whole of his cooperation. He set the keeper's statement down on the desk between them, squared it with the edge of the desk out of a habit so old it had stopped being visible to him, and then set a second paper beside it, older, thinner, with the creases of a file that had been folded into a jacket and carried. But the badge has a history, and the history is why the runner came to us and not to the building's own wardens. The badge was reported missing in the spring.

Mira looked at the second paper without touching it. A report form of the kind the stores used, ruled lines, a printed heading, entries in ink. Even at a distance of an arm's length she could see that the hand which had filled it was a careful hand, upright, letters formed one by one with the patience of a man writing in a language he respected more than he used.

Reported by the holder, she said.

Reported, Leyden said, in the name of the holder. Whether by the holder, this office will determine. He let that stand a moment, and she understood that he had chosen the phrasing the way he chose all phrasing in a tasking, to leave a door neither open nor shut. The recorded holder is a stores clerk. Anselm Ferro. He works the stores window in the bonded quarter, or he did when the badge was issued to him at that window and entered against his name in the issue book. The badge number is spoken, in this room, as it will be spoken everywhere in this case, in words. He recited it slowly, digit by digit as words, the way a man reads a number off a wet page, and Reni wrote it down in words in the margin of the file, and Mira held it in memory in words, because a number in this case was not a decoration. It was the thread's first knot, and she treated it with the respect due to knots.

And Ferro, she said.

Ferro reported his badge missing in the spring and was issued a replacement and has worked since under the replacement without incident, Leyden said. Ferro is, on paper, the most satisfactory man in this story. He did what the charter requires, promptly, in writing, through the proper window. His stores returns are complete. His window book is clean. If the badge on that landing is his lost badge, then the man carrying it has some explaining to do, and the explaining will be short.

If, Rask said, from the door.

If, Leyden agreed, without looking at him. The report is the report. What the office has noticed, and what I am telling you before you go and not after, is the hand. He touched the second paper with one finger, not sliding it toward her, simply marking it, the way a man marks a place in a book he intends to return to. The report is written in a hand the office has seen before. Not Ferro's hand, or not the hand on his stores returns, which the office also holds. A second hand. It has appeared in one other place in our recent work, and Reni will tell you where, because Reni has done the reading.

Reni coloured slightly, not with embarrassment but with the effort of keeping a discovery at the temperature at which discoveries are supposed to be delivered in a handler's room. Two entries in an infirmary record, she said. The wharf night infirmary. The record of Vaun Mell, the labourer who carried alloy consignments and was treated for the injury to his hand. Most of the record is in the infirmary clerk's ordinary hand. Two entries are not. They are in a second hand, upright, careful, letters formed one by one. She looked down at the file, then up. It is the same hand as the missing report. I have compared them twice. I would compare them a third time before I said it in any other room.

Vaun Mell, Mira said.

The name settled in the room with the weight of a thing placed on a table rather than dropped. She knew the name from the infirmary work, from a bed read in a ward that smelled of boiled cloth, from eleven days of pain without fear recorded in a man's bedding and belongings by an injury she would not name and a pattern she would not forget, bands across the palm and bands across the back, meeting at the edges of the hand. She knew it from a clerk named Sabel Orry, a bonded chain clerk whom Mira had put in the way of an unidentified man on the quay, and Orry was now known to someone, and that knowing sat in Mira's private accounting of this season where she put the costs that did not appear in any file. The man on the landing had a scarred hand, by the keeper's statement. The keeper had noticed the hand because the man held it the way Mell held his, close, palm guarded. The keeper had not been asked to notice anything else and had noticed nothing else, which made his statement, in Mira's judgement, worth more than most.

He discharged himself from the infirmary within the hour his record was secured, Leyden said. He was not followed. That was a decision taken above my desk and I record it here as taken. Now he has walked into an Accord building on the strength of a badge reported missing in the spring, climbed to an upper floor, and checked a door he has no right to open.

Which door, Mira said.

The door of the requisition records room, on the floor above the second landing. The keeper is precise on the point because the keeper found the marks of the visit in his morning round, a hand on the handle where no hand should have been during the locked hours, dust disturbed at the threshold, nothing forced, nothing taken, nothing opened. The man checked the door. Then, when the lock turned at the stairwell's foot, he sat down on the landing and declined to come down, and he has been sitting there, by the keeper's account and the locking log together, for two hours since the building opened and made its offer, and for the whole of the night before it, if sitting is the word for what a man does alone on stone until morning.

Two hours, Rask said. Since opening.

Two hours of declining, in daylight, with the door open below him, Leyden said. The night he gave himself for free. It is the two hours that interest me, Mira. A man who wants to escape does not sit for two hours in a lit stairwell. A man who wants to be arrested makes more noise. This man is waiting for a particular ear, and he has chosen his stage with some care, and I dislike being chosen, even by a man whose choice I may end up respecting.

There was more. There was always more, and in this tasking the more arrived in Leyden's driest voice, the voice he used for costs he did not want argued with before they had been heard to the end.

Commissioner Harrand has authorised the interview, he said. She will be in the room.

Reni's pen stopped. Rask came away from the door by a step, which for Rask was a speech.

Authorised, Mira said.

The building is an Accord building, Leyden said. The badge is an Accord badge. The man entered under Accord paper and sits under Accord lock. Oversight claims, and on the narrow point oversight is within its rights to claim, that the interview of a badged man in a requisition building is an Accord proceeding before it is a Ninth Office one. Harrand will attend. She will hear whatever he says at the moment he says it, in the room, first hand. There will be no interval in which this office holds the thread alone, no softening, no sequencing, no deciding what oversight needs and when it needs it. Whatever comes off that landing comes into two sets of books at once.

Mira thought of her private register of absences, the record she had kept in her own hand across the season, absence by absence, place by place, given voluntarily to Harrand in an earlier case and now an Accord document in oversight's keeping with a numbered Accord label on its cover, and she thought of what it meant that the woman who kept that register under label would now sit in the interview room and hear a carrier talk. She did not say any of it. Leyden watched her not say it, and his expression, which was ordinarily a closed door, opened by the width of a keyhole.

That is the cost, he said. Named up front, as you prefer it. It is the ugliest yet, and I will not pretend otherwise to the officer I am sending up those stairs. You bring him down, Mira. You bring what he knows down with him, into a room where we do not control the hearing of it. That is the task. The landing, the badge, the report, the door. In that order, and the paper first wherever paper can speak, because paper, in this case, is already speaking louder than the man.

She stood. The chronic pain she carried, a month of somebody else's pain taken from an attacker's coat in Valtessa and still with her, woke as she moved and settled as she stilled, as it woke and settled with her own movements every day now, so faithfully that on bad mornings she could not always tell it from her own, and this was not a good morning. She put the keeper's statement and the missing report into the file in the order Leyden had laid them down, badge first, paper after, and she felt the day arrange itself around the four things she had been given, each of which would have to be established again on the ground, by eye and by hand and by record, before any of them could be used.

Badge, she said, for Reni's pen. Seen and described, its number spoken in words, its recorded holder named from the issue book. Report, produced and kept where hands can be compared. Door, named and placed before anybody reads it. Two hours on the landing, from the keeper and the log, before the landing is read.

Leyden inclined his head by the smallest degree that counted, in his ledger, as approval. Go and establish them, he said.


## Ground Truth

The building, entered in daylight with a warrant of tasking instead of a badge, gave up its facts in the order in which buildings prefer to give them, which is the order of the ground itself, from the hall upward, from paper to stone.

The keeper kept his ledger in a hand so regular that Reni, reading over Mira's shoulder in the hall while the morning light came grey through the tall windows and the radiators ticked their inadequate heat into the damp, said it looked printed, and the keeper, who had the hearing of a man accustomed to listening for keys in locks at a distance, corrected her without offence. Not printed, miss. Practised. Forty years of entries will practise a hand. He turned the book toward them on the desk, and the entry for the evening before stood in its line with the others, the hour of locking, the floors secured in their order, the stairwell at the foot locked last as it was locked every night, and the entry for the morning stood below it, the hour of opening, the offer made to the man on the landing, the offer declined, the refusal recorded in the same practised hand without comment or emphasis, as a keeper records weather. Two hours, the ledger said in effect if not in so many words, between the opening of the building and the arrival of the runner at Leyden's rooms, two hours in which the door at the stairwell's foot had stood open, the hall had filled with clerks, the square outside had filled with carts, and the man above had sat within sound of all of it and had not come down. Mira read the two entries twice. A log is a kind of weight that paper carries without cost, duration made legible, and she wanted the duration fixed in her mind by the building's own testimony before she went near the landing and asked the stone for its version, because the stone would give her the quality of those hours and the ledger gave her their measure, and a case built on quality without measure is a story, while a case built on measure without quality is a timetable, and she intended, as she always intended, to build neither.

The keeper described the badge. He had seen it twice, once at the man's entry during ordinary hours on the day before, held low in the left hand at the angle at which porters present badges, and once in the morning, when he had climbed as far as the turn below the landing and the man, polite as a tenant, had held it out again for inspection at a distance. A plain Accord badge, the keeper said, heavy for its size, the kind issued at the stores window for work that passed through shut doors. He had noted its number in his day book at the entry, and he showed them the note, the number written in figures in the keeper's book as keepers write numbers, and Mira read it and translated it in her head into the words in which Leyden had spoken it and in which Reni had written it in the file margin, and the two agreed, figure against word, the badge on the landing and the badge in the tasking the same badge. The recorded holder, from the issue book which the keeper fetched from its shelf with the reluctance of a man lending out a bible, was Anselm Ferro, stores clerk, issued at the stores window in the bonded quarter, entered in the issue book in the issuing clerk's hand with the date of issue and the nature of the work for which it was issued, bonded carrying work, which phrase Mira read twice as well, because it placed the badge at its birth in the same world as the man who now held it, the world of crates and quays and consignments carried under bond.

Rask took the stairs first, as Rask took all stairs first, not from gallantry but from a Stiller's professional preference for arriving in a space before its weight had been stirred by a Weigher's attention, and because his body, broad and unhurried, made a kind of weather of its own in a stairwell that discouraged haste in others. Reni carried the file. Mira carried nothing, her hands empty and cooling already in the stairwell air, and she climbed slowly, attending to the building as she went, the worn centres of the treads, the rail dark iron fixed into the stone, the windows at the half landings giving onto the canal where a barge moved under a bridge with the slow indifference of all traffic that is not waiting for her. On the first landing a door stood open on the room of shelves, and the woman copying figures did not look up, as she had not looked up for Mell. On the second landing the corridor ran to the rooms of record, and there, at its end, Mira stopped and named the thing she had come, in part, to place before any reading was done anywhere in this building.

The door, she said, for Reni's pen and for the record they were building as they walked. The requisition records room. She went down the corridor alone and stood before it in the ordinary light of the morning, a good door, solid, painted the colour of all the doors in the building, its handle worn bright where hands took it, its lock plate fine with the scratches of many keys, and she did not touch it. She looked at the threshold where the keeper had reported the dust disturbed, and the dust was disturbed, a fan of lighter stone where a shoe had turned, once, the mark of a man who had stood here and tried a handle and stepped back. She looked at the painted words on the panel that named the room's function. She committed the door to the file in words, its place, its state, its witness marks, before she put any part of her gift near it, because a read taken before a place is established is a read that can never be checked, and this door, she already suspected, was going to be checked against everything.

Above them, beyond the next turn of the stair, the landing waited with its occupant, and the building, which had been talking to her all morning in ledger and issue book and dust, fell for the first time silent, in the manner of a witness who has said the easy parts and arrived at the part that must be approached properly.

They did not approach it yet. Paper first, where paper could speak, Leyden had said, and the paper in this case lived on the second floor, in the rooms where oversight kept its watching brief over buildings like this one, and so it was that Mira found herself, before noon, in the room the Accord gave to Commissioner Ilse Harrand when Harrand's duties brought her to the requisitions building, a plain room made plainer by her use of it, one desk, one chair for a visitor, shelves of bound returns, a window onto the canal, and on the desk, squared to its edge in a precision that matched Leyden's own and came, Mira thought, from the opposite philosophy, her private register of absences, the record she had kept in her own hand across the season and given voluntarily to Harrand in an earlier case and which was now an Accord document in oversight's keeping with a numbered Accord label on its cover.

What followed was framed by the room and by the woman, and Mira understood, even while she stood in it, that she was being shown oversight's ground at oversight's chosen angle, and that the showing was itself a kind of argument.

---

Commissioner Harrand did not rise, because rising would have made the meeting a courtesy and she had no courtesy to spend on the Ninth Office in a building where a badged man sat above a locked stair with a story nobody had yet heard. She kept her hand flat on the register's cover a moment after Mira's eye had gone to it, not possessively, Harrand thought, or not only possessively, but as a clerk keeps a hand on a book that is in dispute, to establish that the book is somewhere, in someone's keeping, and that the keeping can be pointed to. The register had come to her voluntarily, which the Office remembered as generosity and she remembered as evidence, because a gift of evidence is still evidence, and what the register recorded, in the Officer's own hand, was a pattern of absences across a season, places where weight should have been and was not, a phenomenon the Office had observed privately, recorded privately, and disclosed, in the end, to oversight, because even the Ninth Office, Harrand reflected, eventually reached the edge of what it could decently keep to itself.

She intended to be fair. Fairness, in Harrand's private accounting, was not warmth and was not concession. It was the discipline of stating the other side's position in a form the other side would recognise, and then stating her own without borrowing strength from caricature. She stated it now, to the officer standing in her visitor's space with empty hands and a file she had not been offered a chair to open.

A requisition chain inside the Accord's own paper is not the Ninth Office's private thread. That was the first sentence, and she watched it land. A badge issued at an Accord window, signed out against an Accord requisition, carried on Accord bonded work, reported missing under an Accord form, and found in the pocket of a man sitting in an Accord stairwell. Every link of that chain is oversight's business by charter, not by appetite. She paused. And the chain, unless the morning's work has already dissolved it, runs through requisitions that your own handler's office has touched, as all requisition traffic in this quarter is touched, sooner or later, by every office that works in it. An office that investigates a chain its own paper forms part of, and which holds besides the only register of absences in existence, cannot be the sole keeper of what a carrier confesses at the end of that chain. It is not a question of your integrity, Officer. It is a question of structure. A compromised keeper is compromised by position, not by character, and position is exactly what oversight exists to correct.

She was right about the conflict, and she knew she was right, and she knew that the officer knew it, because the officer, to her credit, did not argue the point, and Harrand marked that silence down in the private ledger she kept of the Ninth Office's people, in the column she used so seldom that its heading had nearly faded. She thought of the interview to come, which she had authorised and would attend, and of the notebook she would carry into it, a new notebook, bought for this proceeding, in which whatever the carrier said would be written in her hand at the moment of its saying, beyond the reach of any office's sequencing or softening. If the man on the landing had a confession in him, it would enter the Accord's record through two doors at once or it would not enter at all. That was not cruelty toward the Office. That was the charter, working as the charter was written to work, in the one case all season where the charter and the truth plainly required the same thing.

You may tell Leyden, she said, that oversight will be in the room, and that oversight will be listening. It was a dismissal, and she made it cleanly, and the framed moment closed on the flat of her hand resting, still, on the labelled cover of somebody else's register.

---

Mira carried the opposition down the corridor with her, unanswered, because there was no answer that did not concede the point or evade it, and she had not been sent to this building to win an argument in a plain room. She had been sent to establish four things and then to climb one flight of stairs, and two of the four were established and two remained, and the fairness of Harrand's position sat in her mind alongside the fairness of the three possibilities for the badge, which she now set out for Reni in the corridor, in the order in which the case required them to be held, all three live, none preferred, while Rask listened with his back to the wall and his eyes on the stair.

The badge is stolen, she said. The badge is lent. The badge is his. Three doors, and we do not go through any of them until the paper shuts two. Stolen is the possibility with a document behind it, which makes it the strongest and therefore the most dangerous, because a document can be a wall built to be leaned on. The report exists. It is dated in the spring. It fits the season, it fits the replacement issued at the window, it fits Ferro's clean book. If it is true, the case is short and the man above us is a thief or a thief's customer. If it is not true, then somebody built the wall, and the building of it is the case. Lent has no document and one implication, that the holder parted with the badge willingly, which Ferro's record nowhere suggests. His, the third, has the implication that the issue book itself is the wall, and we are not ready to say that of any book yet. Hold all three. Write them down. Reni wrote them down.

The file gave them the stores returns next, fetched by a clerk who handled the request with the speed of a man who has discovered that the Ninth Office and the Commissioner are in the same building on the same morning and has decided that promptness is the safest of all postures. Ferro's returns lay in their folder in the clerk's delivery, month on month of stores accounting, issues and receipts and balances carried forward, all in one hand, a working clerk's hand, quick, slanted, economical, the letters leaning like men walking into weather. Mira spread three months of it on the table in the record room they had been lent, and beside it, when Reni brought it from the file with the care of a nurse carrying a dressing, the missing report. The two hands did not resemble each other. It was not a subtle difference, not a matter of formation under haste or age or a changed pen. The returns were written by a man who wrote all day. The report was written by a man who wrote carefully, upright, letter by letter, the patience Reni had named in Leyden's room, the patience of a writer respecting a language he used seldom. Mira looked at the report a long time. She thought about [the foundry list, where four places made the alloy and one sold it](/2026/11/15/nightjar-foundry-list-015/), and how a list, too, is a piece of paper that claims to close a question, four names and a fifth that sold, and how the work since had consisted of learning which papers closed questions and which papers merely stood in front of them. The report stood in front of its question. She could feel that now, in the ordinary way, with her eyes, before any other faculty was consulted.

She turned the report over. Nothing on the back but a filing mark. She turned it back. Name of holder, entered in the careful hand. Date, in the spring, entered in the careful hand. Circumstances of loss, three ruled lines of the careful hand, a form of words so correct that it read like a copy of correctness, a badge mislaid in the course of bonded carrying work, loss discovered at the window, report made at the first opportunity. Every element the charter required, present. Every element a genuine loser of a badge might have forgotten, supplied. Rask, reading upside down across the table with the ease of long practice, said that if he ever lost anything he hoped he lost it as completely as that, and Reni, who believed the Accord charter as other people believe in the behaviour of tides, said quietly that a complete report was what the charter asked for, and that completeness could not be held against a man, and Mira said that it could not, and that they were not holding it against Ferro, and that the hand was the point, only the hand, and Reni wrote that down too, completeness not held, hand in question, in her neat junior's script that would one day, if the season allowed it, practise itself into something like the keeper's.


The badge chain, when they walked it, walked them backward through the whole working life of a small metal object, and Mira had the walking of it set out in the record room before any of them climbed to the landing, because a chain of custody is a story told by paper and she wanted the story complete in front of her, link by link, with every weakness marked where it lay and not where rhetoric would prefer it. The issue book gave the first link. The stores window gave the second. The wharf gave the third, and the third was corroborated in a quarter she had not expected to be glad of, because the carrying runs on which the badge had travelled were the same runs that ran through [the photograph of a palm pinned to a board beside three others that looked nothing like it](/2026/11/18/nightjar-marked-hands-018/), that season of marked hands and photographed palms, and the badge had been present, entered in run sheets and counter notes, on the days when alloy consignments moved under bond and men were hurt in ways that photographed badly and explained worse. The fourth link was the report. The fifth was a pocket on a landing, attested by a keeper who wrote like a printing press and noticed hands.

Reni read the table back in a whisper when Mira had finished dictating its sense to the file copy, entry by entry, and her whisper slowed at the fourth entry and stopped altogether for a breath at the fifth, because the chain, walked in order, did something that no single document in it had done alone. It made the stolen possibility walk uphill. A stolen badge travels in the dark, in the ordinary course of things. It is hidden, it is sold, it is used at night at doors that are not watched, it is discarded when its heat rises. This badge had travelled in the light for a run of weeks under its own number, had been signed out and returned and signed out again under paper, had been shown to keepers and counter clerks by the man who held it as a matter of routine, and had at last been carried into an Accord building in ordinary hours and shown again, twice, to the same keeper, by a man who then sat down in a lit stairwell and waited to be asked about it. Theft could still be made to fit those facts, Mira said, when Reni looked up with the question plainly forming. Anything can be made to fit any facts by a sufficiently determined advocate, which is why this office does not employ advocates. But the fit has changed its price. The report was supposed to make stolen cheap. The hand makes it expensive. Hold the three doors open. We go up now and ask the landing what it holds, and after the landing, the door.

The landing, approached at last in the late morning with the building working below and around it and the canal light standing grey in the half landing window, was a plain place that had been made remarkable only by occupation. Stone treads, worn in their centres. A rail of dark iron, fixed into the wall with plates that had been painted and repainted until the screw heads wore coats of their own. A door frame at the corridor's mouth, its paint polished by shoulders. And on the turn of the landing, where the rail turned with it, the man himself, sitting as the keeper had described him, back to the wall, legs drawn clear of the treads, the scarred hand open on his knee in a posture Mira recognised with a small interior jolt, because it was the posture of a man displaying a thing he has decided not to hide any longer, palm up, bands across it, the injury offered to the room like an exhibit whose label he was tired of reading aloud.

He looked at the three of them without rising. His eyes went to Rask's breadth and discounted it, to Reni's file and discounted it, and came to rest on Mira with the recognition, not of her face, which he had no reason to know, but of her kind, the empty hands, the stillness, the patience of a person who has come to read a place rather than to shout in it.

You are the Office, he said. It was not a question.

We are the Office, Mira said. She did not give names. Names, on a landing, are a currency spent too early. You have been sitting a long time.

I have been sitting, he said, for as long as it takes. His voice was better educated than his coat, a discrepancy she filed beside the infirmary's finding, the man wrong about his own trade in the way of a man repeating words he was given, educated in his speech and reciting at the edges. He did not move to rise. He did not move to flee. The stair below was held by Rask's presence as a gate is held by its own weight, and Mell glanced at it once and then, visibly, put the glance away, as a man puts away a tool he has decided not to use.

Mira set Rask at the turn below and Reni at the corridor mouth with the file, and she went onto the landing alone, not close, not yet, and she put her hand, her left hand, on the rail where the iron turned, and she read the landing at its anchors, the rail, the stone tread, the door frame, in the order in which the place offered them.

Weight came as it always came, not as a picture and never as a voice, but as a pressure of significance in the materials themselves, the iron and the stone holding what paper and cloth would have let fall in a day. Two hours, the ledger had said, and the rail agreed, and more than agreed, deepened, because what the landing held was not the weight of a man merely sitting, which thins and idles as sitting does, but the weight of a man deciding, sustained intent laid down in layer on layer in the same small compass of stone, one decision made and remade and remade again as the hours of the night and the two hours of the morning passed over him. She felt it in the tread under her feet as a density, a repeated return to the same point of resolve, fear absent from it in a way that struck her cold, grief present in it like damp in a wall, and underneath both a patience so complete it had stopped feeling like patience to the man enduring it and had become simply the shape of his staying. The landing gave her the duration and the deciding. It gave her, faithfully and within its limits, the quality of the two hours and of the night behind them. It did not give her, and she did not ask it for, who he was deciding about, or what he had decided, because weight records intensity and affect and has never once, in all her years of reading it, recorded an identity or a name. The man on the stone had sat there making one decision over and over. That was the whole of the landing's testimony, and it was, she thought, standing in the cold that was beginning now to take her hands and her shoulders as the read exacted its familiar price, a considerable testimony. Men who are merely hiding do not decide. They rehearse escape. This man had rehearsed a staying, all night, at the cost of stone cold and a ruined hand and the slow narrowing of every other possibility, and he had arrived at morning with the decision worn smooth as the centre of the tread he sat beside.

She took her hand off the rail. The cold stayed. It would stay for hours, through whatever the day still held, and the pain that was not hers stirred in her back and settled again with her movement as she stepped back from the landing's turn, and she was aware, with the part of her mind that kept accounts even while the rest was reading, that she had spent something here she could not spend many more times in succession, and that the door at the corridor's end still waited to be read, and that the door, by every law of her gift that she trusted, should hold at least the echo of the hand that had tried its handle in the night, a man in Mell's state laying a palm's worth of intent on bright worn metal.

Mell watched her face, not her hands. You read it, he said.

I read the landing, she said. It says you decided.

His mouth moved, not a smile, a recognition of accuracy, as if a measurement he had taken of himself in the dark had been confirmed by an instrument he respected. Then I decided right, he said, and said nothing more, and the landing held the three of them and the Office in a silence that Reni, at the corridor mouth, did not break with her pen.


Before they left the record room, Mira had the window book brought, and the run sheets with it, because the chain in the table had been dictated from summaries and she did not intend the file to rest on summaries where originals could be had. The window book was a heavy ledger with a cracked spine, kept at the stores window in the bonded quarter and carried over by a messenger who held it against his chest with both arms, as a man carries a sleeping child, and set it down with relief. Its pages smelled of the bonded quarter, of rope and tar and the dust of stores. The entries for the badge ran in the issuing clerk's hand and then in the hands of the window clerks who succeeded to the duty, signing out and signing in, date by date, across a run of weeks that Mira read with her finger resting in the margin and Reni reading aloud, softly, the nature of each errand as the book recorded it. Bonded carrying work. Bonded carrying work. A consignment received, a consignment delivered, a return to store. The ordinariness of it accumulated on the table like ballast. Nothing in the window book hurried. Nothing in it hid. A badge that is being misused by stealth does not ordinarily generate, week by week, a paper trail in three different hands at a public window, each entry witnessed by the traffic of a working stores, and Mira said so, and Reni wrote it down, and Rask, who had been turning the run sheets over one by one with the tips of his fingers, as if the sheets were hot, looked up and said that the run sheets agreed with the book, which in his experience of paper was rarer than anybody in the room would want said aloud in a building full of clerks.

The run sheets told the wharf end of the story. They named the consignments by their marks and their weights, in the wharf manner, and they named the carriers by their badge numbers where the carriers were badged men, and the number that Leyden had spoken in words and Reni had written in words appeared on sheet after sheet in the wharf clerks' figures, against loads of alloy carried under bond between the warehouses and the stores. Mira translated as she read, figure into word, keeping the number in the form the case had adopted for it, and she found a steadiness in the work that steadied her in return, because the cold from the landing read was still in her hands and the pain that was not hers was awake in her back, and paper, patiently read, asks nothing of the body but eyes. On one sheet a wharf clerk had noted a delay, a load held at a gate while a seal was checked, and the note had the unselfconscious completeness of a man recording a nuisance. On another, a carrier had been changed at midday and the change recorded with the reason, a hand injured, though the injured carrier named there was not Mell and the injury was a crush, plainly come by and plainly recorded. The wharf, in its paper, was a place where injuries happened in the open and were written down in the open, and Mira thought of the injury that had not been written down in the open, the bands across a palm and a back, and of the two careful entries in an infirmary record that concerned themselves, of all the entries that might have been made, with the custody of the file. Paper had a conscience in this case, she thought, or at least a memory, and the memory kept pointing, with a clerk's indifference to drama, at the same narrow place. Not the wharf. Not the window. The filing. The places where paper changed hands in quiet rooms, where a second hand could write an entry or a report and set it among honest papers like a false coin among true ones, relying on the honesty of the company to carry it.

The keeper came up to the record room at midday, summoned for his morning round to be taken down properly, and he gave it standing, with his keys in his hand, in the order in which he had walked it. He had found the dust disturbed at the threshold of the requisition records room, he said, and he showed them, with a keeper's pride in a well kept floor, how the dust lay in that corridor, fine and even, renewed by no traffic in the locked hours, so that a single shoe turning at a threshold wrote itself as plainly as a signature. He had found no other disturbance on the floor. No shelf had been touched that he could swear to, no lock but the stairwell's had been turned from inside, no window unlatched. The man had come up, the keeper said, and checked his door, and gone to his landing, and sat. It was, the keeper observed, with the air of a man who has spent forty years watching buildings and the people who pass through them, the behaviour of a man delivering something, not taking something, and he could not say what the something was, and did not pretend to, and Mira entered the observation in the file in his words and marked it as his, because a keeper's forty years are a kind of instrument and instruments should be credited, not borrowed from.

She asked him, last, about the morning, and he told it again without variation, the offer of the open door, the note, the apology, the refusal, polite and complete, and the question the man had asked about the Ninth Office keeping rooms in the building, asked once and not repeated, as if the answer, whatever it had been, had been filed away for use rather than argued with. Two hours he had sat after that, the keeper said, while the building filled and the day began, and he had sat in the hearing of the open stair and the hall and the square, and anybody who has sat two hours in the hearing of his own escape, the keeper said, and not taken it, has come for the other thing, whatever the other thing is. Mira thanked him. Rask, at the door, moved aside for the keeper with a deference that surprised nobody who knew that Rask respected exactly two categories of person, those who carried loads and those who kept keys, and the keeper, who belonged to the second and had the shoulders of the first, went down the corridor with his keys sounding softly, back to the building that had found this man, in the end, by the simple means of locking itself around him and waiting for morning.

## The Collapse

The collapse, when it came, came on paper, in a borrowed record room, with the rain starting again against the canal windows and the man on the landing waiting above them with the patience of a load that has been set down and will not shift until it is lifted properly.

It began with the infirmary record. Reni had requested it by runner before they climbed to the landing, using the authority of the tasking and the shorter authority of Harrand's presence in the building, which between them moved paper at a speed that Leyden, informed later, described as unseemly and then entered in the file without further comment. The record arrived in its jacket in the early afternoon, carried by a clerk who set it down with both hands, and Mira, who had last seen this record's contents in the wharf night infirmary in the course of the work that had ended with a living example walking out of a ward and into the season's lengthening list of costs, opened it on the record room table and turned its pages with her left hand while the cold from the landing read still sat in both of them impartially, like weather that has got indoors.

Most of the record was in the infirmary clerk's ordinary hand, a hand she knew, hurried and humane, abbreviations crowding the margins where the night work pressed. The entries ran in their order. Admission. The hand described, palm and back, bands meeting at the edges, in the clerk's plain professional language that managed, by refusing ornament, to be more affecting than ornament would have been. Dressings. Pain, recorded day by day in the clerk's graded vocabulary, and beside the record of pain, on day after day, the observation that the patient slept badly, ate little, complained of nothing, and showed no fear, an observation the clerk had underlined once, on the fourth day, not as a triumph but as a puzzle, the underlining of a woman who has dressed wounds for years and knows what eleven days of that order of pain ordinarily does to the spirits of the man inside it. Mira read the eleven days through, though she knew them, because the case required the record to be present in the room and not merely in her memory, and because she was thinking, as she read, of [a clerk walking a patient's file out of an infirmary on her ordinary round](/2026/11/19/nightjar-living-example-019/), of Sabel Orry, the bonded chain clerk she had put in the way of an unidentified man on the quay, and of how paper in this season kept leaving rooms in people's hands and arriving in other rooms with consequences attached, and she wanted this paper, for once, to stay on the table and do its work in the open.

Two entries in the record were not in the clerk's hand. Reni had said so in Leyden's room, and Reni was right, and the entries lay now under Mira's eye in the afternoon light, one on the second day and one on the ninth, brief entries, administrative in character, concerning the securing of the record itself, its copying, its custody, the forms by which a patient's file becomes a document that can be requested by offices. They were written upright, carefully, letter by letter, in the hand of a writer who respected the language more than he used it.

Mira set the missing report beside the record, on the left. She set a month of Ferro's stores returns beside the record, on the right. She stood back from the table and let the three papers occupy it like the three possibilities made visible, and she called Rask and Reni to the table, and the clerk who had brought the record, who had stayed by the door in the manner of clerks who sense that their errand is not finished, she called him too, because a comparison of hands wants witnesses the way a measurement wants a second ruler.

Read them, she said. Not the words. The hands.

Rask read them the way he read everything, once, completely, with his arms folded and his judgement withheld behind his face like a dog behind a gate. The clerk read them leaning, with a professional's involuntary attention, his own hand rising once toward the report and stopping itself, as if it wanted to point and had been trained not to. Reni read them last and longest, because Reni had made the claim in the handler's room and was now watching it stand up in public or fall down, and Mira watched Reni's face instead of the papers, because she had already read the papers and because the education of a junior officer, conducted at a table in a borrowed room while a case turned on it, was also part of the work, though it appeared in no file.

The report and the two entries are one hand, Reni said at last, quietly and exactly. Upright. The letters formed separately. The same long stroke on the tall letters. The same pause, you can see it in the ink, before the names. The returns are another hand altogether. Quick, slanted, joined. The man who wrote the returns wrote all day every day. The man who wrote the report and the two entries wrote seldom and carefully. They are not the same man writing differently. They are two men.

The clerk by the door said, before he could stop himself, that the returns were Ferro's, that he had taken papers from Ferro at the window across two years and would know the hand among fifty, and then apologised for speaking, and Mira told him, with a gratitude she did not trouble to hide, that his speaking was now part of the record and that he would be asked to say it again to a person with a notebook, and that he should say it in exactly those words.

So the collapse assembled itself, piece by piece, in the order that collapses prefer, which is the order in which supports are removed and the structure, given no further reason to stand, sits down.

The missing report was not filed by Ferro. That was the first support gone, and it was gone completely, killed by paper and comparison, by the report's hand against the infirmary record's second hand and Ferro's own true hand on his stores returns, which was different, witnessed, and beyond argument. The report was filed by someone who was not the badge holder. Someone, that is, had gone to the proper window in the spring, in the proper form, with the proper words supplied in their proper places, and had reported another man's badge missing, in a hand that the Office had now seen in exactly one other place in all its work, writing two entries in the infirmary record of Vaun Mell, the same second hand that had attended, on the second day and the ninth, to the securing of the record of the man who now sat on the landing with that very badge in his pocket. Mira let the shape of that stand in the room without decorating it. The second hand in the infirmary record was the second hand on the report. The report concerned the badge. The record concerned the man. The man held the badge. The circle was not a circle she intended to close aloud, because a circle closed aloud becomes a claim, and a claim becomes a target, and the case at this hour needed the circle to remain what it was, a set of papers on a table that four witnesses had read with their eyes.

If the report is false, Rask said, into the quiet, in the tone of a man laying a beam across a gap to see whether it reaches, then the badge was never stolen in the way the paper says.

It was never stolen in the way the paper says, Mira said. Say it no larger than that. The paper says a loser, a window, a date, a discovery. Every one of those four may still hold some truth in it somewhere, bent. But the filing was not the holder's filing, and a filing that is not the holder's is not a report of a loss. It is an act performed in the shape of a report. She looked at the careful upright letters, the patience of them, the respect. Somebody wanted this badge to be officially missing from the spring onward. Somebody wanted any future finding of it to arrive with a story already attached, a story in which the man holding it was a thief or a thief's customer and the badge itself was contraband. And somebody, months later, was writing in the same hand in the record of the carrier who held it, attending to the custody of his file. The chain has to be walked again from issue, because the link that was supposed to explain the whole chain, the loss, is the one link in it that is not what it claims to be.

Reni, writing, said without looking up that the stolen door was shut, and Mira corrected her, gently, because precision at the moment of collapse is a form of mercy toward everyone who will read the file later. The stolen door is not shut. It is unpriced no longer, it is priced honestly. A badge can be stolen and a false report filed over the theft by a second party for a second purpose. It can happen. It is merely no longer the cheap explanation it was this morning, and in this office we follow the explanations in the order of their cost, cheapest first, and stolen has moved to the back of the queue behind lent and behind his, and both of those now have to be walked with the same rigour we have just spent on the report. Reni wrote that down too, and Mira saw the young woman's shoulders settle as she wrote, the charter believer accommodating, not without effort, the discovery that a paper could be complete in every particular the charter required and false in the only particular that mattered, which was whose hand had held the pen.

There remained the door.

Mira climbed to the upper corridor alone, in the middle of the afternoon, with Rask posted at the stair's turn within call and Reni below with the papers and the witnesses, and she stood before the door of the requisition records room for the second time that day, and this time she read it. She read it at its anchors in their order, the handle first, bright worn metal, the best of holders, the kind of surface that keeps what hands lay on it for days and gives it back faithfully to a reader who pays the price. She laid her own hand over the place where Mell's hand had been, left hand to left hand's work, and she waited in the cold that was still on her from the landing, and she read.

Nothing.

She read the lock plate, scratched by many keys, metal again, a holder as good as the handle. Nothing, in the same register, the same quality of nothing, not the faint residue of an old handling worn thin by months, not the blurred remainder of a touch that time had eaten, but nothing entire and clean, as if the door had spent the night in a world from which hands were absent. She read the frame, the painted wood, the shoulder polished place at its edge, a poorer holder and she allowed for the poverty, and the frame gave her the building's ordinary traffic, clerks passing, doors opening on other errands, the small change of a working corridor across days, all of it faint and all of it ordinary, and none of it the man on the landing, none of it a checked door in the locked hours, none of it Mell.

A door handled by a man in the state Mell was in should hold something. She stood in the corridor and let the proposition and the fact stand side by side without forcing them to speak to each other, because forcing, she had learned across a season of absences, is how a reader manufactures the reading she came for. Mell had come up the stair in the evening with the decision already forming in him that the landing later recorded so densely. He had stood where she was standing. The keeper's round had found the dust disturbed at the threshold, a shoe's fan on the stone, and Mira's own eyes had confirmed it in the morning. He had taken the handle in his left hand, the hand that worked, and tried it once, gently, and the handle, bright worn metal, the best of holders, held nothing of it at all. Not fear, which would have been strange in him, whose bed had recorded eleven days of pain without it. Not intent, which the landing held in layers. Not even the plain pressure of a man confirming a lock, which is its own small event and leaves its own small mark on metal that keeps marks. Nothing. The door read as nothing when Mira read it, and she took her hand away, and she did not read it a second time, because a second reading of nothing is a temptation and not a method, and she had a private register, now in oversight's keeping under a numbered label, in which nothings of this exact quality had been accumulating all season, in places where something should have been, a binding that left no weight at all, which should have been impossible and was instead becoming, entry by entry, the most consistent fact she owned.

She did not write this door into that register in the corridor. She did not say the word absence to Rask when she came down past him at the turn, though he looked at her face and then, with the tact that made him worth every other quality he lacked, looked away from it. She said that the door held nothing she could use, which was true, and that the landing held a decision, which was true, and that the paper held a false report, which was true and witnessed, and that the case, therefore, was no longer about how a badge had travelled. It was about a man who had been given a badge and a trade and a set of words for both, who had checked a door he could not open and sat down on a landing he would not leave, and who could not be made to talk by any gift she possessed, because weight cannot compel speech and cannot test it, cannot reach into a man's mouth and sort his sentences into true and false like coins. She had known that limit all her working life. She stood in the stairwell in the afternoon cold, hers and the read's together, with the carried pain waking in her back as she shifted her weight from one foot to the other, and she considered the limit without resentment, almost with gratitude, because what it left her was the only instrument that had ever worked on men like Mell in any case she could name. Patience. Silence, kept in the right place for the right length of time, by a person who has established, on paper and in stone, that she already knows the shape of the story and is waiting, without appetite and without threat, for the man himself to supply its small remaining contents.

He will talk or he will not, Rask said.

He has been deciding all night, Mira said. The landing told me that much, and the landing does not lie, because it cannot. Men decide toward something. We have spent the day making sure that when he finishes deciding, the room he decides in is a room where what he says can be checked. Small things, checkable against paper. That is all I am asking the rest of this day to produce.

Below them the building went on locking its doors in their order as the light failed, office by office, corridor by corridor, the keeper's keys turning in the ordinary sequence that had, the night before, made a siege out of a sit by the simple act of being ordinary in a stairwell. Rask went down to arrange the stair for what would come next, the clearing of it, the sending out of everyone whose presence would flatten what the landing still held or crowd a man who would not talk to three. Reni stayed with the papers, which now included a comparison witnessed by four people and a chain walked twice, once forward in the morning and once, in the collapse of its central link, backward from the pocket to the window. And Mira stood a while longer in the corridor before the door that held nothing, her hand at her side cooling and cooling, and let the nothing stand beside everything else the day had established, unexplained, unforced, one notch of a thing she was not going to name in this building on this day, while above her, beyond the turn, the man on the landing sat in the gathering dark of the stairwell and went on, patiently, deciding.


They walked the chain again from issue before the light went, because Mira wanted the walking done while the witnesses were still in the building and the books still on the table, and because a chain walked once is an argument and a chain walked twice, in the opposite direction, is a test. Reni read the entries back from the file copy, beginning at the pocket and ending at the window, and at each entry Mira set the original paper beside the copy and the room compared them, the keeper's day book, the run sheets, the window book, the issue book, the report, the record, a table full of paper that had begun the day as six separate custodies and had become, by the slow work of comparison, a single account with a single wound in it. At the pocket, the keeper's record, sound. At the wharf, the run sheets, sound, week on week of open carrying under bond. At the window, the signing out and the signing in, sound, in three hands that all agreed with one another. At the issue, the book, sound, a named clerk and a lawful purpose. And between the window and the wharf, where the loss should have stood like a gate through which the badge had passed from law into theft, the report, unsound, written by a stranger to the holder in a hand that had also written, months later and streets away, two entries in the infirmary record of the man in whose pocket the badge had come to rest. The chain did not break when they walked it backward. It did something worse, from the point of view of anyone who had relied on the report. It held everywhere except at the link that had been manufactured, and a chain that holds everywhere except at one manufactured link is telling its examiners, in the plainest language paper possesses, where the work was done and what the work was for. The badge had not been lost and found. The badge had been declared lost, on paper, by a person who needed it to be losable, while it went on working in the open, and the declaration had waited in its file, patient as everything in this case was patient, for the day when the badge would be found somewhere compromising and the story would already be written.

Reni asked, when the books were closed, whether Ferro should be sent for, and Mira said no, and explained it for the file as much as for the junior officer, because the reasoning was the kind that looks like inaction from outside and is, from inside, a form of protection extended to a man who had not asked for it and might never know he had received it. Ferro does not appear in this case in person. He appears in his hand, on his returns, month on month, quick and slanted and honest, and his hand has already given the only testimony he has to give, which is that he did not write the report of his own loss. To send for him would be to put a stores clerk into rooms with the Ninth Office and the Commissioner on the same day, and to mark him, in the eyes of every watcher whose existence the season had taught them to assume, as a man of interest in a matter where his sole service had been to keep clean books. If Ferro is wanted later, he can be wanted later, when the wanting will cost him less. Rask said that this was the first time he had heard the Office decline to interview a man on the grounds that the man's handwriting was too honest, and Mira said that the Office declined nothing, that it sequenced everything, and that Ferro's turn, if it came, would come after the turn of the man whose hand was on both the report and the record, whoever that man might prove to be, and that no name for that man existed anywhere in the papers on the table, and that the absence of the name was itself to be recorded exactly, as an absence of a name and not as an invitation to supply one.

She had the comparison set down in a fair copy before the clerks went home, four witnesses named, the hands described by their qualities and not by any flourish, the conclusion stated at the size the evidence would carry and not a fraction larger. The report was not filed by the holder. The two entries and the report were one hand. The holder's true hand was different, and was attested. Nothing else was concluded. Nothing about who the second hand belonged to, nothing about why the report had been filed, nothing about the door, which stood in a separate paragraph of the fair copy recorded as read and as holding nothing, with the method of the reading stated and the reader's name under it, so that whoever came after her, in oversight or in the Office or in some later season altogether, would find the nothing documented with the same care as the somethings, and would not be able to say that the Office had hidden the shape of its own ignorance. Reni made the copy. Her hand, Mira noticed, had steadied across the day, the neat junior script settling into its lines like a boat settling into its berth, and she thought that whatever else the case cost, it had already paid one small dividend, in a borrowed record room, to a young officer who had learned in a single afternoon that a complete paper can be a false one and that the charter she believed in was not diminished by the discovery, because the charter, properly read, had never asked her to believe papers. It had asked her to keep them, compare them, and say plainly what the comparison showed.

When the fair copy was done, Mira went up alone as far as the turn below the landing and stood where Rask would stand when the stair was cleared, and she looked up at the man she could not yet see around the turn of the stone, and she rehearsed, not words, because words rehearsed for a man like Mell would arrive dead, but the order of her own knowledge, the things she could lay out in stillness when the time came, in the order in which a fair chance requires them to be laid. The badge, and its five custodies, from a window in the bonded quarter to a pocket on a landing. The missing report, in the wrong hand. The second hand, again, in the infirmary record, where it had attended to the custody of his file. Eleven days of pain without fear, which the record attested and which his own bed, read in its season, had attested before it. She held the four things in their order and found that they made, together, neither an accusation nor an absolution, but a shape with one opening in it, exactly the size of the small things only Mell could supply. Who gave him the badge. Under what paper he was paid. Whose voice, and it would be a badged voice, had told him to check a door and wait to be asked. The shape did not need her gift to complete it. It needed his speech, freely given or not given at all, and the recognition, which she carried back down the stair with her into the lamplit record room, that the day's work had not been the gathering of evidence against a man but the building of a room, out of paper and stone and witnessed comparison, in which a man's small truth, when he finally set it down, would be recognisable as the truth because everything around it had already been made honest.

## Pressure

The lock turned at the foot of the stairwell.

The sound came up the stone well clean and final. A key in a heavy plate. A bolt drawn across. Then the keeper's steps going away on the lower floor, unhurried, because for him the building was only closing for the night in the ordinary way it always closed.

Mira stood where the stairs narrowed before the first turn and listened until the steps were gone.

Above her the landing was quiet. Mell had not moved when the lock turned. He had not called down. He had not tried the door again. The quiet had a held quality to it, the kind that came from a man listening as hard as she was.

Rask stood below her on the last tread before the floor. Reni stood beside him, too close to the rail, her hand resting on it without thinking, the way young officers touched things to steady themselves. Mira could feel both of them waiting for the shape of what came next to be given to them.

She gave it plainly.

She told Rask to take Reni down through the side passage before the keeper barred it as well, and to wait on the lower floor where the interview rooms opened off the hall, and to come up only if she called by name. She told Reni to carry the file down with her, the whole file, badge chain and all, and to keep it closed and keep it dry.

Reni began to ask whether one officer should go alone up a locked stair to a man who would not come down.

Mira answered before the question finished forming. He would not talk to three. He had sat for two hours making one decision over and over, and three faces at the turn of the stairs would make the decision for him in the wrong direction. One person he could refuse. One person he could also, in the end, choose to answer.

Rask understood the other reason without her saying it, and she said it anyway, quietly. A Stiller on the stairs would flatten what was left on the landing. She still needed what the rail and the tread held, undisturbed. If Rask came up, his gift would settle over the stone like a hand pressed flat on a page, and the page would go blank under it, kind and useless.

Rask nodded once. He put his hand on Reni's sleeve and turned her toward the passage. Their steps went down and away. A door opened and closed somewhere below. Then the stairwell held only her and the man above her.

She went up slowly. The stairs were stone and worn in the middle of each tread, and the damp of Carrowmere lived in them. Her boots found each tread whole before she trusted it. She kept one hand clear of the rail, not wanting her own passage muddled into what the rail already held.

The pain she carried woke as she climbed. It had been with her a month now, somebody else's chronic pain taken from a coat in Valtessa, and it woke and settled with her own movements until she could not always tell it from her own body. It lived low in her back and down through her left side and into the arm on that side, not sharp, never sharp. It simply taxed every movement a little, and the next movement a little more, until the account came due in small delays.

She had entered the building reduced and she had known it, and by the turn below the landing she was low, low in strength and low in reserve, and the knowledge sat in her as a fact to be managed, not a grievance to be nursed.

She stopped at the turn, where the landing above was still hidden by the angle of the wall, and spoke first. She said his name, Vaun Mell, at ordinary volume, the way a clerk might say it across a counter.

Nothing answered.

She said the building was locked for the night, the keeper gone, the lower doors barred, and the only way down now was past her and by arrangement, or by waiting until morning in a stairwell that would get colder by the hour. She said she was alone, and the others had gone down, and she had nowhere else to be that night, which was the whole truth of her orders.

Still nothing. But the quiet changed. She heard a boot shift on stone, a heel settling. A man who had decided not to listen did not adjust his footing to hear better.

She went up the last flight.

The landing opened before her as landings in such buildings always did, a widening of the stair rather than a room, stone floored, railed on the open side, with the door of the requisition records room set in the far wall and one high window giving onto nothing but the night and the rain that had begun again outside, fine and steady against the glass. The light was a single caged lamp at the turn, and it left the far end of the landing in a dimness that was not darkness but was enough for a man to feel less looked at than he would have felt under a brighter bulb.

Mell sat with his back against the wall beside the door he had no right to open, the door that had given her nothing when its handle and lock plate and frame were read, though she did not dwell on that now. His knees were drawn up. His hands rested on his knees, open, the scarred one uppermost, the bands across palm and back meeting at the edges of the hand in the pattern she already knew from Sabin Roche. His coat and hair were damp from his walk across the city, because he had come here on his own feet and by his own choice, a fact that mattered more than any lock in the building. A man who came by choice could also speak by choice, if the speaking was made bearable.

His eyes went past her, once, to the empty stairs below, checking what she had told him. Something in his face eased and tightened at the same time, relief and the loss of an excuse in a single movement.

She sat down on the top tread, a little below the level of the landing, with her legs drawn to one side so the passage remained, in principle, open. She was in the way the way a person sitting on a step is in the way, present and undeniable, but she had not barred the stairs like a guard. The distinction mattered. She felt him register it.

She said she was not there to take him anywhere that night, and an interview room below was ready and would still be ready in an hour. She said the badge in his pocket had a history paper could tell without him. What she did not have was any reason for a carrier to walk across Carrowmere in the rain to check a locked door in a building he was entitled to enter and then sit down beside it and refuse the stairs.

Mell looked at the door. He looked at it the way a man looks at a thing he has already finished with and has not finished with at all.

He said the door should have been open. His voice was rough from disuse, but the words were formed with an education that sat oddly on a wharf labourer, a man repeating, perhaps, words he had been given until the words had worn grooves in him. His instructions had said the room would be open at the hour he came, and the crates would be listed inside, and he would sign and carry as he always signed and carried. When the door was locked he had thought the hour was wrong, then the day. Then the lock had turned below him and he had understood that going down made him a man found in a locked building beside a door he could not explain, and staying made him the same man, only later and colder, and he had sat down to think and the thinking had taken two hours and had come to nothing.

She did not answer at once. Patience was the craft, and she let him hear his own account settle in the air between them without her hand on it.

He said he had done nothing that was not carrying. He said it to the door rather than to her.

She said carrying was exactly what she wanted to understand, who gave the crates and under what authority the whole plain transaction proceeded, because plain transactions did not usually end with a man afraid to come down a stair.

He flinched at the word afraid, a small and honest movement, and she set it beside what she already held, that in eleven days in the wharf night infirmary, in pain throughout, he had by the read of his bed never been afraid, and she wondered what had changed on this landing that the infirmary had not held.

Time went on the landing by small surrenders of comfort. The cold deepened through her coat and through the tread under her. Her left arm, when she shifted it, answered with the small delay she had been tracking all evening, a fraction behind her intention, the carried pain taxing the signal between wanting and moving. She flexed the hand once, slowly, and made a private adjustment to every plan she had for the next hour. If she had to move quickly, she would move from her right side and from her legs and from the architecture of the place. She would not trust the left arm to arrive on time.

Mell watched her flex the hand. His gaze went, unwillingly, she thought, to his own scarred hand on his knee. Two damaged instruments on a landing, each taking the measure of the other. Neither of them remarked on it.

He said, at last, that he would go down if she would walk in front of him. He said he did not want hands on him on the stairs. He said he did not want the Stiller below to be in the room, at first, because the Stiller made his thoughts go flat and he wanted his thoughts to be his own when he said what he had to say. He said it as a set of conditions, carefully, as if he had been composing them for some time, which, she understood, he had. Two hours of deciding had produced, at the end of them, a list.

She considered the conditions where he could watch her consider them. The Stiller absent at first was a difficulty, because Rask's absence from a confession would be used, by oversight among others, as a gap in the record. But a confession refused altogether was a larger gap, and Leyden and Harrand would both be there. She said Rask would wait outside the room at first and come in for the formal statement. She said no hands would be laid on him on the stairs unless he laid hands first. She said she would walk in front of him, and that was as much choreography as she would agree to in advance.

He nodded. He began, with the deliberation of a man whose legs had gone stiff with sitting on stone, to get to his feet.

That was when he changed his mind, or when his body changed it for him. Half risen, with the door at his shoulder and the drop of the stairwell suddenly present to him through the rail and the cold air coming up the well, he looked down past her at the flight and the turn and the flight below, and whatever he saw there was not an escorted walk to a warm room but the whole apparatus of his capture arranged in stone, and he moved.

He did not move at her. The distinction held even in the moment itself and she recorded it even as she rose, because it would matter later and because it was the truth. He moved through the place where she stood, toward the stairs as toward a door that had to be reached before it closed, his shoulder coming round, his scarred hand coming up not as a fist but as a man puts a hand out to push past an obstruction in a crowd on a quay, to make a passage where no passage had been left him.

She stopped him with the stair itself.

She did not strike him. She did not grapple for the scarred hand or for his coat or for any hold that would have made a contest of strength out of it, a contest her depleted state would have taxed past fairness. She stepped, from her right side as she had resolved to do, not back and not forward but across, so that the turn of the landing and the angle of the rail came between his momentum and the flight. She put the rail at his hip by putting herself at the rail. She let the tread do the rest. His forward weight, committed to a passage that no longer existed, found the low stone lip where the landing met the top tread, and she turned him, with her right hand flat between his shoulder blades and her body braced against the rail post, along the wall and away from the drop, the way water is turned by a bank rather than stopped by a wall.

It was brief. It was unshowy. There was no blow and no fall. But in the middle of it her left arm was needed, needed to catch at his sleeve as he slewed toward the rail gap, and she asked it to move and it answered half a second late, half a second of dead water between the asking and the arriving, because the month of pain she carried had taxed that arm past its honest speed and the bill came due exactly when the bill always comes due, at the worst moment the evening had. Her hand closed on his sleeve late and therefore harder than she intended, and the jerk of it checked him roughly against the wall, and she heard the breath go out of him in a sound that was pain and surprise together, and she was sorry for the roughness even as she held the position until his weight settled and his feet found the landing again and stayed there.

Then it was over. He was sitting again, slid down the wall by degrees she did not hasten, until he sat where he had sat before, beside the door, with his back against the stone and his scarred hand cradled now in the other hand, not because she had hurt the hand, she had not touched it, but because a man who has been turned and checked and has spent the last of his resolve holds the damaged part of himself as a child holds it, without thinking.

Her own breath was short. The delay in the arm had frightened her more than his rush had, and she made herself breathe until the fright subsided into information. Cost, not damage. Nothing in him was broken, and nothing in her was newly broken either. But a margin had been spent that would not come back that night, and she knew her state at the pressure of the case for what it was, low and now lower, and that everything after this had to be done with words, because words were what she had left in reserve.

Mell said, from the floor, that he was sorry. He had not meant her harm. The stairs had looked like the inside of a trap when he stood up.

She said the offer stood as before, the room below, the walk in front, no hands unless he ran. She added one thing only. She would not turn him a second time, because she was not certain the stair would be as gentle twice, and neither, she thought, was he.

He laughed then, a short exhausted sound with some recognition in it, and pushed himself upright slowly, keeping to the wall, and waited for her to go first down the flight as agreed.

She went first. She went down slowly, from her right side, with her good hand trailing the rail and the carried pain riding every tread, and she heard him follow at the distance she had promised him, step for step, a carrier following an officer down out of a locked stairwell toward a room where, at last, he had decided to talk.

## The Silence

Before any of that could be carried down the stairs and into a room, there was the stillness on the landing, and the stillness had work in it. She sat on the top tread with the cold coming through the stone and said nothing for a long time, and in the nothing she laid out everything the reader of this case already held and everything Mell did not know was held.

She held the badge first. An Accord badge, carried in his pocket onto the landing, its number spoken in words at the tasking and its recorded holder named from the issue book, a stores clerk who did not stand in the stairwell and never had. The badge had let him into the building in ordinary hours without question, and up a stairwell he had every right to be in as a badged employee. It had brought him to a door he had no right to open, and there it had stopped being a key and become a question.

She held the chain of the badge in its five entries. Issued to the clerk at the stores window, against his name. Signed out against a requisition for bonded carrying work, so that its absence from the stores had authority. Carried on the wharf carrying runs, seen in use. Reported missing in the spring, in a hand that was not the holder's, the entry that looked like an ending and was instead the hinge of the whole case. Found, at the last, in the pocket of Vaun Mell on the landing, beside a locked door. Together they described a badge that had travelled by permission at every step except the step where permission was reported to have ended, and that report was the one piece of paper in the chain that lied about who had written it.

She held the report itself, produced at the tasking, dated in the spring and fitting the facts it was meant to fit, and beside it the hand that had written it. Not the clerk's hand. The clerk's true hand stood on his stores returns, unpractised and consistent. The hand on the report was a second hand, fluent and disguised only in its claim to be routine, and it was a hand the Office had seen before, because the same second hand had written two entries in Mell's infirmary record. It did not mean she knew whose hand it was, or why. Paper could kill a false lead, and had. Paper could not yet walk the chain backward to a face.

She held the eleven days. Eleven days at the wharf night infirmary, in pain throughout, and by the read of his bed never afraid in all that time. A man could hide fear from nurses and from clerks, but not from the bed he lay in, and the bed had given her endurance without fear. He had believed he was inside a system that would hold him. His fear had come later, beside a locked door, when the paper failed to open what it had promised.

She held the landing itself in a clause, already read at its anchors, a man sitting for two hours deciding, and the door in a clause only, that it had given her nothing at all, and she let that nothing stand at the edge of the laying out without leaning on it.

She held, last, the limit of her own gift, plainly. Weight could not compel speech, and could not test speech for truth. She could not read Mell as she read a rail or a bed, and anything he said below would stand or fall by paper and by checking. Patience would have to do the work that power could not, and patience, on the evidence of two hours on a landing, was a currency Mell himself understood.

That was the whole of it. Badge and chain and false report and second hand, endurance without fear and fear arriving late, a decision rehearsed for two hours and a gift that could not force the deciding. She laid it out without speaking any of it, because speaking it would have made it an accusation he could refuse. When she spoke at last, she asked him whether the cold had got into his hand, and whether he wanted it seen to before or after he talked, and the smallness of the question, arriving after so long a quiet, was the fair chance itself.

## Close

The interview room was on the lower floor, off the hall where the keeper's office stood, and it had been chosen by oversight rather than by the Office, a fact Leyden mentioned once on the stairs and did not labour. It was a plain room and a warm one after the stairwell, with a stove ticking in the corner and a table scarred by years of files and cups, and four chairs, and a window whose blind was drawn against the night and the rain. The warmth reached Mira slowly. It reached her coat first and then her hands, and it did nothing at all for the deeper tiredness that the landing had spent out of her, which warmth could not repay.

Harrand was already in the room when they came down.

Commissioner Ilse Harrand sat at the far side of the table with her notebook closed in front of her and her pen laid across it, in the posture of a woman who had authorised an interview and intended to be able to say afterwards exactly what she had heard in it. Leyden stood until Mira entered, then took the chair at the table's end, between oversight and the Office in the seating as in everything else, his own file open, his own pen ready. Rask, by the terms agreed on the landing, waited outside the door. Mira heard him settle there, a partner reduced to a presence in a corridor, and she felt the irregularity of it and accepted it as the price Mell had set and she had agreed to pay.

Mell sat where she indicated, on the near side, with his back to the door and the stove at his left. He had asked, on the last flight, whether the scarred hand had to be shown again, and she had said no, that the hand was in the record and the record was in the room in the file Reni had carried down, and that no one would ask him to perform his injury for the table. He had nodded at that with a gratitude out of proportion to the concession, and she understood that the hand had been looked at by many people in eleven days and that looking had become its own small injury, repeated by officials.

Harrand opened the proceedings without warmth and without hostility, which was her manner and, in its way, its own fairness. She stated for the record that she had authorised the interview. She stated that Mell was present voluntarily, having come down from the landing of his own decision. She stated that he might stop at any point and that stopping would be recorded and not argued with. Leyden wrote. Mira, who had brought the man down, said nothing in the preliminaries, because the room was not hers and the sooner Mell understood whose room it was, the sooner he would understand what his speaking there meant and what it would cost everyone at the table, herself included.

Then Harrand looked at Mell and asked him, simply, what he did for a living.

It was a better opening than Mira would have chosen and she acknowledged it privately, because it asked for the smallest true thing first.

Mell said he carried crates.

He said it to the table, to the scarred wood between his hands, which lay flat and open on either side of nothing, no cup, no paper, only his hands and the table. He said he carried crates on the wharf and between bonded warehouses and, when the requisition called for it, inside Accord buildings where a badged carrier was wanted for loads that clerks were not to lift. He said the crates were heavy and sometimes hot in the way that newly worked metal is hot through its packing, and that the work had marked his hand in the course of an ordinary lift gone wrong, a crate settling, a band of packing metal taking his grip across palm and back before he could shed the load. He said the work was paid work and the pay was regular and the regularity had mattered to him more than the amount, though he did not name any amount, and no one at the table asked him to.

Leyden asked, mildly, by whom he was paid.

Mell said he was paid by an Accord requisition. He said the pay came against a requisition number, as all his engagements came, the number heading the paper that authorised the carrying and the paper that released the pay, the same number at both ends of the work like a thread through a seam. He said he had never been paid in any other way. He said he had never been paid by a person.

The room was very quiet. The stove ticked. Leyden's pen moved and stopped and moved again.

Harrand asked who engaged him. She asked it in the tone of a woman asking for a name she expected to be given, a foreman, a clerk, a face at a desk, the ordinary human machinery of employment.

Mell shook his head, not in refusal but in correction, as if the question had been aimed slightly beside the thing it sought. He said he had never met anyone who was not also an employee. Every instruction had come on paper or from a badged mouth. A stores clerk had handed him a requisition and pointed him to a stack. A warehouse keeper had signed his sheet and pointed him to a door. A night porter had waved him through a gate he was badged to pass. He said there was no stranger in his story at all. He searched, visibly, for the kind of figure the room seemed to want from him, a man in a doorway, a voice without a badge, a payment without paper, and he reported, with something like apology, that he could not supply one because there had never been one to supply. His whole working life in the alloy trade, as he laid it on the table in that warm room, was Accord paper and Accord badges and Accord doors, one of which, on this one night, had been locked when the paper said it would be open.

Mira believed the smallness of it was the point and let the belief sit without expression. A confession that produced a stranger would have been a gift to everyone, a direction to ride in, a name to arrest before morning. This confession produced a system. It produced a carrier whose innocence and guilt were alike beside the question, because the question was never really Mell. The question was the paper that had moved him around the city like a crate himself, authorised at every step, and had then failed to open a door and had left him sitting beside the failure for two hours, deciding.

Harrand asked about the badge. Her pen was in her hand now, though her notebook remained closed, a detail Mira registered and filed.

Mell said the badge had come to him with a requisition, handed over at a stores window by a clerk whose name he had never known, because carriers did not ask the names of clerks who were only doing what the paper told them to do. He said he had understood it to be issued for the work and returnable at the end of the engagement, and that the engagement had never ended in the tidy way engagements were supposed to end, one requisition succeeding another, so that returning the badge had never presented itself as a step anyone asked for. He said he had not known it was reported missing. He said the word missing with care, as if testing whether it could possibly apply to a thing that had been in his pocket, in use, in the open, on the wharf and at the gates, for all the time the report claimed it was gone. He said a thing seen every day by badged men could not be missing in any sense that mattered, and then he stopped, hearing perhaps how the sentence sounded in a room full of the people who owned the paper, and he looked down at his hands.

Leyden asked whether he would give the requisition number under which he was engaged, the number heading the paper that had sent him to the locked door that night.

Mell gave it.

He gave it slowly, digit by digit as words, the way men who work from paper learn to give numbers so that no clerk can mishear them, and the number was nine, four, two, seven, one, spoken into the quiet of the interview room without emphasis and without any understanding, Mira thought, of what he was handing over. It was only his pay number to him. It was the authorisation of his presence in the building and the authorisation of the crates on his back and the authorisation, by extension, of the hot band of packing metal across his hand. He gave it as a man gives the time when asked, a fact he happened to be carrying.

Leyden wrote it in the Office file. The pen scratched. The number entered the Office's keeping in the ordinary way, in a handler's hand, on a handler's page, where the Office could hold it and sequence it and choose, as it always chose, the moment and the manner of its use.

And in the same minute the Office lost it.

That was the cost, and Mira named it plainly to herself as it happened at the table, because costs unnamed have a way of being paid twice. Harrand had heard the confession first hand. She had heard the crates and the requisition and the badged mouths and the absence of any stranger, and she had heard the number spoken digit by digit in a room she had authorised, in proceedings she had opened, under a statement of voluntariness she had made for the record in her own voice. The Office could not hold the thread, could not soften it, could not sequence it, could not decide that oversight would learn of it next week in a summary shaped by the Office's judgment. Oversight already knew. The thread ran through Harrand's hearing as directly as it ran through Leyden's file, and whatever the requisition number opened, it would now be opened by two keepers at once, one of whom believed the Ninth Office should not exist and was sometimes right. The Office had got its confession and lost control of it in the same minute, and the two events were not a bargain struck but a single fact seen from two sides, like a hand scarred across palm and back in bands that meet at the edges.

No one at the table said any of that aloud. Harrand thanked Mell, formally, for his account. Leyden set out the formal statement for signature, and Rask was called in from the corridor for the signing, entering with the file and with an expression that closed over his opinion of the arrangement the moment he saw Harrand's face. Mell signed where he was shown to sign, in a hand that was his own, unpractised and consistent, nothing like the fluent second hand that had written him into an infirmary record and a badge out of a stores. He asked whether he would be kept overnight. Harrand said that was a matter for the Office and the building keeper, and looked at Leyden, and Leyden said he would be kept comfortably and seen by a surgeon for the hand in the morning and charged with nothing that night, which was as much mercy as the paper allowed and Mell received it as such.

Mira walked with him as far as the room where he was to wait, because she had promised on the stairs that no hands would be laid on him and she intended the promise to be seen kept in front of Rask and the keeper alike. At the door of the waiting room he stopped and said, without looking at her, that the crates had only ever been crates to him, and she said she knew, and he went in, and the door closed on him gently, an unlocked door, which seemed to her a better ending for his night than the locked one he had sat beside for so long.

## The Notch

She found Leyden in his room an hour later, with the file open and the night's work squared away in his handler's order, and Harrand's requirements already listed on a separate sheet in his hand, because oversight wasted no time when oversight had been present at the making of the evidence.

He told her what would happen next in the order it would happen. The requisition would be traced through the requisitions books. The stores returns would be called for again, all of them, not only the clerk's. The infirmary record would be copied under seal, because a hand that appeared in two places would be looked for in a third. Rask would take the wharf end of it in the morning. Reni would take the paper end, under supervision, because the girl believed the charter exactly as written and paper was where exact belief was an asset rather than a wound. Mira would rest. He said the last item without looking up, and she received it as the order it was dressed as advice to resemble.

She asked what Harrand had taken away from the room.

Leyden said oversight had taken away what oversight had heard, which was everything, and that there was no longer any question of the Office presenting the matter in its own time. He said Harrand had been fair in the room and would be fair on paper, and that fairness from that quarter was a heavier instrument than favour, because it could not be appealed against as prejudice. He turned a page of the file toward her, not for signature but for sight. The requisition number stood at the head of the statement copy in his hand, nine, four, two, seven, one, and in her own notes it would live as words still, nine four two seven one, a thread of sound rather than a row of marks.

The notch, when she set it down in her own reckoning on the walk back through the building, was a single step and she was careful to take no more than the one step the evidence had earned. A rung had been reached that night and it held her weight when she tested it, which not every rung in this season had done. The man with the injury held an Accord badge and was paid through an Accord requisition. That was the whole of it. Not who had arranged the holding. Not whose hand had written the false report or the infirmary entries, though a hand there plainly was. Not what lay behind the requisition chain or who stood, if anyone stood, at the head of it issuing paper downward through badged mouths until it reached a carrier's scarred hands. The badge and the requisition. A carrier inside the Accord's own paper, moved by the Accord's own authority, injured in the Accord's own work, sitting at the end of it beside an Accord door that would not open for him. One notch. The season's long question about the absences she had been registering, the register she had given voluntarily to Harrand and that now rested in oversight's keeping as an Accord document under its numbered label, did not advance that night by so much as a hair, and she let it not advance, because a notch honestly taken was worth more than a leap that would have to be given back.

The carried question, the one question that walked out of the installment unresolved and by design, was not a person. It was not Mell, who was sleeping by then, she hoped, in the waiting room with his hand dressed afresh. It was not the clerk whose name stood in the issue book and whose true hand had cleared him of the report that bore his loss. It was not the second hand, though the second hand would be hunted. The carried question was a requisition number, nine four two seven one, a thread that now ran in two directions at once, into the Office file and into oversight's keeping, and wherever it led when the books were opened, both keepers would arrive there together whether either of them wished it or not.

She passed the interview room on her way to the stairs, meaning to look once at the landing's door from below and to think about trains of paper in the quiet, and the interview room door stood open and the stove had been damped and Harrand was still there.

Harrand sat alone at the far side of the table where she had sat for the confession, with the room empty around her and the blind still drawn, and her notebook, which had stayed closed through the whole of Mell's account, was open now at last in front of her, and her pen was in her hand and moving, unhurried, in the lamplight, writing the requisition number down in her own notebook, nine, four, two, seven, one, word by word in her own hand, while Mira stood in the doorway and watched her do it.

## Case File

### Q: How is an Accord badge issued at the stores window and entered in the issue book?
An Accord badge is issued only at the stores window of the building to which it belongs, and only to a person whose name already appears on the employment roll for that building or for work directed from it. The clerk checks the roll, records the name of the holder, the work for which the badge is required, and the date of issue in words in the issue book, and requires the holder to sign beside the entry in his own hand. The badge is then handed across the window in the presence of the clerk, and no badge leaves the store without that entry being completed first and noted as complete.

### Q: What procedure governs the recovery of an Accord badge found in the possession of a person other than its recorded holder?
When a badge is found in the possession of a person other than its recorded holder, the officer who receives it does not return it at once to either party. The badge is placed in a sealed packet, the place and manner of its finding are written on the packet by the receiving officer, and the packet is lodged with the stores clerk of the issuing building pending inquiry. The recorded holder is notified through his place of work, and the issue book is marked to show that the badge is held and not in circulation. No new badge is issued to replace it until the custody inquiry has closed and the marking has been formally cleared.

### Q: How is payment made under an Accord requisition for bonded carrying work?
Payment for bonded carrying work is made only against a requisition raised in advance of the work and bearing a number quoted in full on every paper that follows from it. The carrier is engaged for the run named in the requisition, the load is checked out of the bonded warehouse against that number, and the receiving clerk signs for its arrival at the place named. The signed papers return to the requisitions office, where the number is matched against the original raising before any payment is authorised. Payment is then made to the carrier named or to the gang master named in the requisition, and the requisition is closed in the book.

### Q: Who may authorise an interview in an Accord building, and who may attend it?
An interview in an Accord building may be authorised by the controller responsible for the case concerned, or by an officer of oversight acting within her own authority over Accord paper and premises. The authorisation is given in writing before the interview begins and names the person to be interviewed, the officers who will conduct it, and any other person permitted to be present. No person attends who is not named in that authorisation, and a person admitted as an observer does not question the person interviewed unless the authorisation expressly allows it. The authorisation is filed with the record of the interview and remains part of the case paper.

### Q: What must a report of a missing Accord badge contain before it is accepted as filed?
A report of a missing badge is accepted as filed only when it gives the name of the recorded holder, the building from which the badge was issued, the work for which it was held, and the circumstances in which its loss was discovered, each stated plainly and without abbreviation. The holder must sign the report in his own hand, and the receiving clerk must countersign it and enter its receipt in the day book on the same shift. A report signed by another person on the holder's behalf, or lacking the holder's signature, is held as a notice only and does not alter the standing of the badge in the issue book.

### Q: How is access to the stairwells of an Accord records building controlled during ordinary hours and after locking?
During ordinary hours the stairwells of a records building stand open to badged employees moving between floors on the business of the building, and no separate permission is required for that movement. Doors opening from a stairwell into restricted rooms remain locked, and a badge gives no right to open them. When locking for the night begins, the keeper locks the foot of each stairwell in turn and enters the hour in the locking log, after which no person may pass up or down without the keeper in attendance. A person found above a locked stair foot is brought down by the keeper or remains where he is by the keeper's direction until officers attend.

### Q: What duties does a stores clerk owe in respect of badges held in store and badges in circulation?
A stores clerk keeps the unissued badges of his building under lock in the store, separate from badges returned for holding, and accounts for both classes at each shift change against the issue book. He receives badges signed out and badges signed back, checks each return against its entry, and reports any badge overdue, damaged, or returned by a hand other than the holder named. He does not issue a badge on a verbal request, does not alter an entry after it has been signed, and refers any dispute about custody to the controller of the building rather than deciding it at the window himself in every such case and without exception.

### Q: How is handwriting compared when a paper record is suspected of having been written by a hand other than the hand it bears?
Comparison begins with paper known to be genuine, gathered from ordinary records made in the course of duty rather than from samples written for the purpose. The examiner lays the questioned writing beside the genuine writing and compares the formation of letters, the spacing of words, the pressure of the pen, and the habits of abbreviation and punctuation that a writer repeats without thinking. A single resemblance proves nothing, and a conclusion requires agreement across many small habits together with the absence of the questioned writer's true habits. The finding is written as an opinion with its grounds stated, and the papers compared are listed so that another examiner may test it.

### Q: What custody paper accompanies an Accord badge when it is signed out against a requisition for carrying work?
When a badge is signed out against a requisition, the signing out is recorded on a custody slip that travels with the work rather than with the badge itself. The slip gives the holder name, the requisition number in words, the warehouse from which the load is to be carried, and the place to which it is to be taken, and it is signed by the issuing clerk and by the person receiving the badge. On completion of the work the slip is returned with the receiving clerk signature for the load, and it is filed with the requisition so that badge, load, and payment can be traced together and checked together thereafter.

### Q: How is a carrier engaged for wharf carrying runs, and under whose direction does he work while so engaged?
A carrier is engaged for wharf work through the gang master or warehouse foreman who holds the requisition for the run, and his name is entered on the run paper before the load leaves the warehouse. While engaged he works under the direction of that foreman as to route, order of loads, and place of delivery, and under the warehouse rules as to seals, weights, and the handling of bonded goods. He has no authority to vary the destination, to open a sealed load, or to engage another man in his place. His engagement ends when the run paper is signed complete, and any further work requires a fresh entry.

### Q: Who may enter a requisition records room, and how is each entry into that room recorded?
Entry to a requisition records room is limited to the clerks assigned to that room, the controller of the building, and officers admitted by written authorisation naming the papers to be consulted. The door is kept locked, and each opening is entered in the room book with the name of the person entering, the purpose of the entry, and the hour of leaving. Papers are consulted within the room and are removed only against a receipt signed in the book and cleared on their return. A badge alone gives no right of entry, and an unauthorised attempt to open the door is reported to the keeper and entered in the day book.

### Q: How is an Accord building locked for the night, and how is the locking recorded so that it may later be proved?
Locking begins at the hour set for the building and proceeds in a fixed order, from the outer doors inward and from the ground floor upward, so that no locked door cuts off a person still lawfully inside from a way down and out. The keeper tries each door after locking it, calls the stairwells and landings as he passes them, and enters each locking in the locking log with the hour and his initials. The log is signed complete when the round is finished and is kept with the day book. Any door found unlocked, obstructed, or already fastened out of order is specially entered and reported before the keeper leaves the building.

### Q: What standing does Accord oversight hold when an officer of oversight is present at an Office interview?
An officer of oversight present at an interview attends in her own right as keeper of the Accord interest in its paper, its premises, and its employees, and not as a guest of the Office conducting the case. She may hear everything said, may make her own notes, and may require that a question touching Accord administration be recorded in full even where the officers conducting the interview would summarise it. She does not direct the course of the interview unless her authorisation says so, but her presence means that the substance of what is said passes into oversight keeping at once and cannot afterwards be withheld from it by any course.

### Q: How is a requisition number raised, quoted, and closed upon Accord paper?
A requisition number is raised by the requisitions office when work is authorised, and it is entered at the head of the requisition in words before any other writing is added to the form. Every later paper arising from that work, including warehouse checks, run papers, custody slips, and payment authorisations, quotes the number in full and in the same form, so that the whole chain can be gathered by that one reference. When the work is complete and payment has been made or refused, the requisition is closed by a closing entry signed by a clerk of the office, and no further paper may be raised under a number once it has been closed.

### Q: What are the rules for wearing, showing, and lending an Accord badge while the holder is on duty?
A holder wears his badge where it can be seen while he is within an Accord building or upon Accord work outside it, and he shows it on request to any keeper, clerk, or officer who has reason to check his presence. The badge remains the property of the issuing building and is held by the named holder only. It may not be lent, exchanged, or handed to another person to gain entry or to perform work, even for a single run or a single hour, and a holder who parts with his badge must report the parting at once. Breach of these rules is entered on the holder record and may end his badged employment.

### Q: What happens when a badged person is found still within an Accord building after locking for the night has begun?
The keeper who finds a person still within the building after locking has begun does not unlock the building to let him out on his own word. He records the name the person gives, the badge the person shows, and the place where he was found, and he notifies the duty officer named for that night. The person remains in the keeper's company or in a room set aside for the purpose until the duty officer directs whether he is to be escorted out, held for interview, or allowed to remain under watch until morning. The finding and the direction given are both entered in the locking log and in the day book.

### Q: How do the stores returns of a clerk serve to establish that clerk's own hand for later comparison?
A stores clerk makes returns in the ordinary course of his duty, listing stock received, stock issued, and stock remaining, and he signs each return and writes its entries in his own hand without assistance. Because these returns are made regularly, over a long period, and for purposes unconnected with any later inquiry, they show the settled habits of his writing rather than a manner adopted for an occasion. For that reason they are the papers first sought when his hand is in question. Returns are kept in the store in date order, and any return taken away for comparison is receipted and its absence marked in the file for its return.

### Q: What paper records a wharf carrying run from the bonded warehouse to its place of delivery?
A carrying run is recorded on the run paper raised under its requisition, which names the carrier, the warehouse of loading, the number and marks of the crates or bales to be carried, and the place of delivery. The warehouse clerk signs the paper when the load is checked out, noting any seal applied and the state of the load at handing over. The carrier signs on taking charge, and the receiving clerk signs on arrival, noting shortages or damage at once rather than afterwards. The completed paper returns to the requisitions office, where it supports the payment entry and is filed with the other papers bearing the same requisition number.

### Q: How is an interview held under oversight attendance recorded, and where is the resulting record kept?
The record of such an interview is made by a clerk or officer named in the authorisation, who writes the questions and answers in substance and in order, and marks any passage where exact words matter by writing those words as spoken. At the close the record is read over, corrections are entered openly rather than by overwriting, and the persons present sign to show that the reading took place. One copy is filed with the case paper in the Office, and oversight keeps its own note or copy under its own arrangements. Neither keeping may alter its copy afterwards except by a further signed entry explaining the change and its reason.

### Q: What is done with an Accord badge after an inquiry into its custody has closed?
When an inquiry into the custody of a badge has closed, the badge is dealt with according to the finding recorded. If the finding restores it to its recorded holder, the holding mark in the issue book is cleared by a signed entry, and the badge is handed back at the stores window against the holder signature. If the finding assigns the badge to store as withdrawn, it is cancelled in the book, removed from circulation, and kept with the inquiry paper so that it cannot be issued again in error. In neither case is the earlier history of the badge erased, and every entry made during the inquiry remains legible in the book.
