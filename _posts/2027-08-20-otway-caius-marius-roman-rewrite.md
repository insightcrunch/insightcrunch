For roughly two generations, the English stage knew the lovers of Verona only in Roman dress. Thomas Otway's *The History and Fall of Caius Marius*, first acted by the Duke's Company at the Dorset Garden Theatre in the autumn of 1679, took Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet* apart, carried its love plot into the civil wars of Marius and Sylla, and then held the stage in the original's place. Romeo became Marius junior, son of the great general. Juliet became Lavinia, daughter of Metellus. The feud became a struggle for the consulship of Rome, fought with votes, armies and proscriptions rather than street brawls. Shakespeare's play, in anything like Shakespeare's shape, vanished from the London repertory behind it.

![The stage history of Romeo and Juliet and its Restoration adaptations](/assets/images/blog/blog-66.webp)

This article treats *Caius Marius* as what its own prologue admitted it to be: an adaptation, openly built from stolen materials, and not a corruption to be apologized for or a curiosity to be patronized. The verdict argued through the evidence below is that Otway was the most consequential editor the tragedy had between Shakespeare's own company and the age of Garrick. He cut the duel that turns Shakespeare's play, dissolved its comedy into a political tribune who outlives his model, moved the lovers' marriage to the front of the action, and rebuilt the tomb scene so that Lavinia wakes while Marius junior still lives and the two can speak. That single change, made in 1679, governed the stage's handling of the ending for well over a century afterward, passing through Theophilus Cibber's revival of 1744 into the Garrick tradition and beyond it into opera. A playwright remembered for *Venice Preserved* and *The Orphan* deserves a second entry in the history of *Romeo and Juliet*: not as its vandal, but as the adapter who kept its story alive by refusing to keep its text.

The evidence for that verdict is unusually concrete, because *Caius Marius* survives in its 1680 quarto exactly as acted, with its cast list, its prologue confession and its stage directions intact. Where the record disagrees with itself, the disagreements are part of the story. Older authorities dated the premiere 1680, after *The Orphan*; the modern stage calendar settles it in October 1679. Handbooks round the play's reign to sixty years or to seventy, while the documented performances run at least to 1717 and the original's absence runs to 1744. Critics have called the borrowing theft since 1691, and the borrowing's most famous fruit, the waking in the tomb, has been credited both to Otway's invention and to the Italian tale Shakespeare himself had used and altered. Each of those disputes is taken up in its place below, with the documents set against each other and a judgment given.

The method throughout is to separate three kinds of evidence that writing on this play habitually blends. The first is the quarto itself, the 1680 print of the play as acted, which settles what Otway wrote, whom he cast and what he confessed, and which is quoted from directly wherever a scene's mechanics matter. The second is the record of performance, the calendars, cast lists and edition histories that settle how long the play lived and what the restorers of the next century did with its devices. The third is the verdicts, from Langbaine in 1691 to the modern scholarship of adaptation, which settle nothing by themselves and are treated here as exhibits in the play's reception rather than as authorities over it. Where the three kinds of evidence conflict, the quarto outranks the verdict and the record outranks the handbook round number, a hierarchy that decides both the date of the premiere and the true length of the reign. Readers who keep that hierarchy in mind can check every claim in the article against the same documents, most of which are short, printed and public.

## Before Otway: The Play the Restoration Actually Knew

The *Romeo and Juliet* that Otway inherited was already an acting text, not a sacred one. When the theatres reopened after the Commonwealth closure, the old plays were divided between the two patent companies under the monopoly that governed the Restoration stage, two houses sharing the whole of London's legitimate drama between them, and this tragedy fell in the allotment to the Duke's Company under Sir William Davenant. The company staged it on 1 March 1662, with Henry Harris as Romeo, Thomas Betterton as Mercutio and Mary Saunderson, often described as the first woman to play Juliet on the professional stage, in the heroine's part. The performance entered the play's permanent record through a hostile witness: [Samuel Pepys's diary verdict on the 1662 revival](/2026/09/25/samuel-pepys-hated-romeo-and-juliet/) called it the worst play he had ever heard in his life, and Pepys was recording a first impression of a text already being handled as repertory property rather than literature.

Davenant's company treated the play as material to be adjusted. At some point in the 1660s James Howard prepared a version in which the lovers survived, recasting the tragedy as a tragicomedy with a happy ending, and the two versions, Shakespeare's ending and Howard's, were for a time acted on alternate nights, so that the same company sold opposite catastrophes on successive evenings. The stage history printed with the Yale Shakespeare summarizes the arrangement plainly: Howard altered the play, provided the happy ending, and the original and the alteration shared the bill in alternation. A play that could be performed with its ending reversed on alternate nights was not a text its custodians regarded as fixed. Its plot was the property; its words were negotiable. Both of the fountainheads of Restoration stage history stand behind this picture of the play's condition, and both are insiders rather than critics: Pepys in his diary, noting what he saw and what he thought of it on the day, and John Downes, the Duke's Company's own prompter, whose *Roscius Anglicanus* of 1708 preserves the company's traditions of casting, alteration and success from within the building. A repertory documented by its prompter and its most famous playgoer is a repertory whose practices can be known rather than guessed, and its practices, for this play, were alteration as routine and revival as re manufacture. Otway's rewrite is what that system produced when it was handed to a genuine poet.

The house Otway wrote for explains part of the rewriting habit. Dorset Garden, opened in 1671, was the Duke's Company's second home and the most technically ambitious theatre in London, built for the scenic spectacle the age demanded, with machines, painted shutters and room for the musical entertainments that patent audiences expected between the acts. A company carrying that overhead needed plays that filled the scenic stage and justified the prices, and it held a finite stock of proven plots with which to do it. The old tragedies were capital assets in a literal sense, assigned between the companies when the patents divided the pre war repertory, and their custodians sweated them the way managers sweat any asset: by refreshing the dressing, enlarging the spectacle, recutting the roles for the company's stars and rewriting whatever the house's taste had outgrown. The quarto of *Caius Marius* preserves the house style in its directions as faithfully as in its cast list: in the last act the printed text directs that the scene draws off and shows the temple and monument, a shutter discovery of exactly the kind Dorset Garden's machinery existed to perform, so that the tomb toward which the whole evening has been travelling arrives not as a property carried on by stagehands but as a revelation of the scenic stage itself. The adaptation's Rome was built to be looked at as well as heard, and its author's borrowings from Shakespeare's words were housed in a visual frame that Shakespeare's own bare platform never offered the story. Betterton, the company's leading tragedian and from the mid 1670s its effective artistic manager, was himself a product of that system, and his presence at the head of the *Caius Marius* cast, as the elder Marius rather than as the lover, tells its own story about where the rewrite located its centre of gravity. The tragedy Otway built was designed around the company's senior tragedian and its leading comedian, not around its young lovers, and the design shows in the finished play, whose Rome is crowded with the politics its star could carry.

That was the general condition of Shakespeare on the Restoration stage, and it matters for judging Otway that his rewrite was an extreme case of a normal practice rather than a lone outrage. Between 1660 and 1777, as Jean I. Marsden's *The Re-Imagined Text* establishes, more than fifty adaptations of Shakespeare's plays reached print or the stage, with new characters, new scenes, new endings and new words. Dryden refashioned *Antony and Cleopatra* as *All for Love* in 1677, and Dryden's version, not Shakespeare's, held the stage for some eighty years. Nahum Tate's *King Lear* of 1681, in which the old king survives and Cordelia marries Edgar, was still four years in the future when Otway wrote, but the direction of the repertory was already set. Shakespeare's stories were the capital of the Restoration stage; Shakespeare's language was treated as raw material that a working dramatist was expected to refine.

Otway came to the material as a professional under pressure. He was twenty seven in 1679, a former commoner of Christ Church, Oxford, who had left without a degree, failed in a single attempt at acting, and written for the Duke's Company since *Alcibiades* in 1675. *Don Carlos* in 1676 had been a major success in the rhymed heroic manner. In 1678 he had taken an ensign's commission in an English regiment serving in Flanders, and the biographical tradition holds that part of *Caius Marius* was drafted during that unhappy military excursion and finished after his return to London in 1679, when he arrived home, by his own later account of the expedition, ragged and unpaid. The play he produced was therefore a working dramatist's solution to a repertory problem: the Duke's Company needed a tragedy with a proven story, and Otway, short of money and time, reached for the strongest love plot in the company's stock and a Roman history he could graft it onto.

### Who was Thomas Otway when he wrote Caius Marius?

Thomas Otway was a twenty seven year old professional dramatist of the Duke's Company, educated at Winchester and Christ Church, lately returned from service as an ensign in Flanders, with the rhymed success of *Don Carlos* behind him and *The Orphan* and *Venice Preserved*, the tragedies that made his name, still unwritten ahead of him.

## Rome in the Year of the Exclusion Bill

The Roman frame was not a neutral antiquarian choice, and the first audience cannot have heard it as one. *Caius Marius* reached the stage in the autumn of 1679, in the middle of the crisis that followed the Popish Plot revelations of 1678, with Parliament pressing a bill to exclude the Catholic Duke of York from the succession and the court resisting in the name of hereditary right. London in that season was a city rehearsing arguments about legitimacy, popular power and the danger of standing armies, and a tragedy about a popular general who packs an election, defies the senate, is driven into exile and returns at the head of an army to slaughter his enemies offered those arguments a Roman mirror whether its author intended the reflection or not. The play's election scene, in which citizens are drilled to vote for Marius and to knock down opposition, is a study of manufactured popular consent that no audience of 1679 could watch as ancient history alone.

The prologue advertises the topical charge before the action begins. After the confession of theft, Betterton's lines turn from Shakespeare to the state of the kingdom: the poets mourn the absence of wit and of Caesar together, ask when he and poetry shall return, and look to a blessed day when his cares are banished and the nation's fears with them. The absent Caesar of the usual reading is the Duke of York, withdrawn from court while the exclusionists pressed their advantage, and within months Otway would write the prologue to *The Orphan* openly congratulating the Duke on his return from Scotland, a gesture the *Dictionary of National Biography* records as evidence of his strong Tory sympathies. Read in that company, the *Caius Marius* prologue is doing political work: it frames the evening's Roman civil war inside a lament for an exiled heir and a prayer for his restoration, addressed to a patent audience that included the court the crisis was dividing.

The play beneath the prologue refuses the simplicity the frame promises, and that refusal is part of its interest as a political document. Marius senior is the prologue's problem in person, a demagogue who bribes his way upward, packs the Forum and drowns Rome in blood on his return, and he is also the tragedy's wronged exile, driven to the marshes and the ruins by a senatorial faction whose spokesman, Metellus, opens the play sneering at the peasant's brat whose rise his own patronage made possible. Otway distributes the guilt the way Plutarch distributes it, across a system in which the senate's contempt manufactures the demagogue it fears, and the result is a civil war tragedy that a Tory audience could read as a warning against popular faction and a sceptical one as a warning against oligarchic pride. What no reading of 1679 could make it is escapism. When Lavinia follows the exiles through barren deserts and the Forum crowd is taught its lines by a tribune, the Roman dress is transparent, and the transparency is the adaptation's second subject: Shakespeare's private feud, which explains nothing and implicates everyone, becomes in Otway an anatomy of how republics talk themselves into slaughter. Later chapters of this series on Garrick and on the restoration of the text deal with what the eighteenth century made of the play's ending; what the seventeenth century made of its politics is the half of the adaptation that died with its occasion, and recovering it is necessary before the theft charges can be fairly weighed, because a dramatist writing topical civil war under a confiscated plot is engaged in something more deliberate than rifling.

Rome was available for that work because Rome was the period's shared political language. Plutarch's *Lives* stood in every grammar school curriculum and on every gentleman's shelf in North's translation, Sallust's Marius was a school author, and the vocabulary of consulships, tribunes and proscriptions carried precise contemporary charges that an audience could decode without a key. An adapter choosing Marius in 1679 was therefore choosing the most legible political allegory his culture possessed, and the choice disciplines how the whole play should be read: the love plot is the cargo, but the ship is a state tragedy, built from the timbers of the republic whose fall every educated playgoer had already studied as a warning to his own.

## Plutarch's Marius: The Roman Frame Otway Built On

The Roman half of the graft came from Plutarch's *Life of Marius*, in the translation tradition descending from Sir Thomas North, supplemented, as Gerard Langbaine noted as early as 1691, by the second book of Lucan's *Pharsalia*. The historical Gaius Marius, who lived from 157 to 86 BC, was the great military reformer of the late Roman republic: a new man from Arpinum who rose without ancestry, opened the legions to the propertyless poor, and was elected consul seven times, a run without precedent. He took the credit for finishing the war against Jugurtha in Numidia, where his rival Sulla, serving under him, had arranged the king's capture, and the quarrel over that credit, symbolized in Plutarch by Sulla's signet ring engraved with the surrender, seeded the hatred between the two men. He saved Italy from the Cimbri and the Teutones in 102 and 101 BC. In 88 BC, when Sulla marched an army on Rome, the first Roman general to do so, Marius fled, hid from soldiers in the marshes at Minturnae, escaped by sea, and was found sitting among the ruins of Carthage, where, in Plutarch's famous scene, he told the officer sent to him to report that Gaius Marius had been seen sitting there in exile. In 87 BC he returned with Cinna, entered Rome, and his soldiers slaughtered his enemies in a proscription of rage rather than of lists. In January 86 BC, days into his seventh consulship, he died, of pleurisy and drink in Plutarch's account, after a banquet.

Three episodes from the *Life* deserve separate notice because the play's architecture rests on them. The first is the military reform and its resentment. Marius had risen by making the legions a profession open to the poor, enrolling men without property whom the senate despised, and the soldiers, carrying their packs on the march like beasts of burden, were nicknamed Marius's mules. The sneer and the loyalty are the same fact seen from two sides, and Otway's first act is built on it: Metellus opens the play by retailing the great man's low beginning, a peasant's brat born in the outmost cottages of Arpinum and fostered in a corner, while the Forum crowd of the second act roars for the same man against the lords. The second episode is Minturnae. Ordered killed in the hovel where he sheltered, Marius was spared when the slave sent to execute him, a Cimbrian by Plutarch's account, dropped his sword in the dark, crying that he could not kill Caius Marius, and the town let its prisoner slip away to the coast by night. The third is the deathbed. Plutarch gives the seventh consulship an ending of pure delusion: the old man, ill and drinking, entertained his friends with his generalship and fell into a delirium in which he imagined himself already in command against Mithridates, gesturing through a battle that existed only in his fever, and died seventeen days into the office he had killed to reach. Otway discards the delirium and substitutes grief and dread of Sylla, but he keeps the shape of the thing, a conqueror destroyed at the summit by the war machine he built.

The Roman chronology also exposes the graft's boldest compression, and an account of the play should state it plainly. The historical Sylla spent the years of Marius's last consulship in the east, fighting Mithridates, and did not march on Rome in force until 83 BC, three years after the elder Marius was dead; the proscriptions that made his name a byword belong to that later return, when Marius the Younger, consul in 82 BC at an age the constitution did not permit, died at Praeneste as the town fell. Otway telescopes a decade into a single action: his Sylla is simultaneously the bridegroom waiting for Lavinia, the exile whose return the Forum dreads and the avenger whose approach closes the play, and his Marius junior dies in a tomb in his father's lifetime rather than alone in a captured town four years after it. The compression is not ignorance. Plutarch's two lives, of Marius and of Sylla, lay open beside the quarto's readers, and the play's first audience included men who had construed both at school. Otway is performing the standard operation of Roman tragedy, gathering a generation's scattered violence into one representative catastrophe, and the love plot is what makes the gathering cohere: without Lavinia, the fall of Marius is a chronicle; with her, the chronicle acquires a household to destroy, and the destruction can be counted in named bodies rather than in the anonymous thousands of the proscription lists.

Otway's fidelity to that outline is closer than the play's reputation for absurdity suggests. The opening act is a senatorial debate drawn straight from the *Life*: Metellus recalls that he raised Marius, took him to Africa as his lieutenant against Jugurtha and was robbed of honour there, which is precisely the historical grievance of Quintus Caecilius Metellus Numidicus, the commander Marius supplanted in Numidia and then in popular favour. The choice of Metellus as the heroine's father is itself a piece of historical rhyming that the graft's critics rarely credit. The historical Metellus was, like Marius after him, driven into exile by tribunician politics, banished in 100 BC for refusing to swear obedience to a popular land law and recalled the following year, so that the two fathers of the Roman play are mirror exiles, each destroyed in turn by the popular machinery the other rode. Otway cannot dramatize both exiles inside one action, and he kills his Metellus in the churchyard instead, but the selection shows a reader of Plutarch choosing the one senatorial name whose biography comments on his hero's, rather than reaching for the nearest Roman label to pin on Capulet. The play counts the consulships as Plutarch counts them, noting that Marius has borne the office six times and is reaching for a seventh. The Forum scene in the second act stages the mechanism of his power, an election crowd instructed to vote for Marius and to knock down anyone who speaks against the choice, with Sulpitius, historically Marius's tribune ally Sulpicius Rufus, presiding as tribune of the people. The citizens speak prose, in the Shakespearean convention that reserves verse for their betters, and their debate is the play's one sustained piece of political comedy. One of them explains the attraction of the system with a candour no senator matches, remarking that there is nothing like a civil government where good subjects may have leave to knock brains out to maintain privileges, while another observes that the great objection to lords is that they do nothing but run in debt and lie with other men's wives. Otway, who is often treated as a mere rifler of scenes, is here writing in Shakespeare's manner rather than from his page, constructing the clown's eye view of the catastrophe that Shakespeare himself builds into the first scene of *Romeo and Juliet* with Sampson and Gregory. The graft has an inner logic the plagiarism charge misses: a playwright stealing a tragedy about a city divided against itself reached instinctively for the original's own device of letting the city's smallest citizens anatomize the division.

The elder Marius's own Forum oration, delivered from the shoulders of his supporters in the same scene, shows the adapter working from the classical tradition at first hand rather than through Shakespeare at all. The candidate presents himself to the crowd as no noble but a freeborn man, a citizen of Rome as they are, and pleads his wounds, got in the people's service, as his credentials against the birth of his rivals. The strategy descends from the most famous speech Sallust ever wrote, the oration he composed for Marius as consul in the *Jugurtha*, in which the new man displays his scars to the people and asks whether nobility of deeds or of ancestors should command Rome's armies. Otway's general electioneers in Sallust's argument two generations before the historians of the play noticed the debt, and the scene's existence is a standing answer to the charge that the Roman half of the play is wallpaper: the adapter who could rebuild Marius's populism from its Sallustian source was reading Rome in the original historians, and expecting his pit to follow. The exile, the return with Cinna and the terror that follows all stand in the printed text. What Plutarch does not contain is a son in love with a daughter of Metellus. Marius the Younger existed, and died in 82 BC at Praeneste, by his own hand as Sulla's forces closed on the town, four years after his father. Otway compressed that separate, later death into his father's tragedy and attached it to a bride Plutarch never mentions. The graft, in other words, runs both ways: Shakespeare's plot is Romanized, but Marius's history is also Shakespeareanized, its chronology folded so that father and son can fall inside one action.

Read down the right hand column, the table shows a play that has been re-motivated at every joint. Shakespeare's feud has no origin and no object; the Prologue calls the households alike in dignity and the play never explains the grudge, which is the point of it. Otway's conflict has an origin any reader of Plutarch can recite and an object that can be counted in consulships. Shakespeare's catastrophe turns on a street duel and a quarantined letter, accidents stacked on a private quarrel. Otway's turns on a civil war, in which the lovers' marriage is one alliance among several and their deaths are two items in a much longer casualty list that includes a murdered priest, a murdered father and a broken conqueror. Whether that re-motivation improves the story is the critical question the rest of this article keeps open; that it was deliberate, systematic and complete is what the table establishes.

## The Shape of the Play: Five Acts Between Senate and Tomb

The printed scene list of the quarto maps the redesign more honestly than any summary, because it shows where Otway spends his stage time. Shakespeare's play moves between two households and a friar's cell, with the street as contested ground. Otway's moves between the institutions of a republic at war with itself. The first act moves between senatorial debate, in which the state's elders rehearse Marius's rise, his ingratitude and his ambition while the crowd shouts for him offstage, and the Marian headquarters, where Otway inserts the Queen Mab material, putting Mercutio's fairy machinery into the mouth of a tribune diagnosing a lovesick young officer in his commander's hearing. The second act divides between the walled garden of Metellus's house, where the love plot is contracted in Shakespeare's own words, and the Forum, where the election crowd is drilled in prose. The third act divides between Metellus's house and the Forum again, domestic conspiracy and public crisis alternating as the marriage and the coup ripen together. The fourth act opens in the garden, moves out to the country, the landscape of the exile, and returns to Metellus's house for the scene in which the Priest hands Lavinia the vial. The fifth act is a progress through the geography of a collapsing state: Cinna's camp before the walls of Rome, Metellus's house, the Forum again, now furnished with the consul's tribunal, the seat from which the returned Marius governs by terror, and at last the churchyard, where every strand of the action, lovers, priest, father, conqueror and tribune, is gathered into one set for the reckoning.

Two features of that map mark the adaptation's priorities. The first is the weight of the public scenes. By scene count, half the play stands in the Forum, the camp or the senate's orbit, spaces Shakespeare's tragedy never enters, and the love plot is repeatedly made to wait while the state conducts its business in full view. The second is the doubling of locations between the acts: garden and Forum in the second act, house and Forum in the third, so that private and public Rome comment on each other in alternation, the betrothal answered by the election, the marriage answered by the coup. Shakespeare's alternation in the corresponding acts runs between the lovers and the feud's street violence, a private rhythm. Otway's runs between the lovers and the constitution, which is why the finished play feels less like a love tragedy with a political background than like a state tragedy that has annexed a love plot and then, in the churchyard, been annexed by it. The churchyard is the one location the two plays share without translation, a temple and monument in place of the Capulet vault, and the adaptation reserves its most faithful copying for that shared ground, as though Otway recognized that the story's centre of gravity had never moved, whatever he built around it.

## What Otway Kept: Shakespeare's Scenes in Roman Dress

The prologue told the first audience exactly how much had been taken, and the quarto bears the confession out. Betterton, speaking it, praised Shakespeare as the happiest poet of his time and the best, remarked that succeeding poets have humbly gleaned from the crop of his luxuriant pen ever since, and then turned the admission into a joke at the author's expense: the poet of the day, the most unworthy of the throng, fears he has done Shakespeare wrong, for, like greedy beggars that steal sheaves away, he has rifled him of half a play. Half is a fair estimate. Whole scenes of *Caius Marius* are Shakespeare's scenes with the names changed and the metre tidied, and the garden scene of the second act is the most complete transfer of all.

Even the approach to the scene is a transfer. Shakespeare opens his second act's night action with Benvolio and Mercutio searching the Capulet orchard for a Romeo who has hidden from them, Benvolio observing that blind is his love and best befits the dark. Otway opens his garden sequence with Sulpitius and Granius searching for the younger Marius among the trees, in the same disposition and nearly the same words, Granius carrying Benvolio's share of the search while Sulpitius carries Mercutio's, except that the searchers are not wedding guests killing an evening but officers of a faction on the eve of a coup, and their talk runs from the missing lover straight to the morning's politics: Sylla means to enter Rome, the elder Marius demands the consulship, and the younger man is lost in a maze of love when he should be thinking of cutting throats. The comedy of Shakespeare's search, Mercutio conjuring Romeo by Rosaline's bright eyes, is compressed into disgust, because Otway's Mercutio figure has an army to raise and experiences his friend's passion as a military problem. The adaptation's whole method is visible in the exchange of one speech's cargo: where Shakespeare's young men carry wit into the dark, Otway's carry mobilization orders, and the love scene they are interrupting has to compete for the audience's attention with a revolution scheduled for dawn.

The scene is laid in a walled garden belonging to Metellus's house. Marius junior enters below; the printed stage direction reads, without embarrassment, "Lavinia in the Balcony", and she speaks the most famous speech in Shakespeare's play with its grammar intact and its names translated."O Marius, Marius! wherefore art thou Marius? Deny thy Family, renounce thy Name: Or if thou wilt not, be but sworn my Love, And I'll no longer call Metellus Parent." The rose argument follows in its original order: it is but the name that is her enemy; that which is called a rose, by any other name, would smell as sweet; so Marius, were he not Marius called, would be still as dear to her desiring eyes. The exchange of vows, the refusal of the moon as a witness, the bounty as boundless as the sea, the Nurse calling within, the appointment for the hour of nine the next morning, all stand where Shakespeare put them. What Otway adds is a pressure Shakespeare's Juliet does not face in the scene itself: Lavinia has been told that night, before the scene opens, that she is to be given to Sylla. Her father broke the dreadful news that evening and threatened, when she urged the right of love, to turn her out of his house naked and shiftless to the world. The orchard scene in Shakespeare is a betrothal negotiated under the threat of a marriage arranged elsewhere and later; in Otway it is a betrothal negotiated against a wedding already announced to the bridegroom who commands an army.

The balcony direction deserves its own emphasis, because it is the second of the play's two permanent gifts to the staging of *Romeo and Juliet*. Shakespeare's early texts bring Juliet in above, at a window; no early edition names a balcony, and the word itself was barely naturalized in English in Shakespeare's lifetime. Otway's quarto names the structure outright, in a direction an acting company could build from. When later centuries staged the garden scene with Juliet on a raised balcony as a matter of course, they were following a scenic specification that the printed record first supplies in *Caius Marius*. The point is argued in the reference tradition on the play and is visible on the face of the 1680 text: the balcony is part of the adaptation's furniture that the original's tradition absorbed.

The comedy survives the crossing in two carriers. The Nurse remains the Nurse, unnamed in both plays, and was entrusted not to a boy or a character actress but to James Nokes, the Duke's Company's leading comedian, whose performance became so identified with the part that he carried the nickname of Nurse Nokes afterward. The *Dictionary of National Biography*, summarizing the first production, judges that such enthusiasm as the performance evoked was due chiefly to the acting of Cave Underhill as Sulpitius and of Nokes as the Nurse, which is a verdict on where Restoration audiences located the play's pleasures: in the comic playing of parts Shakespeare had written, transplanted intact.

The epilogue, spoken by Elizabeth Barry in character as Lavinia, confirms that verdict from inside the production and adds a piece of evidence no other source supplies so directly. Barry rises from the tomb to open the address with a joke on the waking itself, hoping the audience will believe that, though she is again alive, the play of theirs shall thrive, and she goes on to describe what the house has actually been watching: a drumming, trumpetting and fighting play, whose poet, when it was made, had nothing but drums and trumpets in his head, having banished poetry and all her charms in his determination to be a man at arms. Then comes the admission that converts the epilogue into a casting document: the spectators in their cloaks, she tells the pit, have come only for love of Underhill and Nurse Nokes. The company's own afterpiece thus testifies that the production's drawing power was understood, by the people selling it, to rest on its two comic performances, and it closes with an appeal that fixes the play's place in its author's biography, asking the audience to help a poor disbanded soldier home. *Caius Marius* was presented to its first public not as a monument but as a benefit performance for a cashiered ensign, whose Roman tragedy was expected to earn its keep through its clowns. That the tragedy then held the stage for decades says something about the clowns, and something about the scenes the clowns were carrying. The second carrier is Sulpitius himself, and his case belongs as much to the changes as to the survivals, because Otway kept Mercutio's voice and abolished Mercutio's function. Sulpitius has the Queen Mab material, compressed into a speech that begins from the same premise, of Mab busy in a sleeping man's brains, and he has Mercutio's scorn for love as a little scarecrow that frightens fools with a painted bow of lath. The Mab catalogue itself is the adaptation in miniature, because Otway rewrites its dreams for Roman dreamers: Mab still gallops through lovers' brains and over a courtier's nose to make him dream of begging, but she also courses over a soldier's neck, and he dreams of cutting foreign throats, of breaches, ambuscades and false musters, and she tweaks a poet by the ear to fill him with visions of panegyrics, flattering dedications and mighty presents, a joke at the dedication trade that Shakespeare's catalogue does not contain. The speech ends by annexing the fairy to the plot outright, since Mab has been with Sylla too, and he dreams now of nothing but a consulship. Mercutio's set piece, which in Shakespeare stops the action for a display of pure verbal energy, is made in Rome to deliver intelligence about every faction in the play. But he says these things as a tribune of the people with an election to run, Marius senior's political lieutenant in a faction fight, and the wit runs in harness with the war plot from his first entrance.

### What did James Nokes's Nurse do for the production?

James Nokes, the Duke's Company's leading comic actor, took the Nurse at Dorset Garden and made the part the production's comic centrepiece: played in women's dress in the Restoration convention, his Nurse earned him the lasting nickname of Nurse Nokes and, with Underhill's Sulpitius, the warmest notice the first run received.

### Is the balcony in the garden scene Shakespeare's staging or Otway's?

Otway's, on the printed evidence. Shakespeare's early texts place Juliet above, at a window, and name no structure; the 1680 quarto of *Caius Marius* directs "Lavinia in the Balcony" in so many words, the first surviving specification of the balcony that later staging of the garden scene made standard.

The darkest survival is the Apothecary. Shakespeare's Romeo, in Mantua, remembers a starved shopkeeper and buys poison with a speech that turns on the man's poverty: famine in his cheeks, need and oppression staring in his eyes, contempt and beggary hanging on his back, the world not his friend nor the world's law. Otway's Marius junior, in Rome, delivers the same speech to the same figure with the pronouns barely disturbed, buys the same vial, and pays with the same reflection, that the gold he hands over is worse poison to men's souls, doing more murders in the world, than the poor compounds the law forbids the seller to sell. Nothing in the Roman plot requires an apothecary; Rome has no Mantua and Marius junior is not in exile under sentence of death for a duel. The scene is present because Shakespeare's scene is magnificent, and Otway, who had confessed to rifling half a play, was not going to leave its finest set piece of rhetoric behind. Restoration adaptation is often described as improvement, the correction of an irregular genius by a regular age. Passages like this one record the opposite transaction: the adapter kneeling to the original, keeping what his own plot cannot justify because the words are better than anything his plot could generate.

The first act shows the same fidelity working at the level of dialogue rather than set piece, and it catches Otway in the act of thinking in Shakespeare. Shakespeare's opening act gives Benvolio the physician's advice for a discarded love: take in a new infection to the heart, and the rank poison of the old will die. Otway hands the advice to Sulpitius, who delivers it to the lovesick Marius junior in headquarters, and hands Romeo's answer to the patient, who replies that a plantain leaf is excellent for that, and, pressed, explains: for broken shins. The exchange is lifted entire, speakers, rhythm and punchline, and set down in a scene that also contains the adaptation's most brutal invention, Sulpitius reporting that he has left a present at the elder Marius's house, the head of a Metellus, a gay tall young thing who is but wormsmeat now. Shakespeare's dialogue and Otway's severed head share a page without embarrassment, and the juxtaposition is the graft's whole physiology: the borrowed tissue carries the feeling, the native tissue carries the horror, and the audience of 1679 was expected to metabolize both at once. The act closes under a sky of portents in the Roman manner, ensigns catching fire untouched where they stood displayed, ravens bringing their young into the streets and devouring them before the people's eyes, trumpet blasts and cries of dying men in the air, the prodigy language of civil war epic doing the work that Shakespeare's prologue sonnet does for Verona, warning the house that the order of things has already cracked.

## What Otway Changed: Marriage, Exile and a Doubled Death

Against those survivals, the structural changes are extensive enough to make *Caius Marius* a different kind of tragedy, and they begin with the order of events. In Shakespeare, the lovers marry in the second act and the marriage is a secret kept from both households until the tomb reveals it. In Otway, the marriage also comes early, solemnized by the Priest, but the plot then does something Shakespeare never attempts: it sends the bride into exile with the bridegroom's family. When Marius senior is driven from Rome, Marius junior goes with him, and Lavinia goes too, following the exiles through what her final speech calls barren deserts, and saving the old man's life on the journey when she found him fainting on dry, parched earth beneath a scorching noon, hungry and without food or friend, and relieved him. The love story is therefore not a four day fever, as Shakespeare's is, but a marriage tested by a campaign: the lovers are separated not by a duel and a banishment decree but by a war, reunited in Rome's fall, and parted again by Metellus's plan to marry his daughter to Sylla as the price of the city's settlement. The Priest's vial enters the plot at that point, as the instrument for escaping the Sylla marriage by a feigned death, and the failed message that follows is entrusted to a boy with a letter, whom the Priest dispatches to find young Marius while he himself goes to the monument alone.

The marriage itself has a prehistory Shakespeare never gives his lovers, and it changes the meaning of their secrecy. In the second act's opening scene, before the garden, Lavinia lets slip her lover's name to her father and covers the slip with a fact: the elder Marius had once demanded her in marriage for his son, a public proposal from the head of one faction to the head of the other, and Metellus had refused it. The secret passion therefore grows in the ruins of a failed public alliance, and the Priest's ceremony repairs in private what Roman politics had attempted and rejected in the open, which gives the marriage a diplomatic ghost that Verona's never carries. Otway also ages his heroine past Shakespeare's. Metellus states her age in the same scene as a bare sixteen, and the Nurse's great set piece of comic chronology, the reckoning of Juliet's age by Lammas and the earthquake and the weaning, is transferred with its calendar exchanged: the Nurse counts the years since Marius entered Rome in triumph, thirteen of them, and dates the child's weaning, her broken forehead and her husband's joke from the public event rather than from a feast day. Even the household's memory, in Otway's Rome, is kept in consular time.

The duel that turns Shakespeare's third act has no equivalent. There is no Tybalt in *Caius Marius*: Metellus has no son in the list of persons, no kinsman of the heroine steps forward to be killed in a street fight, and the printed text contains only two killings by stage direction before the final scene, one amid the Forum violence of the second act and one in the churchyard at the end. Mercutio's death, the hinge on which Shakespeare's comedy swings into tragedy, is abolished along with the duel, and its consequences are redistributed. Banishment falls on the lovers together rather than on Romeo alone. The catastrophe is prepared not by a private killing but by public events: Marius senior's return, the slaughter of his enemies, and Sylla's approach on Rome with an army from Capua. Where Shakespeare's Prince threatens death for the next brawl and then cannot prevent the deaths that follow from private hands, Otway's Rome has no prince at all; authority in the play is the thing being fought over, and it changes hands by battle while the lovers wait in the monument.

Three quieter abolitions complete the redesign. The Chorus disappears: Shakespeare's sonnet prologues, which frame the action as a tale of star crossed lovers whose deaths bury their parents' strife, have no Roman counterpart, because Otway's frame is not fate but history, and history in his play needs no chorus, only a senate. Rosaline disappears with her: Marius junior has no discarded mistress, no first love whose bright eyes Mercutio can conjure him by, and his passion for Lavinia is therefore not a transfer but a condition, which simplifies the hero and removes the play's built in irony about how quickly one face replaces another. Cibber's revival of 1744 would later cut Shakespeare's Rosaline references as well, travelling in Otway's direction without knowing it. And the timescale stretches past recognition. Shakespeare compresses his action into some four days, from a Sunday brawl to a Thursday dawn, and the haste is the tragedy's engine. Otway's action spans a marriage, an exile, a journey through deserts, a return and a siege, a matter of campaigning seasons rather than days, and the lovers the audience meets at the monument are not children overtaken by speed but adults worn down by duration. The effect on the catastrophe is the opposite of Shakespeare's: where the original's deaths feel like accidents that a slower world would have prevented, the Roman deaths feel like the final entries in a ledger the war had been keeping all along, which is why Otway can afford to end without a reconciliation. A four day fever can end in statues and a prince's pardon. A civil war ends in a joke and a body count.

The Priest is the change on which the ending turns, because Otway loads two of Shakespeare's figures into him. He is Friar Laurence: he marries the lovers in secret, he supplies the sleeping draught, he plans the reunion and he comes to the monument with a mattock and a wrenching iron to free the waking bride. But at the monument he meets Marius junior in disguise and darkness, is taken for a tomb robber, refuses to leave, and is killed by the man he came to save, dying with the recognition on his lips that his killer has slain the only man who could have done him good. That death belongs, in Shakespeare's design, to Paris, the bridegroom who keeps watch at the tomb and dies accusing Romeo of murder. Otway has no Paris at the tomb, because his Paris figure is Sylla, far too large for a graveside scuffle; Sylla never appears in the churchyard at all. The watchman's part therefore falls to the plot's own engineer, and the irony is sharpened past anything in the source: Shakespeare's Romeo kills the rival, Otway's Marius kills the rescuer, the one person alive who knows that Lavinia is not dead. After that blow the catastrophe needs no further machinery. The letter has already failed, the Priest is dead, and the poison is already bought.

The heroine's part grows under the same redesign, and the growth is systematic rather than decorative. Shakespeare's Juliet is not yet fourteen, acts under her father's roof for four days and dies by her husband's dagger minutes after waking to his corpse. Otway's Lavinia is sixteen, argues the theology of marriage with her father to his face in the play's second scene, warning him against violating a sacred rite into what she calls a lawful rape and loading her soul with hateful bonds, follows her husband's family into exile when the match her father refused collapses into war, keeps the exiled conqueror alive with her own hands in the desert, returns to face a second forced marriage, takes the Priest's vial on her own judgment, and dies at last not in privacy but in accusation, indicting the elder Marius with her history before the whole guard and choosing his sword as her instrument. The Restoration stage, which had invented the professional actress a generation earlier and built its repertory around women who could carry a fifth act, required heroines with more action than Shakespeare's child bride performs, and Barry's Lavinia is built to that specification. What the adaptation loses in the poetry of first love it partly recovers in the politics of a wife's fidelity maintained against two states and both fathers, and the recovery explains why the part kept attracting the leading actress of each succeeding generation long after the verse around it had dated.

## The Tomb: Lavinia Wakes and the Stage Inherits the Moment

The final scene is laid in a churchyard, before the temple and monument of the Metelli, and its sequence repays exact attention, because the change Otway makes is small in clock time and enormous in effect. The scene has been prepared by an irony Shakespeare never permits himself. Marius junior arrives at the churchyard already carrying Romeo's Mantua dream on his tongue, adapted line for line: his dreams presage some joyful news at hand, an unaccustomed spirit lifts him above the ground, and he dreamed that Lavinia came and found him dead and breathed such life with kisses on his lips that he revived and was an emperor. Shakespeare's Romeo dreams of being revived by Juliet's kiss and reads the dream as comfort on the morning of his death. Otway keeps the dream and then stages its mirror image within the hour: the dreamer will kiss the sleeper, and the sleeper, not the dreamer, will revive. Catulus, the friend who carries Balthasar's office in the Roman cast, arrives with the news and without the letter, and Marius puts the question that exposes the plot's wound in a single exchange, asking whether Catulus has no letters for him from the Priest and receiving the answer that he has none. The defiance of the stars follows in Romeo's own cadence, and the Apothecary is remembered, described and visited in Shakespeare's sequence, tortoise, alligator, empty boxes and all, with fifty drachmas doing the work of Romeo's forty ducats and Roman law standing where Mantuan law stood. Marius junior forces the monument open with a speech adapted from Romeo's address to the detestable maw, the womb of death gorged with the dearest morsel of the earth. He looks on Lavinia and finds death powerless over her beauty, the ensign of crimson still in her lips and cheeks. He drinks the poison, seals the eternal contract on her lips with a last embrace, and then, in the middle of the kiss, the stage direction turns the play: she breathes and stirs; Lavinia wakes. Shakespeare's Juliet wakes to a corpse, a friar's panic and a dagger. Otway's Lavinia wakes into her husband's arms while he can still speak, and the scene Shakespeare compressed into Romeo's dying error becomes a dialogue between two people who understand, for a few lines, that the scheme has worked. She tells him she has slept a long ten thousand years. She refuses, in her first confusion, the marriage that the sleep was designed to escape: she will not wed Sylla; Marius is her husband. He answers as a man already leaving the world, that ill fate shall part them no more, that this world's gross air grows burdensome on him already and that he is all a god, transported by joys that mortal sense cannot sustain. Then he dies, mid rapture, in the arms of the woman whose waking was meant to save him. The exchange is bought with a small fraud on the audience's sense of time, and the fraud is worth naming because every later adapter who kept the waking repeated it. Shakespeare's poison is famously quick, and Romeo's own valediction remarks on the speed. Otway's draught, bought with the same urgency and described in the same terms, is required to work slowly enough to sustain a dialogue of recognition, rapture and farewell, and the text simply declines to apologize for the delay, stretching the poison across the scene's emotional business the way an opera stretches a death across an aria. The convention thus founded, in which a poisoned lover sings, speaks or duets at length on the far side of the dose, descends directly from this scene into the Garrick tomb and the nineteenth century's operatic versions, where the delay becomes the entire point. Verisimilitude was the Restoration's professed standard, the quality its critics demanded of Shakespeare and found wanting; in the tomb at Dorset Garden, its own adapter traded the standard away for a duet, and the audience's two centuries of applause ratified the trade.

The waking also redistributes the tragedy's blame, and the redistribution explains why the scene satisfies audiences in a way the original's ending, for all its poetry, was felt not to. In Shakespeare, the catastrophe is an epistemic disaster: Romeo dies for a false report, Juliet wakes to the consequences of a truth that arrived minutes late, and the moral weight falls on accident, on the stopped letter and the mistimed dawn. In Otway, the lovers are granted the knowledge Shakespeare denies them. Lavinia wakes in time to be told, and to understand, exactly what has happened; Marius dies knowing that the scheme worked and that the poison, not the plan, is the murderer; and Lavinia's suicide that follows is not an error but a verdict, delivered with her father's corpse at her feet and addressed to the conqueror who made the world in which the scheme was necessary. Accident shrinks to the single failure of a boy's errand, and responsibility migrates outward to the war, the fathers and the state, which is where the Roman frame had been pointing the blame all evening. The eighteenth century, which kept the waking while professing to restore Shakespeare, was not merely indulging its actors. It was keeping the version of the ending whose moral arithmetic it preferred, in which the lovers die informed and accusing rather than deceived, and the city, not the stars, stands indicted at the curtain.

The scene continues past the point where Shakespeare's ends, and its remaining turns show Otway spending the capital the waking has earned. One refinement of the machinery deserves notice before the turns themselves, because it shows the adapter repairing a joint that Shakespeare left weak. In the original, the message fails far away and offstage, stopped by a plague quarantine in another town, and the Friar's ignorance of the failure is a reported fact the audience must accept. In Otway, the Priest dispatches his boy with the letter from the churchyard itself, at the very threshold of the monument, in the same minutes in which Marius junior is forcing his way in at the other side of the set. Help and catastrophe arrive at the same door at the same moment, and the audience watches the rescue being launched too late rather than being told that it miscarried elsewhere. The failure is thus converted from an accident of distance into an accident of simultaneity, visible in stage time, and the Priest's subsequent death at the hands of the man he came to save completes the design: the plot's engineer is not detained by authority or disease but destroyed by his own device arriving sixty seconds behind the poison. Lavinia finds the empty vial and repeats Juliet's reproach to it, that he drank all and left no friendly drop for poor Lavinia. Before she can act on it, the war enters the churchyard: Marius senior's guards drive Metellus in, and her father is killed before her eyes by the faction she married into. She turns on the old conqueror with the play's most terrible courtesy, reminding him that she once saved his life in exile, that he took her in his arms then and used her like a father, and that her reward is a butchered parent and a dead husband. She seizes his sword, the sword still reeking, in her phrase, with her father's gore, and stabs herself with it, driving the blade into her own breast while he watches. Marius senior is left alive among the bodies to learn that Sylla has returned and that Sulpitius lies mortally wounded, and he is led away to die offstage, a hopeless vessel, in his own words, bound for the dark land of loathsome death and loaded deep with sorrows. The last speech of the play then belongs to the dying Sulpitius, carried in by guards, who reprises Mercutio's wound jokes in prose as a man expires: the hurt is not so deep as a well nor so wide as a church door, but it is enough, it will serve, and he is peppered for this world. He dictates his own comic epitaph, for a troublesome slave who sent many honester men to the grave and died like a fool having lived like a knave, and the play ends. Rome gets no reconciliation speech. The golden statues are never promised. The curtain falls on a joke told by the Mercutio figure over the wreckage of both houses, and the effect is colder than Shakespeare's closing order: a world in which wit survives the catastrophe and nothing is repaired.

The waking itself is the adaptation's most debated bequest, and the debate is best settled by separating the two questions it confuses. The first question is where the device came from. The Yale Shakespeare's stage history, edited by William Allan Neilson and Ashley Horace Thorndike, states that Otway's ending was based on one of the old Italian stories of the lovers, in which Juliet awakens before Romeo dies and both learn of his fatal mistake. That claim has a real foundation: in Matteo Bandello's novella, the tale as Shakespeare's predecessors transmitted it, Giulietta wakes while Romeo, poisoned, still lives, and the two exchange words before his death. Shakespeare's own handling was the innovation against the tale, since Arthur Brooke's poem and the play after it keep Juliet asleep until Romeo is dead and make her waking an encounter with a corpse. Otway, who can have known the story's Italian form only at a remove if at all, restored on the stage a configuration the novella had contained and the play had discarded.

### Did Otway invent Lavinia's waking in the tomb?

As a stage device, yes; as a story element, no. The waking had stood in Bandello's novella, which Shakespeare's version set aside, but no English acting text before 1679 let the lovers meet awake in the tomb; Otway put the meeting back into performance, and the stage tradition descends from his text.

The second question is what the stage did with the device, and there the line of descent is documentary rather than speculative. When Theophilus Cibber brought Shakespeare's play back to the London stage at the Haymarket in 1744, ending the original's long absence, the Yale history records that he retained this feature of Otway's play, the waking, and omitted the references to Rosaline besides. The restoration, in other words, was partial and knew it: Shakespeare's play returned wearing Otway's ending. The adapter who then fixed the play's eighteenth century form inherited the device rather than inventing it, and the comparison that Article 295 of this series owns in full can be stated here in its bearing on Otway alone: [David Garrick's adaptation of 1748](/2027/08/27/david-garrick-adaptation-1748/) took over a tomb scene whose central stroke, [Garrick's tomb scene, in which Juliet wakes in time](/2027/09/10/garrick-tomb-scene-juliet-wakes/), had been naturalized on the English stage by the Roman rewrite seventy years before, and Garrick's alterations, for all their fame, preserved the waking that Cibber had preserved from Otway. From Garrick the device passed into the nineteenth century's acting versions and into the operatic tradition, where Gounod's *Roméo et Juliette* of 1867 still lets Juliet wake for a final duet over Romeo's body. A change made by a twenty seven year old adapter in a borrowed plot thus outlived the play that contained it by centuries: *Caius Marius* is rarely read and almost never staged, but every Juliet who wakes in time to speak to her Romeo is performing Otway's revision of Shakespeare, whether the programme credits him or not.

The device's tenacity had a professional logic that the documentary record lets a reader trace. The waking enlarges the heroine's part at the exact point where Shakespeare's design withdraws it: a Juliet who wakes to a corpse has a mad scene and a dagger, while a Lavinia or a Garrick era Juliet who wakes in time has a love scene, played opposite the evening's Romeo at the climax of the evening, and the actresses who inherited the role defended the enlargement accordingly. The part's line of descent runs through performers whose names mark the role's prestige across three generations, from Elizabeth Barry, who created Lavinia and spoke the first epilogue out of the tomb, through Anne Bracegirdle and Mary Porter in the Roman play's own revivals, and into the Shakespearean Juliets of the Garrick age and beyond, for whom the waking was not an interpolation to be apologized for but the scene the audience had paid to see. The Yale history records how long the borrowed ending survived even after the restoration of Shakespeare's own conception began in earnest: Charlotte Cushman restored Shakespeare's disposition of the tomb in 1845, letting Juliet wake to silence again, yet the additions to the death scene that descend from Otway remained in use in an American production as late as 1899, and nineteenth century managers such as Henry Irving, while professing fidelity, still cut most of the scene that follows Juliet's death in Shakespeare's arrangement. A stage device that outlasts the movement organized to remove it is not surviving on inertia. It is surviving because it gives the two leading performers the one thing Shakespeare's ending denies them, a final exchange in which both are conscious, and audiences rewarded that exchange for two hundred years.

## Theft or Craft: Langbaine, Schlegel and the Adaptation Aesthetic

The earliest surviving verdict on the borrowing is also the bluntest. Gerard Langbaine's *An Account of the English Dramatick Poets*, published in 1691, six years after Otway's death, enters the play in its catalogue with the remark that part of it is stolen from Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet*, and assigns the Roman plot to Plutarch's *Life of Marius* and the second book of Lucan. Langbaine was no enemy of Otway; his account is the foundation document of English dramatic bibliography, and the entry treats the theft as a bibliographical fact to be recorded, like the dedication to Lord Falkland printed in the same entry. The sentence matters because of its calm. In 1691 a man cataloguing the national drama could call half a successful play stolen property without heat, because the categories that would later make the word an accusation, originality as the measure of worth and the author's text as inviolable, were not the categories his theatre worked by. The age's own hierarchy of reputations points the same way. John Dryden, the dominant dramatist and critic of the Restoration stage, publicly attached his name and his praise to Otway's work, contributing the prologue to *Venice Preserved* three years after *Caius Marius* and esteeming its author in print, which is not the conduct of a literary culture that regarded its rising tragedian as a thief to be shunned. Otway's contemporaries ranked him by the plays that were wholly his and treated the borrowing in the Roman play as a workshop fact, interesting to a cataloguer and irrelevant to a playgoer.

The prologue itself contains the period's most compact theory of why the borrowing happened, and it is a theory about patronage rather than about talent. Its opening movement argues that poetry follows power: when great Augustus held the world's empire, Horace and Ovid excelled, the one lashed by favour and the other melted hearts by it, and Shakespeare, in the prologue's parallel, wrote in an age as blessed, since a gracious prince's favour cheered his muse, a constant favour he never feared to lose, and therefore he wrote with fancy unconfined and thoughts immortal as his mind. The present age, by the argument's logic, has lost its Caesar, and a muse without a patron must feed where it can, gleaning after the great crop and rifling a play when its own invention runs short. The confession of theft is thus framed as an economic symptom, the disbanded soldier poet of the epilogue stealing sheaves because the harvest's owner is absent and the gleaners are hungry. Schlegel read the confession as an aggravation. The prologue intends it as a diagnosis, and on the evidence of the repertory, where every company in London was reworking old plays for the same structural reasons, the diagnosis is the more accurate document.

The accusation acquired its heat later, and its most quoted form belongs to A. W. Schlegel, whose lectures on dramatic art, delivered in Vienna in 1808 and read across Europe in translation, used Otway as the type of the adapter's offence. How little Otway understood the true rules of composition, Schlegel wrote, may be inferred from the fact that he has taken half the scenes of *Caius Marius* verbally, or with disfiguring changes, from Shakespeare's play; nothing more incongruous could be conceived than such an episode in Roman manners and in a historical drama; and, in the sentence that refuses the prologue's defence in advance, this impudent plagiarism is in no manner justified by his confessing it. Schlegel's standard is the Romantic one, by which a work is a made thing whose parts must grow from one conception, and by that standard the graft is indefensible on its face: Queen Mab does not belong in the Forum, and a Roman tribune delivering a fairy speech is a solecism no confession can launder.

Between Langbaine's calm and Schlegel's contempt stands the evidence of what the transaction actually was, and Marsden's account of the adaptation culture supplies the terms for judging it. The Restoration and eighteenth century stage, as Marsden demonstrates, inherited a critical framework in which a play's identity lived in its plot and its moral design rather than in its verbal texture; words were the working clothes of a story, replaceable by any competent tailor, and the fifty and more adaptations that framework licensed were not acts of vandalism against monuments but the ordinary maintenance of a repertory. Shakespeare's canonization as a verbal monument, the process by which his lines became the thing to be preserved, was completed only later, and it was completed partly by editors and critics who needed the adaptations as the darkness against which the restored text could shine. On Marsden's showing, the question Schlegel asks, by what right did Otway take the words, would have puzzled the audience of 1679, for whom the prior question was whether the story, in its new dress, still moved. The Dorset Garden receipts, the revivals and the sixty year absence of the original are the answer that audience left on record. Marsden's frame also explains the timing of the practice's long decline, which her study bounds at 1777: the adaptations flourished exactly as long as the theatre's hierarchy of values put plot above language, and they withered as the editorial century rebuilt Shakespeare's authority on the verbal text, until by the age of the great actor managers the adapter's licence had shrunk to cutting and arrangement. *Caius Marius* stands near the head of that arc as one of its most thorough products, written before Tate's *Lear* had shown how far an ending could be reversed, and it demonstrates that the licence at its height was not mere vandalism but a coherent rival theory of what a play is: a design of situations and passions that each generation re-clothes, rather than a fixed verbal object that each generation preserves. The theory lost. The waking survived its defeat, which is the kind of detail a history of the play should not let the winning side explain away.

The adjudication this article gives is therefore split, and deliberately so. On the verbal charge, Langbaine and Schlegel are right and Otway's own prologue pleads guilty for him: half the play is Shakespeare's, taken verbally or with disfiguring changes, and the changes to the letter are mostly losses, since Otway's tidying hand smooths Shakespeare's roughness into Augustan regularity wherever it touches a line it keeps. On the theatrical charge, the plagiarism verdict fails, because it measures a repertory transaction by a law of property that theatre had not enacted. What Otway took verbally he repaid structurally: the balcony specification, the doubled Priest, the abolished duel and above all the waking are original contributions to the play's stage life that no merely faithful staging of Shakespeare's text in 1679 would have produced, and two of them are still in use. A thief who leaves the house richer than he found it has committed a crime the ledger cannot quite record. The Restoration had a plainer word for the whole operation, and Otway's prologue used it of Shakespeare himself: gleaning, the gathering of what a great crop leaves behind, practised humbly by every poet since. The prologue's humility is a pose, but the economics are exact.

The cooler reference verdicts of the nineteenth century show the charge settling into its permanent form, neither acquittal nor Schlegel's fury. The *Dictionary of National Biography* treats the play as the least successful of Otway's major works, produced by Betterton very soon after *The Orphan* in its mistaken chronology, and remarks that such enthusiasm as the performance evoked belonged to the comic acting rather than to the design. The eleventh edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* compresses the whole affair into a single dry sentence, calling the play a curious grafting of Shakespeare's tragedy on the story of Marius as related in Plutarch's *Lives*. Grafting is the honest metaphor, and it quietly replaces the theft metaphor Langbaine had planted, because a graft is judged not by the purity of its stock but by whether the joined thing lives and bears. By that test the reference books' own records convict their tone: the same authorities that call the play curious or less successful also record that it was acted for decades and that its ending governed the stage's handling of the original for a century beyond its own life. Meanwhile the ground was shifting under the whole question, as the editorial tradition that runs from Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709 rebuilt Shakespeare as an author whose words, not merely whose plots, constituted the work. Once editors had made the verbal text the monument, the adapters who had treated words as clothing became retrospectively criminal, and Otway, the most thorough of the tailors, attracted the longest sentence. The history of the charge is therefore also a history of the standard applied, and an essay on the play that does not say which standard it is applying, the repertory's or the editor's, will convict or acquit by accident.

## The Long Reign: Performance Record and the Return of the Text

The claim that *Caius Marius* displaced Shakespeare's play for decades is often repeated and rarely itemized, and the itemization makes it both smaller and more interesting. The displacement itself is not in doubt: between the autumn of 1679 and Cibber's Haymarket revival of 1744, no London company is recorded staging *Romeo and Juliet* in a form its author would have recognized as his text rather than as Otway's Roman substitute or, earlier, Howard's reversed ending. The original was absent from its own stage for sixty five years. What the round numbers of the handbooks conceal is the difference between the substitute's active life and the original's exile. The exile lasted until 1744 by definition, since it ended only when the play returned. The substitute's own performances are another matter, and they thin out far sooner.

The fullest early summary of the afterlife is the introduction Robert Adger Law prepared for his 1916 edition of Shakespeare's play, which records that the hybrid continued to be acted at least as late as 1717, and that in its later life it played at both Drury Lane and the Haymarket, with Anne Bracegirdle and afterward Mary Porter in Lavinia's part, while the Nurse, at every revival Law lists, was again taken by a man.

The casting thread in Law's summary deserves a sentence of its own, because it tracks the adaptation's pleasures across its whole life. The Nurse begins as Nokes, the company's star comedian in skirts, and remains a man's part through four decades of revivals, outlasting changes of management, theatre and regime, while the Lavinia line passes from Barry, the age's defining tragedienne, to Bracegirdle, the defining actress of the next generation, to Porter, a leading tragedienne of the early Georgian stage. A play whose heroine is inherited by the best actress of each succeeding age and whose comic lead is a permanent drag institution is not being kept alive by antiquarian piety; it is being kept alive by performers who found the parts worth their reputations. The print record runs in parallel. The 1680 quarto, sold by Thomas Flesher at the Angel and Crown in St Paul's Churchyard, presents the text as it is acted at the Duke's Theatre, with the dedication to Otway's Christ Church contemporary Lord Falkland, in which the author records that he first grew in love with books under Falkland's example, and with the cast list that lets a reader reconstruct the first night entire. That quarto is the reason the adaptation can be studied at the level of the line at all, and its survival makes the contrast with Shakespeare's own play instructive: for the first century of its stage life, the Roman rewrite existed in a clean, authorial, cast carrying printed text, while the original circulated in the corrupt and competing states that its eighteenth century editors would spend their careers repairing. A documented stage life of thirty eight years, from 1679 to 1717, is a formidable run for a Restoration tragedy, longer than the active lives of most new plays of the period, and it carried the part of Lavinia into the repertories of the two most celebrated actresses of the succeeding generation. After 1717 the record falls silent, and the honest conclusion is that the play died as a stage piece a full generation before the exile it had imposed on Shakespeare's original came to an end. In print it lived on without interruption, passing into the collected editions of Otway's works that the eighteenth century produced for the library market, where a reader could study the Roman rewrite at leisure while no manager would risk it on a stage. That divergence, a text continuously available and continuously unperformed, is the normal afterlife of the great adaptations, and it matters to the play's history because it kept the evidence intact for the scholars who would eventually reverse the verdict of the stage: everything the modern argument about Otway needs, the prologue, the cast list, the balcony direction and the waking, survived in print through the very decades when the play itself had vanished from the boards. For roughly the last twenty five years of the displacement, the London stage had neither Otway's Rome nor Shakespeare's Verona: it had the story in excerpts, in other men's alterations preparing in the wings, and in the memory of a tomb scene that managers were determined to keep.

That distinction resolves the quarrel of the round numbers. René Weis, introducing the play in the Arden Third Series edition, writes that Otway's version displaced Shakespeare's original in English theatres for the next sixty years, while other handbook accounts, including the widely read lives of the poets laureate tradition, give seventy. Both are measuring the exile and rounding it, down or up, from sixty five. Neither is measuring the substitute's own run, which the bills support for thirty eight years. The brief behind this article repeated the seventy year figure, following one handbook tradition; the documents support the more precise statement that Shakespeare's play was absent for sixty five years, that Otway's replacement is documented in performance for at least thirty eight of them, and that the waking the replacement introduced survived the whole period and everything after it. Precision here is not pedantry, because the two spans carry different verdicts: a play acted for seventy years would be a phenomenon of taste, while a play acted for thirty eight years whose single stage device was then stolen by the restoration is a phenomenon of influence, which is the more durable kind.

### When did Shakespeare's own play return to the London stage?

In 1744, when Theophilus Cibber revived it at the Haymarket after an absence of sixty five years. The return was not a restoration of the untouched text: Cibber kept Otway's waking in the tomb and cut the Rosaline material, so the original came back already edited by its substitute.

The revival of 1744 begins the long process, completed only in the nineteenth century, that [the campaign of restoring Shakespeare's text to the stage](/2027/10/08/restoring-shakespeare-text-stage/) had to carry out against the accumulated authority of the adapters, and the order of that process is the final measure of Otway's position in the play's history. Cibber restored the Verona names and kept the Roman ending's best idea. Garrick restored more of the text and kept the waking still. The nineteenth century, in the person of Charlotte Cushman and her successors, at last restored Shakespeare's own disposition of the tomb, in which Juliet wakes to silence, and the Yale history notes that even then the Otway derived additions to the death scene lingered in American production as late as 1899. Each restorer defined the work against the adaptation tradition, and the adaptation tradition, for this play, has one name at its head. Davenant had handled the play, Howard had reversed it, but the version the restorers had to displace, in the tomb scene above all, was Otway's. To be the obstacle that two centuries of restoration had to clear is a strange species of fame, and *Caius Marius* has it entire.

The verdict on Otway's role can now be stated without remainder. He was not Shakespeare's rival and never claimed to be; his prologue ranks him the most unworthy of the throng gleaning after the great crop. He was the tragedy's most effective custodian during the long period when its text had no defenders, because he kept its story, its scenes and its strongest rhetoric in active repertory by the only method his theatre trusted, rewriting. The cost of the custody is written in the same quarto: a duel abolished, a friar murdered by his own penitent, a wit's death postponed into a closing joke, and half the verbal surface smoothed or stolen. The gain is written in the subsequent record: a balcony the original never specified, and a waking in the tomb that the original's restorers themselves refused to surrender for a hundred and fifty years. Criticism since Schlegel has mostly counted the cost. The stage, which is the court the play itself recognizes, counted the gain, and kept counting it long after the play that earned it had left the bills.

For the larger argument of this series, the Roman rewrite is the standing counterexample to the assumption that Shakespeare's play held the English stage by right of genius from the beginning. It did not hold the stage at all for two generations; a rival play held it, built from its own scenes, and the original's return was negotiated with the rival's inventions rather than proclaimed over their corpses. Performance history written honestly therefore has to treat adaptation not as the noise around the signal but as, for long stretches, the signal itself: the form in which audiences actually encountered the story, the standard against which restorations defined their fidelity, and the channel through which at least two permanent features of the play's staging reached the modern theatre. Otway's name belongs in the first rank of that history, not in a footnote of shame. The twenty seven year old who confessed in his prologue that he had rifled half a play turned out to have rifled it into the future, and the future, in the form of every balcony built and every Juliet permitted a last duet with her dying lover, has been living in the rifled house ever since.

## Reading the Two Plays Side by Side

*Caius Marius* rewards the kind of study its structure invites, which is parallel reading. The 1680 quarto is short, accessible in facsimile and in the Early English Books Online transcription, and its second and fifth acts can be laid directly against Shakespeare's second and fifth acts, speech by speech, so that a reader can watch the adapter's hand at work: the line kept entire, the line regularized, the line replaced by politics, the stage direction that adds a balcony where the copy had a window. For classroom presentation of that comparison, a slide deck built from paired passages, Shakespeare's garden scene on one side and the Roman garden on the other, makes the argument faster than any summary; assembling and rehearsing such a deck is straightforward with [the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which suits the exercise because the evidence in this case is visual, a matter of two texts held in the eye at once. The questions that parallel reading answers are the questions this article has pressed throughout. Which speeches did Otway judge untouchable. Where did his own invention concentrate. What did the Restoration hear in Shakespeare that later centuries stopped hearing, and what did it refuse to hear that later centuries prize. The answers are all on the page, in the one form of criticism the adapter himself practised: another text, set beside the first, arguing with it line by line.

Two exercises sharpen the comparison fastest. The first is the prologue and epilogue read as a pair, since between them the production explains itself: the prologue confesses the theft and prices it as gleaning, the epilogue confesses the occasion and prices it as a benefit for a disbanded soldier, and a reader who starts from those two frames will not mistake the play for either a monument or a prank. The second is the pair of first acts, where the adapter's priorities are least disguised by fidelity: Shakespeare opens with servants brawling and a prince's edict, Otway with senators debating ambition and a tribune presenting a severed head, and the distance between those two openings measures the whole redesign before a line of the love plot has been borrowed. A seminar that works through the openings, the garden scenes and the churchyards in that order possesses the argument of this article in primary evidence, and can test its verdict, that the adapter was the tragedy's most effective custodian in its weakest century, against the only witnesses whose testimony cannot be coached: the two printed plays, open side by side.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Otway comparison, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Otway comparison is the table in this article aligning Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet* with Otway's *Caius Marius* element by element: hero, heroine, feud, suitor, friend, go-between, churchman, night meeting, escape device, catastrophe and last word. The pattern it exposes is that Otway changed the causes and kept the machinery. Every device that generates feeling in the original survives the move to Rome, while every explanation of the catastrophe is replaced, the empty feud by a contest for consulships, the street duel by civil war, and the Prince's judgment by a dying wit's joke.

### Q: When and where was Caius Marius first performed, and by which company?

*Caius Marius* was first performed in the autumn of 1679, in October by the modern stage calendar, at the Dorset Garden Theatre in London. The company was the Duke's Company, the patent troupe managed from Davenant's foundation and led on stage by Thomas Betterton. Older authorities, including the *Dictionary of National Biography* and the Yale Shakespeare's history, dated the premiere 1680, placing it after *The Orphan*, but the calendar evidence assembled in *The London Stage* supports 1679, and modern reference works follow it. The printed quarto followed in 1680, from Thomas Flesher's shop in St Paul's Churchyard.

### Q: Who played Lavinia, Marius junior and the elder Marius in the first production?

Thomas Betterton played the elder Caius Marius, William Smith played Marius junior, and Elizabeth Barry played Lavinia. The cast list printed in the 1680 quarto assigns the remaining parts across the Duke's Company's strength: Joseph Williams as Sylla, Thomas Gillow as Metellus, Thomas Jevon as Cinna, Thomas Percival as Granius, Cave Underhill as Sulpitius and James Nokes as the Nurse. Betterton also spoke the prologue, and Barry, who had created leading parts for Otway since *Alcibiades*, spoke the epilogue in character as Lavinia, newly risen from the tomb the epilogue jokes about.

### Q: What does Otway's prologue say about Shakespeare and the borrowing?

The prologue calls Shakespeare the happiest poet of his time and the best, says that succeeding poets have humbly gleaned from the crop of his luxuriant pen ever since, and then confesses the theft in a joke: the poet of the day, the most unworthy of the throng, fears he has done Shakespeare wrong, since, like greedy beggars stealing sheaves, he has rifled him of half a play, whose stolen matter will shine amid the baser dross around it. The confession is complete, cheerful and unrepentant, and it doubles as an advertisement, promising the audience Shakespeare's best scenes inside a new Roman frame.

### Q: Which two Roman houses replace the Montagues and Capulets, and what replaces the feud?

The house of Marius and the house of Metellus replace them. Marius junior is the son of Caius Marius, the seven times consul and popular general; Lavinia is the daughter of Metellus, the senatorial commander Marius supplanted in Numidia. What replaces the feud is a struggle for power in the late republic: elections to the consulship, command of the war against Mithridates, exile, armed return and proscription. Where Shakespeare's households fight for no stated reason, Otway's factions fight over offices and armies that Plutarch's *Life of Marius* itemizes, and the lovers' marriage becomes one alliance inside a civil war.

### Q: Who is Sulpitius, and which of Shakespeare's characters does he absorb?

Sulpitius is Marius senior's tribune, the political lieutenant who presides over the Forum election and carries the faction's case to the crowd. He absorbs Mercutio. Otway gives him the Queen Mab material, compressed, and Mercutio's contempt for love as a painted scarecrow, but withholds Mercutio's death: there is no duel in the third act, and Sulpitius survives until the final scene, where he is carried in mortally wounded from the street fighting. His dying reprise of Mercutio's wound jokes, capped by a comic epitaph he dictates for himself, closes the play, so the voice Shakespeare silences at the midpoint speaks the last word in Rome.

### Q: What role does Sylla play in the love plot?

Sylla is the destined bridegroom. Metellus, seeking terms with the rising patrician general, promises him Lavinia, and she is told the news on the night of the garden scene: all Rome brings Sylla sacrifices of some sort, and she must be a victim to his bed. The part corresponds to Paris in Shakespeare's design, the authorised suitor whose claim drives the heroine to the sleeping draught, but Otway enlarges it past recognition, since Sylla is also the enemy whose approaching army frames the whole action and whose return, announced in the final scene, breaks Marius senior. The rival never appears at the tomb; his function there is inherited by the Priest.

### Q: How does Lavinia escape her marriage to Sylla?

She escapes it by a feigned death arranged by the Priest who had married her to Marius junior. The Priest gives her a vial, in the fourth act, whose draught counterfeits death, so that she can be carried to the monument of the Metelli as a corpse and wake beyond her father's reach. The device is Friar Laurence's potion transferred without structural change, and it fails in the same way: the Priest sends a boy with a letter to find Marius junior and warn him, the message miscarries, and the husband arrives at the monument believing the death real, with poison already bought from the Apothecary.

### Q: Why does Marius junior kill the Priest at the monument?

He kills him by mistake, in darkness, taking him for a tomb robber. The Priest comes to the monument alone, in disguise, carrying a mattock and a wrenching iron to open the tomb for Lavinia's waking, and Marius, who has just forced the monument himself, warns the intruder off as a villain come to rob and spoil the dead. The Priest refuses to leave and moves to seize him, and Marius kills him before recognizing the honest priest who had joined the lovers' hands. The blow transfers Paris's tomb death to the plot's own engineer, and it destroys the one man who knows Lavinia lives.

### Q: How does Lavinia die in Otway's ending?

Lavinia dies by Marius senior's sword, in her own hand, after her father is killed before her. Waking to find her husband dead of the poison, she reproaches the empty vial, but before she can follow Juliet's course the guards drive Metellus into the churchyard and he falls dead in front of her. She confronts the elder Marius with her history, the life she saved in his exile and the reward she has reaped, seizes his sword, still reeking, as she says, with her father's gore, and plunges it into her own breast. Her death is thus a public accusation, staged as evidence against the conqueror, rather than a private act in an emptied tomb.

### Q: Who speaks the last speech of Caius Marius, and in what tone?

The dying Sulpitius speaks it, in the tone of a joke. Carried in by guards, mortally wounded in the street fighting that follows Sylla's return, he dismisses the elder Marius's repentance as whining, repeats Mercutio's measurement of the wound, not so deep as a well nor so wide as a church door but enough, and dictates his own epitaph: here lies the troublesome slave Sulpitius, who sent many honester men to the grave and died like a fool having lived like a knave. The play then ends without reconciliation, statues or princely judgment, its final energy spent on wit at the edge of death.

### Q: What happens to the elder Marius at the end of the play?

He survives the catastrophe and is destroyed by news. Standing among the bodies of his son, Lavinia and Metellus, he laments over them and kisses the dead man's cheeks, and a messenger then brings word that Sylla has returned, that his army marches from Capua and will reach Rome the next day, and that the crowd is in fresh rebellion. Marius reads his ruin in the name of Sylla, warns the great against embroiling their country's peace and dipping their hands in slaughter, and is led away to his bed to die offstage, calling himself a hopeless vessel bound for the dark land of loathsome death.

### Q: What does the garden scene keep word for word from Shakespeare, and what does it change?

It keeps the architecture and most of the language of Shakespeare's orchard scene: the heroine's name speech with Marius substituted for Romeo, the rose argument entire, the refusal to swear by the inconstant moon, the bounty speech, the Nurse calling within and the appointment for nine the next morning. It changes the pressure under which the vows are exchanged. Lavinia has already been promised to Sylla that evening and threatened with expulsion from her father's house if she resists, so the betrothal is negotiated against a named bridegroom and an announced wedding rather than against a marriage her parents have not yet proposed.

### Q: What did Gerard Langbaine say about the play's debt in 1691?

Langbaine wrote that part of the play is stolen from Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet*. The remark stands in his *An Account of the English Dramatick Poets* of 1691, the founding catalogue of English drama, where the same entry assigns the Roman plot to Plutarch's *Life of Marius* and the second book of Lucan's *Pharsalia* and records the dedication to Lord Falkland. Its tone is bibliographical rather than scandalized: the theft is entered as a fact of the play's composition, six years after Otway's death, by a cataloguer who treated adaptation as a normal feature of the drama he was inventorying.

### Q: What did A. W. Schlegel call Otway's borrowing, and on what ground?

Schlegel called it impudent plagiarism, and held that the confession in the prologue justified it in no manner. His ground, in the Vienna lectures on dramatic art of 1808, was incongruity: Otway, he argued, understood little of the true rules of composition, having taken half his scenes verbally or with disfiguring changes from Shakespeare and set them as an episode in Roman manners inside a historical drama, where Mercutio's fairy lore and Verona's love plot could only jar. The judgment measures the adaptation by the Romantic standard of organic unity, a standard the Restoration repertory that produced and rewarded the play did not apply.

### Q: How far does the documented performance record of Caius Marius extend?

It extends at least to 1717. Robert Adger Law's introduction to his 1916 edition of Shakespeare's play records the hybrid as acted at least as late as that year, after appearances at both Drury Lane and the Haymarket, with Anne Bracegirdle and afterward Mary Porter playing Lavinia and the Nurse still assigned to a man at every revival listed. The bills therefore support thirty eight years of stage life, from 1679 into the reign of George I. After 1717 the record falls silent, although the absence of Shakespeare's original, which the play had imposed, continued until Cibber's revival of 1744.

### Q: What did Theophilus Cibber retain from Otway when he revived Shakespeare's play in 1744?

He retained the waking in the tomb. Cibber's Haymarket production of 1744 restored Shakespeare's Verona, his names and most of his text after sixty five years, but, as the Yale Shakespeare's stage history records, he kept this feature of Otway's play, letting Juliet wake while Romeo still lived and the two learn of the mistake together, and he cut the Rosaline material in the same operation. The restoration was therefore an edit negotiated with the adaptation it displaced, and the device Cibber preserved passed from his version into Garrick's, which fixed the play's form for the rest of the century.

### Q: Which classical authors supplied the Roman half of the play?

Plutarch and Lucan supplied it. Langbaine's catalogue entry of 1691 assigns the plot to Plutarch's *Life of Caius Marius*, read in the North translation tradition, and to the second book of Lucan's *Pharsalia*, the epic of Rome's civil wars. Plutarch provides the biography: the Numidian grievance against Metellus, the six consulships, the exile among the ruins of Carthage, the return with Cinna and the terror. Lucan provides atmosphere and, as the scholarship on the play's sources argues, the image of citizens hiding among the dead in tombs during the proscriptions, which stands behind the last act's gathering at the monument.

### Q: How does Plutarch's Marius die, and how does Otway's ending differ?

Plutarch's Marius dies of illness, pleurisy following drink, in January 86 BC, days into his seventh consulship and fresh from the massacre of his enemies. Otway's Marius does not die on stage at all. He is led away broken by grief over his son and by the news of Sylla's return, prophesying his own end as a hopeless vessel bound for the dark land of loathsome death, and the play closes instead on the dying Sulpitius. The son's death differs further: the historical Marius the Younger died at Praeneste in 82 BC, four years after his father, where Otway kills him in the tomb beside Lavinia inside his father's lifetime.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Otway comparison to judge Otway's place in the play's history?

An essay should use the InsightCrunch Otway comparison as the distinction between custody and authorship. Set out the table's two columns and argue from them: the right hand column proves that Otway held the story in active repertory for a generation by rewriting it, keeping Shakespeare's machinery of feeling while replacing every cause; the stage record then shows which of his contributions the original's own restorers could not surrender, the balcony and the waking. Judged by that evidence, Otway stands in the play's history neither as rival nor as vandal but as its most consequential adapter, the custodian whose thefts the restoration itself ended by ratifying.
