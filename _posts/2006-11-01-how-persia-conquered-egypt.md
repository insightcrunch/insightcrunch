Persia conquered Egypt in a single campaigning season in 525 BCE, and then lost the argument about it for the next two and a half thousand years. That double outcome is what makes this campaign worth studying carefully rather than summarizing. The military story is a clean, well-executed operation that solved the hardest logistical problem in ancient Near Eastern warfare and won the decisive battle at the one point where Egypt could be beaten. The reputational story is the opposite: a conqueror who by the Egyptian documentary evidence behaved like a reasonably conventional new pharaoh became, in the Greek tradition, a temple-desecrating lunatic who stabbed a sacred bull and lost an army in the desert, and that version is the one nearly everyone knows.

This article covers both, because separating them is the actual intellectual work. The claim it advances is what this series calls the conquest-and-slander thesis: Persia won the war and lost the propaganda, and the Cambyses-the-madman story is substantially a later hostile construction that Egyptian and Persian documents contradict at several specific points. That is not a rehabilitation of Persian rule, which generated real grievance for real reasons, and this article names those reasons. It is an argument about how to weigh a vivid narrative source against dull administrative ones, which is the central methodological problem of the whole Late Period.

## How Egypt Fought and What Decided Its Battles

Understanding why Egypt fell in 525 BCE requires understanding what the Egyptian military was by that point, because the answer is not the army most readers picture.

The New Kingdom army that fought at Megiddo and Kadesh had been a first-rate integrated force: massed archery, chariotry used as a mobile missile platform, organized infantry divisions, and a professional command structure. That army was eight centuries gone. What Saite Egypt fielded was something else entirely, assembled from three components that did not fully integrate.

The numerical bulk came from the machimoi, the hereditary warrior stratum of largely Libyan descent who held land in exchange for military obligation. They were Egyptianized in every cultural respect and constituted a genuine political constituency as well as a military one. What they were not was heavy infantry trained to fight shoulder to shoulder in armored formation, because Egypt had no tradition of that style of warfare and no economic structure that would have produced it.

The professional edge came from Ionian and Carian mercenaries hired from Anatolia, settled permanently in Delta camps and later at Memphis, and paid in silver. These were hoplites or close to it, fighting in the massed armored style that had become decisive across the eastern Mediterranean. They gave Egypt a capability it could not generate domestically, and they gave Egypt a permanent political problem, since the machimoi resented being paid in land while foreigners were paid in metal.

The third component was the navy, which was the Saite dynasty's genuine innovation. Necho II built triremes on both the Mediterranean and the Red Sea, and Egyptian ships and crews were a real force in the eastern Mediterranean by the sixth century BCE.

What decided Egyptian battles in this period was therefore rarely Egyptian tactics. It was whether the professional component was present, paid, and willing, and whether the fight happened at a place where Egypt's defensive geography did the work that Egyptian formations could not. Pelusium was that place, and in 525 BCE it did not save the country.

### What decided the outcome of the Persian conquest of Egypt?

Logistics and defection more than tactics. Cambyses solved the Sinai water problem before crossing, which was the barrier that had stopped earlier invaders, and a defecting Greek commander in Egyptian service supplied the route knowledge. The battle at Pelusium was then a conventional engagement Persia was equipped to win.

## The Strategic Situation Before the Invasion

Egypt did not stumble into this war unaware. Amasis, who ruled from circa 570 BCE, watched the Achaemenid state assemble itself and understood exactly what it meant.

Cyrus took Media in the 550s BCE, Lydia around 546 BCE, and Babylon in 539 BCE. Within roughly two decades a single power came to control the Iranian plateau, Anatolia, Mesopotamia, and the Levantine coast down toward the Sinai. Every polity that might have balanced Persia had been absorbed by Persia. Amasis attempted the obvious counter, a coalition with Lydia and Babylon and with Greek powers including Samos, and the counter failed for the straightforward reason that most of the proposed partners were conquered before the coalition could function.

Egypt's remaining advantages were geographic and financial. The Sinai crossing was a genuine barrier: roughly a hundred and fifty kilometers of waterless desert between the Levantine coastal settlements and the eastern Delta, impassable for a large army without pre-positioned water. The eastern Delta beyond it was marshy, cut by channels, and dominated by the fortress of Pelusium. And Egypt was wealthy enough to keep hiring the professional soldiers who could hold a fortified line.

The vulnerability was that all of these were purchasable or negotiable. Water could be arranged with the desert tribes who controlled the route. Mercenaries could be outbid. And Egypt had no strategic depth behind the Delta, because a Persian force that broke through the eastern approaches reached Memphis without another natural obstacle.

Amasis died in circa 526 BCE. His son Psamtik III inherited a kingdom, a professional army, a fortified frontier, and an invasion already in motion.

## The Sinai Problem and How Cambyses Solved It

The most consequential decision of the campaign was made before a single soldier crossed into Egyptian territory, and it concerned water.

An army of the size Persia was fielding could not carry enough water to cross the northern Sinai. This was not a marginal difficulty; it was the reason Egypt had been difficult to invade from the northeast for most of its history, and it was the calculation every Egyptian defensive plan rested on. Assyria had managed the crossing, but Assyria had also spent years establishing a presence in the Levant beforehand.

Herodotus reports that Cambyses arranged the crossing through an agreement with an Arab ruler who controlled the desert route, and that water was moved forward along the line of march and stored in advance. The specific mechanism he describes, involving camel transport and stitched skins, may be embellished, but the underlying logic is sound and matches how desert crossings were actually managed in the ancient world. What matters is not the technical detail but the strategic point: Cambyses treated the Sinai as a logistics problem to be solved with diplomacy and preparation rather than as a barrier to be forced with endurance. Solving it neutralized Egypt's outermost defense before the campaign properly began.

The second piece of preparation was intelligence, and it came from a defector. Phanes of Halicarnassus was a Greek commander in Egyptian service, well placed and well informed, who fell out with Amasis and went over to the Persians. Herodotus makes him the source of the advice about the Arab water arrangement and of general knowledge about Egyptian dispositions. The story is embroidered with a lurid episode in which the Greek mercenaries remaining in Egyptian service killed Phanes' sons in front of the army before the battle, which reads as the kind of detail that accretes to a good story. The defection itself, however, is entirely plausible and fits the structural weakness of a defense built on hired professionals: the same market that sells you soldiers sells your enemy the information.

The third piece was naval. Cyprus and the Phoenician cities had come under Persian control, and their fleets were the best in the eastern Mediterranean. That deprived Egypt of naval superiority in its own coastal waters and meant the Delta could potentially be approached or supported from the sea. Polycrates of Samos, previously allied with Amasis, sent ships to Cambyses rather than to Egypt. Egypt's diplomatic position collapsed before its military one did.

## The Battle of Pelusium

Pelusium sat at the eastern edge of the Delta, where the desert road met the cultivated land, and it was the hinge of Egyptian defense. An invader arriving from the Sinai had to pass it. An Egyptian army defending the country had to hold there or fall back into open country it could not defend as well.

The battle took place in 525 BCE, in the first year of Psamtik III's reign. The Egyptian force combined machimoi levies with the Greek and Carian professionals, defending prepared ground near the fortress. The Persian force was the standard Achaemenid combined army: infantry fighting behind large shields with spear and bow, substantial cavalry, and archers in numbers, supported by contingents from across the empire.

Detail about the fighting itself is thin, and honesty requires saying so. Herodotus, our main narrative source, gives little tactical description and instead reports visiting the site long afterward and seeing the bones of the dead still lying there, with an observation about the comparative thickness of Egyptian and Persian skulls that tells us more about Herodotus than about the battle. What the sources agree on is the outcome: the Egyptian army was defeated, the survivors fell back on Memphis, and the defensive line was gone.

The reasons for the defeat are inferable rather than documented. Persian numerical superiority is likely. Persian cavalry, which Egypt could not match, would have been decisive in the open ground near the fortress once the line broke. And the Egyptian army's structural problem, a professional core too small to be everywhere and a levy component not equipped for sustained close combat, is exactly the weakness a large combined-arms force exploits.

### What was the Battle of Pelusium?

Pelusium was the fortress guarding the eastern Delta approach from the Sinai, and the battle fought there in 525 BCE was the decisive engagement of the Persian conquest. Cambyses II defeated the army of Psamtik III, which combined Egyptian levies with Greek and Carian mercenaries, opening the road to Memphis.

There is a famous story attached to this battle that needs handling. Polyaenus, a writer of the second century CE and therefore some seven hundred years after the event, records that Cambyses placed cats and other animals sacred to Egyptians in his front line, so that Egyptian troops would not shoot for fear of harming them. The story is untraceable to any earlier source, appears in a collection of military stratagems assembled for entertainment and instruction rather than as history, and fits a well-established Greek and Roman genre of jokes about Egyptian animal worship. It should be treated as literary invention. It is repeated constantly and it is not evidence.

## The Siege of Memphis and the Fall of Psamtik III

After Pelusium the campaign became a matter of reduction rather than of battle. Egyptian survivors withdrew to Memphis, the ancient administrative center at the junction of Upper and Lower Egypt, and the city was invested.

Herodotus reports that Cambyses sent a herald upriver by boat to demand surrender and that the herald and crew were killed by the defenders, after which the siege proceeded and reprisals followed the city's fall. The pattern is standard ancient siege practice on both sides and there is no reason to doubt its outline.

Memphis fell. Psamtik III was captured, and the Twenty-sixth Dynasty ended after roughly a hundred and thirty-nine years. Herodotus reports that Cambyses initially treated the captured king with a degree of respect, testing his composure with a staged humiliation of his family, and later executed him after he was implicated in a conspiracy. That sequence, clemency followed by execution after a plot, is so common in the treatment of captured monarchs that it neither requires nor receives independent confirmation.

With Memphis taken, the rest of the country submitted. Egypt had no second defensive position and no alternative center of authority to rally around. Libyan communities to the west and the Greek settlements at Cyrene sent submissions without being attacked. The campaign had taken a single season.

### What year did Persia conquer Egypt?

525 BCE. Cambyses II defeated Psamtik III at Pelusium that year, took Memphis shortly afterward, and captured the Egyptian king. Egypt then became a Persian satrapy, counted in later tradition as the Twenty-seventh Dynasty, and remained under Achaemenid control until a successful revolt in circa 404 BCE.

## Who Cambyses Was

Cambyses II was the eldest son of Cyrus the Great and inherited the Achaemenid throne on his father's death in circa 530 BCE. He ruled roughly eight years, spent the majority of them on the Egyptian campaign and its aftermath, and died in circa 522 BCE while returning toward the empire's heartland amid a revolt at home.

Assessing him is difficult because the sources divide sharply along lines of origin. Greek writers, above all Herodotus and later Ctesias, present him as unstable, sacrilegious, and cruel, and the Persian royal tradition preserved in Darius I's own Behistun inscription has its own reasons to portray Cambyses badly, since Darius took the throne in contested circumstances and benefited from his predecessor looking unfit. That is a striking convergence: the two source traditions most often set against each other agree on Cambyses' unsuitability, and both had motives to say so.

Egyptian documents present a different figure. Cambyses appears with a full Egyptian throne name, in Egyptian royal dress, performing the offices of pharaoh. The autobiographical inscription of Udjahorresnet, an Egyptian naval commander and physician who served Amasis, then Psamtik III, then Cambyses and Darius, describes composing the king's Egyptian titulary, persuading him to expel foreigners who had settled in the temple precinct at Sais, and securing the restoration of the temple's revenues and rites. On Udjahorresnet's account Cambyses made offerings to Neith and behaved as a new pharaoh was expected to behave.

Both pictures cannot be right in their entirety, and the resolution is not simply to pick one. Udjahorresnet was a collaborator with every reason to present his own service as protection of Egyptian institutions, and his statue is a self-justification as much as a record. But self-justifications have to be plausible to their audience, and an Egyptian official could not claim to have persuaded a king to restore temple revenues if that king was widely known to have burned the temples down.

## The Cambyses Legend and What the Evidence Shows

The specific charges against Cambyses are worth taking one at a time, because they do not all fail in the same way.

The most famous is that he stabbed the Apis bull, the living manifestation of Ptah housed at Memphis, and that this act of sacrilege was punished with madness. This is the charge the Egyptian evidence contradicts most directly. Stelae from the Serapeum at Saqqara, the underground galleries where the Apis bulls were buried, record an Apis burial during Cambyses' reign carried out with full royal honors, with Cambyses named in Egyptian royal style as the provider of the sarcophagus. A king conducting the burial ceremony correctly is precisely what the story says he refused to do.

The second charge is temple desecration and the plundering of sanctuaries generally. Here the evidence is genuinely mixed, and the honest answer is more interesting than either extreme. A Demotic papyrus preserves what is usually called the Cambyses decree, recording a reduction in the revenues supplied to Egyptian temples, with a small number of major temples exempted. That is not desecration; it is a fiscal squeeze, and it is exactly the kind of measure a new administration takes when it needs revenue and finds an enormous share of national wealth flowing to religious institutions. But to Egyptian priests, whose income and status depended on those revenues, a sharp cut would have felt like an assault on the gods, and the tradition of Persian impiety plausibly grows from that root. The complaint was real; the story it turned into was not.

The third charge is the failed expeditions: an army sent toward the oasis of Siwa that vanished in a sandstorm, and a campaign south against the Kushites that collapsed from starvation. The Kushite campaign is likely enough in outline, since an ambitious king who had just taken Egypt would naturally look at the southern frontier, and desert and river campaigns beyond Aswan defeated better-prepared forces than his. The lost army of Siwa is unverified, has been the object of periodic archaeological claims that have not held up, and functions in Herodotus as a moral illustration rather than as a military report. Neither episode, even if true, demonstrates madness. Ancient commanders lost armies to logistics routinely.

The fourth charge is the desecration of Amasis' mummified body, dragged from its tomb and burned. This one is unfalsifiable from the Egyptian side and fits a recognizable pattern of posthumous punishment that Egyptian kings themselves practiced, including the Saite erasure of Kushite royal names discussed elsewhere in this cluster. If it happened, it was a political act against a dynasty rather than an act of religious madness.

### Did Cambyses desecrate Egyptian temples?

The famous charge that he stabbed the Apis bull is contradicted by Serapeum stelae recording an Apis burial with full royal honors under his name. What he did do, on documentary evidence, was cut the revenues supplied to most Egyptian temples while exempting a few, which priests would have experienced as an attack on the gods.

Put together, the pattern is clear enough to state as a conclusion. Cambyses conducted a fiscal reorganization that hurt temple incomes, probably lost at least one expedition, and executed a captured king. Every one of those is ordinary conquest behavior. The madness, the bull-stabbing, and the wholesale sacrilege belong to a hostile tradition with two independent motives to construct it: Greek writers who defined themselves against Persia, and a Persian successor who needed his predecessor to have been unfit to rule.

## The Persian Conquest Table

The evidence for this campaign and its aftermath falls into categories of very different reliability, and keeping them separate is the whole methodological point of this article. The table below is the reference version.

| Element | What the claim is | What the evidence is | How reliable |
|---|---|---|---|
| The battle | Cambyses defeated Psamtik III at Pelusium in 525 BCE | Herodotus, corroborated by the fact of Persian rule from that year and by Egyptian dating of Cambyses' reign | High for the fact and date, low for tactical detail |
| The satrapy | Egypt governed as a Persian province from Memphis, counted as Dynasty 27 | Egyptian dated documents, Aramaic administrative papyri, Achaemenid royal inscriptions listing Egypt | High |
| Darius I's administration | Canal to the Red Sea completed, Egyptian law codified, temple of Hibis built | Trilingual canal stelae in situ, the surviving temple, Demotic tradition of the law project | High |
| The temple revenue cut | Income to most temples reduced, a few exempted | Demotic papyrus recording the decree | Moderate to high, and the best explanation for Egyptian grievance |
| The Apis killing | Cambyses stabbed the sacred bull | Contradicted by Serapeum stelae recording a royal Apis burial under his name | Rejected |
| The cats at Pelusium | Sacred animals placed in the Persian front line | Polyaenus, writing roughly seven hundred years later, in a collection of stratagems | Rejected |
| The lost army of Siwa | An expedition vanished in a sandstorm | Herodotus alone, no physical confirmation | Unverified |
| Cambyses' madness | Sacrilege punished by divine derangement | Greek hostile tradition plus Darius I's own interest in discrediting his predecessor | Rejected as explanation, though real cruelty is possible |

Read the reliability column downward and the shape of the problem appears. Everything administrative is well evidenced, because administrations generate documents. Everything sensational is thinly evidenced, because sensational stories travel without needing documents. That asymmetry is not an accident of survival; it is the structural condition of writing about this period, and the discipline of noticing it is what separates a serious account from a repetition of Herodotus with the doubts removed.

## How the Persians Ruled Egypt

Conquest is the easy part. What Persia did next determined whether Egypt stayed conquered, and the record shows a system rather than an occupation.

Egypt became a satrapy, one of the wealthiest in the empire, governed by a satrap resident at Memphis who was appointed by and answerable to the Great King. Beneath that appointment, the existing Egyptian administrative machinery continued largely intact, staffed by Egyptians, using Egyptian record-keeping conventions alongside Aramaic, which functioned as the empire's administrative language. Nomes continued to be assessed. Temples continued to operate their estates, under the reduced revenue settlement. Local courts continued to hear disputes under Egyptian law.

The major change was fiscal, and it is the key to the grievance that followed. Traditional Egyptian taxation flexed with the Nile flood, because a state whose officials measured the inundation each year and adjusted assessments accordingly had built responsiveness into its structure. Imperial tribute did not flex. A quota was set, and it was owed whether the flood had been generous or catastrophic. In a bad year the same silver was due, and the burden fell hardest on those with the least margin. That single structural difference is a sufficient explanation for repeated revolt without requiring any story about desecrated temples.

Military presence was real but modest. Garrisons held Memphis, the Delta approaches, and Elephantine at the southern frontier, and they were frequently composed of non-Persian troops settled with land and families. The Jewish and Aramean garrison at Elephantine left an archive of Aramaic documents that shows such a community from the inside across generations: soldiers with property, marriages, lawsuits, a temple of their own, and correspondence with imperial officials that reads as procedural rather than fearful. That archive is the single richest source for how Persian administration actually functioned at ground level anywhere in the empire.

Darius I, who took the throne in circa 522 BCE after suppressing widespread revolt including one in Egypt, ran the most competent and the most Egyptian-facing phase of the occupation. He completed the canal Necho II had begun from the Nile through the Wadi Tumilat to the Red Sea, and marked it with stelae inscribed in Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian, and Egyptian hieroglyphs, presenting himself to Egyptian readers in Egyptian terms. He commissioned a compilation of Egyptian law as it had stood down to the end of native rule, a project carried out by Egyptian specialists over a period of years. He built the temple of Hibis in the Kharga Oasis, which survives as the best-preserved Persian-period temple in Egypt and shows Darius in full pharaonic guise offering to Amun. He also removed and executed the satrap Aryandes, which indicates a Great King paying close attention to how Egypt was being governed.

### How did the Persians rule Egypt?

As a satrapy governed from Memphis by an appointed satrap, with Egyptian administrative machinery, personnel, and law left largely in place beneath him. Aramaic served as the imperial administrative language. The principal change was fiscal: a fixed annual tribute in silver and kind that did not adjust for a poor Nile flood.

The pattern across the occupation is that Persian legitimacy in Egypt tracked closely with willingness to fund Egyptian religious institutions. Darius invested and faced less trouble than his successors. Xerxes and Artaxerxes I invested less and faced more. Artaxerxes III, after the reconquest of the fourth century BCE, was remembered for stripping temple treasuries and is the king to whom the sacred-animal outrages were attached with rather more contemporary complaint behind them than in Cambyses' case. A Persian ruler who declined to pay was, in Egyptian eyes, declining to be pharaoh, and Egyptians drew the obvious conclusion.

## What the Twenty-seventh Dynasty Actually Was

The dynasty numbering deserves explanation because it misleads readers constantly.

Manetho, an Egyptian priest writing in Greek under the early Ptolemies, organized Egyptian history into numbered dynasties, and his scheme still structures the field. He counted the Persian Great Kings who ruled Egypt as the Twenty-seventh Dynasty, running from Cambyses in 525 BCE to the successful revolt of circa 404 BCE, and covering Cambyses II, Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes I, Darius II, and briefly Artaxerxes II.

This is a historiographical decision, not a statement of fact about a royal family. The Achaemenids were not absorbed into an Egyptian line, did not intermarry into one, and mostly did not live in Egypt. What Manetho's numbering preserves is the fiction that Egypt always had a pharaoh, that the office was continuous even when its holder was a foreign king who visited rarely or never. That fiction mattered. Egyptian religion required a king to perform the rituals that maintained cosmic order, and a country with no pharaoh was a country in a state of theological emergency. Counting Cambyses as a pharaoh, and having him crowned with an Egyptian titulary, solved a problem for Egyptian priests as much as for Persian administrators.

The practical consequence is that the Twenty-seventh Dynasty behaves differently from the dynasties on either side of it. Its kings are dated in Egyptian documents by their regnal years, appear on temple walls in Egyptian dress, and are named in Egyptian formulae, while simultaneously appearing in Persepolis records as the rulers of a vast empire in which Egypt was one province among many.

### What was the 27th Dynasty?

The Twenty-seventh Dynasty is Manetho's designation for the Achaemenid Persian kings who ruled Egypt from circa 525 to 404 BCE: Cambyses II, Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes I, Darius II, and briefly Artaxerxes II. They were not an Egyptian family. Counting them as pharaohs preserved the theological continuity Egyptian religion required.

## Egyptian Resistance and the Pattern of Revolt

Egypt was never quietly held, and the pattern of its risings is diagnostic of what actually made Persian rule vulnerable.

The first serious trouble came immediately. Cambyses' death in circa 522 BCE and the disputed succession that brought Darius I to power triggered revolts across the empire, and Egypt was among them. Darius suppressed it and then invested heavily in making Egyptian rule work, which is the behavior of a ruler who has learned what the province costs when mishandled.

Revolt broke out again at the end of Darius' reign, around 486 BCE, and was put down by Xerxes early in his own. Egyptian sources for this rising are thin, but the timing is the point: the end of a long reign and the uncertainty of a succession is exactly when a subject province tests the empire.

The largest rising came in the middle of the fifth century BCE under Inaros, a Libyan dynast who styled himself son of Psamtik, claiming continuity with the Saite line. What made this revolt different was foreign support. Athens, at war with Persia and eager to open a second front, committed a substantial fleet and expeditionary force to the Delta. The rebellion held large parts of the country for several years. Persia responded with a major campaign under Megabyzus, defeated the rebels, and destroyed the Athenian force, in what was one of the worst military disasters Athens suffered in the fifth century BCE. Inaros was captured and executed. A related figure, Amyrtaeus, sustained resistance in the Delta marshes, where the terrain made suppression impractical.

Independence finally came in circa 404 BCE, when Darius II died and the Persian succession was contested between Artaxerxes II and his brother Cyrus the Younger. A descendant or namesake of the earlier Amyrtaeus led a rising that Persia was too preoccupied to answer, and Egypt stayed free for roughly sixty years. Those decades, the last native dynasties, are covered in [the account of the last native pharaohs of Egypt](/2006/12/01/last-native-pharaohs-of-egypt/).

### How did Egyptians resist Persian rule?

Through repeated revolts timed to Persian weakness. Risings followed Cambyses' death in circa 522 BCE, the end of Darius I's reign around 486 BCE, and the mid fifth century BCE under Inaros with major Athenian military support. Independence finally succeeded in circa 404 BCE during a contested Persian succession.

The pattern across all of these is unambiguous and worth stating as a principle. Egyptian revolts succeeded to the degree that Persia was distracted and that an outside power was willing to fund and reinforce them. They failed when Persia could concentrate force. Egyptian independence in this era was therefore substantially a function of Achaemenid difficulties rather than of Egyptian military capacity, and Egyptian leaders understood this perfectly well and timed their moves accordingly. That is not a criticism of Egyptian competence. It is an accurate description of what a medium-sized state can do against a continental empire, and the fact that Egypt eventually succeeded where most Persian provinces never did says something about how hard Egypt was to hold.

## The Achaemenid Army That Took Egypt

Understanding why Persia won requires understanding what the Achaemenid military actually was, because popular accounts, following Greek sources, tend to describe it as an undisciplined horde and it was nothing of the kind.

The core of Persian infantry fought behind a large rectangular shield, often of wicker, planted as a standing barrier, with spear and bow. The tactical logic was different from the Greek hoplite phalanx and effective on its own terms: a shield line held the front while archers behind it delivered volume fire, and the formation was designed to break an enemy with missiles before contact rather than to win a shoving match. Against opponents without heavy armor, which described most of the empire's enemies, this worked extremely well. Against Greek hoplites in close terrain it worked less well, which is why Greek writers, whose experience was largely of that specific matchup, describe it dismissively.

Cavalry was where Persia held a genuine and general advantage. The Iranian plateau and the empire's central Asian provinces produced horses and horsemen in quantity, and Achaemenid armies fielded cavalry in numbers that no Mediterranean state could match at this date. On open ground, cavalry decides battles by threatening flanks, exploiting breaks, and destroying a retreating enemy. The ground around Pelusium, once a defensive line broke, favored exactly that.

Archery was the empire's signature. Persian armies used massed bowmen as a primary weapon rather than as a support arm, and the volume of shooting a large Achaemenid force could deliver was substantial.

Beyond the tactical arms, the empire's real advantage was systemic. It could raise contingents from Anatolia to Bactria, move them along established roads, supply them through a functioning provincial administration, and absorb a defeat without collapsing. Egypt could raise one army. If that army lost, the country was finished. Persia could lose an army and send another the following year. That asymmetry is the deepest reason Egypt fell, and it applies equally to Lydia and Babylon, which fell to the same empire on the same timetable.

The fleet completed the picture. With Phoenicia, Cyprus, and the Ionian cities under Persian control, the empire commanded the best naval forces in the eastern Mediterranean, and Egypt's own considerable fleet was outmatched in aggregate before the campaign began.

## Egyptians in Persian Service

One of the more interesting consequences of the conquest is that Egyptian military capacity did not disappear. It was absorbed.

Egyptian contingents served in Achaemenid armies and fleets for the next two centuries. Egyptian ships and marines formed part of the Persian navy in the campaigns against Greece, and Greek accounts of those engagements note Egyptian troops as distinct and effective, particularly the marines fighting with their characteristic equipment. Egyptian soldiers appear in Persian garrisons elsewhere in the empire. Egyptian shipwrights and sailors served imperial naval requirements.

This has a straightforward explanation and an interesting implication. The explanation is that empires use what they conquer, and Egypt possessed maritime skills and manpower the Achaemenids valued. The implication is that the Egyptian military tradition the Saites had rebuilt continued to develop under foreign command, which is part of why the fourth-century revolts, when they came, produced armies capable of repelling full Persian invasions rather than merely raiding.

The same absorption happened administratively. Udjahorresnet is the famous case but not an isolated one. Egyptian officials, scribes, priests, and physicians served the new regime, and some of them traveled to the imperial center. Persian royal building projects at Susa used Egyptian craftsmen, and Darius' foundation inscriptions acknowledge as much. Egypt supplied the empire with expertise as well as with grain and silver.

## Logistics, the Frontier, and Why Egypt Was Hard to Hold

Egypt was easy to conquer once and difficult to keep, and the reasons are geographic as much as political.

The country's shape works against a garrisoning power. It is a thin ribbon a thousand kilometers long with a fan-shaped delta at one end, and its productive population is concentrated along that ribbon. Controlling it requires either a large distributed garrison, which is expensive, or control of the few nodes through which everything passes, which is cheaper but leaves large areas effectively ungoverned. Persia chose the cheaper option, holding Memphis, the Delta approaches, and Elephantine, which meant that the Delta marshes in particular were never fully under control. Amyrtaeus survived there for years precisely because a cavalry-heavy army has no good answer to a swamp.

The Nile flood added a seasonal constraint that shaped every campaign. For part of each year the Delta was substantially under water, making large-scale military operations impractical. Persian invasion attempts in the fourth century BCE repeatedly failed when a siege could not be pressed to conclusion before the water rose. Any invader had a campaigning window, and Egyptian defense could be built around simply surviving inside it.

The Sinai remained a barrier in both directions, which cut two ways. It made Egypt hard to reach, and it also made Egypt hard to reinforce, so an imperial response to a revolt required the same logistical preparation as the original conquest. That delay is a large part of why Egyptian revolts could establish themselves before Persia arrived.

Finally, Egypt's wealth made it worth the trouble, which is why Persia kept coming back. A satrapy that produced grain surplus, silver tribute, and naval manpower was not something an empire wrote off. The strategic equation for two centuries was that Egypt could usually break free when Persia was busy and could never break free permanently while Persia existed.

## Weapons, Equipment, and How the Two Armies Fought

A warfare account owes its readers a description of the actual instruments, and the two forces that met at Pelusium were equipped along genuinely different principles.

The Egyptian side fielded three distinguishable elements. The machimoi, the hereditary warrior stratum, carried spear and shield with limited body armor, and Egyptian military tradition placed heavy emphasis on the bow, in which Egyptian archers had been formidable for two thousand years. Their equipment was suited to skirmishing, missile exchange, and holding a line against comparably armed opponents, and it was not suited to absorbing a cavalry charge or standing in a prolonged armored press.

The Greek and Carian professionals were the heavy element. They carried the large round shield, thrusting spear, and helmet of the emerging hoplite style, with bronze armor on those who could afford it, and they fought in close formation where the shield of one man covered the flank of the next. That formation is powerful frontally and vulnerable on the flanks and rear, and it requires drilled troops who trust each other, which is exactly what a paid professional body provides and a seasonal levy does not.

The chariot, which had defined Egyptian battlefield prestige in the New Kingdom, was militarily obsolete by this date against disciplined infantry and massed cavalry. Egypt had no equivalent cavalry arm of its own, which is the single most consequential gap in the Egyptian order of battle.

The Persian side fought on a different principle. Its infantry carried a large shield, often of wicker on a wooden frame, tall enough to be planted as a standing barrier, along with a short spear and a bow. The tactical intention was to establish a shield wall and then deliver sustained archery from behind and through it, breaking the enemy with missiles before contact. Persian troops generally wore less metal armor than Greek hoplites, which made them faster and more flexible and left them at a disadvantage in a close armored fight, and Greek writers, whose experience of Persian infantry was overwhelmingly in that specific matchup, describe them accordingly and unfairly.

Persian cavalry was the arm that mattered most and the one Egypt could not answer. Horsemen from the Iranian plateau and the eastern provinces gave Achaemenid armies mounted strength in numbers no Mediterranean power matched at this date. Cavalry does not usually break a formed infantry line frontally; it threatens flanks, forces an enemy to extend or to form defensive squares, exploits any gap that opens, and turns a retreat into a rout. On the ground around Pelusium, once the Egyptian line gave way, that is exactly what would have happened.

Both sides also brought engineering and siege capability, which mattered at Memphis rather than at Pelusium. Persian armies were experienced in reducing fortified cities, having taken Sardis and Babylon within the preceding generation, and a Delta city without relief was not going to hold indefinitely.

The synthesis is straightforward. Egypt had a competent missile-and-spear army with a small elite heavy core and no cavalry. Persia had a missile-and-shield army with abundant cavalry, siege capacity, and the ability to replace losses from an empire's worth of provinces. In a battle at a choke point the Egyptian arrangement had a genuine chance. In anything else it did not, and the campaign was designed by Cambyses to ensure that the choke point was the only fight Egypt got.

## The Frontier at Elephantine

The southern edge of the satrapy is the best-documented military installation in Persian Egypt, and it deserves its own treatment because it shows imperial defense working at a level the narrative sources never reach.

Elephantine sits at the First Cataract, the traditional boundary between Egypt and Nubia and a natural chokepoint on the river. Egyptian regimes had garrisoned it for two thousand years before the Persians arrived, and the Persians kept it garrisoned for the same reasons: it controlled river traffic, watched the frontier with Kush, and secured the quarries and trade routes of the far south.

What makes it exceptional is the archive. The garrison included a community of Jewish and Aramean soldiers, established there before the Persian conquest on the evidence of their own statements, who wrote in Aramaic on papyrus that the dry conditions preserved. The surviving documents cover several generations and include pay records, ration allocations, property sales, loans, marriage contracts, divorce settlements, wills, and litigation, alongside official correspondence.

The picture they give is of a garrison that was also a town. Soldiers owned houses and land, married, divorced, sued each other, borrowed money, and left property to daughters as well as sons. They maintained a temple of their own god and observed their own festivals. They were paid by the imperial administration and answered to Persian officials, and they addressed those officials in routine bureaucratic terms.

The most instructive episode in the archive concerns the destruction of that temple. Local Egyptian priests of the ram-god Khnum, whose own sanctuary stood nearby, objected to the animal sacrifice practiced there and, apparently with the collusion of a local Persian official, had the building demolished. The garrison then spent years petitioning authorities, writing to the governor of Judah and to the sons of the governor of Samaria, seeking permission and support to rebuild. The eventual response appears to have allowed reconstruction while restricting animal sacrifice, which is a compromise recognizable to anyone who has read a modern planning dispute.

Three things follow. First, religious friction in Persian Egypt is documented, and in the best-documented case the aggressors were Egyptian priests rather than Persian officials, which complicates any simple story of imperial impiety. Second, imperial authority was accessible: a garrison of foreign soldiers on the empire's western frontier could petition several layers of administration and eventually get a ruling. Third, and most useful for a military account, the frontier was held by settled soldier-colonists with local roots rather than by rotating imperial troops, which was cheaper, more durable, and characteristic of how Achaemenid provincial defense worked.

## Myths of Invincibility and Other Corrections

Several beliefs about this conquest are widely held and do not survive examination.

The first is that Egypt fell because Egyptians had become soft or unwarlike after centuries of civilization. This is a moralized explanation with no evidentiary content. Egypt subsequently mounted revolts sustained for years, repelled full Persian invasions twice in the fourth century BCE, and regained independence for six decades. What Egypt lacked was the population base, the silver reserves, and the strategic depth of a continental empire, not fighting spirit.

The second is that the mercenary system was the cause of the defeat, and that hiring foreigners was inherently a sign of decay. Every serious military power in the eastern Mediterranean in this era used mercenaries, including Persia, which employed Greek professionals extensively and would rely on them heavily in the fourth century BCE. What the mercenary system did create was a specific vulnerability to defection and outbidding, and Phanes' defection illustrates it. That is a real weakness in the model, not evidence of a decayed society.

The third is that Persian rule was uniformly brutal and Egypt spent two centuries in misery. The documentary record from Egypt shows temples operating, courts hearing cases, contracts being written, property changing hands, and garrison communities living ordinary lives. The grievance was fiscal and religious, it was serious, and it produced repeated revolt, but it was not a reign of terror and describing it as one makes the actual causes of revolt harder to see.

The fourth is that the conquest ended ancient Egyptian civilization. It ended native political rule for a period. Egyptian religion, language, art, law, and temple institutions continued functioning throughout, and the archaizing cultural program the Saites had built intensified rather than lapsed, because Egyptian elites under foreign rule had more reason than ever to assert continuity.

The fifth is the cat story at Pelusium, addressed above, which deserves a second mention only because it is so widely repeated as fact.

## The Conquest-and-Slander Thesis

The argument this article defends can be stated in three propositions.

The first is that the military conquest was competent and unremarkable. Cambyses identified the operational problem correctly, which was the Sinai crossing rather than the Egyptian army, solved it through diplomacy and logistical preparation, acquired route intelligence through a defector, neutralized Egypt's naval and diplomatic position beforehand, and then won a conventional battle at the point where Egyptian defense had to be made. Nothing about this requires explanation by Egyptian decadence or Persian brutality. It was a well-run campaign against a smaller opponent.

The second is that the occupation generated genuine and specific grievance, and that the grievance was fiscal and religious rather than atrocity-driven. Fixed tribute that ignored the flood, and a documented reduction in temple revenues, hit exactly the constituencies that could organize resistance and shape memory. Egyptian priests lost income and status, and Egyptian farmers paid the same in a bad year as in a good one. That is more than sufficient to produce two centuries of intermittent revolt.

The third is that the Cambyses legend is a construction with identifiable motives, and that Egyptian documents contradict its central charge. The Apis burial stelae are the decisive piece: the specific sacrilege the story rests on did not happen. Two independent traditions had reason to build the legend anyway. Greek writers were constructing Persia as the antithesis of Greek self-government and found an impious tyrant useful. Darius I, who came to the throne after a disputed succession and needed his predecessor discredited, had his own reasons to circulate an account of a king gone mad. When a hostile Greek tradition and a hostile Persian royal tradition agree, the agreement is not corroboration; it is two motives pointing the same direction.

The practical upshot for anyone studying this period is a rule of method. Where a narrative source describes character and an administrative document describes an act, prefer the document. Where they conflict on a specific verifiable event, the document generally wins. And where a story is vivid, morally satisfying, and unsupported, its vividness is a reason for suspicion rather than for confidence.

## The Sources and How to Weigh Them

This campaign is reconstructed from four bodies of material that disagree in characteristic ways, and knowing the character of each is most of the skill required.

Herodotus is the principal narrative and the reason we have a story at all. He visited Egypt around the middle of the fifth century BCE, roughly seventy years after the conquest, and devoted much of his second and third books to Egyptian material. He gives us the Sinai water arrangement, Phanes' defection, the siege of Memphis, the treatment of Psamtik III, the Apis episode, the Siwa expedition, and the Kushite campaign. Without him the conquest would be a date and an outcome.

His limitations are structural rather than occasional. He worked through interpreters. His informants were largely priests and temple personnel, whose institutional memory of the Persian revenue cut would have been bitter and whose accounts would have been shaped accordingly. He wrote for a Greek audience in the middle of a long conflict with Persia, and Persian impiety was a theme with resonance. And he was interested in patterns of hubris and divine retribution as organizing principles, which means episodes that illustrate a moral arc are more likely to be included and more likely to be shaped. None of this makes him worthless. It makes him a source whose factual reports must be checked and whose character judgments must be discounted.

Ctesias wrote a Persian history in the generation after Herodotus, claiming access to Persian court sources, and survives only in fragments and summaries. He was regarded as unreliable in antiquity and his account of this period adds color rather than confirmation.

Egyptian documents are the corrective. The Serapeum stelae recording Apis burials, with regnal dates, are the single most valuable class, because they are administrative rather than literary and because they directly contradict the story's central charge. Udjahorresnet's statue inscription gives an Egyptian elite perspective, heavily self-serving and correspondingly informative about what an Egyptian official thought would sound plausible. The Demotic papyrus recording the revenue decree gives the fiscal measure that probably underlies the whole tradition of Persian impiety. Egyptian dated documents from the reigns of Cambyses and his successors establish the chronology of the occupation precisely.

Aramaic papyri, above all from Elephantine, show the satrapy functioning. They record pay, rations, property, marriage, litigation, and one extended episode in which the Jewish garrison's temple was destroyed by local priests of Khnum in a dispute over animal sacrifice, followed by years of petitioning imperial authorities for permission to rebuild. That episode is instructive in itself: religious friction in Persian Egypt is documented, and the perpetrators in the best-documented case were Egyptian priests rather than Persian officials.

Achaemenid royal material supplies the imperial view. Egypt appears in the lists of lands in royal inscriptions. Darius' canal stelae speak to Egyptian readers in hieroglyphs and Egyptian royal formulae. The Behistun inscription records Darius' own account of the succession crisis and the revolts, which is propaganda but propaganda of a datable and specific kind.

Set these together and the method is clear. Use Herodotus for narrative and hypotheses. Use Egyptian and Aramaic documents for what actually happened administratively. Use Achaemenid inscriptions for chronology and imperial framing. Where the narrative and the documents conflict on a checkable point, the documents decide it.

## The Campaign in Sequence

Stripped of interpretation, the sequence of events runs as follows.

Cyrus the Great absorbs Media in the 550s BCE, Lydia around 546 BCE, and Babylon in 539 BCE, bringing Persian power to the edge of the Levant. He dies in circa 530 BCE and Cambyses II succeeds.

Amasis of Egypt attempts to build a counter-coalition and fails as its potential members are conquered or defect. Polycrates of Samos, previously allied to Egypt, sends ships to Persia instead. Amasis dies in circa 526 BCE and Psamtik III succeeds.

Cambyses prepares the invasion. Phanes of Halicarnassus, a Greek commander in Egyptian service, defects and supplies intelligence. An agreement with an Arab ruler controlling the northern Sinai route secures water for the crossing. Phoenician and Cypriot naval forces neutralize Egypt's maritime position.

In 525 BCE the Persian army crosses the Sinai and meets the Egyptian army at Pelusium. The Egyptians are defeated and fall back on Memphis.

Memphis is besieged and taken. Psamtik III is captured and later executed. Libya and Cyrene submit without being attacked. Egypt becomes a satrapy.

Cambyses assumes an Egyptian throne name and royal titulary, with Udjahorresnet's assistance, and an Apis bull is buried during his reign with royal honors. Temple revenues are reduced by decree, with a small number of major temples exempted. An expedition south toward Kush fails, and an expedition toward Siwa is reported lost.

Cambyses dies in circa 522 BCE amid revolt across the empire. Egypt rebels and Darius I suppresses it.

Darius I organizes the satrapy, completes the Red Sea canal, commissions a codification of Egyptian law, builds the temple of Hibis at Kharga, and removes the satrap Aryandes. Revolt breaks out again around 486 BCE at the end of his reign and is suppressed by Xerxes.

Inaros leads a major rising in the mid fifth century BCE with Athenian support. Persia defeats it under Megabyzus and destroys the Athenian force. Amyrtaeus sustains resistance in the Delta marshes.

In circa 404 BCE, with the Persian succession contested, Egypt regains independence under Amyrtaeus and holds it for roughly sixty years.

## Life Under the Satrapy

The political narrative of conquest and revolt sits above a society that continued to function, and the documentary record lets us see it with unusual clarity.

Agriculture proceeded on its ancient rhythm. The flood set the calendar, grain assessments moved through a bureaucracy staffed by Egyptians, and village life continued in forms a farmer of the Middle Kingdom would have recognized. What changed was who ultimately received the surplus and on what terms.

Legal life continued under Egyptian law, and the Demotic contract tradition that had developed under the Saites carried on and expanded. Property changed hands, loans were made and secured, marriages were settled with itemized property arrangements, and disputes were litigated. Women appear throughout this documentation as parties in their own right, owning, inheriting, selling, and suing, exactly as they had before the conquest. Persian rule did not touch Egyptian personal law, which is one of the strongest indications that the occupation was administrative rather than transformative.

Temples continued to operate, under reduced state support after the revenue decree. That reduction pushed temple institutions toward independent income, and the expansion of votive animal cults through the Late Period is at least partly explained by it. Pilgrims purchasing mummified ibises and hawks were funding institutions that could no longer rely on the crown, and the industry that grew around that practice became a significant part of the temple economy.

Foreign communities were a normal part of the landscape in specific places. The Elephantine garrison is the best documented: Jewish and Aramean soldiers with families, property, and their own temple, serving the empire on the southern frontier, corresponding with authorities in Jerusalem, Samaria, and the satrap's office. Greek traders continued at Naucratis. Persian officials and soldiers were present at Memphis and the main administrative centers.

Egyptian elites faced a choice with no comfortable answer, and the record shows all the available options being taken. Some served, as Udjahorresnet did, and framed that service as protection of Egyptian institutions. Some withdrew into temple careers where royal politics could be held at a distance. Some revolted. What is striking is that Egyptian self-presentation under occupation became more emphatically Egyptian rather than less: statues of officials from this period display deep genealogies, archaic titles, traditional dress, and thoroughly conventional hieroglyphic autobiographies. Nothing in their public self-description acknowledges that the king was Persian. That silence is a strategy, and it means the monumental record systematically understates political rupture.

## Why Persia Succeeded Where Assyria Had Only Half Succeeded

Assyria had invaded Egypt twice in the seventh century BCE, taken Memphis, sacked Thebes, and then governed through appointed local clients rather than direct rule. Within a generation those clients had converted themselves into an independent dynasty. Persia took Egypt once and held it, with interruptions, for two centuries. The difference is worth explaining because it isolates what actually makes a conquest stick.

Assyria never intended direct administration of Egypt and probably could not have sustained it. Its Egyptian campaigns were punitive, aimed at ending Egyptian interference in the Levant, and its solution afterward was to install clients and leave. That works only as long as the imperial center remains strong enough to make disloyalty unattractive, and Assyria's center was already under pressure from Babylon and Media.

Persia intended a province and built one. It installed a satrap with a permanent seat, garrisons at the strategic nodes, an assessed tribute, an administrative language, and a communications system linking Egypt to the imperial center. It kept Egyptian institutions and personnel in place beneath that layer, which reduced the cost of governing and gave Egyptian elites a stake in the system's continuation. And it treated Egyptian religion as something to be participated in rather than ignored, at least under its more competent kings.

The second difference is timing. Assyria conquered Egypt while collapsing. Persia conquered Egypt while expanding, with several decades of imperial vigor still ahead. A conquest is held by the ability to send a second army, and Persia could send one for two hundred years.

The third difference is that Persia removed the alternative. Assyria left the Saite house in place as clients, giving Egypt a ready-made native government to consolidate around. Persia captured and executed the last Saite king and installed the Great King as pharaoh in his place, so Egyptian revolts had to manufacture new leadership from Delta dynasts and marsh commanders rather than rally to an existing throne. That is why Egyptian risings, when they came, were led by figures like Inaros claiming Saite descent: the claim had to be constructed because the line itself had been removed.

## The Second Persian Conquest and Why It Was Different

Egypt was conquered by Persia twice, and the two conquests are separated by a hundred and eighty years and by a considerable difference in character.

The second came in circa 343 BCE, when Artaxerxes III invaded after two earlier attempts had failed. By then Egypt had been independent for six decades under the last native dynasties, had built substantial defenses, and had repeatedly repelled Persian expeditionary forces with the help of hired Greek generals and troops. Artaxerxes succeeded where his predecessors had not by committing sufficient force, employing competent Greek commanders of his own, and outbidding Egypt in the mercenary market so that the Greek troops in Egyptian service made their own arrangements as the campaign turned. Pelusium was outflanked rather than stormed. Nectanebo II withdrew and then fled south, and native rule ended.

The occupation that followed lasted about eleven years and is remembered far more harshly than the first. Egyptian and Greek sources alike describe heavy exactions, defensive walls pulled down, and temple treasuries stripped, and the sacred-animal outrages that hostile tradition wrongly attached to Cambyses are attributed to Artaxerxes III with rather more contemporary complaint behind them. Some of that is doubtless amplified by later writers working within an established genre of Persian impiety, but the volume and consistency of it, and the enthusiasm with which Egypt received Alexander of Macedon in circa 332 BCE, point to a genuinely harsher regime.

The contrast between the two conquests is the strongest available evidence for the argument this article makes about Cambyses. Egyptians who experienced both had no difficulty distinguishing them, and the tradition of complaint about the second is documented in ways the tradition about the first is not. When a source base contains a well-attested atrocity narrative and a poorly attested one, treating them as equivalent is a failure of method.

The campaign, the reigns of Nectanebo I and Nectanebo II, and the end of native rule belong to their own article in this cluster, which owns that subject.

## What the Conquest Changed and What It Did Not

Assessing the long consequences requires separating three layers that are usually run together.

At the political layer the change was total and permanent in its direction. Egypt lost the capacity to determine its own strategic future in 525 BCE and, apart from the sixty-year interval in the fourth century BCE, never regained it. From this point Egypt was a province of somebody's empire, Persian, then Macedonian and Ptolemaic, then Roman, then Byzantine, then Arab, then Ottoman, then British, for well over two thousand years. The Persian conquest is where that pattern begins, which is why it is a hinge in Egyptian history rather than merely an episode.

At the institutional layer the change was much smaller. Egyptian law, courts, contracts, scribal practice, temple organization, and administrative geography all continued. Persia governed through Egyptian machinery, and the machinery outlasted Persia, was inherited by the Ptolemies, and in modified form by Rome. An Egyptian scribe of 400 BCE would have found the paperwork of 600 BCE entirely familiar.

At the cultural layer the change was, if anything, in the opposite direction from what conquest usually produces. Egyptian archaism intensified. Officials under Persian rule asserted deeper genealogies, more archaic titles, and more traditional self-presentation than their predecessors had. Animal cults expanded. Temple scholarship continued codifying. The instinct the Saites had institutionalized, which was to answer a legitimacy problem by demonstrating continuity with the deep past, turned out to be exactly the instinct an occupied elite needed, and occupation strengthened it.

That last point is the most consequential legacy of the conquest and the least obvious. By removing native kingship while leaving Egyptian institutions intact, Persia created the conditions in which Egyptian identity became a cultural and religious project rather than a political one. That project proved extraordinarily durable. Egyptian religion, language, and temple practice survived Persian, Macedonian, Ptolemaic, and Roman rule and continued in recognizable form for another seven centuries after the last Persian left. Conquest did not end Egyptian civilization; it changed what Egyptian civilization was carried by.

## Pelusium: The Fortress and the Ground

Because the whole campaign turned on one location, the location deserves description.

Pelusium sat at the easternmost mouth of the Nile, where the Delta's cultivated land met the desert road running from the Sinai. Its position made it the gate of Egypt from the northeast, and every serious invader from that direction had to deal with it. The surrounding terrain was a mixture of marsh, lagoon, sandy ridges, and irrigated ground cut by channels, which constrained an approaching army to a limited number of usable routes and made the fortress a genuine choke point rather than merely a strong place.

The Egyptians understood this and had invested accordingly. Pelusium was garrisoned, fortified, and supplied, and Egyptian defensive doctrine from the Saite period onward assumed that a hostile army arriving from the Levant would be met there. The logic was sound. A defender fighting on prepared ground at a choke point negates much of an attacker's numerical advantage and denies cavalry the open flanks it needs.

What the ground could not do was compensate for a defender's own weaknesses once contact was made. If the defending line broke, the terrain that had constrained the attacker's approach became terrain the defender had to retreat across, and the sandy open stretches near the fortress gave Persian cavalry room to work. A choke point protects an army that holds it and destroys an army that loses it, and in 525 BCE the Egyptian army lost it.

The site retained its importance for the rest of antiquity. Persian invasion attempts in the fourth century BCE were fought around the same approaches, and in circa 343 BCE Artaxerxes III took Egypt by outflanking Pelusium rather than assaulting it directly, which is a measure of how effective the position remained when it was properly held. Alexander of Macedon entered Egypt through Pelusium in circa 332 BCE without a fight, because the Persian satrap had decided not to contest it.

Herodotus, visiting the region generations later, describes seeing the bones of the dead from the battle still lying on the field in two separate heaps, Persian and Egyptian, and offers a comparison of skull thickness that he attributes to differences in headwear and sun exposure. The passage is often quoted for color. What it actually tells us is that the battlefield was known and pointed out to visitors, that the dead were not fully buried, and that Herodotus was shown things by local guides whose interpretations he passed on, which is a useful reminder of how his Egyptian material was gathered.

## The Southern and Western Expeditions

After Egypt was secured, Cambyses attempted to extend Persian control beyond the Nile valley, and both attempts failed. Their significance is disputed and worth setting out carefully.

The southern effort aimed at Kush, the Nubian kingdom ruled from Napata by the successors of the Twenty-fifth Dynasty. Any new master of Egypt would naturally look south, both because Kush controlled access to gold and trade goods and because a Kushite dynasty with a historical claim to Egypt was a standing complication. Herodotus reports that the expedition set out inadequately provisioned, ran out of food in the desert, and turned back after severe losses.

That outcome is entirely plausible without any need for an explanation involving royal derangement. Campaigning south of Aswan meant cataracts, desert, and extended supply lines through territory that could not feed an army, and better-prepared forces than Cambyses' failed there in other periods. Egyptian New Kingdom control of Nubia had rested on generations of fortress-building and administrative penetration, not on a single expedition. Persia appears to have secured the frontier region near the First Cataract and gone no further, which is a rational settlement.

The western effort is the famous one. Herodotus reports that an army sent toward the oasis of Siwa, where the oracle of Amun sat, was overwhelmed by a sandstorm and vanished entirely. The story has attracted repeated archaeological attention over the years and no confirmed physical evidence has emerged. It functions in Herodotus' narrative as a demonstration of divine displeasure, arriving in a sequence of episodes designed to show a king punished for impiety, which is precisely the context in which a reported disaster should be treated with caution.

Whether the expedition happened, and whether it was lost, are separate questions from whether its loss means anything about Cambyses. Armies were lost to desert crossings in the ancient world with some regularity. What the episode demonstrates in the sources is not madness but the moral architecture of Herodotus' account, in which sacrilege at Memphis is followed by disaster in the desert because that is how his histories are constructed.

## The Egyptian Command and Psamtik III

The defeated side of this campaign is usually reduced to a name, and there is a little more to say.

Psamtik III came to the throne in circa 526 BCE, on the death of his father Amasis, with an invasion already assembling. He had no time to build alliances his father had failed to build, no time to reorganize a military his father had left in reasonable order, and no realistic strategic options beyond fighting at Pelusium. The judgment that he was an ineffective king is difficult to sustain when he ruled for roughly six months, all of them in a war he inherited.

The command problem he faced was structural. His army combined machimoi levies with Greek and Carian professionals, and the professionals had just been demonstrated to be a security risk by Phanes' defection. Herodotus' story that the remaining Greek mercenaries killed Phanes' sons before the battle, in full view of both armies, is likely embellished, but the underlying situation it dramatizes is real: an Egyptian king going into a decisive battle knowing that his most capable troops served for money and that his enemy had money.

Egyptian naval forces, which had been a genuine Saite achievement, were largely irrelevant to the outcome because Phoenician and Cypriot fleets under Persian control had already tilted the maritime balance and because the decisive fighting happened on land.

After the defeat, Psamtik III's conduct is reported only through Greek sources, which describe an initially lenient captivity, a staged test of his composure involving the humiliation of his children, and eventual execution following his implication in a conspiracy. Whether he actually conspired is unknowable. Captured monarchs are executed for plotting whether or not they plotted, because a living former king is a rallying point, and the Persians would have understood that as clearly as anyone.

His significance in the longer story is as the terminal figure of a dynasty rather than as an actor. The Twenty-sixth Dynasty ended with him after roughly a hundred and thirty-nine years, and the removal of the Saite line meant that later Egyptian revolts had no legitimate throne to restore, which is why figures like Inaros had to construct Saite descent for themselves.

## How Egypt Itself Remembered the Conquest

One of the more revealing aspects of this period is how little the Egyptian monumental record says about it.

Egyptian public self-presentation had a long-established convention: officials described their careers, titles, ancestry, and piety, and did not describe political rupture. That convention held through the conquest. Statues and tomb inscriptions from the Persian period record deep genealogies, archaic titles, traditional dress, and thoroughly orthodox religious sentiments, and do not mention that the king was foreign. Udjahorresnet's inscription is the great exception, and even it frames the conquest almost entirely in terms of what he did to protect the temple at Sais rather than in terms of national catastrophe.

That silence has two effects on the historical record. The first is that the monumental evidence systematically understates disruption, so a historian working only from statues and stelae would conclude that not much happened in 525 BCE. The second is that the loudest surviving Egyptian testimony about how the conquest felt is not textual at all but behavioral: two centuries of repeated revolt, sustained at real cost, is a statement about Egyptian attitudes that no inscription makes.

There is a third strand, which is the later Egyptian literary tradition. Demotic narrative literature of the Ptolemaic and Roman periods includes stories set in the Persian era and cycles about heroic Egyptian figures resisting foreign domination, and prophetic texts lamenting foreign rule and predicting its end circulated in Egyptian temple circles. Those texts are late and literary rather than contemporary and documentary, and they cannot be used as evidence for what happened in 525 BCE. What they do show is that Persian rule entered Egyptian cultural memory as a paradigm of foreign oppression, and that the memory was still productive centuries later.

Set those three strands together and the Egyptian view of the conquest becomes legible: publicly unmentioned, practically resisted, and eventually mythologized. That progression is worth noticing because it is exactly the sequence a conquered society with strong institutions and no political voice would be expected to follow.

## The Fiscal Machinery of the Satrapy

Because the fiscal settlement is the best-supported explanation for Egyptian grievance, it deserves closer description than a single sentence.

Achaemenid provincial finance worked on assessment rather than on collection at source. Each satrapy owed an annual quantity, expressed largely in silver by weight, and the satrap was responsible for producing it. How he produced it was substantially his own affair, which meant that the empire's fiscal pressure was transmitted downward through whatever local mechanisms already existed. In Egypt those mechanisms were the traditional assessment of agricultural land, the taxation of trade, and the extraction of surplus from temple estates.

Egypt was assessed among the more productive satrapies, which is unsurprising given its grain output and its trade. Herodotus gives a figure for the Egyptian assessment alongside figures for other provinces, and while the absolute numbers cannot be converted into any meaningful measure of burden without knowing the size of the Egyptian economy, the relative ranking is informative: Egypt was expected to pay a great deal.

Three features of the system generated friction. The first is the inflexibility already discussed. A quota set in a distant capital did not adjust for a low flood, and Egyptian agriculture varied enormously year to year for exactly that reason. The second is the silver requirement. Egypt had no silver deposits of consequence, so meeting an assessment in silver meant selling grain and goods for metal, which tied the fiscal system to trade conditions Egypt did not control. The third is the temple revenue reduction, which shifted the burden of supporting religious institutions from the crown to the institutions themselves at precisely the moment those institutions were being asked to accept a foreign king as pharaoh.

The last of these is the most politically consequential, because temples were not merely religious bodies. They were landowners, employers, courts of a kind, and the manufacturers of royal legitimacy. Reducing their income while requiring their endorsement was a policy that saved money in the short run and cost authority in the long run, and the record of revolt suggests the trade was a poor one.

Darius I appears to have understood this better than Cambyses did. His temple building at Hibis, his codification of Egyptian law, and his presentation of himself in Egyptian royal form all read as investment in the legitimacy account, and his reign, once the initial revolt was suppressed, was comparatively stable in Egypt until its final years. That correlation is not proof of causation, but it is consistent with everything else the Late Period shows about what it took to rule Egypt from outside.

## Comparing the Conquest to Egypt's Other Foreign Conquests

Egypt was conquered by outsiders several times, and setting 525 BCE against the other cases sharpens what was distinctive about it.

The Hyksos takeover in the seventeenth century BCE was a gradual settlement and assumption of power in the Delta by a Levantine population already present, rather than a single invasion, and it left Upper Egypt under Theban rule. It ended with an Egyptian war of reconquest that founded the New Kingdom, and it left behind military technology, above all the chariot, that Egypt used to build an empire.

The Kushite conquest of the eighth century BCE was carried out by a dynasty that considered itself the guardian of orthodox Egyptian religion and ruled as thoroughly Egyptian pharaohs, building and restoring temples and reviving Old Kingdom forms. It is barely a foreign conquest in cultural terms at all.

The Assyrian invasions of the seventh century BCE were punitive and indirect, aimed at removing Kushite interference in the Levant, and they ended in client rule that converted itself into the Saite dynasty within a generation.

The Persian conquest differs from all three. It was a single decisive campaign by a state that intended permanent direct provincial administration, that had the systems to deliver it, and that did not culturally assimilate to Egypt in the way the Kushites had. It also differs in outcome: there was no Egyptian war of reconquest founding a new golden age. The sixty years of restored independence in the fourth century BCE were real and produced fine buildings, but they ended in a second conquest rather than in a New Kingdom.

The Macedonian conquest of circa 332 BCE is the useful contrast on the other side. Alexander took Egypt without a battle from a Persian satrap who surrendered, sacrificed to Apis, honored the temples, and was received as a liberator. The comparison shows what Egyptians actually objected to. It was not foreign rule as such, which they accepted from Alexander and from the Ptolemies after him. It was rulers who declined to fund and participate in Egyptian religion, and the Persians, particularly after Darius, increasingly declined.

## Law, Justice, and the Codification Project

One of the more revealing acts of Persian administration in Egypt was Darius I's commissioning of a compilation of Egyptian law, and it deserves attention because it shows what kind of empire the Achaemenids were trying to be.

The tradition, preserved in Demotic material, is that Darius ordered the assembly of Egyptian law as it had stood down to the end of native rule, and that the work was carried out over a period of years by Egyptian specialists, including priests and scribes, and recorded in both Demotic and Aramaic. The project was not an imposition of Persian law on Egypt. It was the opposite: an attempt to record what Egyptian law already was so that the imperial administration could apply it.

That is a distinctive imperial method and worth naming. An empire can govern a province by replacing its legal system, by ignoring it, or by learning it. Replacement is expensive and provokes resistance. Ignoring it leaves the province ungovernable in any predictable way. Learning it costs an initial investment and then makes the province administrable through its own institutions, which is cheap and stable. Persia chose the third option in Egypt, and the same instinct shows up elsewhere in the empire in the treatment of local cults, local languages, and local elites.

The practical evidence supports the picture. Demotic contracts from the Persian period follow the forms established under the Saites without discontinuity. Property disputes were heard under Egyptian procedure. Marriage settlements, inheritance arrangements, and commercial agreements retained their Egyptian character. Women continued to hold and exercise the same legal capacities they had before the conquest, appearing as parties in their own right. Nothing in the personal law of Egyptians changed because a Persian sat on the throne.

Aramaic entered as an administrative overlay rather than as a replacement. Imperial correspondence, garrison records, and communication with the satrap's office ran in Aramaic, while Egyptian internal legal life ran in Demotic. Bilingual and biscriptal administrative practice was normal, and the Elephantine archive shows the two operating alongside each other without apparent friction at the documentary level.

The limitation of the codification project as evidence is that we know it mainly by report rather than from the compiled text itself, which does not survive. What survives is the tradition that it happened and a legal practice consistent with it. That is enough to establish the policy and not enough to describe its content, which is a distinction worth preserving rather than blurring.

## Grain, Silver, and the Canal Under Persian Rule

The economic relationship between Egypt and the empire is the least dramatic part of this story and arguably the most consequential, since it explains both why Persia wanted Egypt and why Egyptians resented paying for the privilege.

Egypt's principal export was food. Grain surplus moved outward to the Aegean and the Levant, and under Persian rule some of it moved to imperial requirements as tribute in kind. Linen, papyrus, natron, and finished goods followed the same routes. Inward came silver, which Egypt lacked and needed in order to pay both mercenaries and, after 525 BCE, imperial assessments.

The completion of the canal from the Nile through the Wadi Tumilat to the Gulf of Suez is the single largest piece of infrastructure associated with Persian rule in Egypt. Necho II had begun it and abandoned or suspended the work; Darius I finished it and marked the achievement with stelae along its length inscribed in Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian, and Egyptian. The Egyptian text presented the project in Egyptian royal terms to Egyptian readers, which is a small but telling instance of how Achaemenid rule presented itself locally.

The canal's purpose was imperial rather than narrowly Egyptian. It connected the Nile system, and therefore the Mediterranean, to the Red Sea and the maritime routes running toward Arabia and the Persian Gulf, knitting a province at the empire's western edge into a network reaching to its eastern one. For Egypt the practical effect was access to Red Sea trade on a scale earlier Egyptian regimes had achieved only through arduous desert portage to the coast.

Naucratis and the Greek trading relationship continued through the Persian period, though the political framework had changed: Greek merchants now operated in a province of the empire that was fighting Greeks elsewhere, which complicated matters at moments of open war and appears not to have stopped commerce for long.

The fiscal burden of all this is the part historians would most like to quantify and cannot. Herodotus gives an assessment figure for Egypt among his list of satrapal tributes, and it places Egypt high. Converting that into a rate of extraction requires an estimate of Egyptian output that we do not have, which means claims that Persian taxation was crushing and claims that it was tolerable both rest on wide margins. What can be said with confidence is structural rather than numerical: the assessment was fixed, Egyptian output was highly variable, and a system with those two properties transfers all the risk of a bad year onto the taxpayer. That is a durable source of resentment regardless of the headline rate.

## Studying the Persian Conquest

For readers preparing an examination answer, a lecture, or an essay, this topic rewards a specific structure rather than a broad narrative.

Hold three facts firmly: the date 525 BCE, the battle at Pelusium, and the resulting satrapy counted as Dynasty 27. Hold two names: Cambyses II for the conquest and Darius I for the administration, since the contrast between them is where most of the analytical marks sit. Hold the mechanism of the conquest, which was the Sinai water arrangement plus a defection plus naval and diplomatic isolation, because reciting a battle without the logistics behind it is a weak answer.

Be able to state the evidence problem and name at least one Egyptian document that contradicts the Greek tradition. The Serapeum Apis burial stelae are the example to know, and being able to say what they are and why they matter is what distinguishes a strong answer from a competent one. Be able to explain the fiscal grievance, since the revenue decree is the honest explanation for Egyptian resentment and it replaces the atrocity story with something better supported.

Be able to describe the pattern of revolt: risings timed to Persian succession crises, most successful when an outside power funded them, culminating in the independence of circa 404 BCE. And be ready to distinguish the first Persian conquest from the second, because examiners like the contrast and most candidates blur them.

## The Honest Verdict

Persia conquered Egypt because it was a larger, richer, better-organized state that solved the one operational problem standing between it and the Nile. There is no need for a more exotic explanation, and the exotic explanations that exist have not held up.

The campaign was competent rather than brilliant. Cambyses identified the Sinai as the real obstacle, arranged water through diplomacy, obtained intelligence through a defector, isolated Egypt diplomatically and navally, and then fought a conventional battle at the only place where the country could be defended. Egypt lost that battle for reasons of numbers, cavalry, and structural military weakness, and once it was lost the country had nothing behind it.

The occupation was administratively effective and politically corrosive in a specific and identifiable way. Fixed tribute that ignored the flood, and a documented reduction of temple revenues, produced grievance in exactly the constituencies that could act on it. Two centuries of intermittent revolt followed, and Egypt eventually broke free, which very few Persian provinces managed.

The reputation is largely undeserved in its specifics and not entirely undeserved in its substance. The Apis killing did not happen; the Serapeum stelae settle it. The cats at Pelusium are a late invention. The madness is a construction serving Greek and Persian purposes alike. But Egyptian priests really did lose income, Egyptian farmers really did pay in bad years, and a captured king really was executed. The legend is false and the resentment behind it was real, and holding both of those at once is the correct historical position rather than a compromise between two wrong ones.

What the conquest ultimately demonstrates is a lesson about sources rather than about Egypt or Persia. The most vivid account of an event is not usually the most accurate one, and when a dull administrative stela and a gripping story disagree about a specific act, the stela is the better witness. That is an unglamorous conclusion, and it is the one the evidence supports.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Persia conquer Egypt?

Through a single well-prepared campaign in 525 BCE under Cambyses II. The decisive work was done before the fighting: Cambyses arranged water supplies for the Sinai crossing through an agreement with an Arab ruler controlling the desert route, obtained intelligence from Phanes of Halicarnassus, a Greek commander who defected from Egyptian service, and neutralized Egypt's naval and diplomatic position by securing Phoenician and Cypriot fleets and the defection of Samos. The Persian army then crossed the Sinai and defeated the Egyptian force under Psamtik III at Pelusium, the fortress guarding the eastern Delta. Memphis was besieged and taken, Psamtik III was captured, and the rest of the country submitted. Egypt became a Persian satrapy within one campaigning season.

### Q: Who was Cambyses?

Cambyses II was the eldest son of Cyrus the Great and ruled the Achaemenid Persian empire from circa 530 to 522 BCE. He spent most of his reign on the Egyptian campaign and its aftermath and died returning toward the empire's heartland amid revolt at home. Greek writers, above all Herodotus, portray him as unstable, sacrilegious, and cruel, and Darius I's own royal inscription has reasons to portray him unfavorably too, since Darius took the throne in disputed circumstances. Egyptian documents present a different figure: a king with a full Egyptian throne name, depicted in Egyptian royal dress, named on a Serapeum stela as the provider of an Apis bull's sarcophagus, and persuaded by the official Udjahorresnet to restore the temple of Neith at Sais.

### Q: What year did Persia conquer Egypt?

525 BCE. The Battle of Pelusium and the fall of Memphis both took place in that year, which is unusually firm by the standards of Egyptian chronology because it can be cross-checked against Babylonian, Persian, and Greek records compiled independently. Egyptian documents dated by Cambyses' regnal years confirm the sequence. Persian rule then ran, with interruptions and repeated revolts, until Egypt regained independence in circa 404 BCE. A second Persian conquest under Artaxerxes III followed in circa 343 BCE and lasted about eleven years until Alexander of Macedon arrived in circa 332 BCE. When a source refers simply to the Persian conquest of Egypt without qualification, it usually means 525 BCE.

### Q: What was the Battle of Pelusium?

Pelusium was the fortress at the easternmost Nile mouth, where the desert road from the Sinai met cultivated land, and it was the gate of Egypt from the northeast. The battle fought there in 525 BCE was the decisive engagement of the Persian conquest. The Egyptian army under Psamtik III, combining machimoi levies with Greek and Carian mercenaries, defended prepared ground and was defeated by Cambyses' combined force of shield-and-bow infantry, massed archers, and cavalry. Tactical detail is thin, since Herodotus gives little and instead describes visiting the site long afterward to see the bones still lying there. Once the line broke, the surrounding open ground favored Persian cavalry and the Egyptians fell back on Memphis.

### Q: How did the Persians rule Egypt?

As a satrapy governed from Memphis by an appointed satrap answerable to the Great King. Beneath that appointment the existing Egyptian administrative machinery continued largely intact, staffed by Egyptians and using Egyptian record-keeping alongside Aramaic, the imperial administrative language. Nomes were assessed, temples operated their estates, courts applied Egyptian law, and Demotic contracts continued to be written. Garrisons held Memphis, the Delta approaches, and Elephantine on the southern frontier, often composed of non-Persian troops settled with land and families. The main change was fiscal: a fixed annual tribute in silver and kind that did not adjust for a poor Nile flood, alongside a documented reduction in the revenues supplied to most Egyptian temples.

### Q: Did Cambyses desecrate Egyptian temples?

The central charge, that he stabbed the Apis bull at Memphis, is contradicted by the evidence. Stelae from the Serapeum at Saqqara record an Apis burial during his reign carried out with full royal honors, naming Cambyses in Egyptian royal style as the provider of the sarcophagus. What he did do, on the evidence of a surviving Demotic decree, was sharply reduce the revenues supplied to most Egyptian temples, exempting only a few major ones. To priests whose income and status depended on those revenues, that would have felt like an attack on the gods, and the tradition of Persian impiety plausibly grows from it. The grievance was real; the bull-stabbing story is not supported.

### Q: How did Egyptians resist Persian rule?

Through repeated revolts, timed with considerable strategic sense to moments of Persian weakness. The first followed Cambyses' death in circa 522 BCE and was suppressed by Darius I. Another broke out around 486 BCE at the end of Darius' reign and was put down by Xerxes. The largest came in the mid fifth century BCE under Inaros, a Libyan dynast claiming Saite descent, and drew a substantial Athenian fleet and expeditionary force into the Delta before Persian forces under Megabyzus destroyed both. Amyrtaeus sustained resistance in the Delta marshes, where cavalry could not operate effectively. Independence finally succeeded in circa 404 BCE during a contested Persian succession and lasted roughly sixty years.

### Q: What was the 27th Dynasty?

The Twenty-seventh Dynasty is the designation the priest Manetho gave to the Achaemenid Persian kings who ruled Egypt from circa 525 to 404 BCE: Cambyses II, Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes I, Darius II, and briefly Artaxerxes II. They were not an Egyptian family and mostly did not live in Egypt. Counting them as a dynasty was a historiographical decision that preserved the fiction of continuous pharaonic succession, and that fiction mattered theologically, because Egyptian religion required a king to perform the rituals maintaining cosmic order. A country with no pharaoh was in a state of religious emergency, so crowning the Great King with an Egyptian titulary solved a problem for Egyptian priests as much as for Persian administrators.

### Q: Why did Egypt lose to Persia?

Because Persia was a larger, wealthier, better-organized state with decisive advantages in cavalry, numbers, and logistics, and because it could absorb a defeat and return while Egypt could not. Egypt's defense depended on the Sinai crossing being impassable to a large army, and Cambyses solved that problem through diplomacy and pre-positioned water before the campaign began. Once the outer barrier was neutralized, everything rested on one battle at Pelusium, and Egypt had no second defensive position behind it. Lydia and Babylon, both older and militarily more experienced powers, fell to the same empire on the same timetable, which suggests the outcome reflected Achaemenid strength rather than any particular Egyptian failing.

### Q: Was Cambyses really mad?

The evidence does not support it, and two source traditions with strong motives constructed the story. Greek writers were defining Persia as the antithesis of Greek self-government and found an impious tyrant useful. Darius I, who came to the throne after a disputed succession, benefited from his predecessor appearing unfit to rule and had his own account circulated. The specific proofs offered fail on inspection: the Apis killing is contradicted by burial stelae, the lost army of Siwa is unverified and functions in Herodotus as moral illustration, and losing a supply-starved expedition toward Kush was an ordinary hazard of desert campaigning. Cruelty and harsh fiscal policy are plausible. Derangement is not demonstrated.

### Q: What was the story about cats at the Battle of Pelusium?

The claim is that Cambyses placed cats and other animals sacred to Egyptians in his front line so Egyptian troops would not shoot. It comes from Polyaenus, a writer of the second century CE, roughly seven hundred years after the battle, in a collection of military stratagems compiled for entertainment and instruction rather than as history. No earlier source mentions it. It fits a well-established Greek and Roman genre of jokes about Egyptian animal worship, and it should be treated as literary invention rather than evidence. The story is repeated constantly in popular accounts, which is a useful illustration of how a vivid late anecdote can outcompete the documentary record.

### Q: Who was Udjahorresnet?

An Egyptian naval commander, priest, and physician who served the last Saite kings and then Cambyses and Darius I, and who recorded his career on a statue inscription that survives. He describes composing Cambyses' Egyptian royal titulary, persuading the king to expel foreigners who had settled in the temple precinct at Sais, and securing the restoration of the temple's revenues and rituals. He is the era in miniature: an elite Egyptian who chose service over withdrawal and framed that choice as protection of Egyptian institutions. His account is self-justifying and must be read as such, but a claim to have persuaded a king to restore temple revenues would not have been plausible to his audience if that king had been notoriously destroying temples.

### Q: How long did Persian rule over Egypt last?

The first Persian period ran roughly a hundred and twenty years, from circa 525 to 404 BCE, punctuated by revolts of which the largest, under Inaros, held parts of the country for several years in the mid fifth century BCE. Egypt then held independence under native dynasties for about sixty years. The second Persian period, following Artaxerxes III's reconquest in circa 343 BCE, lasted approximately eleven years and ended when Alexander of Macedon took Egypt in circa 332 BCE. Adding the two occupations together gives roughly a hundred and thirty years of Achaemenid rule across a span of just under two centuries, which is a poor retention record by the standards of the empire's other provinces.

### Q: What sources tell us about the Persian conquest of Egypt?

Four bodies of material with very different reliability. Herodotus supplies almost all the narrative and must be tested, since he wrote seventy years later through interpreters, relied on priestly informants with institutional grievances, and organized his material around themes of hubris and retribution. Egyptian documents supply the corrective: Serapeum stelae dating Apis burials, the Demotic decree on temple revenues, and Udjahorresnet's statue inscription. Aramaic papyri, above all from the Elephantine garrison, show the satrapy functioning day to day. Achaemenid royal material, including Darius I's multilingual canal stelae and the Behistun inscription, gives the imperial framing and firm chronology. Where narrative and document conflict on a checkable point, the document decides.

### Q: Did the Persian conquest end ancient Egyptian civilization?

No. It ended native political rule for a period, which is a different thing. Egyptian religion, language, law, art, scribal practice, and temple institutions all continued functioning under Persian administration, and in several respects intensified. Egyptian officials under occupation asserted deeper genealogies, more archaic titles, and more emphatically traditional self-presentation than their predecessors had. Animal cults expanded. Temple scholarship continued codifying religious texts. Egyptian civilization survived Persian, Macedonian, Ptolemaic, and Roman rule and continued in recognizable form for centuries, with the last securely dated hieroglyphic inscription falling in the fourth century CE, roughly nine hundred years after Pelusium.

### Q: How was the second Persian conquest different from the first?

The second conquest, under Artaxerxes III in circa 343 BCE, came after sixty years of Egyptian independence during which two earlier Persian invasions had been repelled. It succeeded because Artaxerxes committed sufficient force, employed competent Greek commanders, and outbid Egypt in the mercenary market so that Greek troops in Egyptian service made their own arrangements. Pelusium was outflanked rather than assaulted. The occupation that followed lasted about eleven years and is remembered far more harshly than the first, with Egyptian and Greek sources describing heavy exactions, demolished defensive walls, and stripped temple treasuries. The enthusiasm with which Egypt received Alexander eleven years later suggests those complaints reflected genuine experience.

### Q: Did Egyptians serve in the Persian army and navy?

Yes, extensively. Egyptian contingents served in Achaemenid forces for the next two centuries, and Egyptian ships and marines formed part of the Persian navy in the campaigns against Greece, where Greek accounts note them as distinct and effective. Egyptian soldiers appear in imperial garrisons elsewhere, and Egyptian craftsmen worked on Persian royal building projects at Susa, a contribution Darius I's foundation inscriptions acknowledge. This absorption matters for what came later: the Egyptian military tradition the Saites had rebuilt continued developing under Persian command, which is part of why the fourth-century revolts produced forces capable of repelling full Persian invasions rather than merely raiding.

### Q: Why was Egypt so hard for Persia to hold?

Geography and wealth working together. Egypt is a thin ribbon a thousand kilometers long with a marshy delta at one end, so full garrisoning is expensive and node-based control leaves large areas ungoverned, which is why Delta marshes sheltered resistance leaders for years. The annual flood put much of the Delta under water for part of each year, giving any counter-insurgency a limited campaigning window. The Sinai barrier that made Egypt hard to invade also made it slow to reinforce, so revolts could establish themselves before an imperial army arrived. And Egypt was rich enough in grain, silver, and naval manpower that Persia always came back rather than writing it off.
