John Cranko's Romeo and Juliet, premiered by the Stuttgart Ballet on 2 December 1962, is the production that taught the Western ballet stage how to tell Shakespeare's tragedy whole. The verdict of this article is plain: Kenneth MacMillan's more famous London version of 1965 stands on ground that Cranko surveyed, fenced and built upon three years earlier, and the modern full-length story ballet of the play begins in Stuttgart, not at Covent Garden. The fame order and the founding order run in opposite directions, and dance history has too often repeated the fame order as if it were the chronology.

![John Cranko's Romeo and Juliet performed by the Stuttgart Ballet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-114.webp)

The evidence for the verdict is documentary rather than sentimental. Cranko made his first version of the ballet in 1958 for La Scala, staged in the open air at Venice with Carla Fracci as Juliet, and rebuilt it in Stuttgart in 1962 with designs by Jürgen Rose and a creating cast led by Marcia Haydée and Ray Barra. Within two years a foreign company had acquired the staging. Within seven years the production had conquered New York, where Clive Barnes of the New York Times called it the best of a distinguished field of rival versions. Kenneth MacMillan saw the Stuttgart production in 1964, with his future Juliet Lynn Seymour guesting in the title role, and resolved to make his own. Every later choreographer who has set Prokofiev's score has worked in a landscape that these facts define.

What follows examines the production in the order of its claims: the Venetian first version and the ingenious scenic solution it forced; the Stuttgart premiere and the company Cranko built around narrative dancing; the dramaturgy that made crowds, servants and grieving mothers carry the tragedy's meaning; the pas de deux writing that admirers praised and detractors found under-nuanced; the American triumph of 1969; the documented line of influence running to MacMillan; the critics' quarrel over whether the ballet is a masterpiece or merely a foundation; and the long afterlife that has kept the staging in repertory on several continents. The play underneath all of it remains the measure. Shakespeare's Prologue promises "the two hours' traffic of our stage" and a pair of "star-cross'd lovers" (Prologue); Cranko's achievement was to give that traffic a stage architecture, a crowd and a corps of individualized citizens through whom the star-cross'd story could move at the speed of theatre.

## The Fame Order and the Founding Order

Ask a general audience to name the ballet of this play and the answer, where any answer comes, is MacMillan's: the Covent Garden production of 1965, created in the studio on Lynn Seymour and Christopher Gable and premiered, against its choreographer's wishes, by Margot Fonteyn and Rudolf Nureyev. The preference is understandable. The London production had the two most famous dancers alive, a celebrated film record and the marketing weight of a great house. Fame, however, is a measure of exposure, not of priority, and the priority is Stuttgart's by three years and by the testimony of the London choreographer himself.

MacMillan's debt is not an inference drawn by partisans of the German company. It is the sequence that the standard accounts of his career record. The Playbill history of the MacMillan production states the trigger directly: after seeing Cranko's version for the Stuttgart Ballet in 1964, MacMillan wished to create his own. The DanceTabs history of the Royal Ballet production adds the concrete detail that binds the two works together: MacMillan saw the Stuttgart ballet danced by Haydée and by Seymour as a guest with the company in 1964, and reckoned that he could do even better. Seymour had caught his eye, in the Arts Desk's phrase, when rehearsing Cranko's version. The muse of the London ballet learned the role of Juliet inside the Stuttgart production before she became the dancer on whose body the London choreography was made.

That sequence matters because it reverses the usual direction of condescension. Cranko's ballet is habitually introduced as a precursor, a worthy rough draft for the finished article in London. The documents support a stronger claim. The Stuttgart production demonstrated, before a paying public and a sceptical profession, that Prokofiev's sprawling score could sustain a full evening of legible Shakespearean narrative in the West, that a modest company without a single international star could carry it, and that the telling could put crowds, comedy and secondary grief at the centre of the tragedy rather than at its margins. MacMillan's genius then did what genius does with a proven form: it deepened the psychology, darkened the naturalism and raised the technical stakes of the central duets. Deepening presupposes a depth already sounded. The founding act was Cranko's.

There is a second cliché to clear away. Surveys of the play in dance sometimes treat the Soviet Lavrovsky production of 1940 as the single fountainhead from which all later versions flow. Lavrovsky's Kirov staging, created on Galina Ulanova, is indeed the landmark against which Cranko measured himself: he had seen the Bolshoi's version in London in 1956 and Frederick Ashton's very different Danish staging in Copenhagen, and the double encounter settled his determination to make his own. But the Western line of the full-length Shakespeare ballet, the line that runs through Stuttgart to London and onward to the worldwide repertory, passes through Cranko's theatre craft rather than through Moscow's monumental manner. Where Lavrovsky built an ideological pageant of progressive youth against feudal weight, Cranko built a city: a bustling society background, in Ismene Brown's summary for the Arts Desk, against which the major figures stood out. The city, not the pageant, is what later choreographers inherited.

## The Choreographer Before Stuttgart: Formation of a Storyteller

The man who rebuilt the ballet in 1962 had been preparing for it, in craft if not in intention, since adolescence. John Cyril Cranko was born on 15 August 1927 in Rustenburg, South Africa, and began making dances as a student in Cape Town before travelling to London in 1946 to train at the Sadler's Wells Ballet School. The London years that followed gave him the two educations that the Stuttgart production would fuse: the education of the classical repertory, absorbed as a dancer in the Sadler's Wells company, and the education of the commercial and lyric stage, absorbed as a young choreographer whose Sea Change, designed with John Piper, was performed at the Gaiety Theatre in Dublin in July 1949, and who was asked in 1950 to create a ballet for New York City Ballet during its Covent Garden visit.

His early successes were comedies and character pieces. Pineapple Poll, the Gilbert and Sullivan arrangement of 1951, and The Lady and the Fool established the reputation that the later, graver works would complicate: a choreographer of wit, speed and theatrical timing, able to characterize in a gesture and to land a joke in a jump. Ashley Killar's history of the choreographer notes the uneven years that followed, ballets on subjects as diverse as the Greek heroine Antigone and the murderous barber Sweeney Todd that met little acclaim, and the portrait that emerges is of a talent whose gifts were narrative and humane before they were fashionable. The wit matters to the later production more than its reputation suggests. The crowd scenes of the Stuttgart staging, the servants' bustle, the Nurse's fuss, the market's flirtations, are Pineapple Poll skills set to tragic use: comic characterization deployed not as relief from the story but as the story's social fabric.

The decisive encounters came in 1956 and after. In London that year the Bolshoi Ballet brought Lavrovsky's Romeo and Juliet on its first Western tour, with Ulanova's Juliet at its centre, and the younger generation of British choreographers sat in the Covent Garden audience measuring their ambitions against it; the DanceTabs history names Cranko among them, alongside MacMillan and Rodrigues. In Copenhagen he saw Frederick Ashton's very different staging for the Royal Danish Ballet, lyric and swift where the Soviet version was monumental. Killar's account makes the double encounter the hinge of the story: having seen the Bolshoi's version in London and Ashton's in Copenhagen, Cranko decided to mount his own when the La Scala invitation arrived. The decision carries the germ of the Stuttgart manner. Between Moscow's pageant and Ashton's lyricism lay a third possibility that neither had occupied: the tragedy as the life of a city, told at theatrical speed by an ensemble of actors who danced.

The London institution, meanwhile, had no room for the ambition at the scale he intended. The Royal Ballet of the 1950s had its presiding genius in Ashton and its choreographic hierarchy settled; a staff choreographer with a taste for full-evening literary narrative could expect commissions for shorter works and guest engagements elsewhere, in New York, with Rambert, in Paris and Milan, the itinerary the Boston Ballet biography records for Cranko's fifties career. Germany's opera-house system offered the opposite condition: modest budgets, a resident ensemble, a general director empowered to bet a company on a choreographer, and an audience for whom the ballet was one department of a civic theatre rather than a temple of the art. The prosecution for homosexual activity that the biographical record names among the pressures of his London life belongs to the same history of constraint. Stuttgart's invitation in 1961 was, in the strictest sense, an offer of the one thing London withheld: a company of his own, to build as the instrument of the stories he meant to tell. The Romeo and Juliet that resulted is unintelligible without that institutional fact, because the production's deepest innovation is not a step or a set but a company shaped, dancer by dancer, into a troupe of storytellers.

## Venice 1958: The First Version and the Transforming Set

The ballet began as a commission from Milan. Cranko, then a choreographer of witty shorter works for the Sadler's Wells and Royal Ballet companies in London, received the invitation to stage the play for the ballet company of La Scala, and the first version was performed in 1958 at the Teatro Verde, the open-air theatre on the island of San Giorgio Maggiore in Venice where the Milan company appeared in the summer season. Carla Fracci, then at the start of a career that would make her Italy's defining dramatic ballerina, was the Juliet. The scholarly literature on the score's choreographic history, including Nona Monahin's survey of the Prokofiev versions, dates Cranko's entry in the line to that 1958 La Scala production, four years before Stuttgart made it famous.

The Venetian circumstances imposed the production's most imitated invention. An open-air island stage offered few facilities for the scene changes that Shakespeare's plot demands: the action requires, at minimum, a town square, a rich formal interior, a street outside the Capulet house, Juliet's bedchamber with its balcony, the Friar's cell, a Mantuan street and the Capulet tomb. Seven changes of location, executed under an open sky with minimal machinery, would have broken any evening into fragments. Cranko's answer, as Ashley Killar records in his history of the choreographer, was a fixed set built as a double-storey gallery whose structure could be swiftly re-dressed and re-read from square to hall to chamber to tomb. One architecture stood for Verona entire; the audience learned its geography once and then read every scene against it at speed.

### Why did the first version need a set that could transform in the open air?

The Teatro Verde gave the Milan company an amphitheatre under the sky and almost no machinery for shifting scenery, while the plot demands some seven locations in swift succession. Cranko's double-storey gallery solved the equation: one permanent structure, re-dressed between scenes, could read in turn as square, hall, bedchamber, balcony and tomb, so the story never stopped for carpentry.

The device survived the circumstances that provoked it. When Cranko rebuilt the ballet for Stuttgart in 1962, he and Jürgen Rose retained the fixed set, and Killar's judgment on the result is the one the later record bears out: the gallery solution has been copied in countless other versions of the ballet. A constraint of Venetian logistics became a convention of the genre. Directors of the play in every medium confront the same problem of speed and place; Cranko's gallery taught ballet that architectural continuity, a single built Verona that the eye masters early, serves the tragedy's pace better than a procession of literal locations. The tomb that the audience has known since the first scene as the gallery's lower level carries a charge that no freshly revealed crypt can match: the lovers die in a room of the house that raised them.

The 1958 version itself could not travel. Ismene Brown's account is blunt: the first version, made for an open-air Venetian amphitheatre, was not transportable. What travelled was the conception: the gallery, the bustling civic background, the conviction that the whole play could be danced by an ensemble company, and the choreographer's knowledge, earned in performance, of where Prokofiev's score breathes and where it crowds. When the Stuttgart opportunity arrived in 1961, Cranko did not start again from the score's first bar. He rebuilt a tested design on a company that was, in a sense he intended, the production's second cast and first true home: young, international, hungry dancers gathered under a director who meant to make narrative dancing their native language.

## Stuttgart 1962: A Company Built Around a Story

Cranko came to Stuttgart in 1961 at the invitation of Walter Erich Schäfer, the general director of the Württemberg State Theatre, and found a provincial opera-house ballet of no international standing. The transformation that followed is the institutional half of the production's story. He terminated contracts, recruited dancers from several countries and schools, and assembled the ensemble whose names recur through the company's history: Egon Madsen, Richard Cragun, Birgit Keil, Susanne Hanke and, at the centre of his creative life, the Brazilian dancer Marcia Haydée, whom the company's own biographies call his prime muse and inspiration. The modern Stuttgart Ballet dates itself from his appointment; the encyclopaedia record describes a small group of dancers of diverse national backgrounds and training fused by Cranko into a company with an exciting and visually arresting style.

### How did the Stuttgart company come to be Cranko's to shape?

Walter Erich Schäfer, general director of the Württemberg State Theatre, appointed Cranko ballet director in 1961 and backed the rebuilding of the ensemble: contracts ended, dancers recruited from several countries and schools, and an opera-house company of no international standing became, within two seasons, the instrument on which the era's defining story ballets were made.

The premiere of the rebuilt Romeo and Juliet on 2 December 1962 was the breakthrough that the Stuttgart, Munich and Prague theatre biographies all name in the same terms: acclaimed by audiences and critics, it signalled the beginning of the famous era. The creating cast deserves exact statement, because popular summary repeatedly misassigns its Romeo. Marcia Haydée created Juliet. Romeo was created by Ray Barra, the American dancer whom the scholarly record identifies as the title-role creator in the premieres of both this ballet and Onegin; the journal literature on Cranko's creative process, drawing on John Percival's biography and on Barra's own testimony, states the pairing without ambiguity: Barra danced the title roles in the premieres of 2 December 1962 and of Onegin, with Haydée as his partner in both. Richard Cragun, the Romeo of legend and of the production's American premiere seven years later, stamped the role in performance until, in Robert Greskovic's words, his strength in triple tours en l'air haunted the choreography's text; but the first Romeo of Stuttgart was Barra, and accuracy about the founding night is a debt this article pays before any other.

Jürgen Rose, who had met Cranko in 1961 and received the commission from him, designed the sets and costumes, carrying the Venetian gallery idea into a permanent architectural Verona. The partnership of choreographer and designer became one of the production's signatures: Rose's designs framed Onegin and The Taming of the Shrew as well, and the 1998 New York revival of the staging still unfolded, in Greskovic's report, amid reworkings of his original designs. A production that can be reworked across thirty-six years without losing its identity has a visual grammar, not a backdrop; the gallery, the square's levels, the chamber within the house and the tomb beneath it compose a single argument about a city that contains its own graveyard.

The Stuttgart premiere also fixed the production's scale of ambition. Cranko had seen what the Bolshoi's forces could do with Lavrovsky's version and what Ashton's lyricism could not attempt; his own company numbered its dancers in dozens rather than hundreds, and his answer was density rather than mass: a crowd of individuals, each with a fragment of business, a trade, a flirtation, a grievance. The marketplace of the first act, the ballroom of the Capulets, the street brawl, the wedding morning, the funeral procession: each public scene became a weave of small legible lives through which the principals moved. The play supplies the warrant. Shakespeare's Verona is a city under strain before the lovers meet, its opening scene a servants' brawl that the Prince must threaten with death to quiet: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). A ballet that opens on a decorated corps de ballet misses the play's first fact. Cranko's opens on a town.

## The Score as a Script: Cranko and Prokofiev

Prokofiev's music reached Cranko with two great stagings already attached to it and a reputation for difficulty earned in the 1930s, when the Bolshoi's dancers had found the score ungraspable and the Soviet premiere collapsed into postponement. The Kirov production of 1940, choreographed by Lavrovsky, established the score's stage life; Ashton's Danish production of 1955 offered a lyrical alternative. When Cranko revamped his La Scala staging for Stuttgart in 1962, Greskovic counts, only a dozen other productions of the score had preceded his; in the decades after, nearly six times as many followed. The ratio is the statistical form of the influence argument: the score's choreographic population explodes after Stuttgart, and the explosion begins with the version that made the music legible as continuous drama to Western companies and audiences. The score itself, its numbers and its themes, has its own study elsewhere in this series: [Prokofiev's ballet and the Dance of the Knights](/2028/11/24/prokofiev-ballet-dance-of-knights/) examines the music from within. The question here is what a choreographer does with it.

Cranko's treatment begins from a refusal: the refusal to treat any number as an occasion for abstract display. The score's public music, the dances of the knights and the street festivities, becomes civic theatre; its lyric music becomes the lovers' private world; its grotesque and its laments become character studies for Mercutio, the Nurse, Lady Capulet. The Boston Ballet's account of the choreographer summarizes the resulting reputation in a triad that critics repeated across decades: a gift for nuanced storytelling, clear dramatic structure and an exquisite mastery of the pas de deux. The first two terms describe the dramaturgy; the third names the lyric instrument through which the dramaturgy speaks at its most private. A Cranko evening is built like a well-made play that happens to be danced: exposition through crowds, complication through encounter, catastrophe through the progressive isolation of the lovers from the civic world that the first act built so thickly around them.

The dramaturgy also explains the production's celebrated treatment of the score's awkward inheritance. Prokofiev, Piotrovsky and Radlov had first designed the ballet with a happy ending, then rebuilt it toward Shakespeare's tragic close under institutional pressure; the tragic score that choreographers inherit carries the seams of that reversal, passages of celebration pressed into service around a catastrophe. Lavrovsky solved the seams by monumental emphasis, letting scale carry conviction. Cranko solved them by character: where the music rejoices beyond the story's warrant, his stage fills with the city's oblivious life, the carnival energy of a Verona that does not know it is inside a tragedy. The device reaches its height in the second act's street scenes, where festivity and fatal brawling occupy the same square within minutes, and the score's brightness becomes the tragedy's irony rather than its embarrassment.

Shakespeare's text sanctions the method. The play keeps interrupting its lyric ascent with the city's noise: Mercutio's Queen Mab fantasy curdles into the quarrel, the wedding preparations run into the funeral, and the Friar's plans are undone by a quarantined letter, an accident of civic machinery. A choreographer who threads public life through every act is not decorating the tragedy; he is staging its mechanism. The feud kills the lovers through intermediaries, missed messages and public violence long before it reaches them in the tomb, and Cranko's crowd is that mechanism made visible. The production's claim on the score is therefore interpretive, not merely efficient: it reads Prokofiev's music as the sound of a city and two children inside it, and it held that reading steady for three acts.

## Reading the Play Without Words: The Three Acts

A scene-by-scene reading shows how completely the production trusts the play's own architecture. The first act corresponds to Shakespeare's first two movements, the city and the meeting. It opens in the square, with the civic world at full density, servants, traders, citizens, the brawl's threat hanging over the festivity, and establishes the production's governing ratio: the lovers are figures inside a population. The Capulet ball follows within the gallery's transformed interior, the knights' dance giving the house its masked formality, Rosaline present as the errand's object, and the meeting staged as the sonnet the play writes for it, two dancers composing a single form between them. The act closes toward the balcony, the gallery's upper level converting the ball's public architecture into the night scene's private one, so that the transition from crowd to couple is a change of lighting and altitude rather than of place.

The balcony scene carries the act's lyric weight and the production's most imitated solution. Shakespeare's text for the scene is a negotiation across a height, Romeo below in the orchard, Juliet above at the window, the dialogue building its ladder of vows and warnings: "But, soft! what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2). A ballet cannot speak the negotiation; it must dance the approach, the retreat, the return. Cranko's entwining solution, the lovers held in near-continuous contact across the level change, converts the scene's verbal structure, advance and check, vow and caution, into partnering: each lift is an approach permitted, each separation a warning remembered, until the parting unthreads what the scene has woven. The wedding in the Friar's cell then shrinks the world to three figures and a rite, the still centre of the ballet, before the second act returns the action to the square.

The second act is the production's tragic engine room, and its construction is a lesson in the play's mechanics. Shakespeare builds the catastrophe from public material: the street at noon, Mercutio's wit sharpening toward the quarrel, Tybalt's grievance, the intervention that kills the intervener's friend. Cranko's crowd, established across two acts of festivity, performs its darkest function, supplying the audience, the obstacles and the witnesses for the duels, so that Mercutio dies in a populated place that empties around him, the festivity draining out of the square as the joke curdles into the curse on both houses (3.1). Lady Capulet's mourning over Tybalt converts private grief into public policy, and the act closes with the machinery of exile and arranged marriage engaging: the Prince's sentence, the Capulets' promise of Juliet to Paris, the wedding date set like a trap. The dramaturgical point is the one the play insists on: nothing in the catastrophe is a private misfortune. Every gear is civic.

The third act contracts the world by stages, exactly as the text contracts it. The aubade in the bedchamber, the lovers' dispute with the dawn, "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5), plays within the gallery's chamber while the house below prepares a wedding; the potion scene then isolates Juliet entirely, the production granting her the solitude that the play grants her great speech of imagined horrors before she drinks (4.3). The wedding morning's dreadful discovery, the funeral procession, Romeo in Mantua receiving the false report, the apothecary's poverty making the poison purchasable: the sequence is Shakespeare's, compressed with a fidelity that trusts the audience to follow a plot it may never have read, because each step has been given a body and a place. The tomb closes the design. Paris dies at the vault's mouth, the lovers' final duet reworks the balcony's entwining in the grammar of stillness, and Juliet's death answers Romeo's with the economy the play gives her last lines: "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3).

Measured against the text, the production's boldest choices are its retentions. It keeps the city's full weight where a star vehicle would cut it; it keeps Rosaline as a presence where the text keeps her a rumour, the better to stage the conversion the text describes; it keeps the mother's grief at the centre of the feud's arithmetic; and it refuses the ceremonial reconciliation pageant with which the Soviet tradition closed, ending instead in the vault, where the play's own last movement begins. The Prince's closing couplet, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3), is spoken over bodies in a tomb, not over a pageant of peace, and Cranko's ending honours that placement. Fidelity of this order is not literalism. It is the recognition that the play's structure is itself the strongest choreographic idea available, stronger than any invention a choreographer could impose on it, and the production's discipline in serving that structure is what later versions, including greater dance-makers' versions, had to reckon with.

## Crowds, Servants and a Grieving Mother: The Character Detail

The production's most quoted distinction is the fullness of its secondary world. Histories of the staging return to the same inventory: market crowds with individualized business, carnival dancers and gypsy figures in the street scenes, comic servants whose errands knit the acts together, a Nurse built from fuss and affection rather than from a single joke, and, at the tragedy's dark hinge, a Lady Capulet whose grief over Tybalt's body becomes a scene in its own right. The filmed record of the production, made at the Stuttgart State Theatre in 2017, preserves the breadth of that world in its cast list alone: alongside the lovers stand the Duke, both sets of parents, Rosaline, the Nurse, the Friar, carnival dancers and gypsies, a small city mustered for a single evening.

The Lady Capulet mourning scene carries a documented pedigree. Ismene Brown's survey of the play in ballet notes that Cranko picked up several of Lavrovsky's Bolshoi ideas, especially the iconic Lady Capulet mourning scene: the Soviet production had discovered that the mother's collapse over her nephew's corpse could bear the weight of a full choreographic paragraph, and Cranko, who had watched the Bolshoi in London in 1956 with that discovery in mind, naturalized it in the West. The scene matters beyond its pathos. Shakespeare gives Lady Capulet the tragedy's coldest arithmetic of grief, begging the Prince for Tybalt's death to be answered in kind: "Romeo slew Tybalt, Romeo must not live" (3.1). A ballet that builds her mourning into a set piece restores the feud's emotional engine to the older generation; the hatred that kills the children is shown to be grief wearing the mask of justice, and the final reconciliation over the lovers' bodies answers a sorrow the audience has watched her dance. When Haydée, the creating Juliet, returned to the production in later years as Lady Capulet, Greskovic recorded that she made a star turn of the mime and mad-scene dramatics of the role: the production's memory of itself, embodied in the dancer who had founded its Juliet, taking up the mother's grief.

Rosaline offers the opposite case, an invention rather than an inheritance. Shakespeare keeps Romeo's first love entirely offstage, a name in other mouths: the woman who "hath forsworn to love" and against whose beauty Romeo measures the ball (1.1, 1.2). Cranko's production gives her a danced presence, credited in the cast lists of the filmed performance, so that the Capulet ball gains a visible term of comparison: the audience sees the face Romeo came to see, and watches it become irrelevant in the instant of the shared sonnet. The device is pure theatre and also pure fidelity, because the play's own dramaturgy depends on the speed of that replacement. "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight! / For I ne'er saw true beauty till this night" (1.5): the lines argue that the earlier love was a tale told, not a thing seen, and a production that lets the house see Rosaline lets it judge Romeo's conversion with its own eyes.

### Why does Rosaline appear on stage in Cranko's ballet when the play keeps her offstage?

Shakespeare's Rosaline never enters; she exists as report, the beloved who forswore love and the standard against which Romeo measures every other face. Cranko embodies her at the Capulet ball, where cast lists credit the role, so the audience watches Romeo's errand collapse in a moment: the face he came to find is present, seen and displaced by Juliet.

The servants deserve their paragraph because the play gives them its first and last words about the feud's social reach. The tragedy opens not with princes but with Sampson and Gregory, Capulet servingmen trading boasts in the street, and the brawl that follows climbs the social ladder rung by rung until the Prince himself must climb down into it. Cranko's crowd scenes honour that ladder. The comic business of the production, the errands, the market haggling, the servants' dances, is not relief pasted between tragedies; it is the feud's lower storeys, the Verona that will supply the brawlers of the first scene, the revellers of the second act and the mourners of the last. MacMillan's later Verona pursued naturalism of a darker, more psychological cast, and the comparison between the two productions is sharpest here: where London individualized the principals' inner lives, Stuttgart individualized the city. Both are forms of realism. Stuttgart's came first, and it is the one that a company of modest size can perform without stars, which is a large part of why the production travelled so far into the world's repertory.

The deaths complete the character argument. Cranko stages the sequence of killings, Mercutio, Tybalt, Paris, the lovers, as the progressive emptying of the populated world the first act established: each death removes a figure the crowd scenes have made familiar, and the square that opened the ballet in noise closes it in the silence of the tomb. Mercutio's end keeps the play's terrible joke intact, the dying wit cursing both houses in the line that every staging must honour: "A plague o' both your houses!" (3.1). The production's reputation for the death scenes rests on that fidelity of proportion: the catastrophe is danced at the scale of the city that caused it, so the final stillness measures everything the noise has cost.

## Juliet's Arc: The Role as Cranko Built It

The production's surest claim on a dancer's imagination is the heroine's arc, which Cranko constructs as a progressive solitude. The Juliet of the first act is a daughter inside a household: presented at her family's ball, partnered decorously by Paris in the ballroom duet whose formality Greskovic noted, watched by her mother and her Nurse, a figure the crowd scenes keep embedded in the social weave. The play authorizes the embedding. Shakespeare's Juliet enters as the child of the house's dynastic planning, the subject of Paris's suit and her parents' negotiations before she is the subject of her own desire: her consent is canvassed in her mother's chamber as a formality of the match, in the scene where the Nurse's memories measure how completely the household owns her past (1.3). A production that gives its Juliet a star entrance falsifies that beginning. Stuttgart's Juliet is discovered inside her world, and the discovery is the point: the audience watches the world that will kill her holding her, fondly and fatally, before she has a single private thought.

The balcony scene begins the extraction. The duet removes her, in stages, from the household's level to the garden's darkness and back, and the choreography's entwining contact dramatizes the play's central event in her: the acquisition of a private language. Shakespeare's Juliet in the scene is already the tragedy's most lucid mind, discounting names, demanding substance over phrase, "doff thy name, / And for that name, which is no part of thee, / Take all myself" (2.2). Cranko's Juliet carries that lucidity in the duet's exchanges, initiating as much movement as she receives, so that the partnership reads as a mind meeting its equal rather than a maiden overwhelmed. The wedding in the cell completes the separation from the household: she has a husband her parents have not given her, a secret that converts her obedience into performance, and the production's third act exploits the conversion relentlessly.

The aubade, the potion scene and the discovery morning trace the solitude to its end. In the bedchamber she argues with the dawn beside a husband already half departed (3.5); in her father's house she submits, in a performance of repentance, to the marriage she means to escape (4.2); alone, she dances the potion scene's terror, the imagination of waking in the vault among the bones of her ancestors that the play gives her in its most harrowing soliloquy (4.3); and the morning's discovery returns her, apparently dead, to the centre of the crowd that opened her story, the household's grief breaking over a body that the audience knows is breathing. The arc's shape is the production's thesis in miniature. The city that danced around her in the first act dances around her, unknowing, at every stage of her withdrawal from it, and the tomb's final solitude, two bodies and a witness arriving too late, lands with the weight of everything the crowd scenes built. Choreographers inherit problems; the arc of Juliet from household child to solitary agent is the problem this production solved most completely, and its solution, rather than any single step, is what the role's interpreters from Fracci and Haydée to Badenes have been handed.

Romeo's arc is built as the mirror image, a progressive expulsion from the male world that opens the ballet. The first act gives him friends, disguises, the city's freedom; the second act takes the friends away in blood, Mercutio dead by his intervention, Tybalt dead by his hand, and the Prince's sentence converts the citizen into an exile. Where Juliet withdraws inward, Romeo is driven outward, to Mantua, to the apothecary's shop, to the vault's mouth and the killing of Paris that the production keeps at the tragedy's threshold. The final duet's stillness is therefore a reunion of two opposite journeys, the girl who left her world by secrecy and the boy who lost his by violence, meeting in the one room where neither world can follow. The symmetry is Shakespeare's, and the production's fidelity to it, holding both arcs in balance across three acts of crowds, is the deepest sense in which the staging earns the word classical: not in its steps, but in its proportions.

## Jürgen Rose's Verona: The Design as Dramaturgy

The production's visual text deserves a section of its own, because Rose's contribution is structural rather than decorative. The partnership began in 1961, when the designer met the choreographer and received the commission for the Stuttgart production; the collaboration extended across the founding repertory, Rose designing Onegin and The Taming of the Shrew as well, until the pairing of names became a Stuttgart signature in itself. The design problem Rose inherited was the Venetian one restated for an indoor stage: seven locations, one architecture, an audience that must never wait. His solution kept the double-storey gallery and gave it the finish of a permanent building, a Verona of arcades and levels whose upper storey holds the chamber and the balcony and whose lower holds the street, the cell and, in the design's final transformation, the tomb.

The dramaturgy of the scheme repays attention, because it converts the play's social geography into architecture. The Capulet house in Shakespeare is a machine for generating the tragedy: its hall hosts the ball, its orchard wall the trespass, its chamber the wedding night and the discovery, its vault the end. A design that holds all of those spaces within one structure lets the audience feel the machine's enclosure, the sense that the lovers move through rooms of a single fate. The street outside belongs to the same building's shadow, so the brawls happen, in effect, on the family's doorstep, and the Prince's authority must enter a space that private architecture dominates. When the tomb opens within the gallery's lower level, the design completes its argument: the vault was inside the house's footprint from the first scene, present at the ball, under the balcony, beneath the wedding chamber. Death in this production is not a journey to a distant crypt. It is a descent to the building's foundation, and the audience has been looking at the foundation all evening.

The design's afterlife in revival offers the record's most interesting testimony to its strength. When the Stuttgart company brought the production to New York in 1998, Greskovic reported that the three-act staging unfolded amid reworkings of Rose's original designs: the scenery grayer, if more elaborately rendered, and the costumes fussier. The observation is a critic's reservation about a revival's refurbishment, but its premise is the design's durability: thirty-six years after the premiere, the production's visual identity was still Rose's, to be reworked rather than replaced, and the filmed performance of 2017 again credits his sets and costumes. Ballet designs rarely survive their decade; the fashions of stage painting and the economics of storage conspire against them. A design that remains the production's face across three generations of dancers has passed from décor into text, and the custodians' treatment of it, reworkings within the original conception rather than a new designer's replacement, matches the treatment of the choreography itself under the letter of the legacy.

The costumes perform the same civic argument as the crowds. A Verona dressed as a hierarchy, the Capulet household's formality against the street's motley, the carnival figures and gypsies of the public scenes marking the calendar's licensed disorder, lets the eye read the feud's social structure before a step is danced. In the ball scene the design's discipline peaks: the knights' dance requires a floor of masked uniformity against which the sonnet's intimacy can register as transgression, and Rose's hall supplies it, a room built for ceremony that two children convert, in the space of a dance, into the most private place in the city. Designers of later versions inherited the problem in the form Stuttgart defined it: one building, every location, the tomb inside the house. The inheritance is so complete that its origin is rarely credited, which is the standard fate of solutions that work.

## The Balcony, the Bedroom and the Tomb: The Pas de Deux Question

If the crowd scenes are the production's public signature, the duets are its private one, and the critical quarrel over the ballet begins here. The admirers' case is consistent across five decades. The Stuttgart company's own biographical tradition, repeated in the Boston Ballet's account of the choreographer, credits Cranko with an exquisite mastery of the art of the pas de deux, a mastery that conquered New York in 1969. The duets carry the lovers from the ball's first encounter through the balcony scene, the wedding morning's aubade and parting, to the tomb, and their choreographic logic is the logic of the play's own verse. Shakespeare writes the first meeting as a shared sonnet, two voices completing a single form, "saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake" answered within the same quatrain's architecture (1.5). Cranko's meeting duet works by the same principle of exchange and completion: phrases begun by one body are finished by the other, and the lifts that detractors call gymnastic read, at their best, as the physical form of a verse structure in which neither speaker owns a line entire.

The balcony duet extends the principle into the production's most influential passage. The gallery set places Juliet above and Romeo below, and the choreography must solve the vertical problem that every staging of the scene confronts: desire across a height, conducted in whispers, at night, against the threat of discovery. Cranko's solution keeps the lovers in near-continuous contact across the level change, the balcony less a barrier than a hinge, so that the parting, "Good night, good night! Parting is such sweet sorrow" (2.2), arrives as a physical unthreading of two bodies that the choreography has spent the scene entwining. Later choreographers inherited the problem and the terms in which it could be solved; the balcony duet became the test piece by which versions of the ballet are compared, and the comparison itself is a Stuttgart legacy.

Against this stands the reservation that the cooler critics entered and that any honest verdict must weigh. Greskovic, reviewing the production's return to New York in 1998, found that its frequent pas de deux are not especially nuanced: gymnastic, tricky lifts occur regularly, he wrote, whether for a decorous ballroom duet between Paris and Juliet or for the lovers' first meeting and nighttime reverie, and the lack of gradation gives little narrative or dramatic distinction to the intimacy, tenderness or formality that the physical moments imply. The charge is precise and, within its terms, partly just. Cranko's partnering vocabulary is an athletic one, built for the strong young dancers he recruited, and its voltage does not always fall when the drama asks for quiet. The wedding-morning duet and the ballroom duet for Paris share a family of lifts whose kinship a strict dramaturge would rather disguise.

The adjudication turns on what a duet in a narrative ballet is for. MacMillan, working three years later with greater resources and a psychoanalytic temper, graded his partnering by interior states, so that each duet marks a distinct stage of knowledge between the lovers; by that standard Cranko's voltage is indeed under-graded. By the standard of legibility, the standard a founding production must meet when it teaches an audience to read a whole tragedy without words, the Stuttgart duets succeed at the harder task: they make the relationship's public meaning unmistakable at every stage, courtship, rapture, marriage, parting, death, to a house that includes spectators who have never read the play. The balcony scene's entwining and the tomb's final stillness are not nuances, and do not pretend to be; they are punctuation marks large enough for the back of a large theatre. Nuance in this repertory was MacMillan's contribution, and it is a genuine one. It was also a second-generation luxury, purchased with the confidence that the form itself, the full-evening danced tragedy, had already been proved in Stuttgart to work.

## Two Romeos and One Juliet: The Interpreters

The production was made on particular bodies, and its history is partly the history of what those bodies taught it. The scholarly literature on Cranko's creative process, drawing on John Percival's biography and on the testimony of the dancers themselves, describes a studio method of collaborative give-and-take: the choreographer proposed, the dancers' own movement instincts answered, and the roles were shaped in the exchange. Ray Barra, who created Romeo, left an account of that process that the journal literature quotes as evidence for the making of both this ballet and Onegin. A role created in that manner carries its first interpreter inside its steps, and the distinction between the production's two Romeos is the distinction between a role's composition and its legend. Barra composed it; Cragun, arriving at the interpretation with a virtuoso's equipment, stamped it, until the triple tours en l'air that Greskovic noted became a haunting presence in the choreography's text, a standard written into the role by the body that made it famous rather than by the body that made it first.

Haydée's Juliet is the complementary case, a role whose composition and legend share one artist. Brazilian by birth, recruited into the young company as its emerging ballerina, she became, in the institutional phrase repeated across the company's biographies, Cranko's prime muse and inspiration, the dancer on whom Juliet, Tatiana in Onegin and a sequence of later heroines were formed. The Juliet she created was an ensemble ballerina's Juliet rather than a guest star's: girlish within the crowd scenes, where the production keeps her among her household and her Nurse before it isolates her, and gathering authority as the acts contract around her, through the potion scene's solitude to the tomb. Later Juliets of the production, from the company's own ranks and from acquiring houses, have had to find their readings inside a role whose proportions were set by a dancer-choreographer partnership of unusual intimacy, and the filmed record of 2017, with Elisa Badenes in the part, shows the role's architecture surviving its creator by two generations of interpreters.

Around the central pair, the founding company supplied the production with its bench of character artists, and the bench became a school of choreographers. Egon Madsen, Birgit Keil and Susanne Hanke stand in the company's foundation lists; the wider record of the Stuttgart ensemble notes the dancers who emerged from it to become choreographers of international standing, John Neumeier, Jiří Kylián, William Forsythe and Uwe Scholz among them. The connection between the production and that harvest is the connection between a repertory and a training: a company whose evenings are spent inside full-length narratives, playing citizens, servants, mourners and clowns as well as princes, educates its dancers in dramaturgy as a daily practice. Neumeier, who danced under Cranko at Stuttgart and made his own first Romeo and Juliet in 1971, carried the narrative inheritance to Frankfurt and Hamburg; the family tree of the European story ballet in the later twentieth century runs through the Stuttgart ensemble of the 1960s to a degree that no single production explains, but that this production, as the ensemble's founding triumph and daily discipline, did much to plant.

The interpreters' story has a coda that the record supplies and no novelist would improve. In the filmed performance of 2017, the Nurse is danced by Haydée and Friar Laurence by Egon Madsen: the creating Juliet attending on her successor in the role, the founding generation of the company still inside the production's frame in its character parts. Ballet is an art of bodies that age out of their roles; a production that can reabsorb its creators as its elders has achieved the condition of a repertory classic in the theatrical sense, the condition of a play that a company keeps in performance across the whole span of its members' working lives. The Stuttgart staging reached that condition within its founder's own institution, and the condition explains, better than any review, why the work's custodians have guarded its text with such tenacity: the ballet is not a property the company owns. It is the company's biography, danced.

## New York 1969: The American Premiere and the Stuttgart Miracle

The production's international life began early and widened fast. In 1964 the National Ballet of Canada acquired the rights, and Cranko travelled to produce the staging himself: the Canadian company's own history stresses that it remains the only company in North America for which Cranko personally mounted the work, and that the production served as its first performance at Toronto's O'Keefe Centre. The first foreign staging of a German opera-house ballet by a choreographer then known in North America chiefly by reputation was a wager on both sides, and its success in Toronto announced that the Stuttgart manner could be transplanted: the ballet did not depend on the founding cast's irreplaceable chemistry, because its strengths were structural, a set that taught the story's geography, crowds that taught its society, duets that taught its love.

The conquest that fixed the production's reputation came in 1969, when the Stuttgart Ballet made its first American tour and danced the American premiere of the staging at the Metropolitan Opera House in New York, with Haydée as Juliet and Richard Cragun, the role's defining interpreter, as Romeo. The season was, in the word every institutional history repeats, triumphant. The company's gift for storytelling, its clear dramatic structure and its pas de deux mastery conquered the New York audience, and American criticism reached for a phrase to hold the phenomenon: the Stuttgart Ballet Miracle, a name that the city's critics attached to the young company's arrival and that German accounts of the tour attribute in particular to Clive Barnes of the New York Times.

### What did Clive Barnes write about the production at its American premiere?

Reviewing the staging for the New York Times at its American premiere in 1969, Barnes placed it at the head of a field of versions: this setting of Prokofiev's score is, in his words, "quite simply, the best of a surprisingly distinguished bunch." The sentence did work, ranking the production and certifying that distinguished competitors existed for Stuttgart to lead.

The Barnes sentence deserves its currency, because it was comparative rather than merely warm. By 1969 a New York critic could set the Stuttgart staging against the Lavrovsky tradition known from Bolshoi visits and film, against Ashton's lyricism, against MacMillan's four-year-old London production, and Barnes, who had championed MacMillan's ballet in its turn, ranked Cranko's first. The judgment of the critic who coined the company's miracle remains the sharpest single sentence in the production's critical record, and the later sections of this article test it against the reservations that time and revival have added. What cannot be taken from it is its date: the verdict was delivered in 1969, when the comparison was fresh, the rivals were in active repertory and the Stuttgart company was the newcomer with everything to prove. First impressions by qualified judges, delivered against the strongest available field, carry an evidentiary weight that retrospective ranking rarely matches.

The tour also completed the production's demonstration of portability. The staging that Toronto had acquired as a script proved itself in New York as a company vehicle on the largest American stage, and the two facts together, the work travels without its creators, and its creators triumph with it abroad, define the double life that the ballet has led ever since. Stuttgart kept it as the cornerstone of the repertory that Cranko's successors inherited; other houses acquired it as a proven full-evening tragedy; and the phrase the tour earned followed the company for the rest of its founder's life, through the further American tours that ended, on the third of them, in the catastrophe of June 1973.

## The MacMillan Lineage: Emulation, Rivalry and the Debt London Owes

The brief of this article requires the lineage to be engaged directly, and the documents permit an exact account. Cranko and MacMillan were near-contemporaries formed in the same London institution, the Sadler's Wells Ballet of the late 1940s, where both danced and both began to choreograph. Both were in the Covent Garden audience when the Bolshoi brought Lavrovsky's production to London in 1956, an encounter that the DanceTabs history names for all three of the young choreographers, Cranko, MacMillan and Alfred Rodrigues, who would go on to create their own accounts of the play. The Soviet evening planted the ambition in both men. Cranko acted on it first, in Venice and then in Stuttgart. MacMillan acted on it after direct exposure to the result: the Stuttgart production, seen in 1964 with Haydée and with Seymour guesting, told him both that the thing could be done in the West and that a rival had done it. His response, in the DanceTabs summary, was the resolve that he could do even better, and the Royal Ballet's commission for his first three-act narrative ballet followed, with the balcony pas de deux he had already made for Seymour and Gable for Canadian television folded into the full design.

The differences between the two ballets are real and were intended. MacMillan pursued a naturalism that broke, in the Playbill account's terms, with grand entrances and picturesque poses: his Verona meets Romeo cloaked in semi-darkness, lurking in pursuit of Rosaline, and lets Juliet slip almost unnoticed into the ball held in her honour. The DanceTabs history frames the intention as a break with the stylised approaches of Cranko and of Lavrovsky alike, a realism of behaviour set against Stuttgart's realism of society. Where Cranko built the crowd, MacMillan built the interior: the London duets grade the lovers' knowledge of each other with a psychological fineness that the Stuttgart original does not attempt, and the London crowd, for all its colour, serves the principals rather than sharing their claim on the stage. The [MacMillan ballet and its Fonteyn and Nureyev premiere](/2028/12/01/macmillan-ballet-fonteyn-nureyev/) has its own full study in this series; the point here is the direction of the current. The later work defines itself against the earlier, in its maker's documented words and in its choreography's visible choices, and a work that must be defined against a predecessor has already conceded the predecessor's founding place.

Two corruptions of this record circulate and should be refused. The first is the fan's charge that the London ballet plagiarized the Stuttgart one wholesale. The charge collapses on inspection: the two productions share a score, a play and a set of dramaturgical solutions that the score and the play jointly impose, the gallery-like concentration of place, the civic crowd, the mourning mother inherited by both from Lavrovsky, and resemblance at that level is the resemblance of two translations of one text, not theft. MacMillan's choreography, his graded duets, his darkened naturalism, his treatment of the heroine's potion scene as external behaviour rather than depicted torment, in the scholarly analysis's contrast, are his own, and no serious account confuses the two men's movement languages. The second corruption is the politer one, the survey sentence that grants Cranko a paragraph as MacMillan's forerunner and passes on. Priority of this order, demonstrated before the profession and acted upon by the successor within a year of seeing it, is not forerunning in the weak sense. It is foundation. The London ballet's greatness, which this article does not contest, is the greatness of a second building on a surveyed plot; the surveyor was Cranko.

The lineage also runs through a dancer, and the human link deserves its sentence. Lynn Seymour guested with the Stuttgart company in Cranko's production in 1964, dancing the Juliet that Haydée had created; MacMillan saw her there, or in the rehearsals that the Arts Desk records, and built his Juliet on her. When the Royal Ballet's management displaced Seymour and Gable from the London premiere in favour of Fonteyn and Nureyev, the box-office logic was plain and the artistic irony complete: the role created through Cranko's Stuttgart production reached the London stage, at its first performance, in other bodies than the ones that had carried the lineage across. The production histories sometimes treat influence as an abstraction that flows between institutions. In this case it flowed through a young dancer's muscles, from a Stuttgart studio to a Covent Garden one, and the documentary record is unusually kind in letting the path be traced.

## Cranko Among the Choreographers of the Play

The scholarly map of the score's choreographic history, set out in Nona Monahin's study of the Prokofiev versions and their endings, gives the field its standard roll: Lavrovsky in 1940, Ashton in 1955, Cranko in 1958, MacMillan in 1965, John Neumeier in 1971, Nureyev in 1977, Yuri Grigorovich's reworking after Lavrovsky in 1979, Angelin Preljocaj in 1990 and Mark Morris in 2008, with Antony Tudor's Delius setting of 1943 and Maurice Béjart's Berlioz setting of 1966 standing as the great exceptions to the Prokofiev monopoly. The roll is instructive in both directions. Before Cranko, the Prokofiev line has exactly two names of the first rank, the Soviet founder and the English lyricist, separated by fifteen years and an iron curtain. After Cranko, the names arrive in a quickening sequence, and several of them stand in documented personal relation to the Stuttgart achievement: MacMillan its rivalrous heir, Neumeier its own company's alumnus, Nureyev a dancer who had measured himself against the repertory's Romeos before making his own. The field's centre of gravity, in the decades after 1962, sits where Stuttgart put it.

Neumeier's position in the roll deserves emphasis, since it converts influence from an argument into a biography. He danced as a soloist in Stuttgart under Cranko before directing at Frankfurt and then Hamburg, and his first Romeo and Juliet came in 1971, within Cranko's lifetime and within the tradition the Stuttgart ensemble embodied. The Hamburg versions that followed made him the narrative ballet's next great German custodian, and the La Fenice programme literature for his production states the descent without ceremony: Neumeier is described there as one of the last heirs of the classical tradition, formed as a solo dancer in Stuttgart under Cranko. An heir is not a copyist, and the Hamburg work departs from Stuttgart's in temper and design; but the lineage is the institution's own, a dancer formed inside the founding production's company carrying its conviction, that a full evening of danced narrative is ballet's central possibility, into the next half-century of the form.

The later names in the roll mark the tradition's diversification rather than its dilution. Preljocaj's 1990 version stripped the story toward abstraction and erotic geometry; Morris's 2008 production restored, in a gesture of scholarly provocation, the happy ending that Prokofiev first composed, reviving the score's original conclusion from the archives with the assistance of the musicologist Simon Morrison. That a choreographer of 2008 could make news by returning to the score's first thoughts demonstrates the settlement that the intervening tradition, Stuttgart's tradition, had imposed: the tragic ending, the civic crowd, the full-length arc had become the defaults that a new version must either honour or conspicuously refuse. Morris refused the ending; Preljocaj refused the realism; Bourne refused the institution of heterosexual Verona itself. Each refusal is legible only against the settlement, and the settlement's terms were drafted in Stuttgart between 1958 and 1969, in the gallery set, the crowd and the tomb without a pageant.

Tudor's exception sharpens the point from the other side. His Romeo and Juliet of 1943, set to Delius, is a poem of the story rather than its narrative, a compressed act of atmosphere that declines the full-length task altogether. The existence of the Tudor miniature, admired and unrepeated, shows what the field looked like before a workable full-length synthesis existed in the West: genius took refuge in the fragment. After Stuttgart, genius could take the whole evening, and did, repeatedly, on three continents. The difference between those two fields is the production's historical substance, and it is why the roll of choreographers, read with dates attached, tells a story that no single review, however warm, could establish on its own.

## The Critics' Quarrel: Best of the Bunch, or Influential Rather Than Impressive?

The production's critical record divides into a founding acclamation and a retrospective reservation, and the disagreement is genuine enough to adjudicate. On one side stands Barnes in 1969, ranking the staging first among a distinguished field in the pages of the New York Times, and behind him the whole institutional tradition of the Stuttgart company, for which the ballet is the founding triumph of the modern ensemble, the work that made the miracle. Anna Kisselgoff, reviewing the Joffrey Ballet's staging of the choreography for the same newspaper in March 1985, found the production, in the Joffrey's rendering supervised by the Stuttgart choreologist, lavish and formally fascinating: the formality that a later eye might call old-fashioned registered, for the Times's chief dance critic, as fascination. On the other side stands Greskovic in Dance Magazine in 1998, the most substantive of the sceptics, whose verdict has been quoted throughout this article because it earns its place: given the string of stagings that followed Cranko's efforts, his version tends to look more influential than impressive; its characters are clear but not all fully developed, Paris and Lord Capulet thinly drawn; its duets are frequent but not especially nuanced; and beside it, in the same New York season, Onegin stood as the more mature theatrical production.

The quarrel is really about the unit of measurement. Greskovic measures the ballet as a self-sufficient artwork inspected in revival, and by that measure his particulars largely hold: Paris is a function rather than a person in Stuttgart as in most versions, the partnering vocabulary does run at a single high voltage, and Onegin, made three years later for the same dancers, is the subtler theatre piece. Barnes measured the ballet as an event in a competitive field, the staging that made the score's tragedy legible and moving to a great city's audience against distinguished rivals, and by that measure his superlative holds as well: no other Western production of the score had, by 1969, carried the whole play to that standard of narrative conviction. Kisselgoff's phrase supplies the synthesis that both men circle: formal fascination is what a dramaturgical invention looks like when its novelty has worn off, the stage grammar still working after the surprise has gone. A production can be at once the best of its bunch in its moment and, a generation later, more influential than impressive to a critic measuring step invention, because influence is precisely the process by which invention becomes convention and stops looking impressive. The gallery set, the civic crowd, the legible full-evening arc: each was a discovery in 1962 and a commonplace by 1998, and the critic of 1998 was surrounded by Cranko's discoveries wearing other choreographers' names.

The verdict of this article follows from that analysis. Barnes is right in the way that matters for the history of the art form, and Greskovic is right in the way that matters for a single evening's inspection, and the first rightness is the larger one, because ballets survive as repertory through the qualities Barnes named, narrative conviction carried by structure, long after the qualities Greskovic missed have been supplied by successors working in the founded form. The dance historian's perspective closes the argument. John Percival, whose biography of Cranko underlies the scholarly record of the tours and the company, treated the Stuttgart years as the reconstruction of narrative ballet in the West after its long eclipse by abstraction, and the production at the centre of that reconstruction is this one. A founding work is not required to be its author's most mature work, nor its form's most nuanced example. It is required to found. By that test the Stuttgart Romeo and Juliet is the most consequential ballet of the play made in the West in the twentieth century, and the cliché that credits one London ballet with the dance tradition of the story should be retired in favour of the chronology.

## 1973: The Flight Home

The production's founding decade closed with its maker. Cranko took the company on a third American tour in 1973, wildly successful by the accounts that survive, and full of plans for new ballets. The tour ended with performances at the Academy of Music in Philadelphia. On 26 June 1973, two days after the engagement closed, the company boarded a charter flight for the journey home to Stuttgart; Cranko, who had taken a sleeping pill during the flight, suffered an allergic reaction and choked, and the aircraft made an emergency landing in Dublin, where he was pronounced dead on arrival at hospital. He was forty-five years old. Anna Kisselgoff's obituary in the New York Times recorded the death of the Stuttgart Ballet's director at the height of the company's fame, and the journal literature on his career, following Percival's biography, preserves the detail that he had asked for a doctor to travel with the company on that flight and that the request was not granted.

The sobriety of the record matters more than its drama. A choreographer's early death freezes a repertory: Greskovic's observation, that with Cranko's untimely death in 1973 his ballets became set, states the institutional consequence exactly. The works could no longer be revised by their maker, and the company adopted what he called the letter of the legacy, stagings supervised by the choreologist Georgette Tsinguirides, whose Benesh notation scores of the Cranko repertory preserved the movement text with a fidelity that dancers and stagers across several companies have relied upon since. The policy has its cost, since a living choreographer tinkers and a dead one's text hardens, and Greskovic weighed the cost honestly. It also has its benefit, and the benefit is the production's survival in recognizable form: the ballet that companies stage in the twenty-first century is, in its choreographic substance, the ballet of 1962, not a tradition of approximations drifting from a lost original.

The succession kept the work at the centre of its home. Glen Tetley directed the company from 1974 to 1976; Haydée, the creating Juliet, then directed it from 1976 to 1996, twenty years in which the production was danced under the authority of the woman who had founded its heroine; Reid Anderson, a dancer of the Cranko generation, followed her. Few ballets have been guarded by so continuous a line of witnesses. When the company filmed the production at its home theatre in 2017, the cast list closed the circle in a way no publicist would dare invent: Haydée appeared as the Nurse, the role of fussing devotion that serves the heroine she had created fifty-five years earlier, and Egon Madsen, a founding star of the company, appeared as Friar Laurence. The production's own first generation, still on its stage in the character parts, is the plainest evidence of what kind of institution the ballet built and what kind of memory it keeps.

## The German Context: An Opera-House Ballet Becomes a National Asset

The production's home conditions explain part of its character that touring fame tends to hide. Stuttgart's ballet descends from the court theatre of the dukes of Württemberg, with a documented presence as early as 1609 and a first golden age in the eighteenth century, when the great reformer Jean-Georges Noverre worked at the court from 1759 to 1766. The modern company's reference histories describe the Stuttgart ensemble under Cranko as the first major German ballet company, a distinction with a specific postwar meaning: German theatrical dance had been dominated by expressionist solo forms and opera-house routine, and the spectacle of a German ensemble conquering the international repertory with classical narrative ballet was a cultural event of the Federal Republic's second decade, not merely a theatrical one. The miracle phrase that New York supplied named an artistic fact; at home, the same fact carried the additional weight of a national stagecraft re-entering the European conversation at its highest level.

The opera-house structure shaped the work's economics and its aesthetics alike. A department of the Württemberg State Theatre, sharing an orchestra, workshops and an ensemble contract with the opera, the company could mount full-evening productions as repertory routine rather than as speculative ventures, and could keep them in performance across seasons while a commercial management would have had to choose between touring them and shelving them. The gallery set is partly an opera-house artifact: built once, stored, re-erected, and refined across revivals, it amortized its ingenuity over decades. The ensemble ethic is partly an opera-house artifact too, since a resident company with salaried dancers can spend an evening's crowd scenes on artists who will play principals on other nights, the rotation that makes individualized crowds affordable. Productions are often discussed as if they were authored in a vacuum of pure intention; the Stuttgart staging bears the marks of its institution at every level, and its example taught European houses what the opera-house system, properly led, could do in dance.

The demand for Cranko's method quickly exceeded one company. He directed the Bavarian State Opera's ballet in Munich in parallel with Stuttgart, from 1968 for several seasons, exporting the story-ballet regime to Germany's other great house, and guest producers came to Stuttgart to study the system as much as the steps. The school he founded in December 1971 institutionalized the pipeline, training dancers inside the narrative tradition from adolescence, and the company's later honours, among them the Laurence Olivier Award for Outstanding Achievement in Dance in 1981, registered the permanence of what the 1962 premiere had begun. The production thus sits inside a double history: the history of one ballet's excellence, and the history of a national ballet culture rebuilt around narrative dancing, with the ballet serving the second history as both instrument and emblem. Accounts that treat the Stuttgart staging as an isolated masterpiece miss the emblem's function; accounts that treat it as a mere institutional product miss the masterpiece. The record supports both readings at once, and the tension between them is the German chapter of the production's meaning.

## The Afterlife: A Production That Became Repertory

The stagings beyond Stuttgart map the production's conversion from company property into world repertory. Canada came first, in 1964, with the choreographer himself producing. The Paris Opéra Ballet took the work in 1983, an adoption with a particular charge: the Paris company is the ancestral home of the classical tradition, and its acceptance of a German narrative ballet into its repertory certified the Stuttgart manner at the tradition's most guarded address. The Joffrey Ballet's production in the 1984 to 1985 season made the Joffrey the first American company to stage the choreography on its own account, distinct from the Stuttgart company's touring performances; Kisselgoff's review of March 1985, quoted earlier, treated the result as lavish and formally fascinating, and the staging, supervised by Tsinguirides, demonstrated the notation system's power to transplant the text entire. The Australian Ballet has danced the production across decades; when the company revived it after twenty years away, opening in Sydney at the start of December 2022, Ashley Killar, who had danced in the Stuttgart company as a young recruit from the Royal Ballet School, marked the arithmetic of the occasion: the revival's second day was the sixtieth anniversary of the world premiere, and the ballet looked fresh as paint. Boston Ballet's performances at the Boston Opera House in 2011 carried the staging to another American house, introduced by the company's account of the choreographer that American presenters had been repeating since 1969.

The home performances bind the list together. Stuttgart revived the production across the directorships that followed its maker, danced it at the Lincoln Center Festival in 1998 in the season Greskovic reviewed, returned it to its stage in stagings supervised from the notation, and filmed it in 2017 in Rose's designs reworked from the originals. A production that remains a living entry in its founding company's repertory for more than six decades, while also serving as an acquisition for companies on four continents, has passed the only survival test that repertory history recognizes: successive generations of dancers, most of them born after its choreographer's death, keep finding their own performances inside its text rather than curating a monument.

Researchers assembling the production's dossier across its many homes work through an unusually rich paper trail: the notation scores in the Benesh tradition, the filmed record of 2017, the prompt materials of the acquiring companies, and the press files of five decades of revivals. Students who gather those dossiers into a single presentable deck for a seminar on the ballet's transmission will find the documents easier to compare laid out side by side, the purpose served by the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) when the slides of casts, dates and designs must be read against one another. The dossier habit suits this ballet in particular, because its history is a history of documented transmissions, each staging traceable to a stager, a score of notation and a date, in a way that purely traditional repertory rarely permits.

The production's position in the longer performance history of the play's music is a study in itself: [music in the performance history of the play](/2028/02/25/music-in-performance-history/) traces how the tragedy has been scored and re-scored across the centuries of its stage life, and Cranko's ballet is the point in that history where a single score, Prokofiev's, became the settled musical text of the danced play. Later dance versions define themselves against the Stuttgart settlement as MacMillan did, whether by deepening it, stripping it or abandoning narrative altogether; [Matthew Bourne's dance version](/2028/12/15/matthew-bourne-dance-version/), the modern dance-theatre successor that relocates the story to an institution for young offenders, is unintelligible except as a deliberate unmaking of the full-length narrative ballet that Cranko founded, since an unmaking requires a made thing to work upon. The founding production thus governs even its negations, which is the definition of a tradition's source.

## Three Problems Every Version Must Solve

The production's founding status becomes clearest when the ballet is set against its rivals at the level of shared problems, since every choreographer of the score confronts the same three. The first is the balcony: a scene of whispered negotiation across a height, which Lavrovsky staged within his production's monumental frame, MacMillan with an emphasis on the lovers' youthful awkwardness and ardour within a naturalistic Verona, and Cranko by the entwining contact that the Stuttgart gallery makes possible. The second problem is the crowd: how much of Verona a version wants. The Soviet answer was mass and ideology, the crowd as the feudal order's weight and the young lovers' opposing energy, in the translation-studies literature's summary of Lavrovsky's conception. The Stuttgart answer was the individuated citizenry this article has described. The London answer subordinated the crowd to the principals' psychology. The third problem is the ending: whether the ballet closes in the tomb's private catastrophe or expands into the public reconciliation with which the Soviet tradition, following socialist realism's demand for a legible social moral, preferred to finish.

On each problem the Western tradition's settled practice descends from Stuttgart's answers rather than from Moscow's or, on the crowd and the ending, from London's. The balcony as continuous contact across levels became the comparison standard for the scene. The individuated crowd became the default Verona of the international repertory, the version of the city that acquiring companies, whatever their choreographer, expect a production to supply. The tomb ending, catastrophe without pageant, became the Western norm so completely that its Stuttgart origin is forgotten, which is the usual fate of successful foundations. Rudolf Nureyev's version of 1977, made for London Festival Ballet with Patricia Ruanne and later reworked for Paris, positioned itself as a young man's darker reading against both predecessors; the dance-theatre unmaking of the story two generations later positioned itself against the whole narrative settlement. A tradition is measurable by the works that must define themselves against it, and by that measure the Stuttgart production's governance of the field extends well past the companies that perform its steps.

The comparison also clarifies what the production is not. It is not the most virtuosic account of the score: MacMillan wrote harder and more graded dancing for the principals, and Nureyev wrote his own formidable equipment into his Romeo. It is not the most monumental: Lavrovsky's conception exceeds it in scale by design. It is not the most psychologically probing: the London ballet's interiority stands ahead of it, and the scholarly contrast between the two potion scenes, Lavrovsky's dream ballet of the unreal, MacMillan's external behaviour, maps a spectrum on which Stuttgart's dramatic clarity occupies the legible centre. The production's supremacy is of a different kind, the supremacy of the workable synthesis: the version that first assembled crowd, character, duet and architecture into a whole that companies of ordinary means could perform and audiences without preparation could follow. In the history of an art form, the workable synthesis is the rarest achievement, rarer than the extreme statement, because it must satisfy the theatre's every constituency at once. Stuttgart's did, and the field rearranged itself around the fact.

## The Verdict on Cranko's Ballet

The claims can be totalled. Cranko's Romeo and Juliet was the first Western production to prove that Prokofiev's score could carry Shakespeare's tragedy entire before a general audience; it invented, under Venetian constraint, the fixed architectural set that became a convention of the genre; it replaced the monumental crowd of the Soviet model with a civic society of individuals, and the replacement became the Western norm; it gave the tragedy's secondary grief, a mother's mourning over Tybalt, choreographic centrality, and embodied Rosaline so that the hero's conversion could be witnessed rather than reported; it conquered New York at its American premiere under the strongest comparative verdict a critic of the first rank delivered in that era; it prompted, by direct documented transmission through a guesting dancer, the London production that popular memory mistakes for the origin; and it has survived, in notation, on film and in the repertory of companies on four continents, for more than six decades. Against those claims stand the reservations that this article has weighed rather than waved away: a partnering vocabulary of sustained high voltage, two secondary roles drawn thinly, and the existence, in the same choreographer's catalogue, of a more mature theatre piece in Onegin.

The reservations describe the limits of a founding work; the claims describe the founding. Ballet history's cliché, the one that credits one London ballet with the modern dance tradition of this play, survives because the London production is the one the English-speaking world films, tours and teaches. The chronology, the cast lists, the critic of 1969 and the successor's own documented resolve all point the same way, and an honest history follows them: the tradition begins in Stuttgart on 2 December 1962, with Haydée's Juliet and Barra's Romeo inside Rose's gallery, in a ballet that taught an art form how a city dances a tragedy. Shakespeare's Prince closes the play with the judgement that never was a story of more woe told than this one (5.3). Cranko's production is the staging that first taught the Western ballet stage to tell that story at its full length and full weight, and its founder's title, the production that shaped the modern dance tradition, is not publicity. It is the record. The companies that acquire the ballet, the dancers who grow up inside it and the audiences who meet the play first through its dancing are still working, whether they know the name or not, in the house that Stuttgart built.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When did John Cranko's Romeo and Juliet premiere in Stuttgart?

The Stuttgart production premiered on 2 December 1962, in the second year of Cranko's directorship of the company. The date is the one carried by the Stuttgart Ballet's own production records and by the biographies issued by the state theatres of Stuttgart, Munich and Prague, all of which name the premiere as the breakthrough that began the company's famous era. The staging rebuilt, for a permanent theatre and a settled company, the version Cranko had first made in 1958 for La Scala and performed at the Teatro Verde in Venice. Designs were by Jürgen Rose, who retained the fixed double-storey gallery conception of the Venetian original while clothing it for an indoor stage and a full evening's repertory life.

### Q: Who created the roles of Romeo and Juliet in the Stuttgart production?

Marcia Haydée created Juliet and Ray Barra created Romeo at the premiere of 2 December 1962. The pairing is stated in the production's cast record and confirmed in the scholarly literature on Cranko's creative method, which draws on John Percival's biography and on Barra's own testimony: Barra danced the title roles in the premieres of both this ballet and Onegin, with Haydée as his partner on both occasions. Reference summaries that name Richard Cragun as the creating Romeo compress the record; Cragun became the role's defining interpreter in performance and danced it at the production's American premiere in 1969, but the founding night belonged to Barra, an American dancer whose partnership with Haydée stood at the centre of the young company's identity.

### Q: What was Cranko's first Romeo and Juliet, and where was it performed?

The first version was made in 1958 for the ballet company of La Scala, Milan, and performed at the Teatro Verde, the open-air theatre on the island of San Giorgio Maggiore in Venice where the Milan company appeared in its summer season. Carla Fracci, at the outset of her great career, created Juliet. The version is sometimes loosely described as a Venice or La Fenice production; the documented record assigns it to La Scala, staged in the Venetian amphitheatre, and the distinction matters because the open-air circumstances of that stage, with few facilities for scene changes, forced the production's most famous invention, the fixed transforming set. The scholarly surveys of the Prokofiev score's choreographic history date Cranko's line from this 1958 production.

### Q: Who designed the Stuttgart production, and what is the fixed gallery set?

Jürgen Rose designed the sets and costumes for the Stuttgart production, beginning a partnership with Cranko that extended to Onegin and The Taming of the Shrew. The fixed gallery is a permanent double-storey structure, inherited from the Venetian first version, that can be re-dressed in view of the audience to read successively as town square, Capulet hall, street, bedchamber with balcony, cell and tomb. Ashley Killar's history of the choreographer records that Cranko devised the solution for the Teatro Verde's open-air constraints and retained it with Rose at Stuttgart, and that the device has since been copied in countless other versions of the ballet. Its dramaturgical point is speed: the story's seven locations flow through one architecture, so the tragedy never pauses for scenery.

### Q: When did the Stuttgart Ballet first dance the ballet in America, and who led the cast?

The American premiere took place in 1969 at the Metropolitan Opera House in New York, during the Stuttgart Ballet's first tour of the United States. Marcia Haydée, the creating Juliet, danced the role, and Richard Cragun, by then the production's defining Romeo, partnered her. The season became the company's American legend: the institutional histories describe a triumphant reception, and the phrase the Stuttgart Ballet Miracle attached itself to the young ensemble in the American press. The distinction between the two Romeos of the production's history is preserved in its cast records: Barra at the creation in Stuttgart in 1962, Cragun at the conquest in New York seven years later, each name bound to a different order of claim.

### Q: How did the production change the Stuttgart Ballet as an institution?

The production converted a provincial opera-house ballet into an international company with a defining identity. Before December 1962 the Stuttgart ensemble had no standing beyond its region; the premiere's success gave Cranko's rebuilding its public proof, and the triumphs that followed, the Canadian staging of 1964 and the New York season of 1969, made narrative ballet the company's signature before the world. The institution built itself around the achievement: Cranko founded the company's school in December 1971, the John Cranko School that still trains its dancers, and the repertory he created, with this ballet at its head, remained the core that his successors, Tetley, Haydée and Anderson, inherited and guarded. The Laurence Olivier Award for Outstanding Achievement in Dance that the company received in 1981 crowned a transformation the 1962 premiere had started.

### Q: How did seeing Cranko's ballet affect Kenneth MacMillan?

MacMillan saw the Stuttgart production in 1964, danced by Haydée and by Lynn Seymour as a guest with the company, and resolved to create his own version. The Playbill history of the MacMillan ballet states the sequence directly; the DanceTabs history adds that he reckoned he could do even better, and that Seymour, on whom he would build his Juliet, had caught his eye in connection with Cranko's production. The Royal Ballet commission for his first three-act narrative ballet followed, and the London production premiered in 1965. MacMillan then pursued a darker naturalism in deliberate contrast to Stuttgart's stylised civic manner, so the influence is documented as emulation and rivalry together: Cranko supplied the proof of the form, and MacMillan supplied the ambition to surpass its founding example.

### Q: Which companies outside Stuttgart have staged Cranko's production?

The first foreign staging was the National Ballet of Canada's in 1964, produced by Cranko himself; the Canadian company's history notes that it remains the only North American company for which he personally mounted the work. The Paris Opéra Ballet adopted the production in 1983. The Joffrey Ballet's staging in the 1984 to 1985 season made it the first American company to produce the choreography on its own account, under the supervision of the Stuttgart choreologist Georgette Tsinguirides. The Australian Ballet has danced the work across decades and revived it in Sydney in December 2022 after twenty years away, and Boston Ballet presented it at the Boston Opera House in 2011. The home company in Stuttgart has kept the ballet in repertory throughout.

### Q: Who preserves the choreography of Cranko's ballets, and how?

Georgette Tsinguirides, the Stuttgart Ballet's choreologist, recorded the Cranko repertory in Benesh Movement Notation and has supervised stagings of the works for successive generations of dancers and for outside companies. Her role became decisive after the choreographer's death in 1973 froze the repertory: Robert Greskovic, reviewing the company's New York performances in 1998, described the stagings under her care as whistleclean in detail and named the policy behind them the letter of the legacy. The notation scores function for this ballet the way a critical edition functions for a text, fixing the movement against the drift of memory and making faithful transmission to companies that never knew the choreographer a practical possibility rather than an act of reconstruction.

### Q: How did John Cranko die?

Cranko died on 26 June 1973, aged forty-five, on the charter flight carrying the Stuttgart Ballet home from its American tour. The tour had closed with performances at the Academy of Music in Philadelphia two days earlier. During the flight he suffered an allergic reaction to a sleeping pill and choked; the aircraft made an emergency landing in Dublin, where he was pronounced dead on arrival at hospital. Anna Kisselgoff's obituary in the New York Times recorded the loss, and the scholarly literature, following John Percival's biography, preserves the detail that Cranko had asked for a doctor to travel on the flight and that the request had not been granted. The company continued its tour-hardened repertory under successors drawn from his own circle.

### Q: What makes Cranko's crowd scenes distinctive?

The crowd scenes are built from individuals rather than from a decorative mass. Ismene Brown's survey of the play in ballet summarizes Cranko's approach as a bustling society background against which the major figures stand out: market traders, carnival dancers, servants on errands, gypsy figures and citizens with fragments of business and grievance fill the square, so that Verona reads as a working city whose feuds climb from servants to princes exactly as the play's first scene arranges them. The method contrasts with the Soviet model Cranko had seen in London, where Lavrovsky's crowds served a monumental pageant of opposed classes. Stuttgart's civic density became the Western norm for the ballet, the standard against which later versions, MacMillan's included, composed their public scenes.

### Q: Why does Lady Capulet's mourning matter in Cranko's production?

Cranko gave Lady Capulet's grief over the body of Tybalt the weight of a full choreographic scene, taking up, in Ismene Brown's account, one of the iconic inventions of Lavrovsky's Bolshoi production, which he had seen in London in 1956. The scene restores the feud's emotional engine to the older generation: the mother's sorrow, curdling toward the demand for vengeance that the play gives her in the third act, explains the hatred that will kill her daughter more completely than any villainy could. The production's own history deepened the passage when Marcia Haydée, the creating Juliet, danced Lady Capulet in the company's later performances; Greskovic recorded that she made a star turn of the role's mime and mad-scene dramatics in the 1998 New York season.

### Q: What did later critics find to criticise in the production?

The most substantive reservation is Robert Greskovic's, entered in Dance Magazine after the Stuttgart company's New York performances of July 1998. He judged that the version tends, among the many stagings that followed it, to look more influential than impressive; that its characters, though clear, are not all fully developed, with Paris and Lord Capulet thinly drawn; and that its frequent pas de deux are not especially nuanced, the gymnastic lifts recurring without enough gradation between formality, intimacy and tenderness. He also ranked Onegin as the more mature theatrical production of the two Cranko ballets in that season. The criticisms are precise, largely just on their own terms, and addressed in this article's adjudication: they measure step invention where the production's historical claim rests on dramaturgy.

### Q: How many acts does Cranko's Romeo and Juliet have, and how does it end?

The ballet is in three acts, following the architecture of Prokofiev's score and the play's own movement from public Verona through the marriage to the tomb. Greskovic's 1998 account names the three-act span directly in describing the production's unfolding within Jürgen Rose's designs. The ending follows the score and the tragedy: the action contracts from the populated city of the first act to the vault of the last, where the lovers' deaths close the design that the crowd scenes opened. Accounts of the staging note the absence of an extended reconciliation pageant of the Soviet kind; the production ends in the tomb's stillness, with the feud's cost measured by the emptiness of the world the first act built, rather than by a ceremonial tableau of peace.

### Q: Is Cranko's Romeo and Juliet still performed?

The production has remained in the repertory of the Stuttgart Ballet across the six decades since its premiere, through the directorships of Glen Tetley, Marcia Haydée, Reid Anderson and their successor, and it continues to be staged by companies that acquire it under the supervision of the Cranko tradition's custodians. The Australian Ballet's revival in Sydney in December 2022, mounted after twenty years away from the work, fell on the sixtieth anniversary of the premiere, and Ashley Killar's account of that revival records a staging that looked fresh as paint. A performance filmed at the Stuttgart State Theatre in 2017, with Elisa Badenes and David Moore in the title roles, fixed the production's modern form on record for houses and students far from Stuttgart.

### Q: Why did Marcia Haydée matter so much to this production?

Haydée stands at three points of the production's life. She created Juliet in 1962, forming the role with Cranko in the studio as his prime muse, and she danced the American premiere in 1969. She then directed the Stuttgart Ballet from 1976 to 1996, two decades in which the production was guarded by the authority of its own creating ballerina. Finally, in the performance filmed at Stuttgart in 2017, she appeared as the Nurse, the devoted character role in service of a Juliet she had originated fifty-five years earlier, with Egon Madsen, another founding artist of the company, as Friar Laurence. No comparable figure binds a production's creation, conquest, custody and commemoration into a single career in quite the same way.

### Q: How does the Joffrey Ballet figure in the production's history?

The Joffrey Ballet presented the first American company production of Cranko's choreography in its 1984 to 1985 season, with performances in New York at the New York State Theater and in Washington at the Kennedy Center, distinct from the Stuttgart company's own touring appearances of 1969 and later years. The staging was supervised by Georgette Tsinguirides, the Stuttgart choreologist, working from the Benesh notation record. Anna Kisselgoff, reviewing the production for the New York Times in March 1985, found the Joffrey's rendering lavish and formally fascinating, a verdict that certified the choreography's power to survive transplantation from its founding company to an American ensemble with no direct memory of its maker.

### Q: Did Cranko invent the story ballet, or revive it?

Cranko revived and refounded the form rather than inventing it. Full-length narrative ballet had a nineteenth-century pedigree and a Soviet continuation in works such as Lavrovsky's staging of this very play, but in the West of the 1950s the prestige forms were abstraction and the one-act mixed bill, and Cranko's own London reputation rested on witty shorter pieces such as Pineapple Poll and The Lady and the Fool. What Stuttgart demonstrated from 1962 onward, with this ballet first and Onegin and The Taming of the Shrew after it, was that a Western company could build its identity on full-evening literary narrative danced by an ensemble without guest stars. John Percival's biography treats those years as the reconstruction of narrative ballet in the West, and later choreographers, MacMillan at their head, built inside the reconstructed form.

### Q: What should a first-time viewer watch for in Cranko's production?

The architecture is the first guide: Rose's gallery serves every location, and watching one structure become square, hall, chamber and tomb teaches the production's thesis that the lovers die inside the house that raised them. The crowds repay attention next, since the market and carnival scenes plant the individuals whose brawling, wedding service and mourning carry the tragedy's public mechanism. Among the characters, Lady Capulet's mourning over Tybalt and the Nurse's bustle mark the production's two poles of secondary invention, grief and comedy given full choreographic paragraphs. The balcony duet then shows the private counterweight, two bodies completing each other's phrases as the play's lovers complete a shared sonnet, before the tomb gathers every thread into stillness.

### Q: How does Cranko's ballet differ from MacMillan's, in one comparison?

The difference is one of emphasis founded on chronology. Cranko's Stuttgart production of 1962 individualizes the city: its realism is social, carried by crowds, servants and secondary grief, and its duets are built for legibility, large statements of courtship, rapture and parting that a whole theatre can read. MacMillan's London production of 1965, made after he had seen Stuttgart's and resolved to surpass it, individualizes the lovers' interior lives: its realism is psychological, its Verona darker and less ceremonious, its partnering graded by states of knowledge between the pair. Critics who prize nuance of duet writing rank London higher; the historical record ranks Stuttgart first in the only sense that cannot be revised, because the London ballet was conceived in answer to it.
