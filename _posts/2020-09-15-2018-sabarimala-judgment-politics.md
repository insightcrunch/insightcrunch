The 2018 Sabarimala judgment, delivered by a five judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court of India on 28 September 2018, did two things at once, and the politics of the season that followed cannot be understood unless both are kept in view. As a ruling, Indian Young Lawyers Association v State of Kerala struck down Rule 3(b) of the Kerala Hindu Places of Public Worship (Authorisation of Entry) Rules 1965 and held, by four votes to one, that women of every age could enter the hill shrine of Lord Ayyappa at Sabarimala in Pathanamthitta district. As a political event, the same ruling rearranged Kerala's parties, filled its roads with prayer processions and counter processions, produced a wall of women six hundred and twenty kilometres long, decided arguments inside the 2019 general election, and ended its first phase with a nine judge bench reference that carried the questions far beyond one temple. The doctrinal reading of the verdict, its testing of the architecture between Article 25 and Article 26, belongs to the companion treatment of [the religious-secular architectural-tension framework](/2020/05/28/sabarimala-religious-secular-adjudication/). The subject here is the judgment's politics: what each actor said, did, organised, won and lost between the morning of 28 September 2018 and the reference order of 14 November 2019, and what that record shows about how a constitutional ruling becomes a political season.

## What the Bench Held on 28 September 2018, and How Each Camp Read It

The bench that decided the case consisted of Chief Justice Dipak Misra, Justice R. F. Nariman, Justice A. M. Khanwilkar, Justice D. Y. Chandrachud and Justice Indu Malhotra, the only woman on the bench and its only dissenter. The petition had been filed in 2006 by the Indian Young Lawyers Association, a body of lawyers none of whom professed the Ayyappa faith, and it challenged the exclusion of women between the ages of ten and fifty from the shrine. The exclusion rested on Rule 3(b) of the 1965 Kerala rules, which barred entry to women at such times as they were not by custom and usage allowed to enter, and on the belief, stated by the temple's custodians, that the deity at Sabarimala is a naishtika brahmachari, an eternal celibate, whose penance the presence of women of menstruating age would disturb. A Division Bench of the Kerala High Court had upheld the restriction in 1991, in litigation brought by a devotee named S. Mahendran, and that settlement had governed the shrine for a generation before the Supreme Court took the question up.

The majority's holdings, stated at the level of what was decided rather than how each opinion reasoned, were these. Chief Justice Misra, writing for himself and Justice Khanwilkar, held that the devotees of Lord Ayyappa did not constitute a separate religious denomination within the meaning of Article 26, that the exclusion was not an essential practice of the religion, and that Rule 3(b) could not survive the equality guarantee of Article 14 or the anti-untouchability command of Article 17 read with the temple entry promise of Article 25(2)(b). Justice Nariman, concurring, held that the exclusion failed the essential practice test on the temple's own materials and that constitutional morality, not social morality, governed. Justice Chandrachud, concurring, held that the exclusion stamped women with a badge of inferiority rooted in notions of purity and pollution, and that constitutional morality supplied the standard against which religious practice had to be measured. Justice Malhotra, dissenting, held that the petitioners lacked standing to press the grievance of a faith they did not profess, that the practice was essential to the denomination that observed it, and that constitutional morality could not be wielded by courts to reform the internal arrangements of a religious denomination. The operative result was a four to one verdict opening the shrine.

The political season that followed ran on compressed versions of these holdings, and the compressions matter as much as the opinions. Devotee organisations carried the dissent into the streets as the judgment's conscience, quoting Justice Malhotra's warning that courts should not assume the role of reformers of faith. The Kerala government carried the majority's operative direction into its administrative orders, treating implementation as a constitutional duty that admitted no discretion. The Congress and the Bharatiya Janata Party in Kerala each selected the strand of the case that fitted a position they could defend before devotees. To keep the compressions visible against the text they compressed, the article names the artifact it uses throughout as the Three-Component Constitutional-Doctrinal Architecture Test Map, a table that sets the three doctrinal components the majority engaged, the dissent that answered them, and the reference that reopened them, each beside the political use the season made of it.

The map is the article's analytical spine, and it carries the article's namable claim. The 2018 Sabarimala verdict tested the constitutional-doctrinal architecture between Articles 25 and 26; the post-2019 nine judge reference tests the architecture beyond Sabarimala-specific content. The politics of the season is the record of that test being fought over by actors who rarely argued about doctrine in the abstract. They argued about a road at Nilakkal, a queue at Pamba, a wall of women on a January afternoon, a parliamentary seat in Pathanamthitta, and a closed sanctum on the morning after two women climbed the eighteen sacred steps. Doctrine supplied the vocabulary. The season supplied the stakes.

## The Prehistory: From the High Court's Settlement of 1991 to the Constitution Bench

The season of 2018 was the third act of a drama whose first two acts set its terms. The first act closed in 1991, when a Division Bench of the Kerala High Court, hearing a petition by the devotee S. Mahendran, upheld the exclusion of women between ten and fifty as a usage integral to the shrine, accepted the Devaswom Board's and the thantri's account of the deity's celibate discipline, and confirmed Rule 3(b) of the 1965 rules as the custom's legal dress. That judgment governed for fifteen years without serious challenge, a period in which the shrine's pilgrimage grew into one of the largest annual gatherings in the world and the exclusion was experienced by most devotees as part of the pilgrimage's given order rather than as a rule under argument. The second act opened in 2006 in two registers. In the courts, five young women lawyers associated with the Indian Young Lawyers Association petitioned the Supreme Court against the exclusion, beginning the litigation the 2018 bench would decide. In the shrine's own world, the Kannada actor Jayamala's public claim that she had, as a young woman in 1986, entered the sanctum and touched the deity, and the astrological inquiry that followed at the shrine, convulsed the custodians and fixed the exclusion in the state's public argument a decade before the verdict. The litigation travelled slowly: a three judge bench referred it to a Constitution Bench in October 2017, the five judge bench heard argument across eight days in July and August 2018, and the shape of the eventual split was visible in the hearing itself, in the majority judges' questions on essentiality and equality and in Justice Malhotra's questions on standing and the limits of judicial reform.

The prehistory matters to the politics because it distributed surprise unevenly. The devotee organisations had lived with the litigation for twelve years and had intervened in it, yet the verdict's arrival found their mobilisation unbuilt: the Karma Samithi, the processions and the Nilakkal camp were all constructions of the fortnight after 28 September 2018. The state government, by contrast, had its answer ready on the day, because the Left's ideological formation supplied it: a communist government did not need a committee to tell it what it thought of a court opening a temple. The Congress's discomfort also had a prehistory, in the party's decades of balancing the NSS and other community organisations inside its coalition arithmetic. And the Devaswom Board's divided voice had been prefigured in the litigation itself, where the Board's affidavits across the years had not always sung together. A season that appeared to erupt from a single morning was, in the record, the settlement of accounts that three decades had been preparing, which is one reason its passions ran so quickly to such depth: every actor arrived at Nilakkal carrying an argument older than the verdict.

## The First Receptions: A Palace, a Board, a Secretariat, Two Party Offices

Within hours of the verdict, the institutions that would contest it had stated their opening positions, and the speed of those statements fixed the season's geometry. The Pandalam royal family, whose palace is tied by tradition to the Ayyappa legend and whose members had intervened in the case, announced its dismay and its intention to support a review. The Nair Service Society, the community organisation founded by Mannathu Padmanabhan in 1914 and headquartered at Perunna in Changanassery, declared through its general secretary G. Sukumaran Nair that the verdict had wounded the faith of devotees and that the society would stand with the believers. The Travancore Devaswom Board, the statutory body that administers the shrine, initially struck a cautious note through its president A. Padmakumar, speaking of the practical difficulties of implementation at a shrine reached by a forest path and thronged by lakhs of pilgrims in season. The temple's chief priest, the thantri Kandararu Rajeevaru, whose office carries ritual authority over the shrine's customs, made clear that the priestly establishment had not been persuaded.

The state government's statement came from Chief Minister Pinarayi Vijayan, who said on the day of the verdict that the government welcomed it and would take the steps needed to implement it, adding that the ruling applied to all temples and all faiths whose entry rules stood on the same footing. That sentence, delivered in Thiruvananthapuram on 28 September 2018, became the pole around which the season turned, because it committed a Left Democratic Front government, in a state where the Congress-led United Democratic Front and the Left had alternated in office for decades, to the active enforcement of an unpopular ruling against the stated sentiment of a large body of Hindu devotees. The political logic of [the post-2018 Sabarimala-question Kerala state-political context](/2020/01/28/kerala-left-congress-alternation/) begins with that commitment and with the alternation pattern it disturbed: Kerala's two fronts had historically competed for the devotee vote by caution, and the verdict forced both to declare themselves.

The Congress party's Kerala leadership moved within days to the opposite pole. The Kerala Pradesh Congress Committee, led by its president Mullappally Ramachandran, and the Leader of the Opposition Ramesh Chennithala stated that the party stood with the devotees, that the sentiments of believers ought to have weighed with the Court, and that the state government should seek review or support legislation to restore the custom. The Bharatiya Janata Party's state unit, led by P. S. Sreedharan Pillai, went further and faster: the party announced protests, its affiliate the Vishva Hindu Parishad and the Hindu Aikya Vedi began mobilising, and the Sabarimala Karma Samithi, a coalition of devotee and Hindu organisations, emerged as the umbrella under which the street campaign would be run. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, whose affiliates supplied much of the mobilisation's organisation, stated through its leadership that the verdict had been delivered without regard to the traditions and sentiments of the devotees who sustained the shrine. Between the government's sentence and the Karma Samithi's umbrella, the season's two armies had their banners by the first week of October 2018.

## October 2018 at Nilakkal and Pamba: The Shrine Opens Under Blockade

The temple's first opening after the verdict came with the monthly rituals of the Malayalam month of Thulam, when the shrine opened on the evening of 17 October 2018 for five days. The road to Sabarimala runs through Nilakkal, the base camp where pilgrims leave their vehicles, and onward to Pamba, from where the climb begins. In the week before the opening, devotee groups established camps at Nilakkal and at Erumely, the town associated with Ayyappa's ally Vavar, and announced that women between ten and fifty would be stopped by peaceful resistance. The state police, numbering in the thousands across the district for the opening, established their own positions. What followed over five days was recorded in detail by the press corps that descended on Pathanamthitta: vehicles checked by protesting devotees searching for women of the barred ages, women journalists assaulted and their vehicles damaged at Nilakkal on 17 October, baton charges and arrests, and a succession of attempts by individual women to reach the shrine under escort, each turned back short of the sanctum.

The attempts defined the week. Rehana Fathima, an activist from Kochi, climbed toward the shrine on 19 October 2018 under heavy police escort led by Inspector General S. Sreejith, reaching the vicinity of the sanctum before protesting priests and devotees blocked the final approach and the police withdrew her. Mary Sweety, a Malayali journalist who had flown in from Dubai, was stopped on the path on 21 October. Kavitha Jakkala, a television journalist from Hyderabad, turned back under a hail of protest on the same first days. A New York Times reporting team was forced to retreat down the path on 18 October after protesters surrounded it. No woman of the barred ages entered the shrine during the October opening, and the Karma Samithi counted the week a victory of presence: the verdict stood on paper, the custom stood on the hill.

### What did the Sabarimala Karma Samithi demand in October 2018?

The Sabarimala Karma Samithi, the umbrella of devotee organisations, demanded in October 2018 that the Kerala government file a review petition against the verdict, that it promulgate an ordinance restoring the customary restriction, that police protection not be used to escort women of the barred ages to the shrine, and that the Devaswom Board defend the custom before the Court.

Behind the demands stood an argument the movement stated with discipline across the season. Its spokesmen, among them the Hindu Aikya Vedi leader K. P. Sasikala and the Ayyappa Dharma Sena leader Rahul Easwar, who was arrested at Pamba on 17 October 2018 and again later in the season, held that the restriction at Sabarimala was not discrimination but the observance owed to a deity whose celibate form was the very reason for the shrine's existence, and that a practice unbroken for centuries could not be unmade by a petition brought by non-believers. The movement's chosen methods in October were those of presence and prayer: the namajapa processions, in which thousands of devotees, women prominent among them, walked chanting the deity's name through the towns of southern Kerala, presented the cause as devotion resisting administration. The largest early procession, held in Thiruvananthapuram in the first days of October, drew women devotees in numbers the movement cited whenever opponents described the cause as male obstruction.

The state answered presence with presence and with law. The police registered cases against protesters in the hundreds during the October days, and in the weeks after the opening the government conducted a sweeping round of arrests of men identified from photographs and video of the Nilakkal violence, a drive that the opposition fronts attacked as vindictive and that the government defended as the ordinary enforcement of order. The Bharatiya Janata Party called a statewide hartal on 18 October 2018, which the Karma Samithi supported and the Congress did not oppose in principle, and trading and transport across Kerala stopped for the day. In Nagpur, the RSS chief Mohan Bhagwat used his Vijayadashami address of October 2018 to say that the verdict had ignored the traditions and the sentiments of the devotees, remarks the Sangh's Kerala unit circulated as warrant for the campaign. By the time the shrine closed on 22 October, the pattern of the season was set: the government would hold the legal ground, the movement would hold the road, and the two fronts of Kerala politics would compete for the devotees caught between a judgment they had not sought and a government that would not retreat from it.

The doctrinal core of the quarrel remained visible beneath the roadblocks, and it is worth naming precisely, because the season's rhetoric constantly blurred it. The devotees' claim sounded in Article 26, the denomination's right to manage its own affairs in matters of religion; the entrants' claim sounded in Article 25, the person's freedom to practise and profess, read through [the constitutional rights framework](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/) that the founding generation built with temple entry among its stated concerns. The majority had resolved the competition in favour of the entrant once the practice failed its test of essentiality. The movement's answer, repeated at every procession, was that essentiality was itself the usurpation: a bench of judges, however learned, could not certify which observances a faith could lose and remain itself. That answer is the standing counter-argument to the architecture test reading of the case, and the article returns to it when the reference order of November 2019 gave it an institutional hearing.

## The Machinery of the Season: Procession, Hartal, and the Arrest Ledger

A political season is also a contest of methods, and the Sabarimala season developed a repertoire whose elements recurred from October to January with the regularity of liturgy. The movement's repertoire had three instruments. The first was the procession, above all the namajapa marches in which devotees walked public roads chanting the deity's name, an instrument chosen because it fused worship with demonstration and made police action against it read, in the movement's telling and in the press photographs, as action against prayer. The second was the blockade of place: the camp at Nilakkal, the pickets at Pamba, the human chains at the shrine's approaches, by which the movement converted geography into veto. The third was the hartal, the general shutdown, deployed at the state level on 18 October 2018 and 3 January 2019 and at district level on other days, which announced that the cause could still Kerala's commerce at a day's notice. Each instrument was defended by its organisers as non-violent in design, and each generated its penumbra of violence in practice: the checked vehicles and assaulted journalists of Nilakkal belonged to the blockade, the burned buses and attacked offices of the hartal days to the shutdown, and the movement's leadership disowned those episodes as the work of infiltrators and the uncontrolled, a disowning the government refused to accept.

The state's repertoire also had three instruments. The first was deployment: police in the thousands, concentrated at the shrine and along the route, with prohibitory orders under section 144 at Nilakkal and, in the Mandala season, a regulated flow of pilgrims that the opposition described as the conversion of a pilgrimage into a cantonment. The second was the case register: first information reports in the hundreds arising from the October days alone, and a photographic identification drive after the October opening in which the police arrested devotees across the state in numbers that ran, on the department's own district figures, into the thousands within the fortnight. The third was the counter-spectacle, the Women's Wall, treated in its own section below. The case register became a political document in itself. The Congress and the BJP published counts of the arrested and the charged as evidence of persecution; the government published the same counts as evidence of enforcement; the Kerala High Court, seized of petitions on the season's policing, pressed the state on the arrests' basis and the restrictions' proportionality; and individual cases, of men arrested from their homes weeks after the October days on the strength of photographs, ran in the press as parables of the state's vindictiveness or of the movement's accountability, according to the reader's camp.

Between the repertoires stood the district administration of Pathanamthitta and the police leadership, whose operational decisions, the timing of escorts, the choice of routes, the withdrawal of Rehana Fathima's party within sight of the sanctum, decided outcomes that manifestos then explained. The season's honest history must record that the Indian state's local machinery, ordered to enforce a right against a mobilisation, repeatedly chose the hour and the method that avoided a storming of the sanctum, and that this prudence, defensible as tactics, was read by entrants as bad faith and by devotees as proof that the verdict could not survive contact with the hill. The January entry, when it came, came precisely by abandoning the announced, escorted, daylight method for a pre-dawn movement in plain clothes, a fact the opposition converted into its most effective single charge: that the government itself had confessed, by its method, that its implementation could not bear the public's sight. The charge was unfair to the officers who planned the entry and accurate about the politics that required it, and both qualities kept it alive through the election.

## The State Government's Stated Position: Duty, Renaissance, and the Wall

The Left Democratic Front government of Kerala, led by Chief Minister Pinarayi Vijayan of the Communist Party of India (Marxist), stated its position in a vocabulary it never abandoned across the season, and the vocabulary deserves record because it explains both the government's resilience and its electoral exposure. The first term was duty. The Chief Minister held, in statement after statement from 28 September 2018 onward, that a judgment of the Supreme Court bound the government to implement it, that the alternative was the rule of the street over the rule of the Constitution, and that his government would protect any woman who sought entry as it would protect any citizen exercising a right a court had declared. The second term was history. The government placed the verdict in the lineage of Kerala's renaissance, the social reform tradition running from Sree Narayana Guru and Ayyankali through the temple entry proclamation of 1936, and argued that the state's progressive inheritance obliged it to stand with the ruling; the proclamation of 1936, by which the Maharaja of Travancore opened the temples of his state to all Hindus, was invoked by the Chief Minister as the season's governing precedent. The third term was conspiracy. As the protests grew, the government described the agitation as a deliberate campaign by the RSS and the Bharatiya Janata Party to communalise Kerala and to manufacture the Hindu consolidation that Kerala's politics had historically resisted.

The government's administrative conduct matched the vocabulary. For the Mandala season, the annual pilgrimage that opened on 16 November 2018, the state deployed police in the thousands along the route and at the shrine, imposed restrictions on movement at Nilakkal under section 144 of the Code of Criminal Procedure for a period, and extended the hours and arrangements of the shrine under the Devaswom Board's administration. The restrictions drew criticism that the state had converted a pilgrimage into a police operation, criticism the Congress and the BJP voiced together, and the Kerala High Court, hearing petitions about the arrangements, pressed the government on the proportionality of its measures. The government also sought the political middle ground it publicly disdained. On 15 November 2018, on the eve of the Mandala season, the Chief Minister convened an all-party meeting in Thiruvananthapuram to seek a common approach; the Congress and the BJP attended, stated that the government should seek a stay or promulgate an ordinance, heard the government refuse, and left without agreement, after which each side blamed the other for the meeting's failure.

### Where is the Nilakkal base camp, and why did it matter in October 2018?

Nilakkal is the base camp on the road to Sabarimala, where pilgrims leave their vehicles and board buses for Pamba, where the climb to the shrine begins. It mattered in October 2018 because whoever held Nilakkal held the road: protesters camped there to check vehicles for women of the barred ages, and the police made it their position.

The season also tested the government's management of individual pilgrims whose attempts became public dramas. On 26 November 2018, the activist Trupti Desai, who had announced her intention to visit the shrine, landed at Kochi airport and found it blocked by protesters who refused to let her leave the terminal; after hours of standoff and negotiation, she returned to Mumbai without reaching the district, an outcome the government regretted in words and accepted in fact. The Karma Samithi counted Kochi with Nilakkal among its victories of presence. The government's critics, including voices sympathetic to entry, asked whether a state that could deploy thousands of police could not escort one citizen from an airport. The episode fixed the season's central ambiguity about the government: its commitment to the verdict was absolute in statement and intermittent in the street, a combination that satisfied neither the women who sought entry nor the devotees who opposed it, and that the opposition fronts exploited from their opposite directions for the rest of the season.

## The Congress and the BJP in Kerala: Two Oppositions to One Verdict

The verdict confronted Kerala's two opposition formations with the same opening and opposite risks. For the Congress, the risk was its base: the party's Kerala coalition rested in part on Hindu voters, including the Nair electorate the NSS speaks for, and a position against the devotees threatened losses the party could measure. For the BJP, Kerala was the unconquered state, the one major arena where its national rise had repeatedly stalled at a tenth or so of the vote, and the verdict offered the consolidation issue its Kerala unit had never been given. Both parties therefore opposed the implementation, but they opposed it in different registers, and the difference governed what each could win in 2019.

The Congress position, as stated by KPCC president Mullappally Ramachandran and Leader of the Opposition Ramesh Chennithala through the autumn of 2018, had three elements. The party stood with the devotees and their sentiments, which the Court, in the Congress account, ought not to have overridden by a petition from outside the faith. The state government bore responsibility for the disorder, having rushed to implement without preparation or consultation and having answered prayer processions with mass arrests. The remedy lay in review and, if review failed, in legislation, and the Congress promised that a government led by it would pursue the restoration of the custom through lawful means. Congress workers joined the namajapa processions, the party observed protest days against the government's arrests, and Chennithala undertook a fast in solidarity with the devotees' cause. The position was calibrated to hold the party's Hindu base without surrendering its secular self-description, a calibration the BJP attacked as opportunism and the government attacked as surrender to the same forces the Congress claimed nationally to resist.

The national leadership complicated the calibration in public. Rahul Gandhi, then the Congress president, initially welcomed the verdict in October 2018 as a step toward equality, a statement his Kerala unit received with visible dismay. Through the following weeks he restated his view in terms that conceded the ground to his state unit's reading of Kerala opinion.

### What did Rahul Gandhi say about his party's Kerala position on Sabarimala?

Rahul Gandhi said in January 2019 that his personal view favoured women's entry to Sabarimala, but that the women of Kerala and his party's Kerala unit held a different view, which he had come to understand and respect, and that the state unit's position would stand. The Kerala Congress thus campaigned against implementation while its national president recorded his dissent.

The BJP's Kerala campaign ran on hotter fuel. Its state president P. S. Sreedharan Pillai declared the agitation a golden opportunity for the party's growth in the state, a phrase first used in an address to a party youth meeting at Kozhikode in early November 2018, the recording of which reached the press and drew charges that the party had planned the protests as an instrument; Pillai answered that he had meant the opportunity to stand with devotees, and the phrase followed the party through the season. The party's national leadership entered the campaign directly. On 27 October 2018, at Kannur, the BJP's national president Amit Shah declared that his party stood with the devotees like a rock, warned the state government against its arrests of Ayyappa devotees, and said that courts should deliver judgments that can be implemented, a sentence that drew criticism from the legal fraternity for its pressure on the judiciary and that the Kerala BJP circulated as its warrant. The state unit's general secretary K. Surendran became the campaign's most visible field leader, leading marchers toward Nilakkal during the Mandala season.

### Who was K. Surendran, and why was he arrested in November 2018?

K. Surendran was general secretary of the Bharatiya Janata Party's Kerala unit during the protest season and a prominent organiser. Police arrested him at Nilakkal on 17 November 2018 as he attempted to proceed toward the shrine against the restrictions; he was remanded and held for weeks on charges arising from the season's protests before his release in December.

The BJP's campaign machinery extended beyond the party. The Vishva Hindu Parishad, the Hindu Aikya Vedi and the Karma Samithi supplied the processions; the RSS supplied trained volunteers and the argument, stated by its chief in Nagpur and repeated by its Kerala leadership, that the judgment offended the faith's own constitution, the body of practice by which a tradition knows itself. The government's counter-charge, that the Sangh had converted a judgment into a communal mobilisation, and the Sangh's counter-claim, that a government of atheists in office had declared war on a shrine, were the season's two master accusations, and each contained the portion of truth its holder needed. The BJP had genuinely been handed a mass Hindu cause in a state where it had never held one. The government was genuinely attempting to implement a judgment against a mobilisation that included organised cadres. Kerala's voters, watching both, made their own calculation in April 2019, and it was not the calculation either accuser expected.

## The Women's Wall and the Counter-Mobilisations: Kerala Argues in Public

The season's most contested question was who spoke for Kerala's women, and both camps organised to answer it in bodies rather than in statements. The devotee movement's answer had been visible since the first namajapa processions: women devotees marched in their thousands, and the Ready to Wait campaign, a movement begun in 2016 by women devotees of the barred ages themselves, supplied the argument in their own words: that they revered the deity's celibate form, that waiting until the age of fifty to climb the hill was their observance and not their oppression, and that the Court had mistaken a vow for a disability. The campaign's name became a slogan on banners across the district, and its existence complicated every attempt to describe the season as men defending a shrine against women seeking entry.

The counter-mobilisation answered in kind. On 26 December 2018, the Sabarimala Karma Samithi and allied organisations held the Ayyappa Jyothi, an evening of lamps lit along the length of Kerala and beyond, in which devotees, women again prominent, stood with lamps in their hands to declare the state's roads a congregation. Six days later the government answered with the larger spectacle. The Vanitha Mathil, the Women's Wall, assembled on 1 January 2019 along six hundred and twenty kilometres of highway from Kasaragod in the north to Thiruvananthapuram in the south. The wall had been proposed at a meeting the government convened on 1 December 2018 with community and reform organisations, and it was built by the ruling front's machinery together with those bodies; the organisers put participation at five million women, and independent estimates, lower, still counted the gathering in the millions. Its stated purposes, printed on its own literature, were to defend Kerala's renaissance values, to uphold gender equality, and to answer the mobilisation against the verdict. The Chief Minister addressed the wall's southern end and read its lesson plainly: Kerala's women, assembled, had rejected the politics of the protest.

Both gatherings were real, and the honest record holds them together. Lakhs of women lit lamps for the custom in December; lakhs of women stood in a line for the verdict in January. The women who sought entry and the women who opposed it each stated their case in the first person across the season, in interviews, in court affidavits and on the roads, and the season's politics is falsified whenever either body is treated as a crowd hired by men. What divided them was not the fact of devotion but its object: for one body, faithfulness meant guarding the deity's form from the state and the Court; for the other, faithfulness to Kerala's reform inheritance meant finishing the work the temple entry proclamation of 1936 had begun. The government claimed the wall as its vindication. The movement answered that a wall organised by a ruling party's machinery measured the party's strength, not the devotees' mind. Within four months the electorate would give its own ambiguous verdict on both claims, and within forty-eight hours of the wall, the season reached the event it is remembered by.

## Women Inside the Argument: Devotees, Entrants, and the Season's First Persons

No account of the season is honest that treats women as its subject rather than its speakers, because the season's defining feature, visible in the record from the first procession to the last affidavit, is that women argued it. The women who opposed entry argued from inside the observance. The Ready to Wait campaign, begun in 2016 when the litigation's progress first made entry a practical prospect, gathered women of the barred ages who stated, in essays, interviews and printed testimony, that the restriction honoured the deity's form and their own discipline, that they experienced the wait until fifty as an austerity they had chosen in the same spirit as the forty-one day fast the pilgrimage prescribes, and that a court which read their observance as their subordination had not asked them. Anjali George and the campaign's other public faces insisted on a distinction the season's commentary repeatedly collapsed: between women kept from the shrine by men and women keeping faith with a shrine on terms the faith itself set. The namajapa processions put the same argument into the street in bodily form, and the NSS's women, the Kudumbashree members who joined the prayer marches, and the grandmothers photographed at the head of columns in Thrissur and Thiruvananthapuram were the movement's most effective witnesses precisely because they could not be described as its dupes without insulting the electorate both fronts needed.

The women who sought entry argued from inside the Constitution and said so. Bindu Ammini, who entered on 2 January 2019, stated her reasons in terms that joined her activism to her scholarship: the exclusion was, in her account, a purity rule applied to women's bodies, kin to the exclusions her own Dalit ancestors had fought on the same Travancore roads, and the verdict gave her generation the instrument the temple entry proclamation had given an earlier one. Kanakadurga, a civil supplies employee without an activist's history, stated the quieter version: she was a devotee, she wished to see her deity, and the law said she could. The women turned back on the path in October and November, journalists among them, recorded the third position of the season's women, the professional's: they had come to witness, and the assaults on their persons and vehicles at Nilakkal, documented by their own footage, became evidence in the argument about what the movement's discipline was worth. Rehana Fathima's October attempt added a fourth figure, the provocateur as citizen, whose activism the government defended as her right and whose treatment the movement cited as proof that entry had become a staged insult; the state's own ambivalence toward her attempt, escorting her to the sanctum's vicinity and withdrawing her at the last barrier, displayed the government's discomfort with the entrants its duty obliged it to serve.

The season's institutional women divided as the street did. The Kerala Women's Commission spoke for the verdict and the entrants. Women legislators of the ruling front addressed the wall; women legislators of the opposition addressed the processions. The Devaswom Board's deliberations, the priesthood's statements, the parties' committees remained overwhelmingly male in composition while pronouncing on an observance of women's bodies, a fact the wall's organisers named and the movement answered by pointing to its own columns. For the architecture test reading, the division among women carries a specific lesson. The Constitution's religion clauses were argued, in 2018 and 2019, by the very constituency the clauses' competition concerned, and the constituency did not agree. Article 25's worshipper and Article 26's denomination turned out to be, at Sabarimala, two bodies of women with incompatible instructions for the state, and the doctrine's abstraction of the individual and the denomination concealed, until the season exposed it, that both abstractions had faces.

## The Second of January 2019: Entry, Purification, and the Hartal

In the early hours of 2 January 2019, the day after the Women's Wall, two women of the barred ages entered the Sabarimala shrine. Bindu Ammini, a Dalit activist and college teacher from Kozhikode, and Kanakadurga, a government employee from Malappuram district, reached Pamba before dawn in the company of plainclothes police officers, climbed the path, ascended the eighteen sacred steps and offered prayers at the sanctum shortly before four in the morning, then left by a route that avoided the waiting press and protesters. Both women had attempted the climb in December 2018 and been turned back at Pamba after confrontations; the January attempt was planned with the police and executed before the shrine's custodians knew of it. When the news spread, the thantri closed the sanctum for a purification ritual, the rite the priestly establishment prescribes for a breach of the shrine's observances, and reopened it after an hour. The state government confirmed the entry and counted it the verdict's implementation at last. The Karma Samithi and the BJP called the entry a betrayal carried out in darkness by a government afraid of daylight, and the phrase day of betrayal ran through the protests that followed.

The two days after the entry were the season's most violent. Protesters clashed with police and with ruling front workers in towns across Kerala; stones were thrown, party offices attacked, buses damaged. At Pandalam, the town of the Ayyappa legend's royal household, a Sabarimala Karma Samithi worker named Chandran Unnithan was injured in stone throwing on 2 January and died of his injuries; the BJP laid his death at the government's door, the government attributed the violence to the hartal's organisers, and the case entered the season's ledger of contested facts where each side's account hardened. The BJP and the Hindu organisations called a statewide hartal for 3 January 2019, which shut Kerala's commerce for the day and brought fresh confrontations; the Congress kept its distance from the hartal's violence while joining the condemnation of the entry's manner. The police made arrests in the thousands across the days of disorder, on figures the department itself published, and registered cases against protesters in numbers that the opposition described as a campaign of intimidation and the government described as the bookkeeping of a riot. Journalists were attacked again in the January days, among them women reporters covering the hartal, and the press clubs of the state recorded their protest at a season in which the camera had become a target for both the street and, on occasion, the police.

### What happened at Sabarimala on the Makaravilakku day of 14 January 2019?

On 14 January 2019, the Makaravilakku festival, the shrine's most attended day, Sabarimala received its lakhs of pilgrims under the heaviest police deployment of the season. Further attempts by women of the barred ages were turned back on the path, and the day passed without an entry and without the disorder that had followed the entry of 2 January.

The Mandala Makaram season closed on 20 January 2019. In its final weeks the attempts continued at intervals and were stopped; the two women of 2 January remained, at the season's end, the only confirmed entrants of the barred ages, and they lived under police protection, Kanakadurga returning to a home whose hostility the press documented when she was injured in a domestic confrontation on her return. The argument then moved from the road to the courtroom, where the review petitions filed in the autumn awaited the hearing the Supreme Court had promised in open court. That hearing, held in February 2019, produced the season's last surprise of position: the Travancore Devaswom Board, which administers the shrine and whose statements through the autumn had stressed the difficulty and the delicacy of implementation, told the Court through its counsel that it supported the verdict of September 2018 and the entry of women. The movement read the statement as capture: a board appointed under a Left government had spoken the government's line. The government read it as the shrine's own custodian accepting the law. The thantri and the Pandalam family, who had filed and supported review petitions, recorded their dissent from the Board's turn. The Board's February statement is a small fact with a large moral for the season: in a contest over who held the shrine, every institution with a voice was pressed to spend it, and the spending changed the institution as much as the argument.

## The Custodians: Board, Thantri, and Palace Under the Season's Pressure

Between the Court and the crowd stood the shrine's custodians, and the season pressed each of them into a political role their offices had not been designed for. The Travancore Devaswom Board, constituted under the Travancore-Cochin Hindu Religious Institutions Act of 1950 and administering over a thousand temples including Sabarimala, is the shrine's legal manager, and its presidency, held through the season by A. Padmakumar, a leader of the ruling Communist Party of India (Marxist), made its every statement a document in the contest between the government's implementation and the movement's resistance. The Board's autumn statements stressed logistics: water, queues, the forest path, the lakhs of the Mandala season. Its conduct satisfied neither camp, and its February 2019 statement to the Supreme Court, supporting the verdict in the review hearing, settled its alignment in the movement's account for good. The movement thereafter treated the Board as an arm of the government and demanded the shrine's liberation from its administration, a demand with a long pedigree in Kerala temple politics that the season revived and the BJP adopted; the government treated the Board's statement as the custodian's acceptance of the law, and noted that the Board's composition reflected the statute every government had worked under.

The thantri, the hereditary chief priest of the shrine drawn from the Thazhamon family, held the opposite instrument: no administration, but ritual authority, including the authority the priesthood claims to declare and repair breaches of the shrine's observance. It was the thantri Kandararu Rajeevaru who closed the sanctum for purification after the entry of 2 January 2019, an act the government criticised and contemplated proceeding against, and which the movement celebrated as the shrine's own immune response to the state. The thantri was also a petitioner in review, giving the case its reference caption, and his dual position, defendant of the custom in court and celebrant of its restoration on the ground, made him the season's clearest example of an officeholder whose ritual acts were indistinguishable from political ones. The Pandalam royal family, custodians of the deity's ornaments and of the legend's geography, played the season's third custodial role: they supported the review, lent the movement the palace's prestige, allowed the Thiruvabharanam procession, the annual carrying of the deity's jewels from Pandalam to the shrine, to proceed under the season's tensions, and received Chandran Unnithan's death in their town as a charge on the government's conscience.

The custodians' triangle explains a feature of the season that purely party accounts miss. The devotee movement could claim, with documentary support, that the shrine's own constituted authorities were divided against the verdict's implementation: the priesthood and the palace against, the Board for, and the Board itself appointed under the government that implemented. The entrants could answer, also with documentary support, that custody is not ownership, that the Constitution's promises run against custodians as against kings, and that the temple entry proclamation of 1936 had been issued over the custodians of its day. Both claims are part of the record, and the reference of November 2019 took the argument back to the one body both camps acknowledged as entitled to settle it, which is why the custodians' quarrel subsided, after November 2019, into a watch on the Court.

## The National Parties and the Centre: Amplification Without Legislation

The season was a Kerala event with national amplifiers, and the pattern of amplification is itself evidence. The Bharatiya Janata Party's national leadership treated the agitation as a front in the party's long project of entering Kerala, and its investments were personal: the national president's Kannur appearance of 27 October 2018, the Prime Minister's interventions, and a stream of national spokesmen on the television debates. Prime Minister Narendra Modi stated his government's disposition in January 2019, in remarks carried across the country, drawing a distinction between the Sabarimala observance, which he described as a matter of tradition held by the devotees themselves, women among them, and practices his government had legislated against as discrimination; addressing a rally in Kerala that month he charged the Left government with attacking the state's culture and the Congress with speaking in two voices. The RSS's national leadership, through the Vijayadashami address and the statements of its senior functionaries, supplied the agitation's ideological frame at the all-India level, presenting Sabarimala as the test case of whether the Indian state would govern faith by consent or by judgment.

The Union government, distinct from the ruling party, did the season's most consequential nothing. It filed no review, sought no legislation, and took no position in the review proceedings that committed it, although demands for central action came from the agitation's own side and although the competence to legislate on religious institutions is shared between Parliament and the state assemblies under the Constitution's Concurrent List. The restraint had a logic each observer supplied according to camp: the devotees' advocates read it as respect for the judicial process and for the division of powers that makes temple administration a subject of the states in practice; the government's critics read it as the convenience of a party whose Kerala unit profited from the agitation while its government in Delhi avoided owning either the verdict or its reversal. The Congress's national position mirrored the restraint in its own idiom: the party's Kerala unit's stand was allowed to stand, its president's personal view was recorded and shelved, and no national legislation was promised in terms that bound a future government. The season thus produced a notable constitutional tableau. A judgment whose implementation convulsed a state was defended in Delhi by no government and attacked in Delhi by no government; the amplifier and the custodian of the Constitution's promises were, for fourteen months, different institutions, and the argument was carried by a state government, two state party units and a congregation. The architecture test reading draws the moral: when doctrine of this depth is unsettled, the political system routes around the doctrine's custodians and settles the question provisionally by mobilisation, until the custodians speak again, which is the office the reference of November 2019 performed.

## The Electoral Reckoning: Kerala Votes in April 2019

Kerala voted in the general election on 23 April 2019, and Sabarimala was the state's defining issue in a campaign otherwise conducted under the national contest between the ruling alliance at the centre and the Congress. The seat that concentrated the question was Pathanamthitta, the district of the shrine, where the Congress fielded its sitting member Anto Antony, the BJP fielded K. Surendran, fresh from his November arrest and the season's most recognisable campaigner, and the Left fielded Veena George of the Communist Party of India (Marxist). The BJP campaigned on the promise, repeated by its national leadership, that a vote for the party was a vote for the devotees and that Parliament could be made to protect the custom; its campaign materials in the district carried the shrine's image and the season's slogans. The Congress campaigned on the devotees' sentiments too, adding the charge that the BJP had staged the agitation for profit and reaped none of its professed aims. The Left campaigned on governance, welfare delivery and the defence of the Constitution, and treated Sabarimala as a wound its opponents kept open. The state's reading of the contest ran through [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) only at a distance; locally, the question was simpler and harder: which front did the devotee trust.

The results, declared on 23 May 2019, were a rout with a twist. The Congress-led United Democratic Front won nineteen of Kerala's twenty Lok Sabha seats; the Left Democratic Front held one, Alappuzha; the Bharatiya Janata Party won none. In Pathanamthitta, Anto Antony of the Congress retained the seat, the Left's Veena George finished second, and K. Surendran, the candidate of the agitation, finished third, though with a vote share in the constituency that the BJP counted as its Kerala high ground. Across the state the BJP-led alliance polled roughly one vote in seven, its best Kerala performance in a general election to that date, and still converted none of it into a seat. The Congress, whose Rahul Gandhi won Wayanad by a margin among the largest in the country, harvested the anti-government sentiment the season had generated, including the sentiment of devotees who had marched against the Left government and then voted, as Kerala's Hindu voters have historically preferred, for the front that could win.

The post-election argument inside Kerala was about causation, and it rehearsed the season in miniature. Congress leaders claimed the result as the devotees' verdict on the government's arrogance, a claim consistent with their campaign. BJP leaders claimed the vote share as the agitation's deposit, to be redeemed at the assembly election, and blamed the state's bipolar arithmetic for the missing seats. The Left's own reckoning was the most consequential. The Communist Party of India (Marxist) conducted reviews at state and district level; voices within the front argued that the government's handling of Sabarimala, its communication and its perceived haste, had alienated sections of the Hindu electorate that the Left had spent a generation courting, while the leadership maintained that the defeat ran on national currents and welfare resentments beyond one shrine. Post-poll surveys by the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies recorded Sabarimala among the issues Kerala's voters named, and recorded the Congress as its principal beneficiary. The safest statement the record supports is the one the arithmetic makes: the agitation mobilised the devotees, the devotees punished the government that implemented the verdict, and the punishment was collected by the opposition that had shared the devotees' platform without owning the agitation's party. The BJP had built the season's movement and the Congress banked its votes, an outcome that the study of [the post-2018 Sabarimala-question Kerala state-political context](/2020/01/28/kerala-left-congress-alternation/) treats as the alternation system's characteristic move, in which a third force's mobilisation is absorbed by the nearer of the two fronts.

## The Courts During the Season: No Stay, Many Hearings, One Watching Brief

While the argument ran on the roads, the courts kept a watching brief whose decisions, mostly refusals, shaped the season as surely as judgments would have. The Supreme Court's first procedural act after the verdict was its most consequential: when the review petitions were mentioned in November 2018, the Court agreed on 13 November 2018 to hear them in open court in January 2019, and it did not stay the verdict. The refusal of a stay was the legal fact on which the whole season turned, because it left the entry right operative and the government obliged, while the promise of an open hearing gave the movement a date to organise toward and a reason to keep its campaign within the forms of pressure rather than defiance. Petitions that sought contempt against officials for statements about the verdict, and petitions that sought the Court's protection for intending pilgrims, were heard and disposed of without altering the operative position. The Court was, in the season's middle months, a referee that had ruled and declined to re-enter the field, and both camps read the declinations as they needed: the government as endorsement of implementation, the movement as a court content to let its judgment be tested against the country's response before review.

The Kerala High Court carried the season's working docket. Petitions challenged the prohibitory orders at Nilakkal, the conditions imposed on pilgrims, the police arrangements of the Mandala season, and the conduct of particular operations, and the High Court's benches pressed the state in hearing after hearing on the proportionality of its measures and the rights of the ordinary pilgrim caught between the state and the movement. Its observations, reported widely, included sharp criticism of aspects of the government's handling and reminders that the pilgrimage's devotees, the lakhs who came to worship and not to argue, held rights the season's combatants were trampling in concert. The High Court also dealt with the litigation spawned by the protests themselves, anticipatory bail applications by arrested leaders among them, and with petitions arising from the 2 January entry, including challenges to the purification closure and the Board's conduct. None of this litigation changed the law; all of it changed the week's headlines, and through the headlines, the parties' calculations. The architecture test reading notes the pattern without embarrassment: in a season when doctrine was being renegotiated in public, the subordinate courts functioned as the architecture's scaffolding, holding the structure steady at the points of daily stress while the constitutional questions waited for their bench.

## The Review Reference of November 2019 and Its Politics

Fifty-six review petitions were filed against the September 2018 verdict, among them petitions by the thantri Kantaru Rajeevaru, by the Pandalam royal family, by the Nair Service Society and by devotee organisations, asking the Court to reconsider a judgment they said had misread the faith's own materials, had entertained petitioners without standing, and had set constitutional morality above the denomination's self-understanding. The Court agreed in November 2018 to hear the reviews in open court, heard them in February 2019 with the Devaswom Board's changed position on the record, and reserved its order. The order came on 14 November 2019, from a bench of five headed by Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi, and it split the Court three to two. The majority, in the judgment authored by the Chief Justice, did not decide the reviews. It kept them pending and referred the larger questions raised by the case to a bench of nine judges, observing that similar questions awaited decision in other pending matters touching the entry of women into mosques and Parsi places of worship and the practice of female genital cutting among the Dawoodi Bohra. Justice Nariman, joined by Justice Chandrachud, dissented, holding that the majority's course departed from the discipline of review and that the original judgment, a considered verdict of a coordinate bench, should stand undisturbed by a reference built on cases not before the Court.

The questions the reference carried to the larger bench, as the order and its February 2020 framing by the nine judge bench settled them, ran to seven: the interplay between the freedom of religion of persons under Article 25 and the denomination's freedom under Article 26; the meaning of constitutional morality in this field; the reach of judicial review into religious practice; the essential practice test and its reformulation; the standing of persons outside a faith to question its practices; the bearing of equality provisions, Articles 14, 15 and 17 among them, on denominational autonomy; and the definition of a religious denomination itself. The list is the architecture test stated as an examination paper. Every component of the September 2018 holding reappears in it as an open question, which is why the reference, not the verdict, became the document the doctrinal argument of the following years organised itself around.

The politics of the reference reprised the politics of the verdict with the signs reversed. The devotee organisations and the BJP welcomed the order as the Court's recognition that the questions were larger than the verdict had allowed, and read the absence of any stay in their favour: the verdict had not been stayed, yet its doctrine had been reopened by the Court itself, and the state government, which had months earlier expended its authority on implementation, announced that it would await the larger bench's guidance before facilitating further entries, a retreat stated in the language of prudence. The Congress in Kerala welcomed the reference and asked why it had not been the government's own prayer from the start. The women who had entered and those who still sought to enter read the order as the verdict's suspension in all but name, since a government awaiting guidance would protect no pilgrim; their counsel noted that the entry right declared in September 2018 remained law and that a reference could not repeal it. The nine judge bench, constituted under Chief Justice S. A. Bobde, began its work in February 2020 by settling the questions it would hear, and the hearings stood adjourned as the pandemic closed the Court's ordinary functioning in March 2020. As the record stands at the date of this article's place in the series, the bench has delivered no judgment, and the architecture test remains what the reference made it: fully framed, argued in part, and unresolved.

## The Dissent's Second Life: Justice Malhotra's Opinion as the Movement's Text

Justice Indu Malhotra's dissent of 28 September 2018 had a career after judgment that few dissenting opinions achieve. Its propositions were quotable in the movement's own idiom: that the notions of rationality the petitioners pressed could not be imported into matters of faith; that the petitioners, not being devotees of the shrine, could not claim its reform as their right; that to allow every practice of every denomination to be tested at the instance of any citizen would open an entire faith's arrangements to a general judicial audit; and that the equality provisions invoked against the custom addressed social evils, not the internal ordering of worship. Within weeks, the dissent was being read aloud at prayer processions, excerpted in the pamphlets of the Karma Samithi, and cited by the review petitioners as the judgment the Court should have delivered. The NSS's statements echoed its standing argument; the Pandalam family's petition adopted its essentiality reasoning; the BJP's spokesmen used its warning about judicial overreach as the respectable dress for a campaign the party was accused of conducting for votes.

The dissent's career in the courtroom matched its career in the street. The review arguments of February 2019 returned repeatedly to its propositions, and the reference order of November 2019, without adopting them, converted several into the larger bench's questions: the standing of non-adherents, the reformulation of the essential practice test, the definition of constitutional morality's jurisdiction over faith. The two opinions of the reference bench that resisted this conversion, the dissent of Justices Nariman and Chandrachud, answered the Malhotra propositions from the majority's ground, insisting that a review is not an appeal and that doubts about a coordinate bench's doctrine are not a warrant to re-open the field. The scholarly argument ran in parallel: the dissent was praised by commentators who fear judicial reformism and criticised by those who held that its standing doctrine would have closed the courts to the temple entry claims of the founding era, a charge its defenders answered by distinguishing the claims of a faith's own adherents from the claims of outsiders. For the politics of the season, the dissent performed a service no pamphlet could have: it gave a mobilisation the authority of a judge's signature, and it let every devotee who marched under it believe, with a text in hand, that the Constitution had two voices in the case and that the marchers carried one of them. The nine judge bench will decide, when it speaks, which voice the architecture keeps.

## Between Seasons: The Pilgrimage of 2019 and the Unfinished Reference

The Mandala season that opened on 16 November 2019, two days after the reference order, tested what the reference had changed. The government of Kerala stated that, the matter being before a larger bench, it would not facilitate the entry of women of the barred ages in the manner of the previous season, while maintaining that the verdict had not been stayed and remained the law. The distinction governed the ground. Women who travelled toward the shrine in November and December 2019 were stopped, counselled or turned back by the police citing the pending reference and the preservation of order; Bindu Ammini, seeking police protection in Kochi on 26 November 2019 for a renewed attempt, was attacked with a chilli spray outside the city police commissioner's office by a man the police then arrested, an episode recorded on camera and entered into the season's archive as evidence, for the entry camp, that the state's retreat had licensed the street. The shrine's season proceeded with the customary lakhs of pilgrims and without an entry, and the Karma Samithi stood down the mobilisation it had maintained for fourteen months, claiming the reference as its monument: the custom, un-restored in law, stood restored in fact, guarded by the Court's own hesitation.

The national parties adjusted to the new posture with the same speed they had shown in September 2018. The BJP, which had welcomed the reference, lowered the issue's temperature as its national leadership turned to other campaigns, and its Kerala unit folded Sabarimala into its standing charge sheet against the Left government rather than maintaining it as a mobilisation. The Congress kept its position of record, support for the devotees and for review, without a street campaign. The Union government, which had taken no position in the review that committed it, maintained its silence, a silence both its Kerala unit and its opponents found convenient at different moments. The litigation itself moved to the nine judge bench's calendar: constituted in January 2020 under Chief Justice Bobde, the bench sat in February 2020 to settle the questions and the order of argument, heard counsel on the framing of the reference, and stood adjourned as the pandemic suspended the Court's ordinary sittings in March 2020. The seven questions remained on its board, undecided, as this article takes its place in the series, and the shrine awaited a season whose rules would again be written by the interaction of a judgment, a reference and a road.

## The Pillar's Long Arc: From Kesavananda to the Sabarimala Reference

This article closes the pillar on the judiciary and constitutional politics, and the Sabarimala season earns the closing place because it staged, in one district and one year, the question the pillar's whole arc poses: what the courts may remake of the Republic's arrangements, and who decides when a judgment has gone too far. The arc begins with the basic structure doctrine, stated in 1973 and read in this pillar through [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), which holds that the Constitution has a skeleton even Parliament cannot amend away. Its first great operationalisation came in 1973 itself, in the thirteen judge decision remembered through [the Kesavananda Bharati reanalysis](/2020/08/02/kesavananda-bharati-1973-reanalysed/), and its shadow side arrived three years later in the habeas corpus decision of the Emergency, the institutional failure recorded in [the ADM Jabalpur study](/2020/08/08/adm-jabalpur-1976-habeas-corpus-case/), where the Court declined to protect life and liberty against the executive that held them. The pillar traced the doctrine's extension into the Ninth Schedule in [the I. R. Coelho article](/2020/08/24/ir-coelho-ninth-schedule-2007/), its early deployment against an election in [the study of the Raj Narain litigation](/2020/08/19/indira-gandhi-raj-narain-1975/), and the long contest over who appoints the judges who wield it in [the collegium and NJAC contestation](/2020/08/13/collegium-njac-contestation-explained/).

The arc's later stations brought the doctrine home to the citizen. The nine judge privacy decision of August 2017 built [the right-architecture-established framework](/2020/08/30/2017-privacy-judgment-puttaswamy/) on which so much subsequent reasoning rests. The long erosion of a federal bargain occupied [the Article 370 long-arc article](/2020/09/04/article-370-1965-2019-explained/), and the proportionality method's operationalisation in a welfare identity system occupied [the proportionality-architecture-operationalisation framework](/2020/09/10/2018-aadhaar-judgment-reanalysed/), delivered two days before Sabarimala in the same crowded September of 2018. Sabarimala differs from each of these in one respect that the season exposed. In the earlier cases the Court's counterparty was the state: a Parliament amending, an executive detaining, a government identifying. At Sabarimala the Court's counterparty was a congregation. The judgment ran not against a ministry but against a practice held by lakhs of ordinary pilgrims to be the condition of their deity's presence, and its enforcement required the state to police a queue of believers. The founding generation had imagined this collision when it wrote temple entry into the Constitution's promises against the background of [the post-1947 plural-civilisational settlement](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/), and the Constituent Assembly's own arguments about the reach of religious freedom, preserved in its debates, anticipated both the majority's confidence and the dissent's caution. The pillar's synthesis is that the post-1973 Court built an architecture of review whose strength was proven against governments, and that Sabarimala tested whether the same architecture could bear the weight of a congregation's answer: not defiance of the Court's authority, which no actor of consequence asserted, but a season-long refusal to let the judgment settle into fact.

That refusal is why the settlement language of the religion and politics pillar fits this case as a closing entry. The Indian secular settlement, as [the contested-settlement framework](/2020/04/09/indian-secularism-contested-settlement/) describes it, was never a wall but a continuing negotiation among state, courts and faiths over the terms of their coexistence, renegotiated at Shah Bano, at Ayodhya, at every point where a judgment met a mobilisation. Sabarimala is the negotiation's clearest judicial chapter: a verdict of impeccable constitutional pedigree, a mobilisation of impeccable democratic scale, and a reference that converted the collision into seven questions for a larger bench. Whether the architecture test reading survives the larger bench's eventual answer, the season has already demonstrated the reading's premise, that the case's deepest content is the structure connecting Articles 25 and 26 and the location of the authority to interpret it.

## What the Season Changed Before the Larger Bench Could Speak

Fourteen months of mobilisation left deposits that no judgment can redistribute. The first deposit is electoral geography. The BJP's Kerala vote, roughly one in seven ballots in the 2019 general election, was assembled substantially on the season's mobilisation and survived the defeat of every one of its candidates, giving the party a floor in Kerala it had never possessed and converting the state's bipolar contests into three-cornered ones in the constituencies where the agitation had run deepest. The second deposit is doctrinal and belongs to the reference: the questions of denominational autonomy, essential practice and constitutional morality, which the 2018 majority had treated as decidable on the materials of one shrine, stand referred at the level of the whole field, joined by the reference order's own gesture toward the pending disputes over women's entry to mosques and Parsi fire temples and over Dawoodi Bohra practice, so that Sabarimala's residue is a general jurisprudence of religious autonomy awaiting construction. The third deposit is institutional: the demand to free temples from government administration, an old slogan of the Hindu organisations, acquired during the season a mainstream carrier and a documentary grievance, the Devaswom Board's February statement, and it will outlast the entry question itself in Kerala's legislative argument. The fourth deposit is archival and human. The women who entered live with the season's consequences in their persons; the women who waited live with its vindication in theirs; the families of the dead of the hartal days, on every side of the barricades, hold the part of the record that no bench will review.

Measured against the ambitions of September 2018, every camp fell short, and the shortfalls are the season's fairest summary. The government implemented its verdict twice in fourteen months, at the cost of an election and by methods it had promised not to need. The movement saved its shrine's custom in fact and lost it in law, and converted its greatest mobilisation into another party's seats. The entrants won the law and, after January 2019, could not use it. The Court won obedience to its judgment's form and watched its substance referred to a bench that has not answered. The architecture test reading draws no triumph from this ledger; it observes only that a constitutional structure which produces, under load, a distribution of partial victories this even is a structure whose joints are exactly where the 2018 opinions and the 2019 reference located them, in the unsettled boundary between the worshipper's right and the denomination's autonomy. That observation is the article's claim restated at ground level, and it is the ground on which the pillar closes.

## Three Readings of One Verdict, and What the Record Decides

Three readings of the Sabarimala verdict compete in the literature, and the article's claim requires adjudicating among them on the season's evidence. The religious-secular tension reading, developed in the companion article, treats the case as a stress test of the Indian secular settlement, a negotiation between a reforming state and a defensive faith. The gender justice reading treats the case as an equality landmark in the lineage of the temple entry movement, its heroines the women who climbed and its meaning the dismantling of a purity regime. The architecture test reading defended here treats the case as an examination of the doctrinal structure joining Article 25 to Article 26, whose outcome governs a class of disputes far beyond one shrine.

The named scholarship marks the positions. Gautam Bhatia, in his essays on the Indian Constitutional Law and Philosophy blog written across the autumn of 2018, read the majority opinions closely and praised the judgment's destination while questioning the route of the essential practice doctrine, which he has long criticised as a judicial theology by another name; his account supplies the architecture reading with its sharpest internal critic, since the doctrine the majority used is the doctrine he would retire. Pratap Bhanu Mehta, in essays written in the verdict's aftermath, warned against the Court's growing appetite for social reform by judgment and asked whether a bench that remakes the internal arrangements of faiths on the petition of outsiders strengthens or spends the judiciary's authority; his caution supplies the dissent's political philosophy in scholarly form. Madhavi Sunder, writing on Indian religion-state jurisprudence from her larger work on religious freedom's cultural turn, has pressed the claims of reformers inside traditions against the stereotype of monolithic faiths, a position the season vindicated in one respect: the women of Ready to Wait and the women of the wall were both insiders, and any reading that casts one body as the tradition and the other as its enemy fails the record.

Adjudicated on the season's evidence, the three readings divide the truth by altitude. The gender justice reading best explains the entrants and their supporters, whose own statements ran on dignity and equality, and it explains the wall. The religious-secular reading best explains the fronts, whose positions tracked the settlement's old bargaining lines, and it explains the election. The architecture reading alone explains the reference. When the five judge bench of November 2019 converted the case into seven questions about denominations, morality, standing and the essential practice test, it declared in institutional form that the case's residue was doctrinal and structural, a set of questions about where authority sits in the Constitution's religion clauses that no settlement narrative and no equality narrative exhausts. The readings are complements at different altitudes, but where they conflict, on the question of what the case will have decided when the larger bench speaks, the architecture reading has the reference order as its evidence, and the reference order is the strongest document in the file.

The strongest counter to the architecture reading must be faced in its own terms. A test that has not been concluded, the counter runs, is a test in name only: the nine judge bench has delivered no judgment, the doctrine stands suspended between a verdict un-stayed and a reconsideration un-decided, and a reading that celebrates structural engagement mistakes the Court's hesitation for its design. The distinction that answers the counter is between resolution and engagement. Institutional resolution is indeed absent, and the article claims none. Architectural engagement is present in the hardest evidence available, the text of the reference order and the February 2020 framing proceedings, in which the Court itself enumerated the structure's joints and set them down for argument. A map of a structure is not falsified by the structure's tenants disputing the survey. The season's politics, which this article has recorded from the roadblocks to the reference, is what engagement looks like when a constitutional architecture is load-bearing: everyone with a stake in the building arrives to argue about the beams.

One further record of the season belongs in this closing assessment, because it disciplines every reading. The journalists who covered Nilakkal and the hartal days worked under attack and under surveillance of a kind the state's own press councils documented, and the protection of sources became a working condition of the coverage; the archivists of contested public events face the same discipline, and the account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) describes the practice by which reporters on such assignments keep their informants' identities out of reach. The season's archive, on which any future bench, scholar or citizen must rely, exists because those protections held. The verdict's politics will be argued as long as the archive is read; the architecture test reading asks only that the argument be conducted on the structure the judges actually built, and it rests its case there.

## The Season Measured: Petitions, Processions, Arrests, and Votes

A season argued in absolutes can be closed in figures, and the figures, assembled from the record each camp itself published, discipline the argument one last time. The litigation's ledger reads: one petition filed in 2006 by five young lawyers; one rule, Rule 3(b) of 1965, struck down; five opinions' worth of reasoning compressed into a four to one verdict on 28 September 2018; fifty-six review petitions; one reference order, three to two, on 14 November 2019; seven questions framed for nine judges; and, at the point the record closes, no judgment from the larger bench. The street's ledger reads: two statewide hartals, on 18 October 2018 and 3 January 2019; one wall of women along six hundred and twenty kilometres of highway; one answering river of lamps; prayer processions in the hundreds across the southern districts; arrests in the thousands across the season on the police's own figures; cases in the hundreds arising from single weeks; one death at Pandalam laid by each camp in the other's account; two women entered, on 2 January 2019, in the season's only confirmed breach of the custom it defended; and one sanctum closed for an hour's purification. The electorate's ledger is the shortest: twenty seats, divided nineteen, one and none, on a turnout in which the shrine's question was the state's loudest.

Set side by side, the three ledgers explain why no camp could declare the season won. The litigation's ledger favoured the entrants at every line except the last, which was blank. The street's ledger favoured the movement at every line except the entry, which was the point. The electorate's ledger favoured the Congress, which had written neither the judgment nor the agitation. A distribution of victories that even is the signature of a constitutional question that the system could process but not settle, and it is the empirical form of the article's claim. The architecture between Articles 25 and 26 held the load the season put on it, in the sense that no actor of consequence broke the frame: the government implemented rather than defied, the movement obstructed rather than rebelled, the parties competed rather than seceded from the argument, and the Court, pressed from every side, answered with a larger bench rather than with contempt. The frame held, and the question inside it stayed open, which is what a test, as opposed to a settlement, looks like when a republic runs one on itself. The nine judges inherit the ledgers along with the questions, and the pillar on the judiciary closes with its docket, fittingly, still before the Court.

One figure stands outside the three ledgers and measures the season's cost to the institution at its centre. The shrine's pilgrimage, which in an ordinary year draws its tens of millions across the Mandala and Makaram months and fills the Devaswom Board's treasury, thinned visibly in the season of the protests: the Board's own statements recorded falling footfall and falling revenue through the autumn of 2018, as ordinary pilgrims from the neighbouring states weighed the roadblocks, the prohibitory orders and the hartal days against their vow. The recovery of the pilgrimage in the season after the reference, when the crowds returned under the reference's truce, completed the measurement: the season's deepest argument had never been with the pilgrimage, and every camp's conduct, in the end, bent around the lakhs who came only to climb.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the 2018 Sabarimala verdict?

The 2018 Sabarimala verdict is the judgment of a five judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court of India in Indian Young Lawyers Association v State of Kerala, delivered on 28 September 2018. By four votes to one, the Court struck down Rule 3(b) of the Kerala Hindu Places of Public Worship (Authorisation of Entry) Rules 1965 and held that women of every age, including the ten to fifty group customarily excluded, could enter the Sabarimala shrine of Lord Ayyappa. Chief Justice Dipak Misra, Justice Khanwilkar, Justice Nariman and Justice Chandrachud formed the majority through three opinions; Justice Indu Malhotra dissented. Politically, the verdict committed the Kerala government to implementation and opened the protest season this article records.

### Q: What is the essential-religious-practice doctrine?

The essential-religious-practice doctrine is the test the Supreme Court developed from the Shirur Mutt case of 1954 onward, under which constitutional protection extends to the practices a religion itself regards as essential and integral to it, while practices found non-essential, or secular activity associated with religion, may be regulated. In the Sabarimala case the doctrine supplied the majority's first gate: the exclusion of women aged ten to fifty was held not essential to the Hindu religion, being specific to one shrine and traceable to rule and custom rather than to the faith's own foundational materials. Because the practice failed the gate, it could claim no shelter under the religion clauses. Critics, including the dissent and scholars such as Gautam Bhatia, have long questioned whether courts should certify what a faith requires at all, and the nine judge reference carries that question forward.

### Q: What is constitutional-morality?

Constitutional morality is the standard, named by B. R. Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly while borrowing the phrase from the historian George Grote, by which the Constitution's own values of equality, liberty, dignity and fraternity measure public and religious practice, rather than the values of social or popular morality. In the Sabarimala majority opinions, and in Justice Chandrachud's concurrence in particular, constitutional morality did the decisive work: a practice resting on notions of women's purity and pollution could not claim protection merely because a section of society held it moral, since the morality that counts under the Constitution is the morality the document itself declares. Justice Malhotra's dissent answered that constitutional morality, so wielded, becomes a licence for courts to reform faiths from outside. The nine judge reference lists the concept's meaning and reach among its questions, which is why the term's career in this case matters beyond it.

### Q: How did Justice Malhotra's Sabarimala dissent operate doctrinally?

Justice Indu Malhotra's dissent operated on four doctrinal levers. On standing, she held that the petitioners, not being Ayyappa devotees, could not invoke the Court's writ jurisdiction to reform a practice of a faith they did not profess. On essentiality, she held that the exclusion answered the essential practice test on the denomination's own showing, the deity's celibate form being the shrine's founding rationale. On the reach of equality, she held that Article 17 and the temple entry provisions addressed social exclusion of the untouchability kind, not the internal ordering of a denomination's worship. On constitutional morality, she held that the concept could not serve as a judicial instrument for rationalising religious practice. Each lever answered a corresponding lever of the majority, which is why the dissent reads as a complete alternative architecture of the religion clauses rather than as a disagreement over result.

### Q: What is the nine-judge reference?

The nine judge reference is the order of 14 November 2019 in Kantaru Rajeevaru v Indian Young Lawyers Association, by which a five judge bench, split three to two, kept the Sabarimala review petitions pending and referred the underlying questions of religious freedom law to a bench of nine judges. The questions, settled in the framing proceedings of February 2020, run to seven: the interplay of Articles 25 and 26, the meaning of constitutional morality, the scope of judicial review of religious practice, the essential practice test, the standing of outsiders to question a faith's practices, the bearing of the equality articles on denominational autonomy, and the definition of a religious denomination. The bench, constituted under Chief Justice S. A. Bobde, had delivered no judgment when its sittings were interrupted by the pandemic in March 2020, and the 2018 verdict stands un-stayed meanwhile.

### Q: How does Article 25 relate to Article 26?

Article 25 guarantees every person freedom of conscience and the right to practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and the Constitution's other rights provisions; Article 26 guarantees every religious denomination the right to establish and maintain institutions, to manage its own affairs in matters of religion, and to administer its property. The two articles protect different holders, the person and the denomination, and Sabarimala is the modern case in which those holders conflicted directly: women claiming entry as persons under Article 25, devotees claiming autonomy for their denomination under Article 26. The 2018 majority ordered the relationship by the essential practice gate, holding that a practice failing the gate cannot use Article 26 to defeat the Article 25 right. The dissent would have ordered the relationship by deference to the denomination's own account. The reference asks the nine judge bench to state the relationship generally.

### Q: What is the post-2019 trajectory?

The post-2019 trajectory of the Sabarimala question runs from the reference order of 14 November 2019 through the quiet pilgrimage season that followed to the unfinished proceedings of the nine judge bench. The Kerala government, which had implemented the verdict through the season of 2018 and 2019, announced after the reference that it would await the larger bench's guidance, and the Mandala season of November 2019 passed without a facilitated entry, women who travelled to the shrine being turned back in the reference's shadow. The nine judge bench took up the referred questions in February 2020, settled their framing, and stood adjourned when the pandemic suspended the Court's ordinary work in March 2020. Politically, the issue subsided from mobilisation into the party record: a standing charge in Kerala's electoral argument, a floor of votes for the BJP, a lesson in the Congress and Left files, awaiting the bench's word.

### Q: What position did the Kerala government take on implementing the verdict?

The Kerala government, led by Chief Minister Pinarayi Vijayan of the Left Democratic Front, stated from 28 September 2018 that the verdict bound it and that it would implement the ruling and protect women who sought entry. Its stated grounds were constitutional duty, Kerala's renaissance inheritance running from Sree Narayana Guru through the temple entry proclamation of 1936, and its charge that the agitation against implementation was a campaign of the RSS to communalise the state. In conduct, the government deployed police in the thousands, escorted attempts through the autumn of 2018, effected the entry of two women before dawn on 2 January 2019, and after the reference order of November 2019 announced that it would await the larger bench's guidance. The position's constancy of statement and variation of method defined its politics: absolute in principle, intermittent in the street, and punished by the electorate in April 2019 on the implementation's account.

### Q: How did the Congress leadership in Kerala respond to the verdict?

The Congress leadership in Kerala, through KPCC president Mullappally Ramachandran and Leader of the Opposition Ramesh Chennithala, stated within days of the verdict that the party stood with the devotees, that the Court ought to have weighed believers' sentiments, and that the state government should pursue review or support legislation restoring the custom. Congress workers joined the namajapa prayer processions, the party protested the government's arrest drives, and Chennithala undertook a fast in solidarity with the cause. The calibration aimed to hold the party's Hindu base, including the Nair electorate, without surrendering its secular self-description. It was complicated publicly by Rahul Gandhi, the party's national president, who first welcomed the verdict and in January 2019 recorded that his personal view favoured entry while he respected his Kerala unit's contrary position. The Kerala stand harvested its reward in the general election, when the United Democratic Front won nineteen of the state's twenty seats.

### Q: What stand did the BJP take in Kerala during the protest season?

The Bharatiya Janata Party's Kerala unit took the stand that the verdict offended the faith of the devotees, that the state government was assaulting a shrine in the name of a judgment, and that the party would stand with the devotees until the custom was safe. Its state president P. S. Sreedharan Pillai called the agitation a golden opportunity for the party, in remarks to a youth meeting at Kozhikode in November 2018 that the opposition circulated as proof the protests were planned for profit. Its national president Amit Shah declared at Kannur on 27 October 2018 that the party stood with devotees like a rock and that courts should deliver judgments that can be implemented. Its state general secretary K. Surendran led the field campaign and was arrested at Nilakkal in November 2018. The stand built the party a Kerala vote floor of roughly one ballot in seven in 2019 and won it no seat, a deposit it banked for later contests.

### Q: What was the Women's Wall of 1 January 2019?

The Women's Wall, the Vanitha Mathil, was a human chain of women assembled by the Kerala government and allied reform and community organisations along six hundred and twenty kilometres of highway from Kasaragod to Thiruvananthapuram on 1 January 2019. Proposed at a meeting the government convened on 1 December 2018, the wall stated three purposes: to defend Kerala's renaissance values, to uphold gender equality, and to answer the mobilisation against the Sabarimala verdict. The organisers put participation at five million women; independent estimates ran lower while still counting the gathering in the millions. The Chief Minister addressed its southern end and read it as Kerala's women rejecting the protest's politics. The devotee movement answered that a ruling party's machinery had measured its own strength rather than the devotees' mind. Six days earlier the movement had held its own answering spectacle, the Ayyappa Jyothi of lamps, and the two gatherings together form the season's paired portraits of Kerala's women arguing in public.

### Q: What was the Ready to Wait campaign?

Ready to Wait was the campaign begun in 2016 by women devotees of Sabarimala, of the very ages the custom barred, stating that they would wait until the age of fifty to climb to the shrine because the wait was their observance and not their oppression. Its public faces, among them Anjali George, argued that the deity's celibate form was the shrine's reason for existence, that the forty-one day austerity of the pilgrimage expressed the same discipline the wait expressed, and that the Court, in reading their vow as their disability, had not asked the women it proposed to liberate. The campaign supplied the protest movement of 2018 with its most credible witnesses and its slogan, and it complicated every account of the season as men defending a shrine against women. Its existence is a standing difficulty for readings of the case that assign the tradition to one body of women and its enemies to another.

### Q: What happened during the statewide hartal of 3 January 2019?

The statewide hartal of 3 January 2019 was called by the Bharatiya Janata Party and the Hindu organisations after the entry of Bindu Ammini and Kanakadurga into the shrine before dawn on 2 January, which the callers named a betrayal carried out in darkness. The shutdown closed Kerala's commerce for the day and brought the season's heaviest disorder: clashes between protesters, police and ruling front workers across the districts, stones thrown, party offices and buses attacked, journalists assaulted, and mass arrests on the police's own figures. At Pandalam, a Sabarimala Karma Samithi worker, Chandran Unnithan, injured in stone throwing the previous day, died of his injuries, and each camp entered his death into its own ledger of the season's blame. The Congress condemned the entry's manner while keeping its distance from the hartal's violence. The day fixed the entry in public memory as an event of disorder as much as of rights, a framing the government's opponents carried into the election.

### Q: How did Sabarimala shape the 2019 Lok Sabha results in Kerala?

Kerala voted on 23 April 2019 with Sabarimala as its defining state issue, and the results declared on 23 May gave the Congress-led United Democratic Front nineteen of twenty seats, the Left Democratic Front one, and the Bharatiya Janata Party none. In Pathanamthitta, the shrine's district, the Congress's Anto Antony retained the seat, the Left finished second, and the BJP's K. Surendran, the agitation's field leader, finished third on a vote share his party counted as its Kerala high ground. Across the state the BJP-led alliance polled roughly one vote in seven, its best general election performance there to that date. Post-poll surveys recorded Sabarimala among the issues voters named and the Congress as its beneficiary. The record supports a precise verdict: the agitation mobilised the devotees, the devotees punished the government that had implemented the ruling, and the punishment was collected by the opposition that had shared the devotees' platform without owning the agitation's party.

### Q: What did Amit Shah say about the verdict in October 2018?

Amit Shah, the Bharatiya Janata Party's national president, addressed a rally at Kannur on 27 October 2018, a month after the verdict, and stated the national party's Kerala position in three sentences that the state unit circulated for the rest of the season. He declared that the BJP stood with the Ayyappa devotees like a rock. He warned the Kerala government over its arrests of devotees and its handling of the shrine. And he said that courts should deliver such judgments as can be implemented, a remark that drew criticism from the legal fraternity, which heard in it pressure upon the judiciary, and applause from the agitation, which heard in it the Court put on notice. The speech marked the moment the Sabarimala campaign ceased to be a Kerala unit's enterprise and became a declared front of the national party, with the national leadership's authority committed to a state electorate that, six months later, gave the party its Kerala high-water vote share and no seat.

### Q: How did the RSS frame its opposition to the verdict?

The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh framed its opposition to the verdict as the defence of a faith's self-understanding against a state and a judiciary that had ceased to consult it. Its chief, Mohan Bhagwat, used the Vijayadashami address of October 2018 at Nagpur to say that the verdict had been delivered without regard to the traditions and sentiments of the devotees who sustained the shrine. Its Kerala leadership and its affiliates, the Vishva Hindu Parishad and the Hindu Aikya Vedi among them, supplied the protest season with organisation and with the argument, repeated at every procession, that the shrine's custom was the deity's own discipline and that a judgment ignorant of it had mistaken a living tradition for a rulebook entry. The state government framed the same organisation's role in opposite terms, as a campaign to communalise Kerala. Both framings are in the record, and the election rewarded neither completely: the Sangh's frame mobilised the season's crowds, and the crowds' votes went largely to the Congress.

### Q: What happened at the all-party meeting of 15 November 2018?

The all-party meeting of 15 November 2018 was convened by Chief Minister Pinarayi Vijayan in Thiruvananthapuram on the eve of the Mandala pilgrimage season, to seek a common approach to implementing the verdict among Kerala's political formations. The Congress-led United Democratic Front and the Bharatiya Janata Party attended and stated their common demand: that the government seek a stay of the verdict or promulgate an ordinance restoring the custom, and that it desist from escorting women to the shrine under police protection. The government refused both demands, restating that implementation was its constitutional duty and that an ordinance against a Supreme Court judgment was not open to it. The meeting ended without agreement, each side blaming the other for its failure in the statements that followed. The Mandala season therefore opened the next day under the arrangements the government had already made, with prohibitory orders at Nilakkal and the largest police deployment of the pilgrimage's history, while the opposition fronts carried the meeting's failure into their campaigns as proof of the government's refusal to listen.

### Q: What role did the Pandalam royal family play in the protest movement?

The Pandalam royal family, whose palace town is tied by tradition to the Ayyappa legend and which holds custody of the deity's sacred ornaments, played the protest movement's role of consecrated witness. The family announced its dismay on the day of the verdict, supported and filed review petitions against it, lent the movement the palace's prestige and its members' presence, and carried the Thiruvabharanam, the annual procession of the deity's jewels from Pandalam to the shrine, through the season's tensions in January 2019. Its town of Pandalam was also a theatre of the season's disorder, where the Karma Samithi worker Chandran Unnithan received the injuries of which he died after the entry of 2 January. The family's position mattered beyond its numbers because it let the movement claim that the shrine's own constituted custodians, priesthood and palace together, stood against the verdict's implementation, a claim the entrants answered by distinguishing custody from ownership under the Constitution.

### Q: What position did the Nair Service Society take on temple entry?

The Nair Service Society, the organisation of the Nair community founded by Mannathu Padmanabhan in 1914 and led through the season by its general secretary G. Sukumaran Nair, took the position that the verdict had wounded the faith of the devotees and that the society stood with the believers for the custom's preservation. The society's members marched in the namajapa prayer processions that opened the campaign in October 2018, its leadership demanded review of the judgment and restraint by the government, and it filed and supported petitions against the verdict. The position carried electoral weight because the society speaks for a constituency both of Kerala's fronts court, and its stand was among the signals the Congress read when it aligned with the devotees and the Left read when it counted its losses in 2019. The society's general secretary stated its doctrine throughout in one line: the sentiments of devotees were not an obstacle to the law but a fact the law had to govern with, a formulation the review petitions later carried into court.

### Q: What did the review petitions ask the Court to reconsider?

The fifty-six review petitions filed against the verdict of 28 September 2018, by the thantri Kantaru Rajeevaru, the Pandalam royal family, the Nair Service Society, devotee organisations and others, asked the Supreme Court to reconsider its judgment on the grounds its majority was said to have erred. They asked the Court to reconsider whether outsiders to a faith could claim its reform as of right, whether the exclusion was in truth an essential practice of the denomination on the denomination's own materials, whether constitutional morality could displace a living tradition's self-understanding, and whether the equality provisions invoked, Articles 14 and 17 among them, governed a denomination's internal worship at all. The Court heard the petitions in open court in February 2019, with the Travancore Devaswom Board stating its support for the verdict, and on 14 November 2019 declined to decide them, keeping them pending while referring the underlying questions to a bench of nine judges.
