Kenneth MacMillan's Romeo and Juliet for the Royal Ballet is the definitive English ballet version of the tragedy, and its claim rests on the choreography rather than on the celebrated names who danced its first night. The production opened at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, on 9 February 1965, with Margot Fonteyn and Rudolf Nureyev in the title roles, Nicholas Georgiadis designing a towering Renaissance Verona, and Sergei Prokofiev's score carrying a drama that the steps, not the words, had to speak. MacMillan was thirty-five, the work was his first full-length three-act ballet, and he had built it in under five months on two young dancers who were then displaced from the premiere by casting politics. Every element of that sentence has been contested in print. The argument of this article is that the contest misses the point: the ballet survived its own premiere, outlived both pairs of originators, crossed the Atlantic as a signature work for a second great company, and remains the standard against which English narrative ballet is measured, because MacMillan solved the problem the play sets any wordless adapter. The statistics state the case in numbers: six decades in one repertory, more than five hundred performances at the Royal Opera House alone, a second national company across the Atlantic treating the identical steps as a birthright, and a filmed archive that runs from the year after the premiere to the high-definition era. The cliche needs speech, and speech is the one thing a ballet cannot use. MacMillan's answer was to restore the tragedy as danced drama, to make movement carry motive, decision and consequence with the precision the verse carries them in the theatre.

The verdict matters because the legend points the other way. In the popular telling, the evening of 9 February 1965 belongs to Fonteyn and Nureyev: forty-three curtain calls, forty minutes of applause, the iron curtain lowered at last to send the audience home. A production remembered chiefly as a star vehicle, however, dies with its stars, and this one conspicuously did not. It became the Royal Ballet's signature Romeo, the work with which the company presents the tragedy to the world, and it did so on the strength of three pas de deux, a potion scene played as pure external behaviour, and a tomb scene of a brutality that ballet had not previously permitted itself. Those are choreographic facts, available to any cast, and they are the facts this article examines: the genesis of the work in a Canadian television studio, the casting decision that shadowed its birth, the balcony duet from which the whole structure grew, the Verona of crowds and harlots that frames the lovers, the bedroom and tomb scenes at its centre, the film that fixed the first cast on celluloid, and the long afterlife in London, Washington and Birmingham. The evidence throughout is the documented record: Jann Parry's biography of the choreographer, the memoirs of the dancers, the first-night criticism, and the scholarship that has since weighed the work as an adaptation.

## A Ballet Commissioned in a Hurry: The Genesis of MacMillan's Romeo

The production exists because of a refusal. The Royal Ballet had hoped to present Leonid Lavrovsky's celebrated Soviet staging, the production that had made Galina Ulanova's Juliet a legend of the Russian stage, but the Soviet authorities would not allow the Lavrovsky version to tour to London. Covent Garden therefore needed a Romeo of its own, and needed it fast: a three-act ballet suitable for the company's American tour, in a repertory moment shaped by the four-hundredth anniversary of the playwright's birth. Frederick Ashton, the company's artistic director and its founding choreographer, gave the commission to MacMillan and, in Parry's account, gave him his blessing to make the work in his own way rather than in the Ashton manner. The blessing was not a small thing. The refusal that created the vacancy deserves a sentence of its own, because it shaped what MacMillan thought he was replacing. Lavrovsky's staging, created for the Kirov in 1940 and crowned by Ulanova's Juliet, was the Soviet era's canonical Romeo, a monument of dramatic ballet in which the story's psychology was carried by a great tragedienne's art; London audiences knew its reputation and had been promised its presence. Its withdrawal on political grounds left Covent Garden with a choice between importing another ready-made Romeo and commissioning a British one at speed, and the company's decision to commission, in the Shakespeare anniversary period, with the American tour as the deadline, determined everything about the work that resulted: its youth, its haste, its contemporary temper and its casting politics were all functions of that single refusal in Moscow.
 MacMillan in 1964 was a choreographer of one-act works, admired and distrusted in roughly equal measure for the psychological darkness of pieces made for the company's smaller stages. He had never attempted a full evening. He was given less than five months.

The seed of the work already existed, and it existed because of two dancers. In September 1964 MacMillan had travelled to Canada to make a short piece for Canadian television honouring Lynn Seymour, the Canadian-born ballerina who had become his muse. The piece he chose to make was the balcony scene: a pas de deux for Seymour and Christopher Gable, the young English dancer whose dramatic intelligence matched her own. Seymour later recalled that the duet took only three rehearsals to choreograph, a speed that tells its own story about the sympathy between choreographer and dancers. That television duet became the structural and emotional template for the full ballet. When the Covent Garden commission arrived, MacMillan, Seymour and Gable planned the whole work together around the lovers and their duets, discussing the characters at length before steps were set. Parry records their governing conception: Juliet was to be the headstrong one, the character who makes the decisions, while Romeo was to be a young man swept off his feet by love. The conception inverts the play's surface distribution of initiative, in which Romeo soliloquises, schemes and swears while Juliet reacts, and it is truer to the play's depths than the surface: it is Juliet who proposes marriage by messenger (2.2 and 2.3 in sequence), Juliet who drinks, Juliet who acts while Romeo weeps on the Friar's floor (3.3).

### How fast was the ballet actually made?

The full ballet was choreographed in under five months, from the autumn of 1964 to the February 1965 premiere, because the Royal Ballet needed a three-act Romeo for its American tour after the Soviet refusal blocked the Lavrovsky staging, and because the balcony duet at its centre already existed in finished form from the Canadian television commission of September 1964.

The design conception was fixed early and never wavered. Georgiadis, MacMillan's closest collaborator, built a Verona drawn from Italian Quattrocento painting and architecture: high walls, steep perspectives, a piazza that could swallow a crowd and a balcony set into masonry that dwarfed the girl who appeared on it. The scale was itself an interpretation. The lovers in this production are small figures inside a large, hard, public world, and the sets state the tragedy's thesis before a step is danced: private feeling exists here on sufferance, in rooms and at hours the city lends it, and the city can withdraw the loan at swordpoint. MacMillan and Georgiadis also studied the immediate stage tradition, including Franco Zeffirelli's 1960 Old Vic production of the play, whose sunlit, crowded, youthful Verona had reset expectations for the spoken version five years earlier. The ballet that resulted was therefore an adaptation twice over: of Prokofiev's 1935 score, written for a Soviet Romeo that politics delayed for years, and of a play whose mid-century stage life had already turned toward youth, heat and public violence. Production historians rebuilding those months from programmes, rehearsal photographs, memoir accounts and the correspondence of the period keep that scattered paper in order with tools such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where a dispersed archive becomes a single searchable record rather than a pile of folders.

One further debt belongs in the genesis, because it explains the ballet's flavour. MacMillan had seen John Cranko's Romeo and Juliet, the Stuttgart production that had established the earlier Prokofiev staging in the West, and Parry's biography and the choreographer's own estate record his admiration for it. The rowdy harlots who animate MacMillan's marketplace scenes came into his Verona under Cranko's example, a debt examined at comparison length later in this article. The point for the genesis is simpler: MacMillan did not build in a vacuum of English decorum. He built as a thirty-five-year-old who had watched the Russians' drama, the Germans' lyricism and Zeffirelli's Italians, and who intended his own version to be more psychologically candid than any of them.

## Georgiadis's Verona: Architecture as the Third Antagonist

The designs by Nicholas Georgiadis are usually praised as beautiful, which undersells their function: in this production the architecture is an antagonist, the visible form of the feud. Georgiadis and MacMillan drew the city from Italian Quattrocento painting and architecture, from the steep perspectives and frescoed walls of the early Renaissance, and built a Verona of towers, arcades and a great staircase, a city rendered in stone tones and heraldic colour that reads at a glance as both magnificent and airless. The ballroom glitters; the piazza blazes; the private rooms, Juliet's chamber, the Friar's cell, the crypt, shrink by contrast into cells of a honeycomb. The lovers' story is told in the alternation of those scales: whenever the pair are alone, the architecture contracts to hold them; whenever the world returns, it expands to crush them, and the crushing is literal in the tomb, where the crypt's masonry closes over the final duet like a lid.

The balcony itself is the design's thesis statement. In the play the balcony is a piece of theatrical convention, a window above a garden, and the poetry does the rest. Georgiadis set the balcony high in a real wall, so that Juliet's first appearance is of a small figure in a large façade, framed by the house that owns her, and her descent to the garden, the production's signature action, becomes an architectural event: the daughter leaving the structure, vertically, of her own will. The same grammar governs the bedroom, where the great bed stands in a room scaled to remind the audience whose house this marriage has been smuggled into, and the potion scene, where the bed waits at the scene's end as both refuge and rehearsal for the tomb. Critics of the designs have occasionally found the sets heavy, slow to change, monumental at the expense of intimacy; the charge is fair about the scene changes and wrong about the meaning. The weight is the meaning. A Verona that could be struck in a blackout would be a Verona the lovers could escape, and the whole point of Georgiadis's masonry is that nobody escapes it. When Birmingham Royal Ballet replaced the designs with Paul Andrews's lighter solution in 1992, the production survived the exchange, proving the choreography's independence; but the London originals remain the standard of comparison because they complete the ballet's argument, giving the feud a body of stone to match the bodies of the dancers it destroys.

## The Casting Dispute: Seymour, Gable and the Price of the American Tour

The best-documented scandal in the production's history is its first cast. The roles of Romeo and Juliet were made on, rehearsed by and intended for Lynn Seymour and Christopher Gable, who had created the balcony duet in Canada and around whose bodies and temperaments MacMillan had designed the entire choreographic argument. The premiere was danced by Margot Fonteyn and Rudolf Nureyev. Between the intention and the event stands one of the coldest transactions in ballet history, and the documentary record allows it to be told from both sides.

The management's case was economic, and it was not frivolous. The ballet had been commissioned for the American tour, and the tour's impresario, Sol Hurok, stated the condition plainly: the new Romeo would be included in the American repertory, and would be profitable there, only if Fonteyn and Nureyev danced the title roles. The pair were, by 1965, the most bankable partnership in world ballet. Their association had begun three years earlier, in 1962, and had converted Fonteyn, then in her forties and presumed to be approaching retirement, into half of a phenomenon that filled theatres on two continents. A Royal Ballet tour of the United States was an expensive machine that ran on exactly that kind of drawing power. From the administration's desk, the equation was simple: the ballet existed because the tour existed, the tour existed because the stars existed, and the stars would open the ballet.

The creators' case was artistic, and Parry's biography documents its cost in detail. MacMillan was furious at the decision and, on Ruth Leon's summary of the record, threatened to withdraw the ballet altogether; Seymour persuaded him to let the premiere proceed. Her own position was stranger and more generous than simple displacement. She remained in the building as the second-cast Juliet, and she coached the interpretation from inside: the British Ballet Now and Then survey of the production, citing the scholarly literature, records that it was Seymour who taught the role to the other ballerinas cast as Juliet, while Fonteyn, in Seymour's recollection, wanted to create a Juliet of her own rather than inherit one. The choreography had been built to display a particular kind of dramatic dancer: Seymour's Juliet was conceived as a creature of impulse and appetite, a girl whose body decided before her mind caught up. Fonteyn's art was of a different order, lyrical, musical, distilled, and she was forty-five years old playing a character the play fixes at not quite fourteen: "Come Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3). Nureyev was twenty-six. Seymour was twenty-five, Gable twenty-four.

### What did the decision cost the dancers it displaced?

The cost fell unevenly and permanently: Seymour left the Royal Ballet after the slight to dance with the German Opera Ballet in West Berlin, absenting herself for three years before returning in 1970, while Gable's disillusion ran deeper still, feeding the frustration that carried him out of dancing altogether and into acting and, eventually, company direction.

The long view complicates any simple verdict of theft. Fonteyn and Nureyev did not merely occupy the roles; they enlarged the ballet's life. The premiere triumphed beyond the company's hopes, the American tour prospered, and Fonteyn's late career acquired one of its defining chapters: she herself, in her autobiography, grouped the partnership's Romeo and Juliet with her new-found Giselle and Marguerite and Armand as the roles that held a special significance for her. She and Nureyev returned to the ballet together at Covent Garden for a final full performance on 10 January 1976, eleven years after the first night. Against that, the second cast's performances were themselves acclaimed: Seymour and Gable danced the roles at an early performance in the run and received, in the standard accounts, rave reviews, if not the same pitch of public delirium. The documentary record therefore supports neither the management's implication that only the stars could carry the work nor the romantics' implication that the premiere was a falsification. It supports a harder judgement, which this article adopts: the first night was a magnificent performance of a ballet that had been built for different bodies, and the proof that the ballet, rather than the occasion, was the achievement is that it has never needed either pair since.

Andrew Porter of the Financial Times, the first critic to put the casting change into print, made precisely that reservation at the time: the ballet, he argued, could not be fully understood until Seymour performed the role designed for her. Porter's dissent is the hinge of the critical history, and it is adjudicated in full in the first-night section below. Its importance here is that it was right in its narrow claim and wrong in its implied one. The narrow claim, that the intended Juliet had not been seen on the first night, was unanswerable. The implied claim, that the work's identity waited on its originals, has been refuted by six decades of casting.

## The Balcony Pas de Deux: The Duet the Whole Ballet Grew From

Every account of the ballet's making returns to the balcony duet, because the duet came first: choreographed in three rehearsals for Canadian television in September 1964, then expanded into the climax of the ballet's first act. Its position in the structure already marks a decision. Prokofiev's score places the balcony scene at the end of the first act's arc, and MacMillan, planning the ballet around the lovers' duets, made the duet the act's culmination and the work's seed crystal. What happens in it can be described plainly. Juliet appears above; Romeo, in the garden below, watches her unseen, as in the play's orchard scene; but where the spoken scene holds the lovers apart for two hundred lines of alternating soliloquy and dialogue, MacMillan collapses the distance. Juliet descends to him. The duet that follows is built on lifts of an abandon that contemporary accounts struggled to describe in a decorous vocabulary: Juliet flung and caught, arched backward over Romeo's shoulder, carried at the run, the pair turning in long, breathless phrases that end, when parting can no longer be postponed, with the two of them straining toward one another, hands reaching for one last contact before the separation closes.

The play's text shows what the choreography is translating, and the translation is exact in spirit. The orchard scene opens in separation and overhearing: "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2). Romeo speaks a soliloquy to a girl who cannot hear him; Juliet speaks her desire to the night, not knowing he is there; the famous exchange, once begun, is an argument about names, safety and haste as much as a love duet. Ballet cannot overhear. It cannot give the audience two private monologues in counterpoint. MacMillan's solution was to convert the scene's verbal suspense into physical distance and its closure: separation is vertical, the girl above and the boy below, and the duet begins at the moment the vertical gives way. The descent is the scene's whole psychology in one action, the daughter of the house coming down into the garden of her own will, which is exactly the quality Parry records in the creators' conception: this Juliet decides. Readers who want the spoken scene's machinery laid out line by line will find it in [the balcony scene's own text, staging problems and verbal architecture](/2023/07/21/balcony-scene-act-2-scene-2-analysis/), against which the duet's liberties can be measured precisely.

The duet also sets the ballet's method for everything that follows. MacMillan, the estate's account of his practice notes, avoided virtuoso display steps for their own sake, holding them too conventionally balletic for the drama he intended; the balcony duet's difficulty is real but it is the difficulty of partnering, of weight given and caught, not of exhibition. The lifts read as trust before they read as technique. Later casts have described the duet's particular hazard: it must look unpremeditated, an overflow, while being timed to the bar. That doubleness is the production in miniature.

Critics fastened on the duet from the beginning, and the later scholarship has kept it at the centre. The recorded 1984 performance by Alessandra Ferri, discussed in the film section below, preserves the duet's closing image in the form that has become canonical: the lovers straining toward each other in their desire to touch one another's hands one more time before parting. The image answers the play's own parting line, "Parting is such sweet sorrow" (2.2), by translating sorrow into reach: two bodies at full extension, the distance between the fingertips measuring everything the feud is about to do to them. A duet that can carry that much meaning in a gesture has earned its place as the ballet's foundation stone, and the fact that it was made first, in three rehearsals, for two dancers who did not dance the premiere, is the production's founding irony.

## Verona in the Round: Crowds, Harlots and the Dance of the Knights

A Romeo that begins and ends with two dancers would falsify the play, whose lovers are destroyed not by each other but by a city's habits. MacMillan's Verona is populated with a density that reviewers noticed at once: marketplaces full of traders, idlers, swordsmen and street women, a ballroom of masked courtiers, a populace that brawls, gapes, mourns and dances as one body. The production opens not with the lovers but with the city at violence, a street fight in the morning marketplace, and MacMillan made an early, deliberate deviation from the play by putting Romeo himself inside the opening brawl alongside Mercutio. The play keeps its hero out of the first scene's "mutiny" (Prologue), reserving him for lovesick solitude. The ballet's Romeo is social from the first bar, one of the boys, and the choice pays off across the evening: when the same young man later kills Tybalt in the same public space, the audience has seen the world that taught his hand the sword.

The marketplace scenes carry the production's most talked-about population: the three harlots, flamboyant street women who tease, provoke and partner the three friends, Romeo, Mercutio and Benvolio, in dances of open erotic comedy. Their presence, adopted under the example of Cranko's Stuttgart staging, does structural work beyond local colour. They establish a Verona in which sex is public, commercial and joked about, the world of the play's own bawdy servants and of Mercutio's Queen Mab wit, so that the lovers' private passion registers against it as something categorically different: not appetite but recognition. They also give the corps de ballet characters rather than decoration. The Harvard Crimson's reviewer of the film version, writing in 1966, praised exactly this dimension, noting that when the stage is full for the crowd scenes MacMillan coordinates the whole company with great skill, and that the close camera work required every dancer to mime convincingly, which the company did. Coordination is the right word: the crowds are choreographed as weather systems, surging and eddying, so that individual destinies cross them like swimmers.

### What does the Dance of the Knights do to the lovers' meeting?

It surrounds the meeting with everything that will kill it: the ball's great set piece is a heavy, processional dance of the Capulet men, all weight and heraldic pride, and Romeo and Juliet first see each other inside its intervals, so that the love the evening celebrates is introduced already framed by the feud's ceremony and the house's armed dignity.

The ball sequence is the crowd principle turned ceremonial. Prokofiev's score gives the ball its famous processional weight, and readers following the music's construction will find its themes and their dramatic duties laid out in [Prokofiev's ballet score and the Dance of the Knights](/2028/11/24/prokofiev-ballet-dance-of-knights/), the companion study of the score itself. MacMillan's choreography answers the music with massed, deliberate movement: the Capulet knights advance like a dynasty on parade, Lady Capulet presides, Paris is presented, and Juliet, introduced to the company as a girl still half a child, meets the masked Romeo in the dance's lee. The meeting is choreographed as an exchange of looks across a pattern, then as a brief duet of discovery while the ball revolves around them, the ballet equivalent of the shared sonnet of 1.5, in which the play lets the lovers compose a poem together in public without anyone hearing it. Ballet cannot hide a duet in a crowd by lowering voices; MacMillan hides his in motion, the lovers still while the room turns, a private axis inside a public wheel.

## The Ball: A Sonnet Danced in Public

The meeting deserves a section of its own, because it solves the adaptation's first great problem. Shakespeare stages the encounter as a formal poem: Romeo, masked, takes Juliet's hand and speaks the first quatrain of a sonnet, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this" (1.5), and Juliet, without missing a beat, completes the form with him, the two of them composing fourteen lines together, pilgrim and saint, before the Nurse's interruption breaks the spell. The scene's meaning lives in the form: two strangers discover, in public, that they speak the same language in the same measure, and the audience hears a harmony that no one else in the room can hear. A ballet has no lines to share. What it has is synchrony, and MacMillan builds the meeting out of exactly that: the pair fall into matching phrases inside the ball's larger patterns, her steps answering his with a precision the corps around them does not share, so that the audience watches two bodies discover a common measure in the middle of a crowd dancing to another one. The sonnet's privacy inside formality becomes the duet's stillness inside motion, and the translation is faithful at the level that matters, the level of what the scene proves about the lovers: they are attuned before they are acquainted.

Tybalt's recognition of the intruder interrupts the play's meeting from outside, Capulet's rebuke holding the peace for one night only, and MacMillan stages the same interruption as a choreographic chill, the favourite's stillness spreading through the dance. The ball then closes with the production's most quietly brilliant stroke of structure: Juliet learns Romeo's name from the Nurse, as in the play, and the act moves directly to the balcony, the public discovery and the private surrender bound into one continuous ascent of feeling. Critics of narrative ballet sometimes complain that the form must choose between the story's incidents and its inwardness; the first act of this production is the standing refutation, an act that contains a brawl, a ball, a sonnet, a recognition and a duet, and still arrives at its climax feeling unhurried.

## Juliet the Decision-Maker: The Production's Central Reversal

The creators' conception, preserved by Parry, was that Juliet decides. MacMillan and Seymour discussed the character at length and settled on a headstrong, passionate girl who makes every crucial choice: the secret marriage against her parents' wishes, the taking of the Friar's potion, the joining of Romeo in death. The choreography enacts the conception with a consistency that amounts to an interpretation of the play, one that later scholarship on the text has largely ratified. Shakespeare's Juliet is the tragedy's true agent. It is she who sends the Nurse to learn Romeo's name and then to arrange the marriage (2.2, 2.3, 2.5), she who answers the Friar's desperate expedient with "Give me, give me! O, tell not me of fear" (4.1), and she who, waking in the tomb, solves the dagger problem in a sentence: "O happy dagger, / This is thy sheath" (5.3). MacMillan simply built the ballet on the character the words describe.

Her first appearance states the starting point. MacMillan introduces Juliet in her room as a child among toys, a girl who plays with a doll and climbs into the Nurse's lap, teased and dressed by the household like a pet of the family. The childishness is not decoration; it is the baseline against which the evening measures one of the fastest maturations in drama. Within the act she has met Romeo, and MacMillan gives her ballroom variation a new alertness, the body discovering its own effect. By the balcony duet she is descending of her own will. The choreography's arc for Juliet is therefore not the standard ballet arc from innocence to victimhood but a progress of decisions, each danced solo or duet a degree more deliberate than the last, until the final act leaves her alone on the stage with a vial and makes her choice the ballet's climax.

The parents' counter-claim gets its full choreographic weight, which is what makes the decisions costly. MacMillan's Capulets are not ogres; the ball shows a household at its most splendid and affectionate, and the later scene of the forced marriage to Paris is staged as a family catastrophe, Juliet pleading, the father raging, the mother withdrawing her protection, the Nurse advising surrender. In the play the same scene (3.5) gives Capulet the language of disposal: "An you be mine, I'll give you to my friend; / An you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets." The ballet translates the speech into handling: Juliet is seized, shaken, pushed between the adults, her body passed around the room like the property the words declare her to be. Against that handling, the stillness of her later solos reads as the recovery of ownership. She is the only character in the production whose movement vocabulary simplifies as the evening proceeds, from the skittish runs of the girl with the doll to the long, unbroken lines of the woman who drinks and dies. Simplification is MacMillan's signature for resolve.

## Romeo Swept Off His Feet: The Hero Reimagined

The creators' conception distributed the tragedy's energies asymmetrically, and the Romeo half of it is as deliberate as the Juliet half. Parry records the formula: Juliet decides, while Romeo is swept off his feet by love. The play supports the reading more fully than its reputation suggests. Shakespeare's Romeo enters lovesick for another woman, is talked into the ball by Mercutio's teasing, falls at first sight, and thereafter reacts at a pitch of feeling that outruns his judgement in scene after scene: scaling the orchard wall, weeping on the Friar's floor, killing Tybalt in a transport, despairing in Mantua. His great actions are precipitated; hers are resolved upon. MacMillan choreographed the distinction. His Romeo is given the ballet's most impetuous movement vocabulary, runs, leaps and turns that arrive before their musical cue seems to permit, partnering of a hunger that the balcony duet barely contains, and a quality the creators prized in Gable's original interpretation, the sense of a boy whose body is the first part of him to understand anything.

The arc the choreography draws for him is the reverse of Juliet's. She simplifies into resolve; he disintegrates out of sociability. The first act shows a Romeo embedded in his world, brawling, courting Rosaline in the marketplace's public theatre of desire, dancing among friends as one of Verona's golden boys; the ball singles him out of that world, the balcony completes the secession, and the wedding makes the secession permanent. From the killing of Tybalt onward his dancing loses its social floor: the bedroom duet is fought against departure, the Mantua exile is the play's own sentence rendered as absence from the ballet's populated scenes, and the tomb gives him the production's most extreme solo statement, the dance with the unanswering body, in which the partnering vocabulary of the whole evening is performed by one partner alone. The conception explains the role's strange difficulty for virtuosi. Nureyev, whose technique could do anything, had to learn to let the character be done to, to surrender the initiative the choreography assigns to Juliet, and the tension between the dancer's native command and the role's required helplessness became the famous friction of his performances. Later Romeos have found the same demand waiting for them: the part is a study in being overtaken, and it exposes any dancer who insists on remaining in charge of it.

## Act Two: The Wedding and the Hinge of Violence

The second act is the ballet's hinge, and MacMillan constructed it as a deliberate descent: it opens in the same marketplace sunshine as the first act, passes through the secret wedding, and ends with two corpses and a mother's grief, the comedy and the catastrophe sharing one public square. The dramaturgy follows the play's own hinge, the third act's first scene, in which the tragedy turns on a point of honour under a hot sky: "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1). In ballet the hinge must be choreographic, and MacMillan made it a matter of styles. The act's early dances are social, patterned, full of the harlots' teasing and Mercutio's wit; the fights that end the act abandon pattern for scramble. The audience watches a world lose its choreography, which is as precise a definition of the play's middle as criticism has produced.

The wedding itself is the act's still centre and its boldest compression. The play marries the lovers almost offhandedly, in the Friar's cell between other business, with the ceremony itself unshown: the audience of the play never sees a wedding, only its before and after. MacMillan likewise keeps the rite brief and private, a short, solemn pas de deux in the cell with the Friar presiding and, in an invention the film preserves, the Nurse in attendance, the whole scene played in a hush that the surrounding acts make audible. The compression is interpretive. A grand wedding ballet would declare the marriage the story's triumph; MacMillan's quiet cell declares it the story's secret, a private sacrament smuggled through a public war, and the brevity means the act can spend its length where the tragedy spends its own: on the square, in the heat, with the swords.

Mercutio's death is the production's test of tone, and MacMillan passed it the way the play demands: the dying man jokes. The choreography keeps Mercutio's wit in his body to the end, the character dancing his defiance, clowning his wound as unreal, "Ay, ay, a scratch, a scratch" (3.1), before the collapse that tells the truth. David Blair, the original Mercutio, set the performance tradition here, and the role remains the production's measure of a dancer-actor. Romeo's killing of Tybalt then reverses the ballet's partnering grammar: the young man whose lifts had been all tenderness turns the same strength to murder, and MacMillan stages the fight as a loss of control rather than a duel, Romeo possessed, Tybalt cornered and finished. The act closes not on the killer but on the bereaved: Lady Capulet cradling Tybalt's body, her grief given a solo of lamentation over the corpse while the court gathers. In the play, Lady Capulet's response to the death is a demand for revenge couched in the language of law, "I beg for justice, which thou, Prince, must give" (3.1). The ballet gives her the body instead of the brief, and the substitution is the production's method in one image: where the text argues, the choreography grieves, and the grief is what the audience carries into the interval.

## Mercutio, Tybalt and Paris: The Men Who Frame the Lovers

A production that lives by its duets still needs its single figures, and MacMillan built the ballet's male world as carefully as its centre. Mercutio is the test role for every company that stages the work, because the choreography asks for a complete dancer-actor: the wit must be danced, not mimed on top of the dancing. MacMillan gives him the marketplace as a stage within the stage, teasing the harlots, sparring with friends, turning the Queen Mab energy of the play's great speech (1.4) into pure mercurial movement, quicksilver changes of direction and attack that make the character's name a choreographic fact. David Blair, the original Mercutio, established the tradition of brilliance curdling into horror: the death, in which the jokes continue past the point where the body can support them, remains the passage by which Mercutios are judged, and its power depends on the earlier gaiety having been real dancing rather than comic business pasted onto steps.

Tybalt carries the feud's other temperature. Where Mercutio is improvisation, Tybalt is procedure: Desmond Doyle's original interpretation fixed the character as the house's enforcement arm, a man whose dancing is all edge and readiness, whose appearances reorganise any crowd around the expectation of violence. The play calls him the "Prince of Cats" (3.1) and gives him a duellist's code; MacMillan gives him a movement signature of feints and lunges inside the ballroom's courtesies, so that the ball scene's polish reads as a truce maintained by effort. Paris, the role created by Derek Rencher, completes the triangle of male claims on Juliet: the sanctioned suitor, correct, decorative, and in the forced-marriage scenes an instrument of the father's will, before the tomb gives him his one moment of genuine passion, fighting the intruder he believes is desecrating a grave. The ballet is fair to Paris as the play is fair to him: his death at Romeo's hands, brutal in MacMillan's staging, is the tragedy's last unnecessary killing, and the production lets its ugliness register. Benvolio, created by Anthony Dowell, anchors the trio of friends as the survivor, the one left to tell the tale, his comparative stillness amid the brilliance around him a quiet piece of casting foresight for a dancer who would become one of the company's defining directors. Together the men form the weather system of the tragedy: wit, honour, propriety and peace-making, four answers to Verona that the city rewards with two corpses, an exile's irrelevance and a lifetime of explanation.

## The Bedroom Pas de Deux and the Potion Scene: Act Three as Juliet's Act

The third act belongs to Juliet, and MacMillan built it as a triptych: the bedroom pas de deux at dawn, the confrontation over Paris, and the potion soliloquy, each panel narrower and lonelier than the one before. The bedroom duet translates the aubade of 3.5, the lovers' argument with the morning: "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day. / It was the nightingale, and not the lark" (3.5). In the play the scene is a debate conducted in birdcalls, each lover arguing the other into staying or going. MacMillan danced it as a duet of reluctance and return: Romeo repeatedly drawn toward departure, Juliet repeatedly recalling him, the partnering full of the balcony duet's lifts, weighted with the knowledge that the marriage has been consummated and the world has already turned murderous outside the window. Critics and memoirists single out the bedroom duet's emotional temperature, its refusal of prettiness: this is not a wedding night celebrated but a farewell resisted, ending with Romeo's descent and Juliet alone at the window with her premonition, the play's "Methinks I see thee, now thou art below, / As one dead in the bottom of a tomb" (3.5) rendered as a held image rather than a speech.

The potion scene is the production's most analysed passage, because it poses the adaptation problem in its purest form. The play gives Juliet a soliloquy of forty-odd lines (4.3) in which inward terror is inventoried aloud: the fear that the mixture will not work, that it will kill her, that she will wake too early among the bones of her ancestors and go mad. Other choreographers reached for the supernatural to stage it: Lavrovsky surrounded his Juliet with the domain of the unreal, and Nureyev, in his later version, peopled the scene with the ghosts of the dead. Karen Bennett's scholarly comparison of the three Prokofiev stagings identifies MacMillan's contrary method exactly: no spectres, no vision, only external behaviour, a frantic, anguished dance in which Juliet hurls the vial to the floor before sinking catatonic onto the bed.

### Why does Juliet hurl the vial to the floor?

Because MacMillan stages the soliloquy as behaviour rather than vision: the thrown vial is the fear speech of 4.3 converted into action, the moment doubt becomes a physical act that must then be physically reversed when she retrieves the potion, composes herself and drinks, deciding with her hands what the play's Juliet decides in words.

This article adjudicates the comparison in MacMillan's favour, on the play's own evidence. The soliloquy in 4.3 is not a dream; it is a mind arguing itself into an act, and its horrors are hypotheses, each raised and dismissed: "What if this mixture do not work at all?" The terror is rational in form, however wild in content, and it ends in a toast: "Romeo, Romeo, Romeo! Here's drink. I drink to thee" (4.3). A staging that supplies the ghosts mistakes the speech for its own nightmare. MacMillan's empty room, a girl, a bed, a vial, keeps the scene's true subject in view: a fourteen-year-old alone, talking herself past fear into the bravest act in the play. Bennett's description of the method as mere externality underrates what the externality performs, since the hurling and retrieval of the vial is the argument of the speech made visible, premise and conclusion in two gestures. The realism for which the production was praised and blamed in 1965 finds here its strongest justification.

## The Tomb Scene: Dancing with the Dead

The tomb scene is where the production's reputation for realism stops being a compliment about crowds and becomes something sterner. MacMillan staged the final scene as a sequence of refusals: no transfiguration, no apotheosis, no lovers folded decorously into a shared death. Romeo enters the Capulet crypt, kills Paris in a fight of documented brutality, the Northern Ballet performances of the choreography described in the British Ballet Now and Then survey having Romeo smash Paris's head against the wall of the tomb, the nearest weapon to hand. He then turns to the bier and does the thing for which the scene is famous and infamous: he dances with Juliet's body. Believing her dead, he lifts the drugged girl and attempts the steps of their earlier duets with a partner who cannot answer, wrapping her limbs around his, carrying her through the choreography of the balcony and the bedroom with a body gone slack in his arms. The Harvard Crimson's film reviewer, in 1966, identified the demand the scene makes: MacMillan passes the toughest test in his delicate handling of the tomb, where the dying are trying to make love to the dead, a potentially macabre situation without the saving grace of the playwright's poetry to dignify it. Delicacy is the surprising word and the right one; the horror of the passage is inseparable from its tenderness, and both are choreographic discoveries, things danced that the play can only narrate after the fact in the Friar's horrified summary.

Romeo's poison and death follow, and then the production plays its final card: Juliet wakes. In the play the waking is a matter of minutes and lines, the Friar at her side, the watch approaching, the dagger found and used with terrible efficiency (5.3). MacMillan strips the scene of its machinery and leaves the girl alone with the corpse. His Juliet drags herself across the tomb toward Romeo's body, reaching for his hand, and dies stretched toward a contact she does not achieve. Lynn Seymour, recording MacMillan's intention in her autobiography, states it as doctrine: the death scene was crucial to the choreographer; his lovers were not reunited in death; they did not die in each other's arms. For MacMillan, in Seymour's summary, the deaths were a complete waste of young life, and the ending was built to indict everyone who had precipitated them. The families' reconciliation, the play's closing machinery of golden statues and mutual pardon, is compressed by the production into the briefest of closing images, the grieving houses confronted with the bodies, and the balance of the ending falls entirely on loss. Where the play ends with a speech, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3), the ballet ends with a tableau that trusts the audience to supply the sentence. It is the boldest adaptation decision in the work, and the one that most divides description from description in the literature: writers formed by nineteenth-century ballet, in which the tomb is a place of reunion and transcendence, have found the ending bleak to the point of refusal, while the production's defenders answer that bleakness is the tragedy's actual temperature, and that the play's own reconciliation has always rung hollow against two fresh corpses and a Prince's exhausted judgement.

### Why does Romeo dance with Juliet's body in the tomb?

Because MacMillan builds the finale as a distorted reprise: Romeo attempts the lifts and phrases of the balcony and bedroom duets with a partner who cannot respond, so that the audience watches the ballet's whole vocabulary of love performed upon a body the potion has emptied, the technique of trust exhibited at the moment trust becomes impossible.

The waking solo that precedes her death has become, in performance tradition, the production's final measure of a Juliet. Eyewitness accounts of the great interpreters dwell on the same physical facts: the girl hauling herself across the tomb slab toward the body, the search for the hand, the refusal of any arranged pietà. Alessandra Ferri's performances were described in those terms by the balletomanes who chronicled them, the dying Juliet crawling to find Romeo's hand for one last grasp before the stillness, and the filmed record of the production's tomb closes in the same spirit, the camera finding Juliet's hands reaching through the crypt's grille toward a contact the choreography withholds. The detail matters because it states the ending's doctrine in miniature: contact in this ballet is always effort, never gift, and death does not repeal the rule.

## Forty-Three Curtain Calls: The First Night and Its Dissenters

The first night, 9 February 1965, entered theatrical folklore before the week was out. Fonteyn and Nureyev took forty-three curtain calls, and the Christie's catalogue summary of the production's history, drawing on the contemporary record, gives the applause at forty minutes and reports that the Evening Standard held it to be a theatrical record; the iron curtain had finally to be lowered to persuade the audience to leave. The response exceeded even the delirium that had greeted the partnership's debut three years earlier. The notices matched the noise: the Observer, the Daily Mail and the Sunday Telegraph were among the papers whose critics praised the production as a triumph and a landmark addition to the company's repertory, and the ballet's commercial destiny was sealed when the American tour confirmed the London verdict in box-office terms.

The aftermath consolidated with unusual speed. The production passed at once into the repertory as a signature work rather than a novelty under probation, the American tour carried the London verdict across the Atlantic, and the ballet began the accumulation of casts and performances that has never since paused. The first season already contained the pattern of the whole afterlife: the star cast generating the legend, the second cast generating the connoisseurs' counter-legend, and the choreography collecting the credit for both. That pattern is why the first night, for all its noise, is best read as the overture to the work's history rather than its climax; the applause measured the partnership's drawing power, while the following sixty years measured the ballet.

Against the chorus stood one reservation, and it has proved the most durable piece of first-night criticism the production received. Andrew Porter, the Financial Times critic and the most musically literate reviewer on the London aisle, was the first to discuss in print the last-minute casting change, and his judgement was a suspended one: the ballet could not be fully understood until Lynn Seymour performed the role that had been designed on her. The reservation deserves its longevity because it identifies a real epistemological problem in the criticism of new choreography. A ballet made on particular bodies carries its meanings in those bodies' qualities; substitute two performers of genius but of entirely different formation, a lyrical classicist of forty-five and a Tartar virtuoso of twenty-six for a pair of dramatic modernists in their twenties, and the premiere audience has seen a performance, not the work. Porter's point was methodological, and within its limits it was unanswerable: the first-night critics who pronounced on MacMillan's Juliet had not seen her.

The adjudication this article reaches is that Porter wins the point and loses the argument. He wins the point because the record confirms that Seymour's Juliet, when she danced it in the run's early performances with Gable, disclosed qualities the premiere had not shown: a rebelliousness and appetite that later critics, Alastair Macaulay among them, praised in terms quite distinct from the Fonteyn legend. Macaulay, recalling the first performances in the New York Times in 2007, wrote that if there was a single moment in his life that turned him into a ballet obsessive, the Fonteyn and Nureyev performance was it, and in the same critical breath lauded Seymour's rebellious Juliet, holding both interpretations in one canon. That double testimony is the resolution of the first-night dispute. The work contained both Juliets, the legend and the original, and has gone on containing their successors for six decades. Porter loses the argument because his reservation implied that the ballet's identity was hostage to its intended cast, and the production's subsequent history, dozens of casts, two home companies, an unbroken place in the repertory, demonstrates that the choreography itself was the durable quantity all along. The stars made the premiere an event. The steps made it a classic.

## The Seymour Tradition: The Juliet the Work Was Built For

Because the premiere went to other dancers, the interpretation the choreography was designed to carry had to establish itself in the run's ordinary performances, and it did so through Seymour herself. Her Juliet, by the testimony of those who saw the early casts and by the critical vocabulary that attached to it, was a creature of appetite and revolt: Macaulay's word for it, four decades later, was rebellious, and the adjective aligns with everything the creators intended, the headstrong girl of the studio discussions made flesh. Where Fonteyn's Juliet distilled the role into line and musicality, a woman remembering girlhood with perfect tact, Seymour's was the girl herself, impulsive, sensual, impatient of the household that caged her, a Juliet whose decisions looked like reflexes because they began in the body. The two readings define the role's poles, and every subsequent Juliet in the production has had to negotiate between them, which is the precise sense in which the ballet, rather than either ballerina, owns the interpretation: the choreography contains both possibilities because it was built on one and premiered by the other.

Seymour's guardianship of the role extended beyond her own performances. She had taught the part to the production's first generation of Juliets even while displaced from its premiere, an arrangement the scholarly literature records with some wonder, and she returned to it as a teacher for the rest of her career, giving a documented masterclass on the balcony pas de deux for the Royal Academy of Dance's Revealing MacMillan project in 2002, in which the duet's construction was unpacked by the woman on whom it had been made thirty-eight years earlier. Her autobiography, Lynn, published in 1984, remains a primary source for the whole episode, generous to Fonteyn, unsparing about the management, and exact about MacMillan's intentions for the ending. Gable's afterlife in the story is the counterweight: the Romeo who created the role's dramatic naturalism left classical dancing, acted, and in time directed Northern Ballet Theatre, for which he devised his own Romeo and Juliet in the 1990s, the creator of MacMillan's hero returning to the tragedy as a maker in his own right. The production absorbed their loss and kept their fingerprints; the duets still bear the shape of the bodies that first rehearsed them in a Canadian television studio, however many celebrated pairs have since worn them.

## Fonteyn and Nureyev: The Partnership Inside the Roles

The first-night partnership deserves analysis on its own terms, because Fonteyn and Nureyev in these roles were not the choreography's intended instruments and became, by performance, one of its definitive readings. The age arithmetic is the first fact every account confronts: Fonteyn was forty-five, an age at which ballerinas of her generation had long retired, playing a girl of thirteen; Nureyev was twenty-six, a defected Soviet virtuoso whose technique and temperament represented the art form's unruly future. The pairing should not have worked as a picture of adolescent love, and in the narrow sense of verisimilitude it did not try to: Fonteyn's Juliet was never a impersonation of a teenager but a distillation of first love into its essentials, wonder, ardour and a musical intelligence that made the role's transitions feel inevitable. Her art had always been the art of phrasing, of breath made visible, and MacMillan's duets, built on long lines and sustained balances, turned out to suit the distillation astonishingly well. Nureyev's Romeo, by contrast, was appetite and attack: a young man whose dancing carried the insolence of someone who had crossed a continent by defection and saw no reason to ask permission of a fiction. The chemistry the era celebrated was the friction between those qualities, her containment against his expenditure, and the balcony duet in their hands became less a boy's courtship of a girl than a negotiation between two complete, incompatible worlds that briefly agreed.

The partnership had begun only in 1962, and the Romeo commission arrived at its height, when their joint appearances were international events and their Giselle and Marguerite and Armand had already rewritten Fonteyn's late career. The production's American destiny, the tour for which Hurok had demanded their names, made them ambassadors of a work that was not theirs, and by every commercial measure the ambassadorship succeeded beyond argument: the ballet's fame in the United States, on which its world standing partly rests, was built in their performances. The American demand that justified the tour's casting condition was real, and its temperature survives in an unlikely source: the Harvard Crimson's film reviewer, writing in November 1966, opened the notice by recalling that people had been doing irrational things to get into the production when Fonteyn and Nureyev danced it in America. The sentence is an undergraduate's hyperbole with a documentary core. The partnership's American appearances were scrambles, the ballet's tour a triumph of exactly the kind Hurok had promised the Covent Garden administration, and the production's rapid elevation from London novelty to international property cannot be separated from the machinery of that demand. The grievance and the gratitude are both permanent parts of the record, and any honest history of the premiere has to hold them together: the same transaction that wronged Seymour and Gable carried their choreography around the world.

Fonteyn's own valuation survives in her autobiography, where the roles she associated with the partnership, the new-found Giselle, Marguerite and Armand and Romeo and Juliet, are singled out as holding a special significance for her, the testimony of a dancer who knew precisely what the collaboration had given her Indian summer of a career. The pair's final full performance of the ballet together at Covent Garden came on 10 January 1976, closing an eleven-year chapter. What the partnership could not do was exhaust the work, as the Seymour tradition running in parallel demonstrated from the first month; and the healthiest evidence for the production's stature is that its history can honour both truths at once, that Fonteyn and Nureyev made the premiere immortal, and that the ballet would have survived, on the evidence of its second cast and its six subsequent decades, without them.

## The Czinner Film: The Production on Celluloid

Within a year of the premiere the production had been fixed on film, and the film, for all its compromises, remains the primary evidence for what the first cast did. Paul Czinner, the veteran director who had filmed stage performances since the silent era, directed a cinema version released in 1966, financed under the joint Rank and National Film Finance Corporation initiative and performed by the premiere cast: Fonteyn and Nureyev, David Blair's Mercutio, Desmond Doyle's Tybalt, Anthony Dowell's Benvolio, Julia Farron's Lady Capulet, Michael Somes's Lord Capulet and Gerd Larsen's Nurse. Duncan Petrie's study of the financing scheme records the outcome dryly: the film received some strong reviews but was a box-office disappointment, a fate shared by much filmed theatre in a decade when cinema audiences wanted cinema, not a proscenium in a can.

The strong reviews repay attention because they adjudicated, early and intelligently, the standing argument about filming ballet at all. The Harvard Crimson's notice took the objection head-on: some critics had complained that the camera crippled the ballet with its frequent close-ups, but the reviewer answered that the best-equipped balletomanes had trained opera glasses on the stars for years to gain precisely the close-up effect, and the lens merely did the work of the glasses. The same review made the clarity claim that matters for this article's larger argument: the production was no mere foil for two virtuosi, since MacMillan's choreography told the story with a clarity the reviewer called no easy achievement for a wordless medium, family relationships established, letters delivered and understood, the plot made clear and moving. That a student newspaper in Cambridge, Massachusetts, reviewing the film in November 1966, isolated exactly the qualities the scholarship would later formalise, the narrative legibility, the ensemble coordination, the tomb scene's delicacy, suggests how immediately legible the production's virtues were to a fresh eye with no stake in Covent Garden politics.

The film's limitations are equally a matter of record: the mono sound buries the score's detail, the stage makeup reads harshly at close range, and Czinner's prefatory claim to have discovered a new way of filming dance theatre overstates a competent, respectful technique. The film preserves, though, what no prose can: Fonteyn's musicality in the role at forty-six, the partnership's particular chemistry of her stillness against his attack, and Blair's Mercutio before the role passed to other traditions. Later recordings took over the evidentiary burden with better technology and equal distinction. The 1984 Royal Ballet performance with Alessandra Ferri and Wayne Eagling, filmed under Colin Nears's direction, became the reference version for a generation and the standing proof, cited in the scholarly survey literature, that MacMillan's fear for his intended Juliet need not have survived contact with the role's later interpreters: Ferri, who had danced Juliet for MacMillan as a very young ballerina, embodies the Seymour line of the interpretation, all appetite and decision, within the production Fonteyn made famous. Ross MacGibbon's 2012 film of Lauren Cuthbertson and Federico Bonelli extended the record into the high-definition era, and the ninety-minute abridgment devised by Michael Nunn and William Trevitt, broadcast in 2020, carried the production to audiences who would never buy a full-length ballet ticket. Each recording re-litigates the first night's question, and each returns the same verdict: the work survives its interpreters.

The film's American career illustrates the production's peculiar double life in the 1960s: a stage phenomenon so hot that, as the Crimson's reviewer opened by recalling, people had done irrational things to obtain tickets when Fonteyn and Nureyev danced it in America, and a cinema commodity that the trade found merely respectable. The discrepancy is instructive. What the stage performances sold was presence, the era's most famous partnership in the flesh, in a theatre, on a night that could not be repeated; what the film sold was the choreography, and in 1966 the choreography stripped of presence had not yet acquired the canonical status that would later make the recording precious. Posterity has reversed the valuation. The presence is gone, recoverable only through the film, and the choreography's canonicity has made Czinner's respectful document into the closest thing the production has to a first edition, consulted by coaches and casts as evidence of tempi, emphases and the originators' choices. Filmed ballet is often treated as a compromise formation between theatre and cinema; in this production's history it functioned instead as an ark, carrying the Fonteyn and Nureyev reading across the decades until scholarship and revival could meet it on the far side.

## A Signature Work Abroad: America, Birmingham and the Long Afterlife

The production's second life began as a homecoming for the man who made it. MacMillan staged his Romeo for companies across the world, the Royal Swedish Ballet among them, and in the 1980s he took an associate directorship at American Ballet Theatre and gave the ballet a second home there. The ABT company premiere took place on 3 January 1985 at the Kennedy Center in Washington, with Leslie Browne as Juliet and Robert La Fosse as Romeo, both chosen and coached by the choreographer. Browne's testimony about the studio work has become a capsule of MacMillan's method: the movement had to say something, she recalled; there could be no empty moments in it. The transmission ran through the original cast as well as through notation and film: Georgina Parkinson, the production's first Rosaline, became a central coach of the ABT tradition, training generations of the company's dancers in the ballet's nuanced underpinnings. ABT made the work one of its signature productions, the Romeo with which America's national company tours the world, and the partnership of the two companies in one choreographic text is a phenomenon without real parallel in the story's stage history: the same steps carrying the tragedy for the Royal Ballet at Covent Garden and for ABT at the Metropolitan Opera House, each house convinced the production is its own.

At home the afterlife was anchored in longevity. The production marked its fiftieth anniversary in 2015 as an unbroken repertory possession, opening the Royal Ballet's 2015 to 2016 season in its anniversary year and relayed live to cinemas on 22 September 2015, while the Guardian marked the milestone with a photographic survey of fifty years of the production's star-crossed dancers, the archive itself testifying to the work's function as the company's living history of itself. and one scholarly survey of the work attended the 531st Royal Opera House performance, a count that measures not nostalgia but utility: companies do not perform a ballet five hundred times out of piety. MacMillan returned to the staging at the end of his life, restaging it in 1992 for Birmingham Royal Ballet, the year of his death, with new designs by Paul Andrews replacing the Georgiadis originals for the touring company, an act of renewal that declared the choreography separable from its first designs just as the casting history had declared it separable from its first bodies. The Birmingham performances, reviewed on tour in London in 2018, showed the text intact and the drama undimmed in a smaller house, the crowds thinned by touring economics but the duets at full voltage.

Longevity of this order imposes a discipline on criticism. A production can be a period piece at fifty performances and a classic at five hundred, and the difference is not marketing but repeated exposure to new dancers, new audiences and new fashions, each revival an experiment the work either survives or does not. MacMillan's Romeo has survived the deaths of its choreographer, its first Romeo and its first Juliet, the transformation of its home company's style, the rise and fall of rival versions, and the migration of its audience from opera glasses to cinema relays to streaming. The surveys of the adaptation's stage life, such as the British Ballet Now and Then project, record the work in the vocabulary of permanence: staged regularly, filling the Royal Opera House, treated by companies and audiences alike as the Romeo against which alternatives are measured. The sixtieth anniversary of the premiere fell in 2025 and found the production exactly where the first night had put it: in the repertory, in the house, cast from the company's own ranks and received as the standard. Anniversaries in ballet are usually acts of rescue or nostalgia; this one required neither, since the work had never been away.

## The Adaptation Audit: What MacMillan Keeps, Cuts and Invents

Judged as an adaptation, the production is unusually literate, and its departures from the play are the kind that illuminate rather than decorate. MacMillan keeps the play's architecture whole: the public feud, the ball, the balcony, the secret marriage, the street killings, the banishment, the forced match, the potion, the tomb. He keeps, against balletic habit, the play's distribution of weight, in which the lovers occupy a minority of the stage time and the world that destroys them occupies the rest. He keeps the feud's anonymity: no invented grievance explains the "ancient grudge" (Prologue), and the crowds brawl because brawling is the city's custom, as causeless and compulsive as in the text.

The inventions serve legibility. Rosaline, a name in the play and never a presence, is embodied at the ball, Georgina Parkinson dancing a girl whose favour Romeo courts and whose connection to Tybalt sharpens the favourite's hostility into something an audience can watch. Romeo is put inside the opening brawl, against the play, to establish the social fact the tragedy needs, that its hero is formed by the feud's world before he is exiled from it. The Nurse is brought into the wedding cell, condensing the play's machinery of go-betweens into a single attendant figure. The reconciliation of the houses, the play's closing transaction, is compressed almost to vanishing, the production judging, correctly in this article's view, that Shakespeare's own ending spends its eloquence on the Prince's summary and the fathers' statues rather than on any felt reunion. Each invention answers a translation problem: how to show what the text can merely mention, how to motivate what the text can merely state, how to end a wordless work on an image rather than a speech.

The standing complication of the production's criticism, choreographic drama against narrative clarity, dissolves under this audit. The fear that psychological richness would blur the story, voiced in different registers from the first night onward, proved unfounded in the theatre: the Crimson's reviewer found the plot clear and moving in 1966, the crowds coordinated, the relationships legible, and sixty years of audiences innocent of the play have followed the story without a synopsis in hand. The clarity is not achieved by simplifying the drama but by assigning each fact a physical carrier: identity by costume and grouping, allegiance by colour and formation, information by prop and messenger, feeling by the duets. What ballet cannot carry, the identity of a letter's contents, the precise terms of a banishment, MacMillan declines to fake, trusting the audience's knowledge of the most famous plot in English drama to fill the legal details while the stage supplies the human ones. It is the strategy the brief literature on this production, and the scholarship since, keeps rediscovering: an adaptation that assumes its audience knows the story, and spends its entire art on the parts of the story that knowledge cannot supply, which are the parts the play itself performs rather than reports, two young bodies deciding, in real time, at the speed of music.

The audit should record the one point where the strategy reaches its limit, because the production's first film reviewer found it in 1966. The Harvard Crimson's notice, for all its praise, stated the medium's boundary exactly: it is hard for mutes to establish the family relationships involved, and when a letter is delivered the dancers can indicate that it contains good or bad news but not what news. The undelivered letter to Mantua is the tragedy's hinge, the single document on which everything turns, and no choreography can dance its text. MacMillan's production accepts the limit rather than disguising it: the Friar's dispatch and Romeo's receipt of the false report are staged as emotional events, their documentary content entrusted to the audience's foreknowledge, and the wager has held for sixty years because the foreknowledge is universal. The Crimson's reviewer, having named the limit, still concluded that MacMillan made the plot clear and moving, and the conjunction is the audit's own conclusion: the ballet is legible not because it solves the letter problem but because it confines its storytelling to the kinds of knowledge bodies can carry, and declines, with a dramatist's discipline, to mime the rest.

## The Friar, the Nurse and the Chorus That Is Not There

The production's handling of the play's supporting architecture shows the adaptation intelligence at its most surgical. The play opens with a Chorus who promises "the two hours' traffic of our stage" and tells the ending in advance; the ballet has no Chorus and wants none, since a danced prologue in mime would spend the audience's patience on information the music can carry. Prokofiev's opening bars and the raising of the curtain on Georgiadis's piazza do the Chorus's work in another medium: the city is shown, the feud is enacted within minutes, and the foreknowledge the sonnet supplies is replaced by the audience's own, since MacMillan could rely on the most famous plot in the language arriving in the theatre with its spectators. The adaptation's confidence is the point: nothing is explained that the story's fame has already explained, and everything saved by the omission is spent on enactment.

Friar Laurence, created by Ronald Hynd, is the ballet's study in good intention outrun by events. The play's Friar is a philosopher, a herbalist and a plotter whose schemes carry the tragedy's machinery; ballet cannot soliloquise about "virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied" (2.3), so MacMillan condenses the character into function and gesture: the cell scenes play as sanctuaries, small enclosed spaces in Georgiadis's scheme where the public world's rules are suspended, and the Friar's blessings, the wedding, the potion's delivery, the letter's dispatch, pass through his hands with a calm that the choreography gradually infects with panic. His failure, the undelivered letter, the mistimed arrival, is the one piece of plot machinery the ballet must trust to mime and to the audience's prior knowledge, and it is the adaptation's acknowledged wager, accepted by the Crimson's reviewer in the general verdict that the plot remained clear. The Nurse, by contrast, gains stage life in translation: the play's great comic garrulousness cannot survive wordlessness, so MacMillan rebuilds her as physical comedy and emotional ballast, the household's warm centre, Juliet's playmate in the nursery scene, her confederate at the wedding, her betrayer in the Paris crisis when the advice to forget Romeo turns the girl finally toward the Friar and the vial. Between them the two figures mark the production's tonal range, the sacred and the domestic, the two refuges the tragedy offers its heroine, both of which fail her in ways the dancing makes unmistakable. Escalus, created by Leslie Edwards, presides over the frame as the city's exhausted law, his appearances bookending the brawls with an authority the choreography deliberately renders ceremonial and impotent, the state as another piece of Quattrocento pageantry. It is a complete political reading of the play, delivered without a word.

## MacMillan and Cranko: Two Choreographers, One Score

The comparison with John Cranko is unavoidable and should be kept to its proper length, since Cranko's Stuttgart production is a subject in itself. The outlines are these: Cranko's Romeo and Juliet, made for Stuttgart Ballet in 1962 to the same Prokofiev score, was the first Western staging to prove the music's full dramatic viability, and MacMillan saw it, admired it and took from it the marketplace harlots as a living detail. The kinship ends at the temperament. Cranko's Verona is lyrical and sunlit, its tragedy an eclipse of happiness; MacMillan's is procedural and airless, its tragedy a machine of customs. Where Cranko trusts the score's lyricism to carry the lovers, MacMillan trusts friction: his duets are arguments and negotiations as much as effusions, his crowds are mobs, his deaths are ugly. The later line of descent confirms the production's position as the trunk rather than a branch. When [John Cranko's Stuttgart ballet](/2028/12/08/john-cranko-stuttgart-ballet/) is set beside the Covent Garden work, the English production emerges as the one that changed what a narrative ballet was permitted to show, and the line runs forward as well as back: the dance versions that followed, down to [Matthew Bourne's dance version](/2028/12/15/matthew-bourne-dance-version/), which relocated the story to a regimented institution for the young, work in a space MacMillan cleared, in which a Romeo may be psychologically explicit, physically candid and formally classical at once. Nureyev's own staging, made for London Festival Ballet in 1977 and reworked for the Paris Opéra in 1984, stands as the control experiment: the first Romeo choreographing his answer filled the work with spectres, symbols and an apparatus of fate, and the comparison, as Bennett's analysis implies and as audiences have generally found, flatters the restraint of the version he had premiered as a dancer twelve years before.

One asymmetry of survival quietly governs the whole comparison. Bennett's scholarly study notes that she could not examine the Cranko choreography on film because no film of it was available to her, while the MacMillan version reached her, as it reaches every student of the work, through the Czinner feature and the recordings that followed it. The accident of documentation became a structural advantage: the English production is the one that can be taught, quoted, revived from evidence and argued about in detail, and its canonicity feeds on that availability. Cranko's admirers answer, with justice, that a ballet's life is in the theatre and not the archive, and Stuttgart kept its Romeo alive on stage across the same decades. The adjudication favours neither absolutism. Transmission is a compound of stage tradition and record, and MacMillan's production is the only one of the Prokofiev Romeos to possess both in full measure, which is a fact about history rather than about merit, and a large part of why its claim to definitiveness is so hard to dislodge.

## The Ballet in MacMillan's Oeuvre: The First of the Great Tragedies

Romeo and Juliet was MacMillan's first full-length ballet, and it fixed the pattern of his maturity. The one-act works of his early career had already marked him as the British choreographer of psychological extremity, of outsiders, obsessions and damaged desire, but the three-act form posed a different problem: whether that candour could sustain an evening inside a classical vocabulary and a beloved classic. The answer of February 1965 was that candour, properly engineered, was what the form had been waiting for. The techniques the ballet established became his signatures across the great works that followed: the pas de deux as psychological negotiation rather than display, in which lifts and supports carry argument, refusal and surrender; the crowd as a moral force rather than a frame; the heroine whose solo variations chart a decision rather than a mood; the ending that declines consolation. Manon and Mayerling, the later monuments of his Covent Garden career, are unthinkable without the Verona experiment that proved audiences would follow a ballet into sexual frankness, public violence and an unredeemed double death, and would call the evening a triumph.

The production also fixed MacMillan's standing in the longer history of the story in dance. Prokofiev's score had been waiting since the 1930s for a Western staging equal to its dramaturgy, one that treated the music not as accompaniment but as the tragedy's second text. Lavrovsky had given it the Soviet monument; Cranko had given it Western lyricism; MacMillan gave it modernity, the version in which the lovers behave like recognisable young people in a recognisable machinery of family pride, public honour and chance. That is why the work, alone among the story's many ballets, is habitually described with the definite article's confidence in Britain, and why its creator's estate and the companies that stage it treat the text as a classic to be transmitted rather than a property to be refreshed. MacMillan restaged the work himself for its new homes, coached its casts into his eighties-born authority, and in the last year of his life authorised the Birmingham redesign, tending the production to the end as the foundation stone of his reputation. The foundation has held.

## The Definitive English Version: Testing the Label

The production is habitually described, in the scholarly literature as in journalism, as the definitive ballet version of the play, and the label deserves testing rather than repeating. Byrne and Watts, the adaptation scholars cited in the British Ballet Now and Then survey, apply the word to MacMillan's staging, and the usage is defensible only if its scope is understood. It cannot mean the finest Romeo in the abstract: Lavrovsky's Soviet staging has the priority of genius and the authority of Ulanova, and Cranko's has its own European sovereignty. It means, more precisely, that within the English-speaking ballet world the MacMillan production became the canonical text of the story, the version companies acquire, audiences expect and dancers measure themselves against, displacing rivals so completely that the Royal Ballet has never needed another Romeo and American Ballet Theatre built its identity in the work for four decades. Definitiveness of that kind is a historical fact about transmission, not a critic's superlative, and on the evidence of performance counts, international stagings and an unbroken sixty-year repertory life, the fact is established.

The label also survives the strongest counter-argument, which is the production's own birth defect. A canonical text ought to be the choreographer's intended one; this one was premiered by substitutes, and its intended Juliet watched her role taught to others from the second cast. Yet the sequel refuted the objection in practice: the work proved transmissible, which is the property that separates a classic from a performance. Scores and steps that depend on one interpreter die in the archive; MacMillan's survived notation, film, coaching lineages and wholesale changes of company style, and each generation of dancers has found its meanings intact and its demands undiminished. Seymour's balcony masterclass of 2002, Ferri's filmed Juliet of 1984, Browne's ABT premiere of 1985 and the Cuthbertson film of 2012 are not footnotes to a legend but successive editions of a text, each correcting and confirming the others. A definitive version, in the end, is one that a culture keeps reissuing. By that standard the label holds, with its geography attached: the definitive English Romeo and Juliet, and the strongest claimant the wider ballet world has produced since the Soviet original, the two judgements differing only in the size of the map.

## The Verdict on MacMillan's Ballet

The disputes that surround the production, stars against creators, realism against poetry, drama against clarity, resolve on inspection into a single finding: the choreography was always the story. The premiere was an act of box-office logic that produced a legendary performance and a lasting injustice to Seymour and Gable; the ballet absorbed both the legend and the injustice and continued. Porter's suspended judgement was the honest criticism of 1965, and history has answered it by making Seymour's rebellious Juliet and Fonteyn's lyrical one successive chapters of a single role. Bennett's taxonomy of realist externality names the method without diminishing it, since the method is the play's own, a drama whose inward states exist to be enacted. The first Romeo's own later staging, lush with ghosts, proves by contrast the discipline of the original. Sixty years of casts have found the work inexhaustible because MacMillan built it the way the play is built, out of decisions: a girl descends from a balcony, declines a bridegroom, hurls a vial and retrieves it, and a boy who has danced through a brawl dances at last with a body that cannot dance back. The cliche needed speech, and could not have it; MacMillan gave it a body instead, and the body has been speaking ever since. Among the hundreds of Romeos the story has generated across the arts, the MacMillan ballet is the one its own art form cannot do without, which is the only definition of a definitive version that survives scrutiny.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When and where did MacMillan's Romeo and Juliet ballet premiere?

The ballet premiered on 9 February 1965 at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, performed by the Royal Ballet with Margot Fonteyn as Juliet and Rudolf Nureyev as Romeo. The date is sometimes misstated in secondary summaries, but the record is consistent across the Royal Ballet's own history, American Ballet Theatre's repertory archive, Jann Parry's biography of the choreographer and the contemporary press: a February evening in 1965, in the house the production has never left. The premiere was the choreographer's first three-act ballet and the climax of a commission completed in under five months, and its reception, forty-three curtain calls and some forty minutes of applause, made it an instant fixture of the company's identity.

### Q: Who was the ballet originally created for?

The roles were created on Lynn Seymour and Christopher Gable, the young principals around whom MacMillan planned the entire work. Seymour, the Canadian ballerina who had become the choreographer's muse, and Gable had first made the balcony pas de deux for Canadian television in September 1964, and the full ballet was then built through long studio discussions in which the three collaborators fixed the characters' psychology: Juliet headstrong and decisive, Romeo swept away. Parry's biography documents the process in detail. That the roles were nonetheless premiered by Fonteyn and Nureyev remains the central irony of the production's history, and the Seymour and Gable interpretation, seen in the run's early performances, became the standard against which the choreography's intentions are still read.

### Q: Why did Fonteyn and Nureyev dance the first night instead of Seymour and Gable?

The substitution was a commercial condition of the American tour for which the ballet had been commissioned. The tour's impresario, Sol Hurok, made clear that the new Romeo would be included in the American repertory, and would be profitable there, only if Fonteyn and Nureyev danced the title roles, since the partnership was the most powerful box-office attraction in ballet. The Royal Opera House administration accepted the condition and gave the premiere to the stars, relegating Seymour and Gable to the second cast. MacMillan, on the documented record, was furious and threatened to withdraw the ballet, and Seymour persuaded him to let the performance proceed. The decision bought the production its triumphant American career at the price of a lasting grievance that reshaped three careers.

### Q: How many curtain calls did Fonteyn and Nureyev take at the premiere?

They took forty-three curtain calls, with applause lasting some forty minutes, a response the Evening Standard held to be a theatrical record. The ovation exceeded even the reception that had greeted the partnership's debut three years earlier, and it ended only when the theatre lowered the iron curtain to signal that the evening was over and the audience should leave. The number has passed into the production's folklore and is sometimes treated as publicity inflation, but it is consistent across independent records of the night, including the Christie's summary of the production's history and the anniversary literature of 2015, and it accurately reflects a premiere that was received as an event in the partnership's legend as much as in the choreographer's career.

### Q: How old were Fonteyn and Nureyev at the premiere?

Fonteyn was forty-five and Nureyev twenty-six on 9 February 1965. Fonteyn, born in May 1919, was at an age when ballerinas of her generation had generally retired, and she played a character the play fixes at not quite fourteen; Nureyev, born in March 1938, was four years past his defection from the Soviet Union and at the height of his virtuoso powers. The displaced creators were closer to the characters in age, Seymour at twenty-five and Gable at twenty-four. The age gap became part of the premiere's critical discussion, yet Fonteyn's Juliet was praised for distillation rather than impersonation: she did not counterfeit adolescence but reduced first love to its essentials, and the performance is generally held to have renewed, rather than closed, her career.

### Q: Who designed the production, and what does the design do?

Nicholas Georgiadis, MacMillan's close collaborator, designed the sets and costumes, drawing on Italian Quattrocento painting and architecture to build a Verona of towers, arcades and frescoed walls. The design is an interpretation in its own right: its scale dwarfs the lovers, so that Juliet appears as a small figure inside the façade of the house that owns her, and her descent from the balcony to the garden becomes an architectural act of self-liberation. Public spaces expand to crush, private rooms contract to shelter, and the crypt closes over the final duet like a lid. The Georgiadis designs remained the production's London standard; when MacMillan restaged the work for Birmingham Royal Ballet in 1992, new designs by Paul Andrews replaced them for the touring company.

### Q: How long did MacMillan take to choreograph the ballet?

He choreographed the full three-act ballet in under five months, between the autumn of 1964 and the premiere on 9 February 1965. The speed was forced by circumstance: the Royal Ballet needed a new Romeo for its American tour after the Soviet authorities refused permission for Lavrovsky's production to be shown in London, and Ashton commissioned MacMillan, who had never made a full-length ballet, against a deadline set by the tour. The schedule was survivable because the work's centre already existed: the balcony pas de deux had been choreographed for Canadian television in September 1964 and, in Seymour's recollection, took only three rehearsals to make. MacMillan, Seymour and Gable planned the remaining structure around the lovers and their duets.

### Q: Where did the balcony pas de deux come from?

The duet began as a separate commission: in September 1964 MacMillan created a short piece for a Canadian television programme honouring Lynn Seymour, and chose the balcony scene, choreographing it on Seymour and Christopher Gable. Seymour recalled that the pas de deux took only three rehearsals to complete, evidence of the creative sympathy between the choreographer and his two dancers. When the Covent Garden commission followed, the television duet became the first act's climax and the template for the whole ballet's method, its lifts and catches carrying the psychology of the play's orchard scene without a word spoken. Its closing image, the lovers straining toward one another's hands as they part, has remained the production's emblem, preserved in the filmed performances of later casts.

### Q: What happens in the balcony pas de deux?

Juliet appears on the balcony set high in the Capulet façade, Romeo watches from the garden, and then, in MacMillan's decisive invention, Juliet descends to him, collapsing the vertical distance the play's scene maintains for two hundred lines. The duet that follows is built on lifts of great abandon, Juliet flung, caught and arched over Romeo's shoulder, in long phrases that avoid display steps in favour of partnering that reads as trust. It ends, when parting can no longer be delayed, with the lovers at full stretch reaching for one another's hands. The duet translates the orchard scene's structure, overhearing, hesitation, surrender, haste, into the single physical fact of distance overcome and then reimposed, and it sets the choreographic grammar the bedroom and tomb duets later quote.

### Q: What makes MacMillan's Juliet different from other ballet Juliets?

MacMillan conceived Juliet as the story's decision-maker, and the choreography sustains the conception from her first entrance to her death. Parry's biography records the studio discussions in which MacMillan and Seymour settled on a headstrong, passionate girl who makes every crucial choice: the secret marriage, the potion, the final joining of Romeo in death. The ballet introduces her as a child with a doll in her parents' house, then charts a progress of decisions in which her movement simplifies as her resolve hardens, from skittish runs to the unbroken lines of the potion scene and the tomb. The reading matches the play, where Juliet proposes the marriage, faces down her father and drinks while Romeo weeps and rages; MacMillan simply built the ballet on the character Shakespeare wrote.

### Q: What happens in the bedroom pas de deux?

The bedroom pas de deux opens the third act and translates the aubade of the play's 3.5, the lovers' argument with the dawn. Romeo repeatedly moves toward departure and Juliet repeatedly recalls him, the partnering reusing the balcony duet's lifts under a new weight, since the marriage has been consummated and the killings have already made Romeo an exile. MacMillan refuses to make the scene a celebration of the wedding night: it is a farewell resisted in real time, ending with Romeo's descent from the window and Juliet alone with her premonition of seeing him as one dead in the bottom of a tomb. Critics and memoirists treat the duet as the production's emotional summit, the point at which its erotic frankness and its tragic knowledge become the same thing.

### Q: How does MacMillan stage Juliet's potion scene?

He stages it as behaviour rather than vision: no ghosts, no dream ballet, only a girl alone in a room with a bed and a vial. Karen Bennett's scholarly comparison of the Prokofiev stagings describes the method exactly, a frantic, anguished dance in which Juliet hurls the vial to the floor before sinking catatonic onto the bed. Where Lavrovsky surrounded the scene with the supernatural and Nureyev later peopled it with the spectres of the dead, MacMillan trusted external action to carry the soliloquy's interior argument: the thrown vial is doubt made physical, its retrieval and the composed drinking are the decision completed. The scene has become the production's most analysed passage and the clearest demonstration of its realism, psychology expressed entirely through what a body does in an empty room.

### Q: How does the ballet end in the tomb?

Romeo forces his way into the Capulet crypt, kills Paris in a fight of notorious brutality, and then dances with Juliet's drugged body, attempting the lifts of the balcony and bedroom duets with a partner who cannot answer, before drinking his poison. Juliet wakes, finds him dead, and dies stretched across the tomb reaching for his hand. Seymour's autobiography records MacMillan's doctrine for the scene: the lovers were not to be reunited in death and did not die in each other's arms, because the choreographer regarded the deaths as a complete waste of young life and an indictment of everyone who caused them. The families' reconciliation, expanded in the play's closing speeches, is compressed to a closing image, and the ballet ends on loss rather than pardon.

### Q: What did the critics disagree about at the premiere?

The disagreement concerned what the first night had actually shown. The majority of notices, in papers including the Observer, the Daily Mail and the Sunday Telegraph, hailed a triumph for the production and its stars. Andrew Porter of the Financial Times entered the reservation that lasted: he was the first critic to discuss the last-minute casting change in print, and he argued that the ballet could not be fully understood until Seymour performed the role designed on her. Posterity has split the verdict. Porter was right that the intended Juliet had not been seen, and Seymour's early performances disclosed the rebellious reading Macaulay later praised; he was wrong if he implied the work depended on its originals, since six decades of casts have kept it at the repertory's centre.

### Q: Is there a film of the original production?

Yes: Paul Czinner directed a cinema version released in 1966, financed under the joint Rank and National Film Finance Corporation initiative and danced by the premiere cast, Fonteyn and Nureyev with David Blair, Desmond Doyle and Anthony Dowell. The film drew some strong reviews, the Harvard Crimson praising the choreography's narrative clarity and defending Czinner's close-ups as the equivalent of the balletomane's opera glasses, but it disappointed at the box office, as Duncan Petrie's study of the financing scheme records. Its value has inverted with time: it is the closest record of the first cast's tempi and emphases, Fonteyn's phrasing and the partnership's chemistry, and later recordings, Ferri and Eagling in 1984 and Cuthbertson and Bonelli in 2012, extend rather than replace its evidence.

### Q: When did American Ballet Theatre first perform the production?

American Ballet Theatre gave its company premiere on 3 January 1985 at the Kennedy Center in Washington, with Leslie Browne as Juliet and Robert La Fosse as Romeo, in a staging MacMillan mounted himself during his period as the company's associate artistic director. Browne, his chosen first American Juliet, recalled the studio rule that governed the transmission: the movement had to say something, and no moment could be empty. The original production's Rosaline, Georgina Parkinson, became a central coach of the ABT tradition. The ballet entered the company's core repertory and became one of its signature productions, giving MacMillan's Romeo the rare distinction of two national homes, Covent Garden and the Metropolitan Opera House, each treating the work as its own.

### Q: Who are the three harlots in the marketplace scenes?

They are street women of Verona, flamboyant comic-erotic figures who tease and partner Romeo, Mercutio and Benvolio in the marketplace scenes. MacMillan adopted the device under the example of John Cranko's Stuttgart production, and he put it to structural use: the harlots establish a city in which sex is public, commercial and joked about, the world of the play's bawdy servants and of Mercutio's wit, so that the lovers' private passion registers against them as a categorically different thing. They also give the corps de ballet characterised work rather than decoration, and their scenes carry much of the production's celebrated crowd choreography, the surging, naturalistic Verona that reviewers contrasted with the lovers' stillness at the ball and in the duets.

### Q: Did Nureyev choreograph his own Romeo and Juliet?

Yes: the first Romeo of the MacMillan production later made his own version, first performed by London Festival Ballet in 1977 with Nureyev and Patricia Ruanne, and reworked for the Paris Opéra in 1984. The staging stands as a revealing control experiment. Where MacMillan trusted behaviour and restraint, Nureyev's version, as Karen Bennett's comparative analysis describes, loaded the story with spectres, dream visions and symbols of fate, peopling even Juliet's potion scene with the ghosts of the dead. The comparison has generally flattered the earlier work: the dancer who had premiered MacMillan's realism spent his choreographic answer adding the supernatural machinery the MacMillan production had famously refused, and audiences and scholars have tended to find the refusal the braver and more durable choice.
