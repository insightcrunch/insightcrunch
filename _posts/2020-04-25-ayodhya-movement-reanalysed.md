The Ayodhya movement is usually told as an election story, a sequence of campaigns whose meaning is exhausted by the seat counts that followed them, yet the movement that carried a disputed shrine in a provincial town to the centre of Indian politics was built out of religious material long before it was harvested for votes. Its builders consecrated bricks in hundreds of thousands of villages, marched sacred water across state boundaries, persuaded assemblies of monks and ascetics to name one plot of earth as the priority of an entire faith, and persuaded millions of households to treat a television serial and a cassette sermon as participation in a sacred cause. This article defends one claim about that record. The movement's substance lay in named organisational and theological choices, made by identifiable people on identifiable dates between 1964 and 1992, and those choices explain the movement's scale in a way that neither a tally of elections nor an account of party machinery can supply on its own.

## A Dispute Older Than the Movement That Claimed It

The ground at Ayodhya was contested long before any organisation adopted it. The Babri Masjid was raised in 1528 by Mir Baqi, a commander serving the Mughal emperor Babur, and Hindu tradition held that the mosque stood where a temple marking the birthplace of Ram had once stood. That tradition was asserted and fought over across the centuries: armed clashes between Hindu and Muslim groups near the site in 1853 and 1855 left scores dead, and in 1885 the mahant Raghubir Das petitioned the Faizabad court for permission to build a temple on the platform outside the mosque, a plea the court refused. The site's geography structured the dispute for a century before the movement inherited it. The mosque stood on a raised mound within a walled compound. Outside the inner gate, on a platform called the Ram Chabutra, Hindu worship had continued in the open air through the later nineteenth century under arrangements the district administration policed after the clashes of the 1850s, with a small structure for Sita's kitchen, the Sita Rasoi, nearby in the same devotional complex. Mahant Raghubir Das's suit of 1885 sought permission to raise a temple on that very chabutra, and its refusal fixed the compromise the town then lived under: the birthplace's outer court in Hindu hands for worship, the mosque itself in Muslim custody, the boundary between them a grille, a gate and eventually a lock. The idols of 1949 broke that compromise by moving the worship from the platform to the sanctum, and every subsequent actor, the Faizabad courts, the Parishad's saints, the BMAC's lawyers, argued over whether the night of December 1949 had restored a sacred geography or violated a legal one. The movement's genius, if the word can be used without endorsement, was to refuse the compromise's scale entirely: it claimed the whole compound, mound and mosque together, as the birthplace, and treated the chabutra arrangement not as a settlement to be honoured but as the record of a subjection to be ended.

The dispute entered its modern legal form on the night of 22 and 23 December 1949, when idols of Ram appeared inside the mosque. Hindu devotees received the appearance as a divine manifestation; Muslim plaintiffs and the district administration's own records treated the idols as having been placed there by men. The state government of Uttar Pradesh, led by Chief Minister Govind Ballabh Pant, declined to remove the idols despite direction from Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Home Minister Vallabhbhai Patel, and the premises were attached, the gates locked, and worship reduced to a priest admitted under guard and darshan through a grille.

Two earlier episodes fixed the pattern that 1949 would repeat. In 1853 and again in 1855, fighting around Hanuman Garhi and the mosque left dozens dead, and in the second clash armed ascetics and Muslim combatants contested the ground in a miniature war that colonial officers suppressed with difficulty; the district record of those years already treats the birthplace claim as the town's standing quarrel. In 1934, after a dispute over cow slaughter, rioting in Ayodhya damaged the mosque's domes, and the colonial government repaired the structure at the town's expense, levying a punitive fine on its residents. The 1934 repair matters to the later record because the movement's historians cited it as proof that even a colonial state had treated the mosque as repairable damage on contested ground, while the mosque's defenders cited the same episode as proof that the structure's antiquity and sanctity had been officially respected. Both sides of the 1980s argument thus mined the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries for precedent, and the mine was deep because the quarrel was old. What no earlier generation possessed was an organisation able to carry the quarrel out of the district, and that absence, more than any difference in belief, separates the Ayodhya of the akharas from the Ayodhya of the movement.

Litigation then froze the site for a generation. Gopal Singh Visharad filed suit at Faizabad in January 1950 seeking the right to worship the installed deity. Paramahansa Ramchandra Das filed a companion suit the same year. The Nirmohi Akhara, an order of Ram ascetics with its own older claim to the site, sued in 1959 for possession and the right to manage worship. The Sunni Central Waqf Board sued in December 1961 for the removal of the idols and delivery of the mosque. Interim orders kept the idols in place and the locks on the gates, and the Allahabad High Court confirmed the arrangement in 1955. For roughly three decades the Ayodhya dispute was a file in the Faizabad courts, tended by akharas, waqf lawyers and local devotees, absent from national politics entirely. No national organisation campaigned on it. No political party carried it in a manifesto. The grievance existed as litigation and local devotion, and it is precisely that long dormancy that makes the movement of the 1980s a thing to be explained. A claim that had slept through four general elections did not wake itself. It was woken, by an organisation that had first to be built, and then to be turned toward Ayodhya by a series of deliberate decisions.

The scholarly record supports that framing. Christophe Jaffrelot, in *The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics* (1996), treats the pre-1980 dispute as a local matter sustained by the Ayodhya akharas and argues that its nationalisation was a work of organisation rather than of spontaneous sentiment. Peter van der Veer, in *Religious Nationalism* (1994), reaches a converging conclusion from the anthropology of Ayodhya itself: the town's pilgrimage economy and its ascetic orders supplied durable religious material, but the conversion of that material into a national campaign required new institutional work. What follows traces that work stage by stage, because the stages, and not merely the climax of 1992, are the movement's substance.

## Ayodhya Before the Parishad: Akharas, Mahants and a Pilgrim Town

To grasp what the Parishad inherited, the town itself needs a hearing. Ayodhya in the middle decades of the twentieth century was a pilgrimage centre of the second rank, overshadowed in wealth by Varanasi and in organisation by Prayag, yet dense with religious capital of a particular kind. Its akharas, the armed ascetic orders of the Ramanandi tradition, maintained establishments around Hanuman Garhi and the janmabhoomi quarter, recruited celibate fighters and administrators in the same bodies, and carried institutional memories of fighting for sacred ground that reached back to the clashes of the 1850s. Its mahants controlled temple trusts, rest houses and festival calendars. Its economy ran on pilgrims who came for Ram Navami and the Kartik bathing season and who expected, as a matter of course, to be told where Ram was born, where he played, where he crossed the Sarayu. The sacred geography the 1980s campaign would survey and harden existed first as the working knowledge of guides, priests and ascetics, transmitted in the town's own idiom long before any pamphlet standardised it.

The men who placed the idols in December 1949 came from this world. Abhiram Das, a mahant of the Hanuman Garhi establishment, was identified in the police record and in later testimony as a central figure in the installation, and the ascetic networks of the town closed around the act within hours, presenting the district administration with a devotional fact that officials feared to reverse. Paramahansa Ramchandra Das, who led the Digambar Akhara and filed one of the 1950 suits, became for four decades the dispute's most tireless local custodian, a litigant, fundraiser and agitator who kept the janmabhoomi claim alive in the courts and in the town when no national body cared to carry it. He would later head the Ram Janmabhoomi Nyas of 1993, a continuity that underlines how much of the movement's personnel predated its national phase. The akharas were not, however, natural allies of a centralised campaign. They guarded their own precedence at the site, quarrelled over offerings and management, and included orders, the Nirmohi Akhara among them, whose claims to the shrine's custody could collide with any trust the Parishad proposed. The movement's later history carries the marks of that friction, in competing suits and in the Nyas's contested standing, and the friction is itself evidence for the substantive reading: the religious field the campaign organised was real, older than the campaign, and never wholly obedient to it.

Ayodhya also supplied the movement with its calendar and its stages. Ram Navami processions, the Parikrama circuits of the sacred town, the Kartik fair on the Sarayu's banks, each assembled crowds in devotional form on predictable dates, and each could be, and was, folded into campaign choreography in the 1980s and 1990s. A kar seva call set for a festival date inherited a pilgrim flow; a procession routed along the Parikrama inherited sanctity. Van der Veer's ethnography of Ayodhya in *Religious Nationalism* (1994) documents this substratum in detail, the priests who specialised in the birthplace story, the pilgrims who arrived already believing, the ascetics for whom the locked shrine was a standing humiliation retold at every gathering. The national movement did not manufacture this town. It connected the town to a country, and the connection, once made, ran in both directions: Ayodhya's local custodians gained a national stage, and the national campaign gained a sacred address with custodians who could authenticate it.

## Building the Vehicle: The Vishwa Hindu Parishad from 1964 to 1980

The vehicle was founded on 29 August 1964, the festival of Janmashtami, at the Sandipani Sadhanalaya ashram in Powai, Bombay. Swami Chinmayananda, the Vedanta teacher whose Chinmaya Mission had carried Hindu teaching to English-speaking urban audiences, took the founding presidency. S. S. Apte, a senior worker of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, became the first general secretary and the organisation's chief builder. The RSS chief M. S. Golwalkar hosted and blessed the founding, and the gathering seated figures meant to advertise breadth: Master Tara Singh of the Akali tradition, K. M. Munshi of the Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan, and representatives of Jain, Buddhist and Sikh bodies alongside Hindu monastic orders. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad, the VHP, declared itself non-political, barred its officeholders from simultaneous party office, and stated three purposes: to organise and consolidate Hindu society, to carry Hindu ethical and spiritual teaching into public life, and to bind Hindus overseas to the homeland's religious institutions. It was registered as a body on 8 July 1966 and launched publicly at the Prayag Kumbh Mela of 1966.

The design deserves attention, because it solved a problem the older Hindu movement had never solved. The RSS possessed disciplined cadres but no standing among the ascetic orders, temple trusts and sectarian lineages that actually governed Hindu religious life, and its uniformed, celibate style kept it at a distance from household devotion. The VHP was built as a bridge into that world. Its core constituency was to be the sadhu and the mahant, the temple and the math, gathered under a single parishad that no one sect commanded. Through the 1960s and 1970s its visible work matched the founding brief: conferences of religious leaders, campaigns against cow slaughter, opposition to Christian missionary conversion in tribal districts, service projects, and a slow extension into the diaspora. It held a second world Hindu conference at Prayag in 1979. It annoyed governments occasionally and mobilised masses rarely. Jaffrelot's judgment on this period is blunt: for its first decade and a half the Parishad remained a modest body whose conferences drew religious notables but whose capacity to move crowds was unproven and largely untested.

The Parishad's internal structure explains how a turn, once decided, could be executed. A central guidance council of senior ascetics, the Kendriya Margdarshak Mandal, stood above its working bodies as the custodian of religious legitimacy, and the Parishad's constitution routed campaign questions of religious substance upward to the saints rather than outward to any political ally. Its full-time workers were placed in temples, pilgrimage towns and district committees, where they maintained the unglamorous infrastructure of mobilisation: registers of maths and their mahants, festival calendars, lists of sympathetic lawyers, printers and transport owners, and the personal knowledge of which ascetic would share a platform with which rival. Andersen and Damle, drawing on interviews inside the Sangh, note that this infrastructure was the Parishad's real capital by 1980 and that the RSS valued it precisely because its own shakha network could not reproduce it. The organisation's weakness was the mirror of its strength. It commanded no votes, little money beside what campaigns raised, and no instrument of discipline over the saints whose names it borrowed. Its campaigns therefore had to be built as invitations the religious world wished to accept, which is why its decisive instruments were festivals, consecrations and processions rather than memberships and resolutions alone.

### Why did the Vishwa Hindu Parishad turn toward Ayodhya in the 1980s?

The turn came from leadership and circumstance together. Ashok Singhal, an engineer from Allahabad who joined the Parishad's work around 1980 and rose through its secretariat, pressed the organisation toward campaigns that households could join rather than conferences that notables attended. Ayodhya offered the one demand that could unite ascetics, householders and cadres behind a single address.

The instruments for such a turn were already at hand. The conversion of Dalit families to Islam at Meenakshipuram in February 1981 handed campaigners a grievance that travelled, and the Ekatmata Yatra of late 1983 proved that processions built around sacred water could draw crowds across regions. Two features of the early VHP matter for everything that follows. The first is its claim to speak across sects. Hindu religious life is divided among Vaishnava, Shaiva and Shakta lineages, among monastic orders that guard their independence jealously, and among regional traditions with their own calendars and saints. A campaign run by any one order would have been read as that order's property. The Parishad's founding architecture, a council in which orders sat together and in which the RSS supplied organisers while renouncers supplied sanctity, was designed so that a demand could be issued in the name of Hindu society as a whole. The second feature is patience. Between 1964 and 1980 the organisation accumulated the directories, contacts and habits of cooperation that a mass campaign would later draw upon: lists of maths and temples, relationships with akhara leadership at Ayodhya, Hardwar and Prayag, and a cadre of full-time workers placed inside religious institutions rather than party offices. Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle, in *The RSS: A View to the Inside* (2018), emphasise this brokerage role, describing the Parishad as the Sangh Parivar's point of entry into a religious world the RSS could not enter in its own name. When the turn toward Ayodhya came, it came through machinery that had been assembled for twenty years, which is why the campaign of the 1980s scaled as fast as it did.

## Meenakshipuram and the Ekatmata Yatra: The Turn to Mass Mobilisation, 1981 to 1983

In February 1981, in the village of Meenakshipuram in Tirunelveli district, Tamil Nadu, around two hundred Dalit families embraced Islam, in most accounts numbering close to a thousand people. The converts, drawn from the Pallar community, stated their reasons plainly in the reporting of the time: conversion was an exit from caste humiliation, from the refusal of temple entry, tea-shop access and physical dignity that their Hindu neighbours enforced. Hindu organisations received the episode as an emergency. For the VHP it was proof, usable in every district, that an unorganised Hindu society leaked its most oppressed members to rival faiths, and that only organisation could hold them. The Parishad poured workers into Meenakshipuram and the surrounding districts, opened service and reconversion work, and carried the village's name across India as a warning. In Tamil Nadu itself the shock built an institution: the Hindu Munnani, founded in 1980 by Sangh workers in the state under Rama Gopalan, grew from a small front into a mass presence on the strength of the conversion question, organising Hindu assertion in districts where the Parishad's north Indian idiom had never travelled. The sequence, local shock, institutional growth, national lesson, was the campaign method in embryo. A grievance that could be narrated as Hindu society's wound, answered by organisation that Hindu society could see, produced both the recruits and the confidence for larger undertakings, and Singhal's secretariat studied Meenakshipuram's returns the way a general staff studies a successful probe. When the same method was pointed at Ayodhya three years later, it was pointed by men who believed, on evidence they had gathered themselves, that religious emergency was the one summons the Hindu household unfailingly answered. Singhal and his colleagues drew the lesson that would govern the decade: a religious question, framed as a threat to Hindu society's integrity and answered with visible collective action, could mobilise people whom doctrinal conferences never reached.

The Ekatmata Yatra of November and December 1983 was the first full test of that lesson. The Parishad organised simultaneous processions across India built around a single substance, Ganga water, carried in vessels from Gangasagar, Rameswaram, Haridwar and other sacred sources along routes that converged and crossed through thousands of towns and villages. Participants drank the water, received it into their homes, and walked stretches of the route behind the vessel. The theological proposition was the yatra's name: ekatmata, the oneness of the Hindu soul, expressed by treating water from the faith's farthest shrines as one water. The organisational proposition was quieter and more consequential. A procession built around a sacred object required no speeches, no party flag and no prior commitment from participants. A villager could join by walking, a householder by offering flowers, a temple by ringing its bells as the vessel passed. Jaffrelot records the yatra as the Parishad's apprenticeship in mass mobilisation, the moment its workers learned to route processions, feed marchers, negotiate with district administrations and harvest lists of local sympathisers in every settlement the columns touched.

### What did the Ekatmata Yatra of 1983 test?

It tested whether sacred objects could substitute for party machinery. The processions moved Ganga water along three routes and a lattice of feeder marches, and at each halt the vessel, not a speaker, held the crowd. Parishad workers recorded the names of householders who organised each welcome, and the yatra proved that devotion itself could be routed, scheduled and counted.

Villages that had never sent a man to a political meeting lined the road to touch the kalash. That proof, that a sacred object could assemble a crowd and leave organisation behind it, became the template for the brick consecrations of 1989.

The Meenakshipuram shock and the Ekatmata experiment together mark the movement's first stage turning into its second. Before 1981 the Parishad accumulated religious legitimacy. After 1983 it possessed a demonstrated method for converting legitimacy into crowds. What it still lacked was a single objective on which the method could be concentrated, a demand concrete enough to organise a decade around, sacred enough to command the ascetic orders, and simple enough to be understood in one sentence from Kashmir to Kanyakumari. The demand was found the following spring, in a hall in New Delhi.

## April 1984: The Dharm Sansad Names Ayodhya the Priority

On 7 and 8 April 1984, the first Dharm Sansad, a parliament of Hindu religious leaders convened by the VHP, met at Vigyan Bhavan in New Delhi. Several hundred sadhus, mahants and sect heads attended, among them figures whose authority rested on lineages centuries old and whose presence converted a campaign proposal into a religious resolution. The Sansad called for the liberation of three sacred sites: the Ram Janmabhoomi at Ayodhya, the Krishna Janmabhoomi at Mathura and the Kashi Vishwanath site at Varanasi, each a temple site standing beside or beneath a mosque of Mughal-era construction. Of the three, Ayodhya was placed first and made the campaign's operative demand. The Sansad set the terms the movement would repeat for a decade: the birthplace of Ram was not negotiable property but sacred ground held under an imposed structure, and its restoration was a religious duty that no court verdict or electoral calculation could dissolve.

To execute the resolution the Parishad built a campaign committee of a new kind. The Ram Janmabhoomi Mukti Yajna Samiti, constituted in 1984, seated ascetics in its presidium, among them Mahant Avaidyanath of the Gorakhnath temple at Gorakhpur, whose Nath order commanded its own following across eastern Uttar Pradesh, and it gave the campaign a working secretariat in which saints supplied authority and Parishad organisers supplied paper, transport and lists. The Samiti's form was the movement in miniature: a yajna committee, not an election committee, whose calls were issued as religious programmes, whose local units were attached to temples and maths, and whose targets were announced as deadlines for a sacred handover. Later committees, for the brick consecration and for kar seva, copied the Samiti's architecture, which is why the movement could summon crowds in the name of bodies most citizens had never heard of and still be obeyed as a religious summons. The state, which knew how to read a party's handbill, repeatedly misread these committees as fronts, and repeatedly discovered that the saints' signatures on their letterheads carried a weight no front could simulate.

The choice of Ayodhya over Mathura and Varanasi was itself an organisational judgment, and the record shows it was argued as one. Ayodhya's dispute was already inside the courts with idols installed and worship continuing under lock, which gave a campaign a living shrine to defend rather than an abstraction to demand. The town's akharas were organised, militant in memory and already in litigation. Ram, unlike Krishna or Shiva in their regional strongholds, was worshipped across every linguistic region of India, and his story was the one epic every household knew. A campaign for Ram's birthplace could be preached in Tamil Nadu and Assam in the same words. The Sansad's resolutions, preserved partially in the VHP's own archives in Delhi, framed the demand as the recovery of a birthplace rather than the destruction of a mosque, a formulation the movement's speakers maintained even as its opponents heard, and documented, the harder slogan work of its street campaigns.

### What did the Dharm Sansad of April 1984 decide?

Meeting at Vigyan Bhavan on 7 and 8 April 1984, the assembled religious leaders resolved that the sites at Ayodhya, Mathura and Varanasi be handed over for temple restoration, and they placed Ayodhya at the head of the campaign. The decision converted a Faizabad court file into a declared priority of organised Hindu religious leadership.

It also fixed the movement's method: demands would be issued in the name of the Dharm Sansad and the ascetic orders, with the Parishad acting as organiser and servant of the saints rather than as a political claimant in its own right. The Sansad did not adjourn into history. It became a standing instrument, reconvened at the campaign's junctures to renew the demand in the saints' voice, and its most consequential later sitting framed the climax of the brick year. At the Prayag Kumbh Mela of January and February 1989, where the Parishad had first launched itself in 1966, the assembled saints proclaimed the programme the year would execute: the consecration of bricks across the country and the laying of the temple's foundation at Ayodhya in the autumn. The Kumbh setting was itself a substantive choice. The largest religious gathering on earth supplied the audience, the sanctity and the calendar, and a programme announced from its banks carried an authority no Delhi press conference could mint. Between the Vigyan Bhavan resolutions of 1984 and the Prayag proclamations of 1989, the Sansad form had travelled from a novel experiment to the movement's recognised parliament, and governments had learned to send emissaries to its sittings as they would to a power.

The institutional innovation of 1984 should not be understated. By routing the Ayodhya demand through a parliament of renouncers, the movement acquired a voice that could not be answered in the ordinary currency of politics. A government could negotiate with a party, arrest a party's workers, or outbid a party's promises. It confronted something else in a resolution of sadhus: a claim presented as dharma, which its holders described as preceding and outranking the state. The Ram Janmabhoomi Nyas structures and campaign committees that followed worked under the Sansad's authority, and the movement's deadline-setting, its ultimatums of 1986 and its calls for kar seva in 1990 and 1992, were all issued as religious summons rather than political programmes. Critics of the movement, in Parliament and in the press, answered that the saints' authority was itself being organised, that the Sansad met when the Parishad convened it and resolved what the Parishad proposed. Both descriptions of the same machinery appear in the scholarship, and the adjudication between them belongs to the later sections of this article. What is settled is the sequence: the demand was sacralised and prioritised in April 1984, and every instrument the movement later used was justified by reference to that spring's resolutions.

## The First Campaign Wave, 1984 to 1986: A Yatra Suspended and an Ultimatum Issued

The Sansad's resolution was followed within months by the movement's first national procession, and its fate taught the organisers lessons they applied for a decade. In September 1984 the Parishad launched the Ram Janmabhoomi Mukti Yajna Samiti's procession from Sitamarhi in Bihar, the town tradition names as Sita's birthplace, carrying an image of Ram and Janaki toward Ayodhya under the slogan of liberating the birthplace. The column reached Ayodhya in October and its leaders took their pledge on the Sarayu's bank, a kilometre and more from the locked shrine, where Ayodhya's own residents, Hindu and Muslim together in the local administration's anxious accounting, were prepared to resist any approach on the mosque. On 31 October 1984 Prime Minister Indira Gandhi was assassinated, the country convulsed, and the yatra's continuation dissolved into the emergency of the hour. The campaign's first wave had demonstrated reach and limits in the same month: a procession could be assembled across states, and a procession alone could not take a guarded shrine.

The Parishad spent 1985 converting the pledge into pressure. Its committees demanded that the shrine be handed over to Hindu custody, set the festival of Ram Navami in 1986 as the horizon for a settlement, and warned of a march on Ayodhya if the locks remained. In October 1984, at Ayodhya itself, it had raised the Bajrang Dal as a youth formation, under the Allahabad organiser Vinay Katiyar, to give the campaign an instrument for street-level work that the sadhus could bless and the secretariat could direct. The Dal's founding belongs to the same architecture as the Sansad: religious legitimacy above, organisation in the middle, and a disciplined young cadre below. When the Faizabad litigation of January 1986 produced the opened locks, the movement could plausibly claim that its ultimatum had been answered, and the claim, repeated in every district committee, was worth more than the order itself. It taught supporters that deadlines worked, and it taught the leadership to keep setting them: for the shilanyas in 1989, for kar seva in October and December 1990, and for the fateful summons of December 1992.

The opposition's reading of these years deserves its place in the record. Muslim organisations and the parties of the left held that the ultimatums were the politics: that a campaign which could not win its demand in court, where the title suits slept, chose instead to manufacture emergencies that governments would settle by concession. The Congress government of Rajiv Gandhi, critics charged, obliged twice, declining to appeal the Faizabad order in 1986 and later permitting the shilanyas, each time calculating the electoral weather. Defenders of the movement answered that peaceful processions and court applications are the ordinary instruments of democratic claim-making, and that a demand does not become illegitimate because it is organised. The dispute between those two descriptions, pressure as blackmail and pressure as politics, runs through every later stage of the Ayodhya story and is adjudicated, for the movement as a whole, in the final analytical section of this article.

## The Theology of a Specific Place

Movements built on general grievance dissipate; the Ayodhya movement was built on one plot of ground, and its theology was a theology of exact location. The claim, as the movement stated it, was that Ram was born not merely at Ayodhya but at a precise spot, the ground beneath the central dome of the Babri Masjid, where a temple commemorating the birth had stood until Mir Baqi demolished it to raise the mosque in 1528. Devotees worshipped the installed deity as Ram Lalla, the infant Ram, present at his janmabhoomi. The spot's sanctity was held to be non-transferable: a temple built anywhere else in Ayodhya, however grand, would not answer the claim, because the claim attached to coordinates, not to the town. This insistence on the exact site is the theological fact from which the movement's intransigence and its legal strategy both followed, and scholars of the movement return to it repeatedly. Van der Veer argues that the modern campaign sharpened an older, looser sacred geography, in which Ayodhya as a whole was Ram's city, into a surveyed claim on one parcel, a sharpening that print, litigation and archaeology each fed.

The historical sub-claims were disputed from the start, and they remain disputed, which is why this article states them as claims with named holders. On the movement's side, the archaeologist B. B. Lal, who directed excavations at Ayodhya in the 1970s, reported pillar bases and sculptural fragments below the mosque's level and argued that a temple structure had preceded the mosque; the Vishva Hindu Parishad published his findings widely in the late 1980s as scientific confirmation. Historians including Romila Thapar, and the authors of the 1989 historians' report to the nation on Ayodhya prepared by R. S. Sharma, Athar Ali, D. N. Jha and Suraj Bhan, answered that the early textual record does not fix Ram's birthplace at the mosque site, that the association of the precise spot with the birth is late, and that no excavation had established a demolished temple beneath the mosque. Muslim parties to the litigation maintained that the Babri Masjid had stood as a mosque, on waqf land, since 1528, that no temple demolition was recorded by contemporaries, and that four and a half centuries of worship gave the mosque its own sanctity which the law was bound to protect. The movement's theologians answered in a different register altogether: faith, they held, was not required to await the historian's verdict, and the belief of millions that Ram was born at the spot was itself a fact a court should weigh, a position later carried into the title suits by the counsel for Ram Lalla.

Ashish Nandy, Shikha Trivedy, Sudhir Kakar and Vinay Lal, in *Creating a Nationality: The Ramjanmabhoomi Movement and Fear of the Self* (1995), pressed the sharpest version of the sceptical reading: classical Hindu devotion, they argued, was comfortable with multiplicity and ambiguity about sacred geography, and the campaign's insistence on one surveyed birthplace, one enemy structure and one remedy imported a Semitic style of exclusive sacred claim into a tradition that had historically not needed it. The movement's intellectuals rejected the charge, answering that attachment to Ram's birthplace was attested in pilgrimage practice for centuries and that defending a shrine against what they described as an invader's monument was a duty older than any modern ideology. Jaffrelot's account mediates the quarrel empirically: whatever the antiquity of the belief, its conversion into a single-issue mass campaign with deadlines, ultimatums and consecrated bricks was a work of the 1980s, and the campaign's form, processional, sacramental and centred on objects, came from the Parishad's religious repertoire rather than from any party's election manual. That mediation is close to the claim this article defends. The theology supplied the content: a birthplace, non-negotiable and exact. The organisation supplied the means to make that content move.

## Ram the King and Ram the Infant: The Devotional Repertoire the Campaign Drew Upon

The theology of place rested on a devotional repertoire far older than the dispute. North India's Ram devotion runs through Tulsidas, whose *Ramcharitmanas*, composed in the sixteenth century in the Awadhi tongue, carried the story out of Sanskrit courts into village recitation, and through the Ramanandi orders who made Ram worship the centre of a monastic network across the Gangetic plain. The Ram of the *Manas* is Maryada Purushottam, the king who embodies righteous order, exiled, wronged and restored, a figure in whom householders read the duties of family and rule. The deity at the janmabhoomi was something else again: Ram Lalla, the infant, whose claim on his devotees is not instruction but tenderness, and whose installation in 1949 let the campaign present a shrine as a child held in a locked room. The movement worked both registers at once. Its processions marched behind the warrior king's bow; its appeals for the birthplace spoke of the infant's home. Scholars of the campaign, van der Veer among them, mark this doubling as part of its reach: the same Ram could sanctify a political programme of national order and a neighbourhood puja for a stolen birthplace, and a participant could move between the registers without noticing a seam.

The repertoire also determined what the campaign could not easily say. A devotion built on recitation, pilgrimage and festival is additive by habit; it absorbs new shrines and new stories more readily than it anathematises old neighbours. Ayodhya's own Muslims appear in the town's devotional economy as neighbours, customers and, in some local traditions, as sharers of the Sarayu's sanctity, and the campaign's harder preachers had to work against that grain, recasting the mosque from a neighbour's house of prayer into an invader's trophy and the neighbour himself into the invader's heir. Rithambara's cassettes did that work in its bluntest form. The movement's mainstream kept the formulation one step removed, directing anger at the structure and at the politicians said to protect it, yet the distinction wore thin wherever processions passed mosques with slogans, as the Bhagalpur violence of October 1989 showed. An honest account of the movement's substance must record both the repertoire's devotional depth, which no cynic invented, and the labour of enmity that its campaigners laid over that depth, which no devotee should be asked to forget.

## The Movement and Caste: Inclusion as Doctrine and as Dispute

No account of the movement's substance is complete without its caste doctrine, because the campaign's theology of Hindu oneness carried a social promise its organisers stated constantly. The Parishad's founding proposition, that Hindu society was one body across sects, extended by logic to castes, and the Ayodhya campaign performed the extension deliberately. Dalit and backward-caste figures were placed at the ritual centre: Kameshwar Chaupal's hand on the foundation brick in November 1989 was the most publicised instance, and the campaign's literature invoked Shabari and Nishadraj, the low-born devotees of the Ram story, as proof that the epic itself honoured devotion above birth. The Ekatmata processions had made the same point in 1983 by carrying one water for all. Organisers argued, in print and from platforms, that a movement which seated Dalit, tribal and upper-caste devotees behind one brick was dissolving caste in practice faster than any programme of the state.

Dalit and Bahujan critics answered, in the same years and in print of their own, that the inclusion on offer was hierarchical: dignity inside devotion, silence on the structures that governed land, wages and marriage, and a demand for unity whose terms the upper-caste custodians of the campaign continued to set. The sharpest form of the counter held that the temple movement and the Mandal movement of 1990 were rival answers to the same crisis of the caste order, the first absorbing backward-caste assertion into Hindu unity under Brahminical symbols, the second organising it against those symbols, and that the two movements' collision in the autumn of 1990, Mandal against Mandir in the season's own slogan, was the decade's deepest contest. Scholars sympathetic to the Parishad disputed the rivalry thesis, pointing to the backward-caste composition of the kar seva crowds and to the campaign's genuine recruitment in the villages Mandal also claimed. The dispute is not resolvable from the crowd photographs either side exhibits, and this article records it as a named disagreement in the scholarship rather than settling it: what the movement's caste doctrine indisputably did was give its Hindu-oneness theology a social content that participants experienced as equality of worship, whatever its critics established about equality of condition.

## 1 February 1986: The Locks Open and Two Mobilisations Form

The campaign's second stage began in a Faizabad courtroom. On 25 January 1986 a local advocate, Umesh Chandra Pandey, applied in the court of the Munsif Sadar, Faizabad, for the removal of the locks that had sealed the shrine since 1949. The munsif deferred the matter on 28 January, Pandey appealed, and on 1 February 1986 the District Judge of Faizabad, K. M. Pandey, heard the appeal and ordered the locks opened, holding that the restrictions were an unnecessary irritant and that Hindu worshippers should face no hurdle in darshan of the idols. The order was executed within the hour, before television cameras that the administration had allowed to gather. The brief for this article describes the order as a civil court munsif's order; the record shows the operative order was the District Judge's, passed on appeal from the munsif's court, and the distinction matters because Muslim applicants who sought to be heard that day were refused impleadment by the same order, a grievance their counsel pressed for decades afterward.

The Union government of Rajiv Gandhi did not appeal the order, and Congress spokesmen presented the opening as evenhanded, a Hindu counterpart to the government's handling of Muslim personal law that same season. The season's parallel track is part of the record and part of the movement's story. In April 1985 the Supreme Court, in the case of Shah Bano, had awarded maintenance to a divorced Muslim woman under the general criminal procedure code and had spoken critically of the personal law's treatment of women. The government's answer, the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act of May 1986, reversed the judgment's effect for Muslim divorces and was denounced by its critics as a concession to conservative religious leadership. Through the same months the Ayodhya locks came off. Ministers and their critics alike read the two moves as a pair, a government balancing constituencies by conceding a shrine to one religious leadership while conceding a statute to another, and the pairing taught both mobilisations that organised religious pressure on this government worked. The movement's campaigners said as much in their literature, citing the 1986 Act as proof that Delhi settled religious questions by the strength of the crowd behind them, a lesson in mobilisation theory that the BMAC's founders, organising in the same weeks, had drawn from the opposite side. The movement read the day differently: a lock imposed by the state in 1949 had been struck off under the pressure of an organised religious demand, and the lesson its campaigners drew was that mobilisation moved courts and governments where litigation alone had not. Worship inside the shrine resumed in public. Pilgrim traffic to Ayodhya swelled. The Parishad announced campaigns to carry the demand from access to possession, and the Ram Janmabhoomi action committees multiplied at district level.

Within weeks the counter-mobilisation stood up in organised form. In March 1986 Muslim leaders, lawyers and litigants constituted the Babri Masjid Action Committee, with the advocate Zafaryab Jilani as its convenor, to defend the mosque through the courts and through public campaigning of its own. The committee's stated case rested on title, on four centuries of the mosque's existence, and on the constitutional promise that minority worship stood under the state's protection. It organised conferences, published its own documentary record, and in February 1987 led a march to the Boat Club in New Delhi that drew a very large crowd, proof that the religious-political form the Parishad had pioneered could be answered in kind. From 1986 onward, India had not one Ayodhya campaign but two, each with lawyers, saints or ulema, processions and print, facing each other across the same locked and then unlocked gate. The scholarly literature marks this as the moment the dispute escaped the courtroom for good. What the Parishad had built as a mobilisation substrate, a network of committees, processions and consecrated objects answering to a sacred demand, the BMAC mirrored in defence, and the symmetry itself raised the political price of every subsequent concession.

### What changed when the Faizabad court ordered the locks opened in 1986?

Before February 1986 the idols sat behind locks under the 1949 arrangement, which admitted one priest and excluded the public. The District Judge's order of 1 February 1986 converted the sealed shrine into an open one, with darshan for all Hindu worshippers, and pilgrim traffic, television attention and competitive mobilisation all followed from that single administrative morning.

The opening gave the movement a functioning temple to defend, gave television its first Ayodhya pictures, and pushed Muslim litigants to build the Babri Masjid Action Committee within weeks.

## The Ramayana in Every Home: Print, Cassette and Television, 1986 to 1989

Between the opening of the locks and the consecration of the bricks, the movement acquired an audience that no procession could have assembled unaided. From 25 January 1987 to 31 July 1988, Doordarshan broadcast Ramanand Sagar's *Ramayan* in seventy-eight episodes, and the serial remade Sunday mornings across India. Streets emptied during broadcasts, sets were garlanded and worshipped in some households, and actors who played Ram and Sita were received on tour with a reverence that blurred performance and darshan. The serial was not a movement production; Sagar was a filmmaker working for the state broadcaster, and the Congress government that commissioned the slot had no Ayodhya campaign in mind. Its effect, however, was to standardise one telling of the Ram story in the national imagination at the exact years the Parishad was arguing that Ram's birthplace was a national question. Van der Veer and other scholars note the sequence without claiming a conspiracy: the television epic supplied the shared narrative, the iconography and the emotional vocabulary, posters of Sagar's Ram, bow in hand, appeared in the movement's own processions, and the campaign supplied the politics.

Print and cassette did the movement's narrower work. Pamphlets in the bazaars of the Hindi belt retold the Ayodhya claim in a few pages, with dates, numbers and maps that lent the sacred story what Gyanendra Pandey called a scientific precision of detail. The movement's history of the site was printed, reprinted and read aloud at tea stalls. On cassette, the speeches of Sadhvi Rithambara, a woman ascetic whose recordings circulated from hand to hand in the late 1980s, carried the demand in its hardest register, denouncing the mosque as a monument of humiliation and calling on Hindus to recover the birthplace. Uma Bharti, who would become a leading campaigner, addressed rallies in the same years. The women's voices mattered organisationally as well as rhetorically: they carried the campaign into domestic spaces that male cadres entered awkwardly, and they embodied the movement's claim that the cause belonged to the whole Hindu household, not to a party's youth wing.

Two further channels completed the circuit. Video arrived in the campaign's later stages: cassettes of speeches, of the yatra crowds and of Ayodhya itself circulated through the video parlours and hired sets of small-town India, and the Parishad's sympathisers screened them at campaign gatherings, so that a settlement which never saw a procession could watch one in devotional detail. The Hindi press, meanwhile, carried the campaign into print households daily. The Press Council of India, examining the reporting of the Ayodhya agitation of 1990 to 1992, censured sections of the Hindi-language press for inflammatory and one-sided coverage that presented the movement's claims as established fact and its opponents' as provocation, a finding the papers concerned disputed as an establishment's judgment on a people's cause. The Council's intervention matters here because it marks the moment the state's own media referee conceded that the campaign's substantive content, its version of the past and of the site, had captured the channels through which most of north India learned the dispute.

The media ecology of the late 1980s thus fed the mobilisation from three directions. Television universalised the story. Print gave the claim a documentary costume, excavation reports, court dates and acreage. Cassette and rally gave it heat. None of the three was controlled by the Parishad, yet all three enlarged the public to which its 1989 campaign could speak, and the Parishad, to its organisers' credit in Andersen and Damle's account, built its brick consecration to fit exactly the audience that television had prepared: families who knew the story as sacred history and could be asked, in its name, to consecrate a brick.

## One Family, Three Instruments: Parishad, Sangh and Party

By 1989 the campaign's labour was divided among three instruments, and the division explains both its power and its later fractures. The Parishad owned the sacred demand and its instruments of consecration. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh supplied organisers, arbitrated disputes among the campaign's egos and, at moments of danger, counselled patience its religious partners did not always take; its annual resolutions through the late 1980s endorsed the temple demand while its cadres staffed the machinery that carried the demand into districts where neither Parishad nor party had roots. The Bharatiya Janata Party, the family's electoral instrument, came to the demand late and by calculation the movement's saints never let it forget. Reduced to two Lok Sabha seats in 1984, the party under L. K. Advani's presidency sought a mass question, and at its Palampur session in June 1989 the national executive resolved to support the Ram Janmabhoomi demand, committing the party to the temple cause in terms its manifesto politics had previously avoided. The movement's historians treat Palampur as the hinge: before it, a religious campaign with political sympathisers; after it, a religious campaign with a national party harnessed to it, for the elections of 1989, 1991 and beyond.

The harnessing changed the campaign's substance as well as its scale. A party must end campaigns in seats, and a sacred trust cannot be ended at all; the tension between those two logics runs from Palampur to the demolition and past it. Advani's own account of the period presents the yatra of 1990 as a mission of public education on an issue the courts had slept over, while the Parishad's saints described the same years as a religious war of recovery in which elections were weather, not climate. Both descriptions were sincerely held inside the same processions, which is precisely why the substantive reading of the movement must keep the religious content analytically separate from the electoral use made of it. When the party later governed and deferred the temple to coalition management and judicial process, the saints' wing remembered the deferral as betrayal, and the Nyas's insistence on holding the site against the state expressed that memory in institutional form. The adjudication below returns to this triangle, because the strongest version of the instrumentalist counter to this article's claim is, at bottom, the claim that the party's logic governed the other two. The record of 1984 to 1989, in which the demand was set, sacralised and operationalised before the party adopted it, is the chief evidence against that ordering.

## The Brick as Sacrament: Ram Shila Pujan and the Shilanyas of 1989

The movement's masterstroke of form came in the autumn of 1989. The Ram Shila Pujan took a single object, a brick inscribed with Ram's name, and multiplied it across the country. From late September 1989, in village after village, bricks were carried in procession, installed on temporary altars, worshipped with the rites due to a sacred object and blessed for dispatch to Ayodhya, where they would one day form the walls of the temple raised at the birthplace. The Parishad counted the ceremonies in some three hundred thousand villages, a figure published by its organisers and repeated by scholars with the caution due to movement arithmetic, and even discounted heavily the scale remains without precedent in Indian campaigning. The count's geography also ran offshore. The Parishad's diaspora network, built since the 1960s among Hindu communities in Britain, North America, the Caribbean and East Africa, collected funds for the temple and consecrated bricks of its own for dispatch to Ayodhya, so that the sacramental supply chain began in Leicester and Toronto as well as in the villages of Bihar. The diaspora dimension mattered to the movement's self-understanding out of proportion to its money. A demand that Indian courts had treated for forty years as a district quarrel was being answered with consecrated bricks from three continents, and the campaign's literature drew the conclusion its founders had designed the Parishad to make possible: that Hindu society existed as one body across the world's map, and that Ayodhya was the address at which that body could be counted. Each pujan required a local committee, a priest or ascetic to conduct the rite, a procession route and a collection, and it left behind all three as standing organisation. A household that might never attend a rally could host the brick for a night. A village divided by caste could process behind it in one line. The consecrated brick was the campaign made portable, a piece of the future temple that could be touched, garlanded and wept over in settlements that Ayodhya's name had never reached.

The pujan's liturgy repay attention, because its steps were the campaign in miniature. The brick arrived in the settlement on a decorated cart or carried on a devotee's head, to the blowing of conches and the singing of Ram bhajans, and was received by the village's elders as a guest of honour is received. It was installed on a cleaned and cow-dung-washed platform or in the village temple, bathed, marked with sandal and vermilion, garlanded and offered aarti by households in turn through the day and night of its stay. A priest or a visiting ascetic recited the birthplace story and the pledge: that the brick would rest only in the wall of the temple at the janmabhoomi. Offerings were collected and receipted. Then the brick was seen off in procession to the next settlement, and the committee that had organised its welcome entered its names in the campaign's register. Every step was a familiar act of devotion, and the sequence of steps was an act of organisation, and the fusion of the two, indistinguishable to the participant, is the precise phenomenon the substantive reading exists to name. A rally can be forbidden, a pamphlet seized, a speaker arrested. A brick being worshipped in ten thousand villages on the same evening presented the district magistrate with no object his powers were shaped to grasp, and the campaign's designers, veterans of the Ekatmata experiment, had chosen the form with exactly that elusiveness in view.

The four stages through which this machinery was assembled can be set out plainly, and Table 1 states them in the form in which the rest of this article cites them.

| Prioritisation | April 1984 | First Dharm Sansad at Vigyan Bhavan resolves on Ayodhya, Mathura and Varanasi, with Ayodhya first | The birthplace named a non-negotiable sacred trust whose recovery is a religious duty |

The season's logistics reward a closer look, because they show the sacrament and the supply chain as one design. State governments read the same processions differently and answered differently, which multiplied the campaign's evidence that it faced a hostile or a friendly state depending on the map. In Uttar Pradesh, the Congress government of Narayan Dutt Tiwari permitted the pujans and negotiated their routes, calculating, as its critics charged and its defenders did not trouble to deny, the electoral cost of stopping a rite in an election season. In Bihar, the administration restricted and in places banned the processions, and the restrictions themselves became campaign material, proof in the movement's telling that Hindu devotion required defenders against the state. The bricks, meanwhile, accumulated. Trucked and trained toward Ayodhya through the autumn, inscribed, garlanded, smeared with the vermilion of a hundred thousand village altars, they were stored in the town as the temple's advance guard, and the movement's counters multiplied their number with each retelling until the count itself became a claim: a quarter of a million consecrated bricks, in the figure the campaign settled on, each one a household that had voted with its hands before any ballot was cast.

The pujan season ended at Ayodhya itself. On 9 November 1989, with the general election a fortnight away, the Parishad conducted the shilanyas, the laying of the temple's first foundation stone, on land adjoining the disputed plot which the Allahabad High Court had ordered held in status quo. The Union government of Rajiv Gandhi, which had earlier in 1989 permitted the ceremony's planning, allowed it to proceed; Home Minister Buta Singh had received Singhal and given the assurance the Parishad sought. The first brick of the foundation was laid by Kameshwar Chaupal, a Dalit worker from Bihar, a casting decision the movement advertised as proof that the temple cause dissolved caste. Muslim organisations protested that the ceremony breached the court's order and the dispute's fragile peace, and communal violence scarred the pujan season in several towns, most gravely at Bhagalpur in October 1989, where more than a thousand people, most of them Muslim, were killed in rioting that began around the brick processions. The pujan's sacramental form and the season's bloodshed belong to the same record, and any account of the movement's religious substance that omits either half falsifies both.

### How did consecrated bricks carry the Ayodhya movement into villages?

A brick needs no literacy, no membership and no travel. Through the autumn of 1989 the Parishad's committees carried inscribed bricks into villages in procession, installed them for worship, collected offerings and names, and sent the bricks onward toward Ayodhya. The rite turned a distant title suit into a local festival with local organisers.

Each ceremony left a committee behind in the settlement it touched. By the time the foundation stone was laid at Ayodhya on 9 November 1989, the movement possessed something no Indian campaign had built before: a sacramental supply chain linking the disputed plot to hundreds of thousands of doorsteps.

## The Cost in Blood: Processions and Riots, 1989 to 1990

The movement's sacramental calendar collided with India's communal geography, and the collisions must be recorded without spectacle and without abbreviation. The Ram Shila processions of October 1989 passed through hundreds of towns in which a consecrated brick, a slogan and a route past a mosque could convert a festival into a confrontation, and in several the conversion happened. At Bhagalpur in Bihar, rioting that began on 24 October 1989 around a brick procession's route burned through the town and its surrounding villages for weeks. The official toll exceeded a thousand dead, most of them Muslim, and villages such as Logain, where more than a hundred residents were killed and buried in a cauliflower field, entered the national record as names for administrative failure as much as for communal hatred. A commission of inquiry under Justice N. N. Singh later examined the Bhagalpur violence and faulted the district's policing and the procession's management alike. The movement's spokesmen answered that its processions were peaceful until attacked and that hostile administrations licensed the attacks by rumour; the inquiry record and the journalism of the season assign responsibility in portions that remain disputed, and this article leaves the portions where the record leaves them, in dispute, while stating the toll and the season plainly, because a movement history that counts consecrated bricks and omits the graves dug in the same autumn is devotional literature, not analysis.

The pattern repeated at smaller scale wherever the campaign's calendar met a prepared administration or an unprepared one, through the pujan season of 1989, the yatra season of 1990 and the kar seva calls that followed. Its analytical importance is that the violence was not incidental to the movement's form. A mobilisation built on sacred objects carried through mixed settlements, under deadlines announced as religious duties, with young men's formations attached to its processions, generated confrontation as a by-product of its ordinary operation, whatever its leaders intended on any given day. The instrumentalist reading of the movement sometimes treats that violence as the point, the polarisation that harvested votes. The substantive reading must treat it more carefully, as the record presents it: a foreseeable consequence of the movement's chosen instruments, foreseen by administrators who wrote their warnings in files that survive, and accepted as a risk by organisers whose correspondence shows the calendar mattering more than the caution. Between intention and acceptance lies the space in which the movement's moral accounting belongs, and the scholars divide over that space as sharply as over everything else in this history.

## 1990 and 1992 in the Substantive Frame

The events of 1990 and 1992 have their own full treatments elsewhere in this series, and this article takes them only as the substantive frame's proving ground. When L. K. Advani rolled out of Somnath on 25 September 1990 in a jeep dressed as a chariot, the crowds that met him had been prepared by seven years of Parishad work: by the ekatmata processions that taught villages the form, by the Sansad's resolutions that supplied the demand, by the opened shrine that gave the demand a living object, and by the brick consecrations that had built a committee in every district the chariot crossed. The [institutional-architectural break reading](/2019/10/10/advani-rath-yatra-ayodhya-movement/) of that journey, its passage from cadre politics to mass politics, is correct as far as it goes; the substantive reading adds why the mass was there to be met. The yatra was stopped at Samastipur on 23 October 1990 by the Bihar government of Lalu Prasad Yadav, the V. P. Singh government fell within weeks over the withdrawal of support, and on 30 October and again on 2 November 1990 kar sevaks pressed toward the shrine at Ayodhya and were fired on by Uttar Pradesh state police ordered to hold the site by Chief Minister Mulayam Singh Yadav. The death toll of those firings remains disputed between the official count and the movement's higher account, and the movement carried the ashes of its dead, the kalash yatras of early 1991, through the towns as it had once carried bricks.

Mulayam Singh Yadav's government, for its part, announced its defence of the shrine in terms its author repeated across the season, that not even a bird would be permitted to alight on the disputed structure, and the phrase entered the movement's own literature as the boast its martyrs had answered. The firings' toll entered the two mobilisations' rival ledgers, the state counting its dead in the teens and the movement counting in scores, and the discrepancy was never reconciled, because each ledger served a memory its keeper required. What is not disputed is the sequence's effect on the substantive campaign: the movement acquired martyrs, and martyrdom, processed through the kalash yatras of 1991 in which the ashes of the Ayodhya dead were carried in procession through the towns, completed the sacramental circuit the bricks had begun. The dead of October and November 1990 were mourned in the idiom of the faith, by the machinery of the faith, and their mourning recruited as no pamphlet could.

The road to December 1992 ran through a season of negotiation in which every institution of the republic tried to price the sacred demand and found it unpriceable. Through the autumn the Parishad and the saints set the kar seva for 6 December and described it as symbolic, a rite of presence at the site. The Supreme Court, seized of the contempt and status questions from the July platform episode, accepted the Uttar Pradesh government's undertakings that the mosque would be protected and appointed an observer for the day. The National Integration Council met in November and heard the demand and the defence stated in full. The Union government of P. V. Narasimha Rao negotiated with the movement's leaders and with the BMAC in parallel, explored formulae of reference and acquisition, and held its paramilitary forces outside the town on the state's assurance. Each of these proceedings assumed, in its own grammar, that the parties were bargaining over interests that a formula could divide. The substantive reading of this article holds that the assumption was the season's central error. A trust does not divide, and the sevaks converging on Ayodhya in the first days of December, in numbers the administration estimated at a hundred and fifty thousand and the movement counted higher, had not travelled to witness a bargain. They had been summoned, by the machinery this article has traced from 1964, to perform labour at a birthplace, in the presence of saints who had told them for a decade that the structure in their way was an intolerable imposition on the sacred. What formula could be read to such a crowd, and by whom, was the question the negotiators never answered, because their proceedings had no chair for the Dharm Sansad.

On 6 December 1992, kar sevaks assembled for that summoned kar seva climbed the domes of the Babri Masjid and demolished it, in defiance of undertakings given to the Supreme Court by the Uttar Pradesh government of Kalyan Singh. The demolition's politics, its planning disputes, its judicial aftermath and the Liberhan Commission's long inquiry belong to the companion treatment of [the demolition and its consequences](/2019/10/16/1992-babri-demolition-politics/), and to the Liberhan record within it. In the substantive frame the day reads as the moment the movement's religious content overran its organisers' control: a crowd summoned as worshippers, armed with consecrated purpose and a decade of teaching that the structure was an intolerable imposition, did what its leaders had alternately promised, postponed and disowned. Violence across India followed the demolition, killing around two thousand people in its waves, most heavily in Bombay in December 1992 and January 1993. A movement whose substance was sacramental had produced, at its climax, an act of destruction that no sacrament authorised in words, and the adjudication of responsibility for that passage, between incitement, conspiracy and spontaneous frenzy, is a dispute the Liberhan record and the criminal trials carried for a generation. This article's narrower point stands clear of that dispute: the crowd of 6 December was assembled by the religious machinery described in these pages, and no account of the day that begins with party strategy can explain why the crowd came, or what it believed it was doing.

## Kar Seva: The Liturgy of Voluntary Labour

One instrument of the movement deserves separate treatment because it fused its theology and its organisation into a single act. Kar seva, voluntary labour offered as worship, is an old practice of temple and gurdwara building, in which the devotee's sweat is itself an offering. The Ayodhya campaign converted it into a summons. To call kar seva at Ayodhya on a named date was to invite devotees to travel as pilgrims, assemble as worshippers and perform, at the destination, labour on the temple: clearing, digging, laying stone. The form dissolved the line between a rally and a rite. A participant was not a protester in the movement's own description, and not a soldier; he was a sevak, a servant of Ram performing service, and the legal and moral categories that governed crowds applied awkwardly to him, as governments discovered each time a summons was issued.

The instrument was tested in stages. The kar seva of 30 October 1990, called after the rath yatra's arrest, brought the firings described above and made martyrs whose ashes toured India in the kalash processions of the following months, turning bereavement into mobilisation with the same sacramental logistics the bricks had used. A second kar seva in July 1992 saw volunteers raise a concrete platform on the acquired land near the shrine in defiance of court restraint, an act of construction as argument that drew contempt proceedings and demonstrated that the summons could produce building, not only assembly. The summons of 6 December 1992 was framed by its issuers as symbolic kar seva, a rite of presence while negotiations and court hearings ran, and what the assembled sevaks did to the mosque that afternoon is the hinge on which the movement's whole history turns. In the substantive frame, kar seva explains both the summons' power and its uncontrollability. Labour offered to a deity cannot be stood down by a press release with the reliability of a party picket, because its participants answer, in their own accounting, to the deity and to the saints who summoned them. The movement's managers knew this and used it, as a pressure that could be threatened more credibly than it could always be governed. When later inquiries apportioned blame for December 1992 between conspiracy and spontaneity, they were, in the vocabulary of this article, arguing over whether kar seva's theology or its organisation should be held responsible for its crowd, and the honest answer the record supports is that the instrument was designed so that the two could not be separated.

## After the Demolition: Trust, Workshop and Court, 1993 to 2019

The brief for this article names a final substantive shift, and the post-1992 record bears it out: the movement that had mobilised through sacrament learned to persist through institutions. The Union government acquired 67.7 acres at Ayodhya under the Acquisition of Certain Area at Ayodhya Act of 1993, froze the disputed plot under central control and referred the title question's history to the Supreme Court through a presidential reference the Court declined to answer in 1994. The movement's answer to the freeze was the Ram Janmabhoomi Nyas of 25 January 1993, a trust of Parishad members under Paramahansa Ramchandra Das, built to hold the claim in religious custody and to prepare the temple in fact while its legality slept. At Karsevakpuram, the volunteers' settlement outside Ayodhya, the Nyas ran stone workshops where pillars and carved blocks for the future temple accumulated year after year in sheds that visitors were shown as evidence of inevitability. The workshops were the brick campaign transposed into a minor key: no longer mobilisation, but material witness, a temple visibly under construction in everything but law.

The trust decades also exposed the triangle's strain in the open. Between 1998 and 2004 a Bharatiya Janata Party-led coalition governed India with the temple deferred, first by coalition compulsion and then by the stated policy of awaiting the courts, while the Parishad's Dharm Sansads issued fresh deadlines for the site's handover and the Nyas's saints denounced delay in the idiom of betrayed trust. In March 2002 the Parishad pressed to perform a symbolic rite at Ayodhya with a carved pillar, and the government of Atal Bihari Vajpayee negotiated the pillar's handover to an official at the town's edge rather than at the site, a small ceremony that displayed the whole post-demolition settlement in one gesture: the movement's sacramental claim intact, its satisfaction postponed, and the state mediating between the saints it could not command and the court orders it could not break. The saints' wing drew its own conclusion, that electoral power used the sacred demand and then shelved it, and carried that grievance into the movement's internal politics for years. The instrumentalist scholars cite these years as their proof text. The substantive reading answers that a shelved demand which its holders refuse to sell, and which returns to settle the republic's largest title suit a generation later, is poorly described as a tool that its users put down.

Litigation absorbed the rest of the movement's energy, and its content changed accordingly. The Allahabad High Court's Lucknow bench, which had held the title suits since 1989, ordered an excavation of the disputed site by the Archaeological Survey of India in 2003; the Survey's report of August 2003 described a large earlier structure beneath the mosque, which the Hindu plaintiffs read as the temple their tradition remembered and which Muslim plaintiffs and their historians disputed as a reading the evidence could not carry. On 30 September 2010 the bench delivered its judgment, dividing the disputed land in three shares among the deity Ram Lalla, the Nirmohi Akhara and the Sunni Waqf Board. All sides appealed, and on 9 November 2019 a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court, in a unanimous judgment, awarded the disputed site for the temple through a trust the Union government was directed to create, held the demolition of 1992 and the placement of idols in 1949 to have been wrongs in law, and directed that five acres at Ayodhya be allotted to the Sunni Waqf Board for a mosque. The movement's spokesmen received the verdict as the recovery their saints had demanded in 1984; its Muslim counterparts received it with stated grief and a decision, by the Board, not to seek review. In the substantive frame this article has defended, the thirty-five years from Dharm Sansad to judgment show a single content passing through four carriers: sacrament, crowd, trust and text. The content, a birthplace exact and non-negotiable, survived every carrier change, which is the strongest available evidence that it was the movement's substance and not its costume.

## The Other Mobilisation's Substance

Evenhandedness requires that the counter-mobilisation be read for its substance and not only as a reaction. The defence of the Babri Masjid rested on its own theology of place and its own doctrine of the republic. The mosque, in the BMAC's literature and in the Waqf Board's pleadings, was a house of God consecrated by four and a half centuries of prayer, held in waqf, a perpetual religious endowment that Islamic law treats as inalienable, so that conceding the site was not a property compromise but a surrender of a trust no living custodian had the right to make. Around that legal theology stood a political one, stated by the committee's convenor Zafaryab Jilani and by Muslim parliamentarians across the decade: the mosque had become the test of whether India's Muslims held their citizenship on equal terms or on the majority's sufferance, and its defence was therefore a defence of the constitutional settlement itself. The parallel with the movement it opposed is exact and was visible to both sides at the time. Each mobilisation treated one structure as non-negotiable, each grounded the non-negotiability in sacred trust, each built committees, processions and print to carry the claim, and each accused the other of manufacturing the emergency. The adjudication that follows concerns the Hindu movement's content, which is this article's subject, but the content's career cannot be understood outside the symmetry that doubled every escalation: the locks, the bricks, the deadlines and the marches each summoned their mirror, until the republic's institutions were mediating not a dispute and a campaign but two sacred trusts in collision.

## Three Readings of One Movement: An Adjudication

Three readings of the Ayodhya movement compete in the literature, and the scholarly disagreement is worth adjudicating rather than merely listing. The first reading, the electoral-consequences account, treats the movement as an instrument of party competition: a declining Bharatiya Janata Party, reduced to two Lok Sabha seats in 1984, adopted the temple demand, rode the rath yatra to eighty-five seats in 1989 and one hundred and twenty in 1991, and harvested the decade. On this reading the religion is costume and the arithmetic is the content. The second reading, the institutional-architectural account carried by the rath yatra treatment in this series, locates the substance in organisational form: the movement marks the passage of Hindu nationalist politics from a cadre architecture to a cadre-plus-mass architecture, with the Parishad and the Dharm Sansad as the transmission machinery. The third reading, defended here, holds that the movement had a substantive religious content of its own, specific doctrines about a birthplace, specific sacraments of brick and water, specific authorities in the ascetic orders, and that this content is what the machinery transmitted. Strip the content away and the machinery has nothing to carry; a party could no more have run the 1989 pujan for a motorway than a parishad could have consecrated bricks for a tax cut.

The strongest counter to the third reading is the instrumentalist one, pressed by historians and political scientists who note the timing: the demand slept while the BJP was strong under other banners and woke precisely when the party needed a mass issue, the campaign's tempo followed election calendars in 1989, 1990 and 1991, and its leaders suspended or moderated the agitation whenever coalition arithmetic required calm. On this account the substance was deployed, and a deployed substance is a tool, not a content. The counter is partly right and the concession should be made cleanly: the movement's custodians were strategists, Singhal and Advani both, and the Parishad's own correspondence of 1984 to 1989, preserved partially in its Delhi archives, shows campaign planning of a sophistication no honest account can call spontaneous. Yet deployment presupposes something deployable. The strategists did not invent the birthplace theology, the ascetic networks, the pilgrimage geography of Ayodhya or the sacramental grammar of consecration; they found them, selected among them, and amplified what they found. The content constrained the strategy as much as the strategy used the content: the demand could not be traded away in negotiation, as a mere instrument could have been, because its holders experienced it as a trust, and on 6 December 1992 the content escaped the strategists entirely. Andersen and Damle's inside account of the Sangh records the leadership's recurring alarm at a devotional energy it had summoned and could not always steer, and Manini Chatterjee's essays on the movement likewise trace how the religious mobilisation acquired a momentum its political managers repeatedly had to chase. A tool does not frighten its makers. The substantive reading survives the instrumentalist counter in qualified form: the content was real, it was organised, and its organisation is the story.

The textual war deserves a paragraph of its own, because both camps fought the movement in print with an intensity that shaped what could be said in public. The movement's publishing wing, the Voice of India house in Delhi associated with Sita Ram Goel, issued the documentary briefs of the temple case, most fully in the two volumes of *Hindu Temples: What Happened to Them* (1990 and 1991), which catalogued mosque sites across India as demolished temples and framed Ayodhya as one entry in a civilisational ledger. Koenraad Elst's *Ram Janmabhoomi vs. Babri Masjid* (1990) argued the temple case as a work of advocacy scholarship and attacked the academic historians who disputed it. On the other side, the historians' collective statement of 1989 and the writings of Romila Thapar treated the campaign's history as a fabrication assembled for mobilisation, and Tapan Basu, Pradip Datta, Sumit Sarkar, Tanika Sarkar and Sambuddha Sen, in *Khaki Shorts and Saffron Flags* (1993), analysed the Sangh's Ayodhya strategy as a calculated passage from cultural organisation to mass communal politics. These were not footnotes to the movement; they were among its battlefields, and the ferocity of the quarrel among historians is itself evidence that the movement's content, its claims about the past, was the ground on which the whole contest stood. An electoral machine does not require a historiography. This movement required one, produced one, and fought one, which the substantive reading counts among its strongest exhibits.

Where does that leave the series' own architecture of accounts? The [contested-settlement framework](/2020/04/09/indian-secularism-contested-settlement/) for Indian secularism holds that the republic's religion-state arrangements were never settled once but are renegotiated at moments of pressure, and Ayodhya is that framework's hardest case: a renegotiation conducted not in a constituent assembly but in processions, courtrooms and consecration grounds. The [tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/) for Hindutva as ideology supplies the doctrinal background against which the Parishad's theology of place stands out as practice rather than text. The movement also reaches forward: the temple politics it normalised frames [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) within which the post-2014 decades are read, and its beginnings lie in the [post-1989 explicit-Hindutva phase](/2019/10/05/1980-founding-bjp-explained/) of the party that adopted it. Its antecedents run through the [religious-place-state-administration template](/2020/04/14/1949-babri-masjid-lock-up-politics/) set in 1949, when the state first answered a sacred claim by locking a gate and posting a guard, and through the [religious-personal-law-state-intervention renegotiation](/2020/04/20/shah-bano-case-1986-act-india/) of the very season in which the locks came off, when the same Union government was recalibrating its posture toward both of India's great religious constituencies at once. The Ayodhya movement sits at the crossing of all these lines, which is why a reading of its substance, and not only of its uses, is indispensable.

A word, finally, on how the second and third readings compose, because the brief for this series sets them as complements and the evidence supports the composition. The institutional-architectural reading explains the movement's timing and reach: why a grievance dormant for decades acquired, between 1983 and 1989, the machinery to summon a country. The substantive reading explains the movement's direction and its limits: why the machinery was pointed at one plot of earth and could not be repointed at any other objective, why its summons took sacramental forms, why its crowd could be assembled and yet not commanded, and why its demand survived the demolition, the bans, the trials and the deferrals to be settled, in the end, on the terms the saints of 1984 had announced. Neither reading is complete without the other, and the electoral reading, which mistakes the harvest for the crop, is the only one of the three the record requires us to demote. The movement was used, certainly, and used brilliantly, by politicians who understood its value. It was not invented by them, and the proof of its independence is written across the whole period in the repeated spectacle of its custodians defying, embarrassing and outlasting the very parties that rode it to power.

One practical corollary concerns the sources themselves. The movement's paper trail is scattered across court records at Faizabad and Allahabad, the Parishad's partially preserved correspondence, committee pamphlets and the files of the Liberhan Commission, and researchers who assemble such dossiers for study reach for aids as plain as [a browser tool that opens office documents directly](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) when scans and transcripts arrive in mixed formats. The evidentiary base matters because the movement's history is still argued from documents: each side's strongest claims, the archaeologist's report, the judge's order, the Sansad's resolutions, are texts before they are memories, and the adjudication offered here has tried to keep them so.

## The Movement's Substance, Restated

The Ayodhya movement reanalysed is, on the evidence assembled here, a campaign whose religious content did causal work. An organisation built across twenty years to convene Hindu religious authority turned, under new leadership and the shock of Meenakshipuram, to mass mobilisation by sacrament. A parliament of saints in April 1984 named one birthplace as the faith's first demand and clothed the demand in an authority governments could not bargain with. A district judge's order in February 1986 opened the shrine and provoked a mirror mobilisation that made the contest permanent. Television, print and cassette carried the birthplace story into the nation's homes. Consecrated bricks in 1989 converted spectators into participants by the hundreds of thousands, and the foundation rite at Ayodhya sealed the conversion weeks before an election. The chariot of 1990 and the catastrophe of 1992 followed from that accumulation, not from party strategy alone. Read the movement through its substance and its scale stops being a mystery of manipulation and becomes a record of choices: choices of vehicle, of doctrine, of sacrament and of timing, made by named people whose names this article has kept in the foreground, where the evidence puts them.

The reading also explains the movement's afterlife better than its rivals do. The charter of April 1984 named three sites, and only one has been settled by judgment. Mathura and Varanasi remain inside the movement's stated programme and inside the freeze that the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act of 1991 laid over every other disputed shrine, and the arguments rehearsed at Ayodhya, the exact birthplace, the demolished temple, the archaeologist's spade, the custodian's trust, recur in those disputes because the substantive grammar was written once, at Ayodhya, and proved portable. Electoral readings cannot explain that portability, since the party arithmetic of Mathura and Varanasi differs entirely from the arithmetic of 1989. Institutional readings explain the machinery's reuse but not the content's. The substantive reading, which treats the Ayodhya campaign as the working out of a doctrine of sacred place by an organisation built to carry doctrine into crowds, predicts exactly what the record shows: that the template would outlive its first campaign, its first party harvest and its first generation of saints, and that each new dispute over a shrine in India would be argued in Ayodhya's vocabulary. On that prediction, as on the evidence of 1964 to 2019 assembled in these pages, the claim defended in this article stands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Ayodhya movement?

The Ayodhya movement was the campaign, organised principally by the Vishwa Hindu Parishad from 1984 onward, to remove the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya and build a temple to Ram at the site Hindus venerate as his birthplace. It worked through religious instruments: resolutions of the Dharm Sansad, processions, the consecration of bricks across India in 1989, calls for kar seva, and the mobilisation of ascetic orders and household devotees. The Bharatiya Janata Party adopted the demand from 1989, and L. K. Advani's rath yatra of 1990 carried it nationally. The movement culminated in the demolition of the mosque by kar sevaks on 6 December 1992, and it reshaped Indian politics for a generation.

### Q: What is the VHP?

The VHP, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad or World Hindu Council, is an organisation founded on 29 August 1964 at Bombay to unite Hindu religious life across sects and regions. Swami Chinmayananda took its founding presidency and S. S. Apte, a senior Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh worker, became its first general secretary, with the blessing of the RSS chief M. S. Golwalkar. It declared itself non-political, recruited sadhus, mahants and temple trusts as its constituency, and worked on conferences, cow protection, conversion questions and diaspora links before turning to mass campaigns in the 1980s. Under Ashok Singhal it led the Ram Janmabhoomi campaign, and it later built the Bajrang Dal as its youth wing.

### Q: What is the Ram Janmabhoomi?

Ram Janmabhoomi means the birthplace of Ram, the hero of the Ramayana and a deity worshipped across India. In the Ayodhya dispute the term names a precise location: the ground beneath the central dome of the Babri Masjid, where Hindu tradition holds that a temple marking the birth once stood and where idols of the infant Ram, Ram Lalla, were installed in December 1949. The exactness is essential to the claim. Devotees and the movement built on the claim held that the sanctity attached to those coordinates and could not be satisfied by a temple elsewhere in Ayodhya, which is why the demand resisted every compromise that offered land nearby.

### Q: What was the Dharm Sansad?

The Dharm Sansad, literally a parliament of dharma, is a gathering of Hindu religious leaders, sadhus, mahants and sect heads, convened under Vishwa Hindu Parishad auspices to pronounce on questions the Parishad places before it. Its first and most consequential meeting for Ayodhya took place at Vigyan Bhavan in New Delhi on 7 and 8 April 1984, when several hundred religious leaders resolved that the sacred sites at Ayodhya, Mathura and Varanasi be restored for temple worship, with Ayodhya placed first. The Sansad's resolutions gave the Ayodhya campaign the authority of the ascetic orders rather than of a party, a form the movement used for its demands and deadlines throughout the following decade.

### Q: What was the Ram Shila Pujan?

The Ram Shila Pujan was the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's brick consecration campaign of autumn 1989. Bricks inscribed with the name of Ram were carried in procession through villages and towns, installed on temporary altars, worshipped with full rites and blessed for eventual use in the temple proposed for the Ayodhya birthplace site. The Parishad counted ceremonies in some three hundred thousand villages. The rite turned a distant title dispute into a local festival that any household could join, and each ceremony left behind a committee, a collection network and a list of sympathisers. The season closed with the shilanyas, the laying of the temple's foundation stone at Ayodhya on 9 November 1989.

### Q: When was the Babri Masjid opened for Hindu worship?

The locks on the shrine were ordered opened on 1 February 1986. An Ayodhya advocate, Umesh Chandra Pandey, had applied before the Munsif Sadar at Faizabad in January 1986 for the removal of the restrictions imposed in 1949; the munsif deferred the application, and on appeal the District Judge of Faizabad, K. M. Pandey, directed the district magistrate and police superintendent to open the locks forthwith and to place no hurdle before Hindu worshippers seeking darshan. The order was carried out the same day. Restricted worship by a single priest had continued under the lock since 1950, but February 1986 opened the inner shrine to the public, and pilgrim traffic to Ayodhya rose sharply afterward.

### Q: Who is Ashok Singhal?

Ashok Singhal (1926 to 2015) was the Vishwa Hindu Parishad leader who directed the Ram Janmabhoomi campaign through its decisive years. An engineering graduate from Allahabad who had worked in the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's student and intellectual circles, he joined the Parishad's leadership around 1980 and rose through its secretariat to become its chief organiser and, in later years, its international working president. Singhal pressed the organisation from conferences toward mass campaigns, drove the Ekatmata Yatra and the brick consecrations, received the Union home minister's assurances before the 1989 shilanyas, and remained the movement's most uncompromising public voice, insisting that the birthplace demand admitted no negotiated dilution.

### Q: What was the Meenakshipuram conversion of 1981?

In February 1981, in the village of Meenakshipuram in Tirunelveli district, Tamil Nadu, around two hundred families from the Pallar Dalit community embraced Islam, in most accounts close to a thousand people. The converts stated that they were leaving a Hindu social order that denied them temple entry, tea-shop service and physical safety, and that Islam offered them dignity. Hindu organisations treated the episode as an emergency and a parable: the Vishwa Hindu Parishad sent workers to the district, began service and reconversion efforts, and carried Meenakshipuram's name across India as evidence that an unorganised Hindu society would lose its most oppressed members. The shock pushed the Parishad decisively toward mass mobilisation.

### Q: What was the Babri Masjid Action Committee?

The Babri Masjid Action Committee, the BMAC, was the body formed in March 1986 by Muslim leaders, lawyers and litigants to defend the Babri Masjid after the Faizabad court ordered the shrine's locks opened. The advocate Zafaryab Jilani served as its convenor. The committee coordinated the Muslim side's litigation, published documentary defences of the mosque's title and antiquity, organised conferences and processions, including a large march to the Boat Club in New Delhi in February 1987, and argued in public that the mosque stood on waqf land, had been a place of Muslim worship since 1528, and was protected by the Constitution's guarantees to minorities. It became the Parishad's organised counterpart in a two-sided mobilisation.

### Q: What role did Swami Chinmayananda play in founding the Vishwa Hindu Parishad?

Swami Chinmayananda (1916 to 1993), the Vedanta teacher who had built the Chinmaya Mission into a network carrying Hindu teaching to educated urban audiences in India and abroad, presided over the Parishad's founding at the Sandipani Sadhanalaya ashram in Bombay on 29 August 1964 and took its founding presidency. His presence gave the new body a religious legitimacy distinct from the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's cadre identity: the Parishad would speak in the idiom of teachers and ascetics, not of a political movement. Chinmayananda embodied the founding bargain, in which Sangh organisers supplied the machinery and revered religious figures supplied the sanctity, a division of labour that the Ayodhya campaign later exploited at national scale.

### Q: How did the Vishwa Hindu Parishad differ from the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh?

The two bodies belonged to the same family of organisations yet worked in different registers. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, founded in 1925, built character and organisation through its shakhas, daily gatherings of uniformed volunteers under a discipline of celibate full-time workers, and it formally kept its distance from both electoral politics and temple religion. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad, founded in 1964, was created to operate where the RSS could not: among sadhus, monastic orders, temple trusts and household devotees, speaking the language of dharma and ritual rather than of national discipline. In the Ayodhya campaign the division held visibly. The RSS supplied organisers and restraint; the Parishad supplied the saints, the sacraments and the sacred demand itself.

### Q: What happened at the shilanyas of November 1989?

The shilanyas was the laying of the proposed Ram temple's foundation stone at Ayodhya on 9 November 1989, performed by the Vishwa Hindu Parishad on land adjoining the disputed plot, which stood under an Allahabad High Court status quo order. The Union government of Rajiv Gandhi allowed the ceremony to proceed, weeks before the 1989 general election, after Home Minister Buta Singh had assured Ashok Singhal of permission. The first foundation brick was laid by Kameshwar Chaupal, a Dalit worker from Bihar, a choice the movement publicised as proof that the temple cause crossed caste. Muslim organisations protested that the rite breached the court's order, and the pujan season around it saw grave communal violence, above all at Bhagalpur.

### Q: What did Muslim organisations argue about the Ram Janmabhoomi claim?

Muslim parties to the dispute, the Sunni Central Waqf Board in court and the Babri Masjid Action Committee in public, argued from title, antiquity and constitutional protection. They maintained that Mir Baqi built the mosque in 1528 on waqf land without demolishing a temple, that no contemporary record attests a demolition, that the mosque had been a place of Muslim worship for over four centuries, and that the placement of idols inside it in December 1949 was a trespass the law should have reversed rather than ratified. Their historians added that early texts do not fix Ram's birth at the mosque's exact site. On these grounds they held the movement's demand to be a majoritarian claim dressed in sacred language.

### Q: What role did sadhus and akharas play in the Ayodhya movement?

The ascetic orders gave the movement its authority, its local roots and some of its least governable energy. Ayodhya's akharas, above all the Nirmohi Akhara, had litigated the site since 1959 and supplied the town's militant memory of the shrine. The Dharm Sansad gathered mahants and sect heads into a body whose resolutions the Parishad could present as the voice of Hindu religious leadership itself, above party and above negotiation. Individual renouncers, from Ramchandra Paramhans of the Digambar Akhara to the cassette preachers of the late 1980s, carried the demand into districts the organisation reached slowly. The same independence that made the saints valuable also limited control over them, a tension that ran through the campaign to its final day.

### Q: How did the televised Ramayan serial shape the movement's audience?

Ramanand Sagar's *Ramayan*, broadcast on Doordarshan in seventy-eight episodes from 25 January 1987 to 31 July 1988, gave India a single shared telling of Ram's story at the moment the Ayodhya campaign was forming. Streets emptied during broadcasts, television sets were garlanded in some homes, and the actors playing Ram and Sita were received with devotional reverence on tour. The serial was a state broadcaster's production with no organisational tie to the Vishwa Hindu Parishad, yet it standardised the epic's iconography, the bow-bearing Ram of its posters entered the movement's own processions, and it prepared a vast audience to hear the birthplace claim as sacred history rather than as one side of a property dispute. Scholars treat the sequence as convergence, not conspiracy.

### Q: What archaeological claims were made about the Ayodhya site before 1992?

The archaeology was contested decades before it reached a courtroom. B. B. Lal, who directed Archaeological Survey of India excavations at Ayodhya in the 1970s, reported pillar bases and carved fragments at levels below the mosque and argued in print, most influentially in 1990, that a temple structure had preceded the mosque on the site. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad circulated his findings as scientific confirmation of the birthplace claim. Other historians and archaeologists answered that pillar bases do not identify a temple, that the fragments' dating and context were arguable, and that no pre-1992 excavation had demonstrated the demolition of a Ram temple beneath the mosque. The claims and counter-claims remained disputed when the movement reached its climax, and both sides cited the spade as authority.

### Q: What role did women play in the Ayodhya movement?

Women carried the campaign into spaces its male cadres reached awkwardly and gave it some of its most heard voices. Sadhvi Rithambara, a woman ascetic, recorded speeches in the late 1980s that circulated on cassette from hand to hand, denouncing the mosque as a badge of humiliation and summoning Hindu households to the birthplace cause. Uma Bharti addressed rallies through the campaign years and stood among its best-known speakers at Ayodhya in December 1992. Vijaya Raje Scindia, the Rajmata of Gwalior and a senior Bharatiya Janata Party figure, lent the demand establishment respectability. At the base, women hosted consecrated bricks, joined pujan processions and staffed the local committees the pujan left behind, making the mobilisation a household enterprise rather than a young men's agitation.

### Q: What was the Ram Janmabhoomi Nyas?

The Ram Janmabhoomi Nyas was the trust constituted by Vishwa Hindu Parishad members on 25 January 1993, weeks after the demolition, to take charge of the Ayodhya site and to oversee the construction of the proposed Ram temple. It was headed first by Mahant Ramchandra Das Paramhans of the Digambar Akhara, who led it until his death in 2003, when Mahant Nritya Gopal Das succeeded him. Its founders argued that a religious trust, rather than the government, should control the site and the temple project, and the Nyas ran workshops at Karsevakpuram outside Ayodhya where stone for the temple was carved through the following decades. Muslim parties challenged its claims in the title litigation, disputing its standing to hold the land.

### Q: Why did the movement insist on the exact spot beneath the mosque's central dome?

Because the claim was attached to coordinates, not to a town. Hindu tradition as the movement received it held that Ram was born at one identifiable place, that a temple marking the birth had stood there, and that the deity installed in 1949, Ram Lalla, was present at that spot in person. A temple built on any other plot in Ayodhya, however grand, would therefore not answer the demand, and the movement's theologians said so explicitly whenever compromise formulas offered adjacent land. The insistence converted a negotiable dispute about property into a non-negotiable question of sacred geography, which is why mediation efforts that treated the site as divisible acreage repeatedly failed, and why the title suits ran for seventy years.

### Q: How did the Ayodhya movement present its demand as religious rather than political?

The movement's custodians framed every instrument in the idiom of faith. The demand itself was issued not by a party manifesto but by the Dharm Sansad, an assembly of sadhus and sect heads, and was described as a sacred trust that no government had the standing to bargain away. Its mass instruments were rites: processions carrying sacred water, consecrations of bricks, a foundation rite, fasts and vows. Participation was solicited as devotion, through darshan, offering and pilgrimage, rather than as party membership, and the Vishwa Hindu Parishad, which ran the machinery, held a formally non-political constitution that barred its officeholders from simultaneous party office. Critics answered that the framing was itself the politics, since the campaign's tempo, alliances and suspensions tracked electoral need, a charge the adjudication in this article examines rather than dismisses.
