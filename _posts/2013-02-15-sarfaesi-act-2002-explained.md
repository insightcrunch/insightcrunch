The SARFAESI Act 2002 did something no Indian statute had done before it: it handed a private lender the power to seize a borrower's property and sell it to a stranger, without first persuading any judge that the debt was due. Under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002, once a loan account is classified as non-performing, the secured creditor issues a notice, waits sixty days, and may then take possession of the secured assets, take over the management of the borrower's business, appoint a manager to run it, or intercept money owed to the borrower by third parties. The borrower who objects does so afterwards, before a tribunal, against a process that is already moving. Enforcement first, adjudication later: that reversal of the ordinary sequence of Indian civil justice is the whole statute in one sentence, and everything else in the Act is machinery built around it or safeguards built against it.

The reversal was deliberate, and it answered a real failure. A secured lender in the India of the 1990s held security that was, in practical terms, an ornament. The mortgage deed said the bank could enforce; the civil courts said the bank could wait. A recovery suit travelled through pleadings, issues, evidence, decree, execution proceedings and appeals, and a decade could pass between default and realisation, during which the machinery rusted, the stock vanished and the borrower's other creditors helped themselves to whatever remained. Security that cannot be realised does not lower the price of credit; it merely decorates the loan file. Every borrower in the country, including every honest one, paid for that delay in higher interest and scarcer loans, because a lender who cannot recover from the defaulting few must charge the performing many. The Act's wager was that the cost of credit is set at the margin of recovery, and that making security real would cheapen borrowing for everyone. Whether that wager has paid off, and what it has cost the borrowers caught inside the machinery, is the honest argument this article has to hold open while it explains the machine itself.

## What existed before, and why it failed

The ordinary route was the civil suit, and its failure was not a failure of law but of time. A bank suing on a mortgage under the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 had to obtain a preliminary decree and then a final decree before the property could be sold, and every stage admitted objection, adjournment and appeal. The borrower in possession had every incentive to stretch the process, because delay cost the borrower nothing and preserved the use of the asset. By the time execution arrived, the decree was often a claim against a husk. The second route, self-help under Section 69 of the Transfer of Property Act, existed only for narrow classes of mortgage, principally certain English mortgages and mortgages in favour of specified public bodies, and was hedged with conditions that made it unusable for ordinary commercial lending. The third route, the State Financial Corporations Act, 1951, did give state financial corporations a summary power to take over and sell the assets of defaulting industrial units, and its existence mattered later, because it proved that enforcement without prior adjudication was neither unknown to Indian law nor constitutionally unthinkable. But the corporations covered a small corner of institutional credit, and the commercial banks that carried the bulk of the system's lending had nothing comparable.

Parliament's first systemic answer was the Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions Act, 1993, which lifted bank recovery out of the civil courts and gave it to specialised Debts Recovery Tribunals. The tribunals were a genuine improvement in forum, but they changed only who decided, not when the creditor could act. The bank still had to apply, plead, prove and obtain an order before it could touch the security, and the tribunal route itself accumulated arrears as filings grew faster than the benches created to hear them. The debt recovery tribunal framework of 1993 was therefore the first attempt and the SARFAESI Act the second, and the distance between them is the measure of the first attempt's inadequacy: within a decade, Parliament concluded that a faster judge was not enough, and that the creditor had to be allowed to act first and answer questions afterwards.

The intellectual work was done by committees, and their diagnosis never varied. The Committee on the Financial System of 1991, chaired by M. Narasimham, began the banking reform programme of which the tribunals were one product. The second Narasimham committee, the Committee on Banking Sector Reforms of 1998, pressed further, recommending legal changes that would let lenders enforce security with real speed and proposing asset reconstruction as a way of moving bad loans off bank balance sheets into specialised hands. The specialist work fell to the committee chaired by T. R. Andhyarujina, a former Solicitor General, appointed in 2000 to examine banking law reform. The Andhyarujina Committee recommended precisely the architecture that became this Act: powers for banks and financial institutions to take possession of securities and sell them without the intervention of a court, on the model the state financial corporations already possessed, with proper safeguards, together with a new central registry of security interests to replace a fragmented record in which the same asset could be quietly pledged to several lenders. The committees spoke for the system's creditors, and said so openly: their premise was that a financial system cannot lend against assets it cannot reach. The counter-premise, that a borrower facing a lender who is judge of his own default needs protection inside the process rather than outside it, entered the statute later and from a different direction, through the courts.

Why the banks' bad loans had grown so large is itself part of the story, because the state stood on both sides of the problem. The great public sector banks, whose ownership dated from [the bank nationalisation Acts of 1969 and 1980](/2013/01/01/bank-nationalisation-acts-india/), carried portfolios shaped by decades of directed credit, and when those loans soured the owner of the banks and the author of the recovery law were the same government. A state that had ordered its banks to lend for social purposes then had to decide how much force those banks could use when the loans failed, and the tension runs through every safeguard debate the Act has generated since.

## Ordinance first: how the Act arrived

The route by which the Act arrived mattered to its reputation, because a statute that arms creditors against borrowers was itself born without waiting for the ordinary legislative timetable. The government promulgated the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Ordinance on 21 June 2002, while Parliament was not in session, and the enforcement regime took practical effect from that date. An Ordinance under Article 123 is emergency law-making, justified by the executive as necessary when immediate action cannot wait; its use for a measure of this consequence, reordering the rights of every secured borrower in the country, drew the predictable political objection that a redistribution of power between lenders and borrowers deserved full parliamentary deliberation before rather than after its commencement. The replacing Bill was then taken through Parliament, debated in the winter of that year against the background of that objection and of the lending industry's insistence that the tribunal experiment had already consumed a decade, and the Act received the President's assent on 17 December 2002 as Act 54 of 2002, with its commencement deemed from 21 June 2002, so that the Ordinance period and the Act form one continuous legal fabric and actions taken under the Ordinance are actions under the Act.

The parliamentary controversy is worth recording at its strongest on both sides, because it prefigured every later debate. The government's case rested on the committees and on the numbers of the banking system: bad assets were choking credit, the tribunals of 1993 had not delivered speed, and the reform had been recommended by bodies whose members were not spokesmen for any single bank. The opposition's case rested on the identity of the beneficiary and the victim in the individual instance: the statute would be used not against the large industrial defaulters of the headlines, who could afford layers of counsel, but against small traders, small industrialists and home buyers, for whom a possession notice is an existential event and a tribunal in a distant city a theoretical remedy. Both predictions proved substantially correct, which is why the statute's politics never settled. The large defaulter and the small borrower travel through the same Section 13, and the Act's defenders and critics have been describing the same machine from opposite ends ever since. The ordinance route also left a doctrinal trace: because the Act was deemed in force from June 2002, and because its provisions applied to enforcement against defaults that pre-dated it, the statute carried a retrospective reach over existing security that borrowers challenged and courts sustained, on the reasoning that enforcement machinery is procedural and that no borrower holds a vested right in the slowness of the old process.

## How the SARFAESI Act 2002 is put together

As enacted, the Act is a short statute of forty-one sections in six chapters, and its three moving parts correspond to the three words of its long title. Chapter II regulates securitisation and asset reconstruction: it provides for the registration and regulation of asset reconstruction companies, their acquisition of financial assets from banks, and the issue of security receipts to investors. Chapter III, containing Section 13, is the enforcement engine, and it is the part of the Act that touches ordinary borrowers. Chapter IV creates a Central Registry in which securitisation transactions, reconstruction transactions and security interests are to be recorded, and a later chapter added in 2016 attaches legal consequences to registration, including a bar on enforcement of unregistered security and a rule of priority. Chapter V deals with offences and penalties, and Chapter VI with miscellaneous matters, including the crucial exclusions in Section 31, the bar on civil court jurisdiction in Section 34 and the overriding effect claimed by Section 35. The enforcement chapter is where the weight sits, and a reader who understands Section 13 and the remedies that answer it understands the Act.

The actors are defined with care in Section 2, because the definitions decide who may use the machine. A borrower is any person who has been granted financial assistance by a bank or financial institution and who has created a security interest to secure repayment, and the definition reaches guarantors as well, a point of real consequence examined later in this article. A secured creditor includes banks, financial institutions, debenture trustees acting for secured liabilities, and asset reconstruction companies that have acquired the debt, so the power travels with the loan when the loan is sold. The institutions that wield this power are not unregulated actors: banks operate under the licensing and supervisory frame of [the Banking Regulation Act 1949](/2012/12/15/banking-regulation-act-1949-explained/), and the Reserve Bank's prudential directions govern the classification decisions that start the SARFAESI clock. A security interest is the right, title and interest of any kind created on property in favour of a secured creditor, and it includes mortgage, charge, hypothecation and assignment. The trigger concept is the non-performing asset, defined as an asset or account of a borrower which has been classified by the bank or financial institution as sub-standard, doubtful or a loss asset in accordance with the directions or guidelines on asset classification issued by the Reserve Bank. Every word of that definition matters. The classification is made by the creditor itself, applying the Reserve Bank's norms, and it is the classification, not any court's finding, that opens the door to Section 13.

That single design choice, the creditor as classifier, is the statute's strength and its standing grievance in one. It is the strength because classification under the Reserve Bank's income recognition and asset classification norms is meant to be mechanical: an account where interest or instalments remain overdue beyond the prescribed period slides into non-performing status by rule rather than by discretion, and a mechanical trigger cannot be negotiated with, lobbied or stayed. It is the grievance because the borrower experiences the trigger as a decision taken by the counterparty, and disputes about whether the classification was correct, whether payments were appropriated properly, whether the account was restructured or wrongly recalled, arrive only after the machine has started. The statute's answer to that grievance is procedural rather than jurisdictional: the borrower gets a right to object inside the process and a tribunal afterwards, but at no point a right to have the default itself adjudicated before the creditor acts. Everything the courts have done to this Act since 2002 can be read as an attempt to make that procedural answer worth something.


## Who counts as a bank: co-operative banks and the reach of the creditor class

One boundary of the creditor class was contested for years and deserves its own account, because it decided whether millions of borrowers from the co-operative banking sector stood inside or outside the summary regime. The Act's definitions speak of banks and banking companies, and the question whether a co-operative bank, registered under state or multi-state co-operative law and regulated jointly by the Reserve Bank and the co-operative registrars, could wield Section 13 was answered differently in different eras. The doubt had a foundation in the recovery field's earlier history, since the Supreme Court had held in Greater Bombay Co-operative Bank v. United Yarn Tex that co-operative banks did not fall within the recovery tribunal statute's scheme as it then stood, and borrowers argued by analogy that SARFAESI likewise stopped at the co-operative sector's edge. The contrary pull was practical: co-operative banks lend against the same security, suffer the same defaults, and operate under the Reserve Bank's banking regulation for their banking business. Parliament moved the position in stages, and the amendment of 2012 expressly brought multi-state co-operative banks within the framework. The Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court then settled the matter in Pandurang Ganpati Chaugule v. Vishwasrao Patil in 2020, holding that co-operative banks are banks for the purposes of this Act and may enforce security under it, reasoning from the constitutional distribution of legislative competence over banking and from the Act's own definitions. The episode is a reminder that the creditor class in this statute is a legislative artefact, expanded by amendment, by notification and by interpretation, and that each expansion re-fights the original argument of 2002 in a new constituency: borrowers of each newly included class discover that a process designed for commercial banks has arrived at their door, while the institutions argue, with force, that a power to lend without a power to recover is an instruction to stop lending to exactly the borrowers the sector exists to serve.

## The demand notice: Section 13(2) and the sixty-day clock

Enforcement begins, under Section 13(1), without the intervention of any court or tribunal, and expressly despite Sections 69 and 69A of the Transfer of Property Act, which are the provisions that confined a mortgagee's self-help to narrow cases. The first formal step is the notice under Section 13(2). Where a borrower defaults in repayment of a secured debt and the account is classified as a non-performing asset, the secured creditor may require the borrower, by notice in writing, to discharge the full liabilities within sixty days from the date of the notice, failing which the creditor will exercise its enforcement powers. The notice must give details of the amount payable by the borrower and of the secured assets intended to be enforced if the borrower fails to pay. Those particulars are not decoration. The demand notice fixes the sum the creditor claims, identifies the property at risk, and starts the only period in the entire process during which the borrower can stop the machine simply by paying or by persuading the creditor that its own record is wrong. A notice that misstates the amount or misdescribes the assets is not a technical blemish; it is a defect in the foundation of everything that follows, and tribunals have set aside enforcement built on defective notices.

The sixty days run from the date of the notice, and what the borrower does inside them decides the character of the whole dispute. Payment in full ends the matter. Part payment does not, unless the creditor agrees to treat the account as regularised, which is a commercial decision the statute does not compel. A representation challenging the classification or the computation is the third possibility, and it has its own statutory treatment, examined below. What the borrower cannot safely do is nothing, because the expiry of the notice period is the point at which the creditor's powers under Section 13(4) become available, and from that point the initiative sits entirely with the creditor. The notice also freezes the borrower's freedom to deal with the property: under Section 13(3), after receipt of the notice the borrower may not transfer the secured assets referred to in it by sale, lease or otherwise, except in the ordinary course of business, without the prior written consent of the secured creditor. A transfer made in breach does not bind the creditor's enforcement, so the borrower cannot defeat the process by conveying the property to a relative or a shell. The asset is, from the notice onward, held in a kind of statutory suspense: still the borrower's, but no longer the borrower's to dispose of.

### What is the difference between symbolic possession and physical possession under SARFAESI?

Symbolic or constructive possession is taken by affixing a possession notice on the property and publishing it, without displacing the occupant; the creditor asserts legal control and the borrower remains in occupation. Physical possession means the authorised officer actually takes the property over, with inventory and, where needed, magistrate assistance, and the occupant must leave.

The distinction is not pedantry, because the two kinds of possession carry different consequences and generate different disputes. Symbolic possession is the creditor's announcement that the property is held against the debt; it preserves the position while valuation, sale arrangements and any tribunal proceedings run their course. Physical possession changes lives immediately, and it is the step at which resistance, occupation by third parties, claims of tenancy and the need for state assistance all concentrate. Borrowers sometimes treat a symbolic possession notice as a formality because nothing visible has changed. That is an error: the measure under Section 13(4) has been taken, the right to apply to the Debts Recovery Tribunal has crystallised, and the forty-five day period for that application is running whether the borrower feels dispossessed or not.

## The borrower's representation: Section 13(3A)

The original Act of 2002 gave the borrower no statutory right to be heard before the creditor proceeded from notice to possession. That omission was the first thing the Supreme Court repaired. In Mardia Chemicals, decided in April 2004 and analysed fully later in this article, the Court held that where the borrower raises objections or places facts before the secured creditor in reply to the demand notice, the creditor must consider them with due application of mind and communicate the reasons for not accepting them, however brief those reasons may be. Parliament then wrote the Court's reading into the statute: Section 13(3A), inserted by the Enforcement of Security Interest and Recovery of Debts Laws (Amendment) Act, 2004 (Act 30 of 2004), provides that if the borrower makes a representation or raises an objection on receipt of the Section 13(2) notice, the secured creditor shall consider it, and if the creditor concludes that the representation is not acceptable or tenable, it shall communicate the reasons for non-acceptance to the borrower. As first inserted, the communication had to be made within one week of receipt of the representation; the amendment of 2012 lengthened that period to fifteen days, where it stands.

The sub-section is carefully limited, and its limits define its place in the design. The proviso states that the reasons communicated, and the creditor's likely action at that stage, confer no right on the borrower to apply to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17. The representation stage is therefore a dialogue, not a hearing, and its product is a reasoned reply, not an order. A borrower who treats the creditor's rejection letter as the moment to litigate will be turned away, because the tribunal remedy opens only when a measure is taken under Section 13(4). Yet the dialogue matters out of proportion to its formal status. A representation that identifies a genuine error, a payment not credited, a classification made against the Reserve Bank's norms, interest computed on a wrong base, forces the creditor to commit itself in writing, and that written reply becomes central evidence when the tribunal later examines whether the creditor acted in accordance with the Act. Creditors who reply with a bare template, or who fail to reply at all, hand the borrower a procedural ground that tribunals take seriously, because Section 13(3A) compliance is one of the few pre-possession duties the statute imposes and courts have treated its breach as vitiating the measures that follow. The honest summary is that Section 13(3A) gives the borrower a right to an answer, not a right to a result, but in a process built on the creditor's paperwork, a compelled answer is a real if modest safeguard.

## The grievance beneath the process: disputing the classification

Every SARFAESI dispute is formally about procedure, but a large share of them are substantively about one antecedent question: whether the account was rightly classified as non-performing at all. The borrower who concedes the default argues about notices; the borrower who contests the classification contests the creditor's right to be in the process, and his grievances have a recognisable anatomy. Payments made before the classification date are said to have been appropriated to charges and interest in an order that manufactured the default, rather than to the instalments whose non-payment constitutes it. A restructuring or a sanctioned renewal is said to have been agreed and then disregarded, so that an account the borrower believed to be under a revised schedule was measured against the old one. Interest is said to have been charged at a rate the contract does not bear, or compounded in a manner the contract forbids, so that the overdue amount on which classification rests is itself inflated. In working capital accounts, the drawing power is said to have been reduced without notice, converting a compliant borrower into a defaulter by the stroke of a limit revision. None of these grievances is exotic; they are the ordinary frictions of a lending relationship, and in the civil-court era they would have been the issues in the suit, tried before any sale. Under this Act they are tried, if at all, after possession, within a proceeding whose statutory question is whether the creditor's measures comply with the Act.

The statute's handling of the classification dispute is the honest weakness in its design, and the brief for this article directs that it be addressed head-on. The design's logic is that classification under the Reserve Bank's norms is arithmetic rather than judgment: an account is non-performing when interest or instalments remain overdue beyond the prescribed period, and arithmetic does not require a hearing. The logic holds where the underlying ledger is accurate, and fails precisely where the borrower needs it most, in the cases where the ledger is the dispute. The remedies the design offers are real but sequenced against the borrower. The representation under Section 13(3A) puts the objection before the classifier, whose institutional interest in the answer is obvious, though the duty to give reasons creates a record. The tribunal under Section 17 can examine whether the classification conformed to the Reserve Bank's norms and whether the account was in truth in default, and tribunals have set aside enforcement where the classification was shown to be contrary to those norms or the demand was founded on a sum the creditor could not substantiate. But the tribunal's examination comes after the measures, its interim relief is discretionary and often conditioned on deposits the tribunal itself fixes, and the borrower litigates it, in the common case, out of possession of the very asset whose income might have funded the fight. Practitioners on the creditor side answer that this sequencing is the statute's purpose: a classification challenge available before possession, with suspensive effect, would re-create the civil suit under a new name and return the system to the delay the Act was passed to end, and that the genuinely wronged borrower retains the tribunal, restoration and compensation. Practitioners on the borrower side answer that a safeguard which operates only after the harm is a remedy for the asset, not for the enterprise, since a business dispossessed in the interval rarely recovers its customers, its workforce or its credit even when possession is formally restored. Both answers are true about different things, and the tension between them is not a drafting defect that a further amendment can dissolve; it is the price schedule of the choice Parliament made in 2002, restated in every case where the classification is honestly contestable.

One refinement of the position deserves emphasis because borrowers chronically misunderstand it. The tribunal's power to examine the classification is not a power to forgive the debt or to refuse enforcement because enforcement seems harsh. Where the ledger is accurate, the norms were applied, the notices were served and the procedure was followed, the tribunal has no commission to save the borrower, and applications built on sympathy rather than compliance fail. The statute's fairness is procedural all the way down: it promises the borrower an accurate ledger, a real notice, a reasoned reply, a lawful sale and a forum that will verify each of those promises. It does not promise the borrower time, indulgence or a second chance at the bargain, and advice that suggests otherwise does the borrower a disservice the statute will not correct.

## The measures: Section 13(4)

If the borrower fails to discharge the liability within the notice period, Section 13(4) arms the secured creditor with four measures, and they are worth stating exactly because their breadth is the substance of the Act. The creditor may take possession of the secured assets of the borrower, including the right to transfer them by way of lease, assignment or sale for realising the secured asset. It may take over the management of the business of the borrower, including the right to transfer it by lease, assignment or sale, a power whose exercise in the case of a company carries statutory consequences for its directors and management. It may appoint any person to manage the secured assets whose possession has been taken. And it may, by notice in writing, require any person who has acquired any of the secured assets from the borrower and from whom any money is or may become payable to the borrower, to pay the secured creditor so much of that money as is sufficient to pay the secured debt. The fourth measure is easily overlooked and practically potent: the borrower's own receivables, in the hands of a buyer of the secured assets, can be diverted to the creditor by notice, cutting off the cash flow that might otherwise fund resistance or revival.

Possession is executed by the creditor's authorised officer, an officer of prescribed seniority, under the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002. For immovable property, the officer draws up an inventory, affixes a possession notice on the property and delivers or sends it to the borrower, and publishes the notice in newspapers, so that the taking is a public act rather than a private seizure. Valuation by an approved valuer precedes sale, a reserve price is fixed, and the sale itself is conducted under the discipline of the Rules, with a notice of sale to the borrower: thirty days' notice before the first sale of an immovable asset, and fifteen days before a later sale after an earlier attempt has failed. Sales are commonly by public auction, increasingly electronic, and the purchaser receives a sale certificate which, for immovable property, is the document of title that completes the transfer. The whole sequence is administrative in form, which is the point: no plaint, no issues, no decree. But its administrative character is also why every step's paperwork is contestable, since where there is no judge supervising in advance, the record is the only thing a reviewing tribunal can test, and a creditor that cannot produce a clean record of notice, valuation and publication will find its sale unravelled afterwards.

### What happens when a SARFAESI sale raises more than the debt?

The money realised is applied to the debt and the costs of enforcement, and the surplus belongs to the borrower. Section 13(7) directs that money received by the secured creditor be held and applied in accordance with the rights of the persons entitled, and that any residue be paid to the person entitled to it.

The surplus rule is a useful index of what kind of power Section 13 confers. The creditor is not confiscating the asset; it is realising a security for a defined sum, and its entitlement stops at that sum together with the costs the statute allows. In practice, surplus disputes arise where the borrower disputes the costs charged, the interest computed into the claim, or appropriation between multiple accounts, and those disputes, like everything else in this statute, are fought before the tribunal on the creditor's statement of account. The rule matters in the other direction too: where the sale falls short, the creditor is not confined to the security, which is the subject of the balance remedy dealt with next.


## The authorised officer and the discipline of the file

Between the statute and the borrower stands a single human functionary, the authorised officer, and the practical character of SARFAESI enforcement is largely the character of that office. The Enforcement Rules require the secured creditor to designate an officer of prescribed seniority, in a public sector bank not below the rank of chief manager, as the authorised officer for the enforcement, and it is that officer who issues the demand notice, considers the file before measures are taken, executes possession, conducts the sale and signs the sale certificate. The concentration is deliberate: a named officer, of a rank meant to carry judgment, answers for the process, and the tribunal's examination fastens on his record. Service of the demand notice is itself regulated, the Rules prescribing delivery and postal modes and permitting substituted service by affixation where the borrower cannot be found or evades receipt, because a borrower who could defeat the statute by refusing his mail would hold a veto the design refuses him. The officer's file, the sanction and loan documents, the statement of account, the classification record, the notice and proof of its service, the representation and the reasoned reply, the possession notice with its inventory and newspaper publication, the valuation report, the sale notice and the confirmation, is the process in documentary form. Creditors lose SARFAESI cases, in the overwhelming pattern of the decided challenges, not because the power was absent but because the file was incomplete: a notice served on the wrong person, a reply never sent, a publication missed, a valuation unexplained. The statute's proceduralism is therefore not a technicality layered over the power; it is the mechanism by which a power exercised without prior judicial supervision is kept answerable, and the authorised officer's discipline is the price the creditor pays for the absence of a judge at the front of the process.

## The sale: valuation, reserve price and the auction

Possession is a means; the sale is the event the whole process exists to produce, and its conduct decides whether the enforcement realises value or merely transfers loss. The Enforcement Rules build a sequence around the authorised officer. The asset is valued by an approved valuer, and a reserve price is fixed on the strength of that valuation, below which the property is not ordinarily to be sold at the first attempt. Notice of the sale is given to the borrower, thirty days before the first sale of an immovable asset and fifteen days before a subsequent sale where an earlier attempt has failed, and the sale is publicised so that the market, and not merely the creditor's circle, can bid: public auction, increasingly conducted electronically, invitation of tenders or quotations, and, within the limits the Rules permit, private treaty. The successful bidder deposits the price in stages, the sale is confirmed, and for immovable property a sale certificate issues, which is the instrument that carries the transfer. Each of these steps is a compliance point at which the process can be attacked, and sales have been set aside for a defective sale notice, for a sale conducted without the prescribed publicity, for a reserve price fixed on a stale or unexplained valuation, and for confirmation in the teeth of an admitted irregularity, because a process that denies the borrower a judge in advance must at least be able to prove that its own rulebook was followed to the letter.

The economics of the sale deserve the same candour as its law. A forced sale is a sale conducted by a seller who must sell, of an asset whose distress is public knowledge, within a timetable the market can read. Bidders price in the risk of litigation by the borrower, the possibility of occupants to be removed, the defects of a title assembled from the creditor's file, and their own knowledge that the next auction, if this one fails, may be cheaper. The predictable result is a discount against the price a willing seller with time could obtain, and the discount is paid by the borrower, because the debt is discharged only to the extent of the proceeds and the balance remains recoverable under Section 13(10). The creditor's duty in this setting is real but limited: the Act and the Rules require a fair process, valuation, publicity and notice, and the tribunal will police them; they do not make the creditor the borrower's selling agent obliged to maximise price at the cost of indefinite delay. Between those poles the case law has worked out a practical discipline, that material irregularity in the conduct of the sale, particularly irregularity that can be connected to the price obtained, is the ground on which sales fall, while technical lapses that caused no prejudice are increasingly unlikely to unravel a confirmed sale after a purchaser has paid and taken the certificate. The borrower who means to attack a sale therefore attacks it early, before confirmation if possible, with evidence about value: an independent valuation is worth more in that proceeding than any volume of complaint about the debt.

It is also worth stating who may not do what at the sale, because folklore fills the gaps. The borrower cannot defeat the auction by the simple expedient of a transfer to a relative after the demand notice, since Section 13(3) froze dealings with the asset from that point. The creditor's officers cannot purchase the asset for themselves through the process they conduct. A stranger who buys in good faith at a properly conducted auction acquires a position the law protects even against the borrower's later success on other points, which is why the sequencing of challenges matters so much: the tribunal's power to restore possession is at its fullest before the sale is confirmed and third-party rights have crystallised, and narrows as the process completes. The sale is thus the point of no return in both directions, for the borrower whose redemption has already expired and for the creditor whose defective process can no longer be cured once the certificate is in a purchaser's hands.

## Redemption, and the shortening of the borrower's last chance: Section 13(8)

A mortgagor's right to redeem, to get the property back by paying everything due, is one of the oldest equities in property law, and the question of how long it survives inside a summary enforcement statute has produced some of the sharpest litigation under this Act. As originally enacted, Section 13(8) protected the borrower in these terms: where the dues of the secured creditor together with all costs, charges and expenses are tendered at any time before the date fixed for sale or transfer, the secured assets shall not be sold or transferred, and no further step shall be taken towards sale. Read with the Transfer of Property Act's conception of redemption, the Supreme Court held in Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar that under this text the borrower's right to redeem endured late, in substance until the transfer was completed by a registered instrument, and that the creditor's procedural obligations under the Rules, including proper notice of the sale to the borrower, had to be strictly honoured before that right could be extinguished. The practical result was that a borrower with money or a funder could rescue the property at a very late stage, and auction purchasers bought subject to that risk.

The amendment of 2016 recast Section 13(8) and moved the guillotine much earlier. Under the substituted text, the tender must be made at any time before the date of publication of the notice for public auction, or of the invitation of quotations or tender, or of the private treaty, for transfer of the secured assets. Once the auction notice is published, the right to redeem is gone, even though the sale itself lies in the future. In Celir LLP v. Bafna Motors, the Supreme Court upheld the amended provision's logic: the borrower who wishes to redeem must act before the sale process is publicly launched, and cannot hold the property hostage until the hammer is about to fall. The change is a deliberate policy choice in favour of auction integrity, because a sale that can be undone by a last-minute tender attracts cautious bidders and depressed prices, and depressed prices harm borrowers as a class even as late redemption helps the individual borrower who can fund it. But its human edge should be stated plainly: the borrower who assembles the money a week after the auction notice appears, through family loans or a settlement that took too long to close, has no statutory right left, and is remitted to negotiation with a creditor who no longer needs to listen. Between the original text and the amended one lies the statute's whole philosophy in miniature: every year of its life, the balance has been adjusted towards the certainty of the process and away from the rescue of the individual case, and the adjustments have been made by Parliament with the courts' blessing.

### What does taking over the management of the borrower's business involve?

Section 13(4) lets the secured creditor take over the management of the borrower's business instead of seizing assets piecemeal. In the case of a company, the takeover displaces the existing management, and the creditor may run the business itself, install an appointee to manage the secured assets, or transfer the business as a going concern.

Management takeover is the least used of the four measures, and the reasons are instructive. A bank that takes over a business takes over its problems: wages, supplies, customers, statutory liabilities and the risk of being treated as responsible for the conduct of an enterprise it did not build. Creditors prefer saleable assets to operating businesses, so the measure functions mainly as pressure and as a route to selling the undertaking whole where the assets are worth more together than apart, for instance a factory whose land, plant and licences realise a fraction of their combined value when severed. Its presence in the section nevertheless tells the borrower something important about the statute's intent: the security is treated as extending to the economic value of what was charged, in whatever form realising that value requires.

## When the creditor still needs the state: Section 14

For all the Act's rhetoric of self-help, taking physical possession of occupied property is where private power meets its practical limit. A bank's officers can affix notices; they cannot lawfully force a door against resistance. Section 14 therefore enlists the state: where a secured asset is required to be sold or transferred, the secured creditor may, for the purpose of taking possession or control or for managing or selling the asset, request in writing the assistance of the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or the District Magistrate within whose jurisdiction the asset is situated, and the magistrate shall, on such request, take possession of the asset and documents relating to it and forward them to the secured creditor. The creditor supports the request with an affidavit from its authorised officer declaring, among other things, that the loan was granted, that default and classification followed, that the demand notice was served and the period expired, and that the Act and the Rules have been complied with. The magistrate acts on that affidavit.

Whether the magistrate inquires or merely executes has generated a generation of litigation, because the answer decides whether Section 14 is a second checkpoint or a conveyor belt. The settled position, stated by the Supreme Court in Harshad Govardhan Sondagar v. International Assets Reconstruction Company in 2014, is that the magistrate's function is ministerial and executive rather than adjudicatory: the magistrate does not decide the rival claims of creditor and borrower, does not sit in appeal over the classification or the amount, and must not convert the assistance application into a trial. Adjudication belongs to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17. Sondagar itself was concerned with occupants claiming to be tenants, and the Court's treatment of them shows the limits of the ministerial label: a person in possession claiming a tenancy created before the mortgage, of a kind the law protects, is not a trespasser to be swept out unheard, and the magistrate may satisfy himself about the genuineness of such a claim at the threshold, while a tenancy invented after the demand notice, or created in breach of the mortgage terms after the security was given, earns no such protection. Parliament added discipline of its own in 2016: the magistrate is to pass suitable orders within thirty days of the application, extendable, for reasons recorded in writing, to not more than sixty days in the aggregate. The Supreme Court has since read those periods as directory rather than mandatory, so an order passed late is not void for lateness alone, a reading that preserves the assistance while draining the deadline of its threat. The pattern repeats the Act's central one: a procedural safeguard is inserted, then interpreted so that it disciplines without derailing.

Possession under this Act must also be distinguished from the state's power to take property for public purposes, because borrowers regularly conflate them and the conflation produces hopeless litigation. Compulsory acquisition, governed by [the land acquisition framework restated in the LARR Act of 2013](/2013/11/01/larr-act-2013-explained/), is the sovereign taking another's land for a public purpose against compensation assessed by the state. SARFAESI possession is the opposite configuration: no public purpose is asserted, no compensation is assessed, and the taker is a private creditor enforcing a security the borrower voluntarily created and defaulted under. The borrower's protection in acquisition law is the award; the borrower's protection in SARFAESI is the tribunal. Arguments borrowed from acquisition jurisprudence, about the sanctity of property and the obligation of the state to justify a taking, miss the character of the process and fail accordingly.


## The guarantor and the third-party mortgagor

The person who loses property under this Act is not always the person who borrowed the money. The definition of borrower reaches anyone who has been granted financial assistance and anyone who has given a guarantee or created a mortgage or pledge as security for that assistance, and Indian lending practice makes the second category large: the promoter's spouse who mortgaged the family home for the company's limits, the parent company that charged its plant for a subsidiary's working capital, the director whose personal guarantee is supported by a charge over his own property. Each such person is inside the enforcement chapter in respect of the security he created, entitled to the demand notice, the representation and the tribunal remedy in his own right, and exposed to possession and sale of his own asset for another's default. The creditor is not obliged to proceed against the principal borrower first, or to exhaust the borrower's assets before touching the guarantor's security, because the surety's liability under Section 128 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 is co-extensive with that of the principal debtor, and the Supreme Court's jurisprudence has consistently refused to convert the guarantor into a secondary target entitled to watch the borrower be pursued first.

The guarantor's distinctive defences come from the suretyship chapters of the Contract Act rather than from this Act, and their fate inside SARFAESI enforcement is instructive about the hierarchy of the two statutes. A variance in the terms of the contract between creditor and borrower made without the surety's consent, a release of the principal debtor, or the creditor's impairment of the security the surety would on payment be entitled to, can discharge the surety under Sections 133 to 139 of the Contract Act. In practice, institutional guarantee deeds are drafted against those very provisions, recording the guarantor's advance consent to variations, renewals, compositions and the creditor's dealings with the security, and courts have generally held guarantors to the deeds they signed, treating the statutory discharges as displaced by consent given in advance. The result is that the family mortgagor's real protection is rarely the discharge doctrine; it is the same procedural armoury as the borrower's, the accuracy of the classification and the ledger, the validity of the notice addressed to him, the compliance of the measures against his asset, policed by the tribunal. The harder question the design poses for this class is one of information: the guarantor typically learns the true state of the borrower's account at the moment the demand notice arrives, having had no right under the Act to be told of the deterioration as it happened, and the sixty days that are merely short for the borrower are shorter still for the guarantor who must reconstruct another person's defaults before he can even frame his representation. Reform discussion has periodically proposed a notice duty towards guarantors at the classification stage; the Act as it stands imposes none, and the prudent guarantor's protection remains contractual vigilance extracted at the signing, the one moment his consent is worth something.

## The borrower's parallel track: payment, settlement and restructuring

The tribunal route is the borrower's legal remedy, but it is not the route by which most SARFAESI matters end, and an article addressed to borrowers that omitted the commercial track would fail its readers. Most demand notices end in one of three commercial outcomes. The borrower regularises the account by clearing the overdue amount with interest and charges, where the facility is of a kind that continues, as a working capital limit ordinarily does; the classification is then revisited under the Reserve Bank's norms and the enforcement stands withdrawn, because the statute arms the creditor against default and a cured default removes the occasion for it. The borrower negotiates a one-time settlement, under which the creditor accepts a sum less than the full dues in full and final satisfaction, a decision commercial lenders take against the expected value and delay of enforcement and sale; settlements are creatures of the creditor's policy and the borrower's credibility, not of the statute, which neither compels them nor forbids them. Or the borrower seeks restructuring of the debt, a rescheduling of principal and interest on revised terms, which in the case of larger exposures travels through the Reserve Bank's prudential framework for stressed assets rather than through anything in this Act. Each of these outcomes is most available early, inside the sixty days, when the creditor's costs are low and its file records a cooperative borrower; each becomes harder after possession, when the creditor has spent money on the process and the borrower's leverage, which is the capacity to pay something real, is usually at its weakest.

Two disciplines govern the parallel track and repay statement. The first is that everything must be in writing. A settlement that is not recorded in the creditor's letter, with the amount, the schedule and the consequence of default specified, is a conversation, and borrowers have lost properties while believing a branch assurance had stopped a sale that the authorised officer, a different desk in the same institution, was proceeding to conduct. The second is that payment and litigation are not alternatives between which the borrower must choose at the outset. The tribunal application has a forty-five day fuse that settlement talks do not extend, and the borrower who spends the fuse negotiating, without filing, discovers that the forum has closed while the talking continued. Competent practice therefore runs the tracks together: the representation is filed inside the notice period whatever talks are on foot, the tribunal application is filed within its period whatever promises have been made, and the commercial negotiation proceeds in the shelter of a preserved legal position rather than in place of one. The statute rewards the borrower who treats its clocks as absolute and negotiates inside them.

## The borrower's forum: Sections 17 and 18

The single adjudicatory door the Act leaves open is Section 17. Any person, expressly including the borrower, aggrieved by any measure taken by the secured creditor under Section 13(4) may make an application to the Debts Recovery Tribunal having jurisdiction, within forty-five days from the date on which the measure was taken. The application carries a prescribed fee, scaled under the Rules to the amount of the debt, and, after the surgery performed by the Supreme Court and Parliament in 2004, no deposit of the debt is required to file it. What the tribunal examines is defined by Section 17(2) and (3): whether the measures taken are in accordance with the provisions of the Act and the Rules made under it. If they are not, the tribunal may declare the recourse invalid and, crucially, order restoration of possession or management of the secured assets to the borrower, together with such other relief as the case requires, including, under Section 19, compensation and costs where the creditor is found to have taken possession of assets it was not entitled to take. If they are in accordance with the Act, the creditor is entitled to proceed to sell.

Two features of this remedy decide its real value. The first is that it is an original proceeding, in the nature of a suit, not an appeal on a closed record: Mardia Chemicals described the Section 17 proceeding as in substance a civil suit instituted for the first time, in which the parties may lead evidence and the tribunal decides the grievance on its merits within its statutory limits. The tribunal can examine the classification, the computation of dues, the validity and service of notices, compliance with Section 13(3A), the conduct of valuation and sale, and the exclusions under Section 31, and it can grant interim relief, including stays of further action, on conditions it considers fit. The second feature is the boundary of its commission. The tribunal tests the creditor's measures against the Act; it is not a forum for rehearing the borrower's entire commercial relationship with the bank, for trying complex disputes of title between strangers, or for decreeing the underlying debt as a civil court would, although in practice the line between testing the measures and contesting the amount claimed is where most Section 17 litigation is fought. A borrower who goes to the tribunal with a case about the process usually has a case. A borrower who goes with only the case that the debt is unfair usually does not.

From the tribunal's order, Section 18 provides an appeal to the Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunal, to be filed within thirty days from the date of receipt of the order. The appeal is where the deposit discipline returns, in the moderated form the courts and Parliament settled on after 2004. No appeal by a borrower shall be entertained unless the borrower has deposited with the Appellate Tribunal fifty per cent of the amount of debt due from him, as claimed by the secured creditor or as determined by the Debts Recovery Tribunal, whichever is less. The Appellate Tribunal may, for reasons to be recorded in writing, reduce the deposit, but not to less than twenty-five per cent of that amount. The deposit is a condition of entertainment, not a payment of the debt, and the Supreme Court has held that it cannot be waived altogether: in Narayan Chandra Ghosh v. UCO Bank, the Court ruled that the Appellate Tribunal cannot entertain the appeal without insisting on the deposit, its only latitude being the reduction to the statutory floor of twenty-five per cent for recorded reasons. A generation of deposit jurisprudence has elaborated the same boundary, that the condition is mandatory in existence and discretionary only in measure. The borrower who plans strategy under this Act must therefore count two campaigns, not one: the tribunal application, which costs a fee and time, and the appeal, which costs money the borrower ex hypothesi is short of. That asymmetry, deliberately retained by Parliament after the Supreme Court struck down the far harsher original version, is the point on which the fairness argument about this statute permanently turns.

Around these two remedies the Act builds walls against every other forum. Section 34 bars civil courts from entertaining any suit or proceeding in respect of any matter which a Debts Recovery Tribunal or Appellate Tribunal is empowered to determine, and bars injunctions by any court or authority in respect of action under the Act. Mardia Chemicals preserved only a sliver of civil jurisdiction, within the narrow scope and on the limited grounds on which suits remain permissible in matters relating to an English mortgage enforceable without the court's intervention, and later courts have kept that sliver narrow, treating fraud and absurdity claims with suspicion when they are the barred grievance in costume. The writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226 survives, as it constitutionally must, but High Courts have consistently declined to exercise it where the Section 17 remedy is available and adequate, entertaining writs mainly for jurisdictional error, violation of the Act's mandatory procedure or cases where the tribunal route is illusory on the facts. Section 35 gives the Act overriding effect over other laws to the extent of inconsistency, and Section 36 imports the Limitation Act, 1963: a secured creditor may not take measures under Section 13 after the period of limitation for the underlying claim has expired, so the summary power cannot revive a time-barred debt. The general position described in this article is information about how the statute works, not advice on any individual case; limitation under this Act is counted in days at several points, and the consequences of missing a period are exactly as unforgiving as the periods are short.

### Does a buyer at a SARFAESI auction get a clear title?

The auction purchaser takes the property under a sale certificate issued by the authorised officer, and a confirmed sale transfers the borrower's interest in the secured asset to the purchaser. Challenges to defects in the sale go to the tribunal, and a confirmed sale is not lightly reopened once third-party rights have crystallised.

Purchasers nevertheless buy with their eyes open in one respect that matters: they take the borrower's title as it stands, subject to encumbrances and claims that the law does not extinguish, and the competition among bidders prices that risk in. This is one of the quieter ways in which procedural disorder harms borrowers themselves, because a sale market that fears later unravelling bids less, and the shortfall between price and debt remains the borrower's liability. Where sale proceeds fall short of the debt and costs, Section 13(10) lets the creditor apply to the Debts Recovery Tribunal, or to a competent court where no tribunal application lies, for recovery of the balance from the borrower. Enforcement under this Act therefore does not cap the creditor's claim at the value of the security: the borrower can lose the property and still owe the difference.


## Inside the tribunal: how Section 17 litigation actually runs

The Debts Recovery Tribunals that hear Section 17 applications are the institutions of the 1993 Act, presided over by judicial officers and organised by territorial jurisdiction, with the Appellate Tribunals above them. A SARFAESI application in the tribunal, conventionally called a securitisation application, is tried on affidavit and documents in the main, with oral evidence where the tribunal permits it, and the contest has a standard choreography that anyone entering the forum should understand. The borrower attacks the record: the classification and the ledger beneath it, the demand notice and its service, the consideration of the representation, the possession procedure and its notices, the valuation, the sale notice and the conduct of the sale. The creditor defends the record, producing the file. Interim relief is the first real battle, because a stay of the sale preserves everything and its refusal usually ends the practical dispute; tribunals grant interim protection on a view of the procedural merits and frequently on terms, requiring the borrower to deposit a portion of the dues as the price of time, a judicial practice that mirrors the statute's own philosophy that time must be paid for. The Supreme Court's description of the proceeding as in the nature of a suit matters here, because it means the tribunal is not confined to the creditor's paper: the borrower may lead evidence, cross-examine on affidavits where permitted, and prove the payment the ledger omits or the tenancy the creditor denies.

The tribunal's remedial powers, where non-compliance is found, are stronger than borrowers often realise and weaker than they hope. Section 17(3) empowers the tribunal, on finding that the measures were not in accordance with the Act, to declare the recourse invalid and restore possession or management of the secured assets to the borrower, and Section 19 adds compensation and costs where the creditor took possession of assets it was not entitled to take. Restoration is a genuinely restorative remedy, rare in Indian commercial litigation, and its existence disciplines creditors in a way damages never could, because a bank that loses possession after a completed process loses the recovery itself and not merely a sum. The weakness lies in time and in the asymmetry of endurance. Tribunal litigation takes time the borrower in distress does not have, the creditor's recovery machinery is staffed for exactly this litigation, and the interim deposit conditions by which borrowers purchase the status quo consume the working capital whose absence caused the default. The honest description of Section 17 is that it is a real remedy with real teeth, accessible in form, and demanding in substance, which is a precise miniature of the Act that created it.

The writ courts stand behind the tribunal in a supervisory posture the borrower should understand before choosing a forum. A petition under Article 226 against SARFAESI action invites the standard response that an efficacious statutory remedy exists and must be exhausted, and High Courts intervene in the main where the creditor has acted wholly without jurisdiction, where the action is against a person or asset the Act cannot reach, where mandatory procedure has been ignored in a manner that goes to the root, or where the tribunal remedy is for some concrete reason unavailable. Borrowers also discover the doctrine that a party who has invoked the tribunal cannot simultaneously prosecute a writ on the same grievance. Forum discipline under this Act is thus strict by design and by judicial habit: one door, entered in time, with the record complete.

## What the Act does not reach: Section 31

Section 31 lists the territory the Act shall not enter, and its items repay attention because they mark the points where other policies outranked recovery. The Act does not apply to a lien on goods, money or security given under the Indian Contract Act, 1872 or the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 or any other law; to a pledge of movable property within the meaning of Section 172 of the Contract Act, which is governed by its own rules of pawnee sale; to the creation of security over an aircraft, or over a vessel, each of which lives inside specialised and international regimes; to the rights of an unpaid seller under the Sale of Goods Act; or to properties that are not liable to attachment or sale under the first proviso to Section 60(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which protects the modest catalogue of a judgment debtor's necessities, although property specifically charged with the debt is carved out of that protection, so a mortgaged home is not saved by it. Three exclusions carry the heaviest practical weight. The Act does not apply to any security interest for securing repayment of a financial asset not exceeding one lakh rupees, a de minimis line that keeps the summary machine away from the smallest loans. It does not apply where the amount due is less than twenty per cent of the principal amount and interest, so a borrower who has repaid the overwhelming bulk of the debt cannot be put through summary enforcement for the residue. And it does not apply to any security interest created in agricultural land.

The agricultural land exclusion is the most litigated sentence in the section, and the most misunderstood. It is commonly repeated as if any land on which crops stand, or which revenue records describe as agricultural, lies outside the Act. The exclusion in truth protects the security interest created in agricultural land, and the contest in case after case has been over characterisation: land recorded as agricultural but diverted to industry, plotted for housing, or built upon as a factory or a hotel does not necessarily carry the character the exemption assumes, and courts examining the question have looked to the real nature and use of the land rather than to the label in the record alone. The converse also holds: a creditor cannot manufacture jurisdiction by describing genuinely agricultural land as something else. The policy beneath the exclusion is livelihood protection of a blunt kind. Agricultural land in India is frequently the family's only asset and only income, its sale market is thin and local, and summary dispossession of a cultivator was a price Parliament was not willing to pay for credit discipline, even though the exclusion also raises the cost and reduces the supply of agricultural credit, a trade-off the committees noticed and Parliament accepted. It is worth adding what the exclusion does not do: it does not cancel the debt, and it does not bar the creditor from recovering through the tribunal or the civil process. It bars only this statute's self-help route.

The exclusions also answer a question borrowers ask in the wrong form. There is no general exemption for a first home, a small business or a sympathetic case anywhere in Section 31, and tribunals have no power to invent one. The protections the Act gives a household borrower are procedural, the notice, the representation, the tribunal, the redemption window, and the two numerical exclusions for very small loans and nearly repaid ones. That is a deliberate legislative choice about where compassion is priced into the design: at the margins of amount, not at the level of the individual story.

## The personal borrower inside a commercial statute

The Act makes no distinction between the industrial group that defaults on a syndicated facility and the family that defaults on a housing loan, and that indifference is a choice worth examining rather than assuming. When the Bill was debated, the argument for a threshold of seriousness, for keeping summary enforcement for substantial commercial exposures and leaving household defaults to slower processes, was pressed and rejected, on the reasoning that a security interest is a security interest and that carving households out would invite the routing of commercial borrowing through personal names. The result is that the statute's most numerous subjects are not its most powerful ones. The small borrower differs from the corporate borrower in ways the procedure does not register: the asset at risk is frequently the home or the single shop or workshop that generates the family's income, so that possession destroys livelihood and shelter in the same act; the borrower's capacity to use the procedural safeguards, a reasoned representation in the creditor's language, a tribunal application drafted to the record, counsel at a distant tribunal city, is a function of resources the default itself has exhausted; and the reputational and credit-record consequences of a SARFAESI proceeding follow the individual for years in a way no corporate restructuring quite replicates. Against this, the household borrower's defenders within the design point to the numerical exclusions, the one lakh floor and the twenty per cent rule, which do remove the smallest and the nearly discharged loans from the machine, and to the plain fact that the pre-2002 alternative for a defaulting household was not gentleness but a civil suit that ended in the same sale with a decade of costs added to the debt. The question the statute leaves open, and which recurs in every reform debate, is whether the procedural safeguards that are adequate between commercial parties are adequate in substance, and not merely in form, where the parties are a bank and a family. It is a question about the price of credit as much as about compassion, since household lending against property expanded enormously in the security this Act made credible, and the households that borrowed on the strength of that expansion are the same households the process reaches when incomes fail.

## Joint lending: when one creditor cannot act alone

Large exposures are rarely held by one lender. A consortium of banks, or several lenders under a multiple banking arrangement, may share the same security, and the Act adjusts its individualist logic to that reality in Section 13(9). Where financial assistance has been granted by more than one secured creditor, or jointly by a consortium, the measures under Section 13(4) are not to be exercised by a single creditor on its own motion: the exercise of the enforcement rights must be agreed upon by secured creditors representing the prescribed majority in value of the amount outstanding on the record date. The provision prevents the first-moving lender from dismantling a common security for its own account and forces the creditor group to move, if it moves at all, as a group. In practice the requirement imports into SARFAESI the politics of the consortium: the lead bank's appetite for enforcement, the smaller lenders' preference for a settlement they lack the machinery to run themselves, and the borrower's opportunity to negotiate with a committee rather than a counterparty. Inter-creditor arrangements, and the pari passu sharing of the security and its proceeds that consortium documents ordinarily provide, govern what each lender ultimately takes from a common sale, and the disputes they generate are disputes among creditors in which the borrower is an interested spectator. For the borrower, the practical significance is narrower and worth knowing: enforcement by a junior or minority lender acting alone, without the concurrence the sub-section requires, is open to challenge, and a demand notice from one member of a consortium is the beginning of an inquiry into the group's position, not a formality to be overlooked.

## The second engine: asset reconstruction

Chapter II builds the market into which bad loans can be sold. An asset reconstruction company must register with the Reserve Bank under Section 3 before it commences the business of asset reconstruction, must maintain the owned fund the statute and the Reserve Bank's directions prescribe, and is subject to the Reserve Bank's regulatory directions, including on the capital it must hold and the conduct of its business. Under Section 5, such a company may acquire the financial assets of any bank or financial institution by issuing debentures or bonds or by entering into an agreement for acquisition on terms the parties settle, and the acquisition transfers the lender's rights to the company: the reconstruction company steps into the bank's shoes and may pursue every remedy the bank had, including enforcement of the security interest under Chapter III, as a secured creditor in its own right. Under Section 7, where an asset is acquired, the company may issue security receipts to qualified institutional buyers, the defined class of financially sophisticated investors, and hold and administer the acquired assets for their benefit, typically through trust structures in which the receipts represent beneficial interests in what is recovered. Section 9 equips the company with measures for asset reconstruction that echo the enforcement chapter: taking over or changing the management of the borrower's business, selling or leasing it in whole or part, rescheduling the debt, enforcing the security interest, settling dues payable by the borrower, and taking possession of the secured assets. Section 10 permits other securitisation and reconstruction functions with the Reserve Bank's permission.

The grievance this machinery attracts is famous and should be stated in its strongest form, because it is the discourse through which many borrowers meet the Act for the first time. A bank sells a loan of one hundred rupees to a reconstruction company for a fraction of that sum, having already provided against the loss; the company then enforces against the borrower for the full one hundred, plus interest and costs, using the same summary powers. The borrower experiences this as a windfall extracted from his distress by an entity that risked little. The market answer is equally real and should be stated at full strength too. The price at which the loan sold was the market's valuation of exactly the enforcement risk the company then bore: the years of tribunal litigation, the possibility of restoration orders, the chance the asset realised nothing. The bank accepted a certain smaller sum in place of an uncertain larger one, and freed its capital and attention; the company bought the uncertainty, not a guaranteed recovery, and where it enforces successfully for more than it paid, the difference is the return on risk and effort that the original lender declined. What can fairly be criticised is not the arithmetic but the asymmetry of information and stamina: the company is a professional repeat player in the tribunal system, and the borrower meets the process once, in distress, against counsel who meet it weekly. The statute's procedural safeguards are the only equaliser it offers, which is why their enforcement by tribunals matters more than their elegance on paper.

## Securitisation: the third word of the title

The least discussed of the Act's three engines is the one its title puts first. Securitisation, in the scheme of Chapter II, is the acquisition by a securitisation company of financial assets from the originator bank or financial institution, funded by the issue of security receipts or other securities to qualified institutional buyers, so that the loans leave the originator's balance sheet and the investors take interests in what the pool of loans yields. The reconstruction company and the securitisation company are governed by the same chapter and the same registration discipline, and in practice the reconstruction function has dominated, because the Indian market's appetite was for the purchase of distressed assets at a discount rather than for the refinancing of performing ones. The distinction still matters for understanding the Act's architecture. SARFAESI was not drafted as a mere recovery statute with an asset sale annexed; it was drafted as a market-making statute, intended to create the legal plumbing, transferable financial assets, enforceable security, a registry of interests, receipt-holding investors, through which a secondary market in bank debt could exist. The enforcement chapter is severe partly because the market chapter requires it: a financial asset is worth buying only if its security can be realised, and the price every reconstruction company pays for a bad loan is a discounted function of the very powers Chapter III confers. Borrowers who ask why an institution that bought their debt cheaply may enforce it in full are asking, without knowing it, about this design: the discount was the market's price for the enforcement risk, and the enforcement power is what the discount bought.

## The registry: CERSAI, registration and priority

The third engine is quiet and structural. Chapter IV establishes a Central Registry, the Central Registry of Securitisation Asset Reconstruction and Security Interest of India, known by its acronym CERSAI, in which particulars of securitisation and reconstruction transactions and of security interests created over property are registered, with a Central Registrar and a public record that lenders can search before lending. The registry's purpose is the one the Andhyarujina Committee identified: to end the era in which the same property could be mortgaged successively to lenders who had no practical way of discovering each other, and in which an intending lender could not verify what it was being offered. Registration was for a decade largely a matter of record. The amendment of 2016 gave it teeth, inserting Chapter IVA. Section 26D provides that, notwithstanding anything in any other law, no secured creditor shall be entitled to exercise the rights of enforcement under Chapter III unless the security interest created in its favour has been registered with the Central Registry. Registration is thus a condition precedent to enforcement: the most powerful recovery statute in Indian law refuses its powers to a creditor who has not done the paperwork of publicity. Section 26E then states the payoff for registering: after registration of the security interest, the debts due to the secured creditor shall be paid in priority over all other debts and all revenues, taxes, cesses and other rates payable to the Central Government, a state government or a local authority.

Priority against the state is an old quarrel in Indian law, because several taxing statutes claim a first charge for government dues, and the common law doctrine of crown debt priority long hovered behind them. Section 26E resolves the quarrel in the creditor's favour in terms that admit little argument, and the Supreme Court has given effect to that resolution, holding in litigation over excise claims that the secured creditor's statutory priority under the debt recovery and securitisation framework prevails over the revenue's asserted first charge. Two qualifications keep the position honest. The priority Section 26E confers is expressly made subject, by its own Explanation, to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code where insolvency proceedings are pending, so the registry priority governs the world outside insolvency and yields inside it. And litigation persists at the edges, in particular over dues that crystallised before the 2016 provisions commenced and over state enactments with their own charge language, so the prudent statement is that Parliament has declared the priority and the courts are enforcing the declaration, with transitional skirmishes still being fought. The deeper point is institutional: priority is no longer a doctrine argued from precedent but a status bought by registration, which converts the registry from an archive into the arena where competing claims are ranked.

One widening of the creditor class remains to be recorded. The Act began as a statute for banks and notified financial institutions, but Section 2(1)(m) lets the Central Government notify other institutions as financial institutions, and by that route specified non-banking financial companies were brought within the enforcement power. The extension was calibrated rather than general: notification S.O. 856(E) of February 2020 confined eligibility to non-banking financial companies with an asset size of one hundred crore rupees and above, enforcing secured debts of fifty lakh rupees and above, and notification S.O. 652(E) of February 2021 reduced the eligible debt threshold to twenty lakh rupees and above. Housing finance companies occupy their own notified position in the same family. The design choice is consistent with the Act's philosophy: summary power is entrusted to institutions of scale, inside the Reserve Bank's regulatory perimeter, and the threshold is lowered by executive calibration as the system absorbs the change, rather than by opening the power to every lender at once.


## Regulation, offences and the protective provisions

The machinery sections close the statute. The Reserve Bank stands behind the reconstruction and securitisation companies as their regulator under the Act, holding the power to determine policy and issue directions to them, including on the assets they may acquire, the capital they must maintain and the returns they must file, which is why the reconstruction company's freedom of contract with a selling bank is exercised inside a supervised perimeter. Contravention of the Act or of the Rules is itself an offence under Section 29, punishable with imprisonment extending to one year, or with fine, or with both, a provision aimed at conduct that obstructs the statutory scheme rather than at default itself, which is never an offence under this Act: the borrower who simply cannot pay faces the loss of the security, not prosecution. The registry obligations acquired enforcement machinery of their own in 2016, when Sections 30A to 30D armed an adjudicating mechanism with the power to impose penalties for defaults in the filing and registration obligations, converting the registry from a passive archive into a policed record. Section 32 protects action taken in good faith under the Act from suit, prosecution or other legal proceeding, the standard shield without which officers would not exercise summary powers at all. The miscellaneous chapter also carries the rule-making powers under which the Enforcement Rules of 2002 and the tribunal procedure rules operate. None of these provisions decides cases in the headlines. Together they explain why the statute works as a system: the power is fenced by a regulator on one side, an offence for obstruction on another, good-faith protection for the officer who follows the book, and penalties for the creditor who neglects the registry on which its own priority depends.

## Mardia Chemicals: the judgment that set the template

The constitutional challenge came early, from borrowers who had received notices under the new Act, and it was decided by the Supreme Court on 8 April 2004 in Mardia Chemicals Ltd. v. Union of India, reported at (2004) 4 SCC 311. The attack on the Act was comprehensive: that it conferred arbitrary and unguided power on lenders, that it denied the borrower any hearing before dispossession, that its classification of borrowers was unreasonable, and, most sharply, that the borrower's supposed remedy was illusory because Section 17(2), as enacted, required a borrower appealing to the Debts Recovery Tribunal to deposit seventy-five per cent of the amount claimed in the demand notice before the application would be entertained. A borrower in default is by hypothesis a person who cannot pay; to require such a person to deposit three quarters of the disputed claim as the price of being heard was, the petitioners argued, to grant a remedy with one hand and price it out of reach with the other.

The Court upheld the statute and cut out the condition. It accepted the legislature's premise that the mounting volume of non-performing assets was a genuine economic problem and that Parliament could constitutionally create an expeditious recovery mechanism for the financial system, including one that dispensed with prior court intervention. The classification the Act draws, between secured creditors armed with summary powers and the rest of the legal world, was held reasonable and connected to that object. But on the deposit condition the Court was blunt: the requirement of deposit of seventy-five per cent of the amount claimed before entertaining an application under Section 17 is, in the judgment's words, an oppressive, onerous and arbitrary condition against all the canons of reasonableness, and Section 17(2) was declared ultra vires Article 14 of the Constitution. And the Court read into the process the safeguard Parliament then enacted: that objections raised by the borrower to the demand notice must be considered with due application of mind and the reasons for rejecting them communicated, so that the borrower receives a reasonably fair deal and an opportunity to place his case before the creditor first and the tribunal after the measures are taken. The borrowers, the Court observed, would get a reasonably fair deal under the scheme as so read, even if some provisions bore harshly on some of them.

Mardia's importance exceeds its holdings, because it fixed the template against which every later creditor-empowering statute in India has been measured, the pattern this article calls uphold and safeguard. The mechanism is upheld, because the Court will not second-guess the legislature's economic diagnosis. The harshest procedural incidence is removed or moderated, because access to the reviewing forum is treated as the irreducible minimum. A duty of reason-giving is inserted at the point of first contact, because a decision that must be explained is a decision marginally constrained. And the prior-permission model is never restored: at no point did the Court suggest that the creditor must adjudicate before acting. The same choreography, defence of the design and judicial insertion of procedural fairness without disturbing the power itself, recurs across the debt recovery field and mirrors, in a commercial register, the pattern the anti-terror statutes exhibit in a criminal one, where courts sustained extraordinary powers while policing the fairness of their exercise. For the advocate, Mardia's practical legacy is that SARFAESI litigation is procedural litigation: the battleground is notice, classification, consideration and compliance, because the substantive constitutionality of the scheme was settled in 2004 and has not been reopened.

The decisions that followed worked inside the template. Transcore v. Union of India, (2008) 1 SCC 125, resolved the relationship between this Act and the tribunal recovery route of 1993: the Court rejected the doctrine of election in this setting, holding that a bank which had filed a recovery application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal was not required to withdraw it as a condition of proceeding under SARFAESI, the two remedies being cumulative rather than alternative, subject to the discipline of the 1993 Act's own provisions on withdrawal. The deposit question returned repeatedly at the appellate stage, and the line drawn by the courts has been consistent: the fifty per cent deposit under Section 18 is a condition of the appeal's entertainment which the Appellate Tribunal may moderate to twenty-five per cent for recorded reasons but may not waive, because a right of appeal is the creature of the statute that grants it and carries the statute's conditions with it. The redemption litigation, from Mathew Varghese under the original Section 13(8) to Celir under the amended text, shows the courts first protecting the borrower's equity while the statute allowed it and then enforcing Parliament's deliberate curtailment of that equity once the text changed. Read together, the case law is not a tug of war but a division of labour: Parliament sets the balance between recovery and protection, and the courts enforce whichever balance the text declares, intervening only where a condition destroys the remedy itself.

## The amendment arc in brief

Four waves of amendment have reshaped the Act, and their direction is uniform enough to state as a rule: each wave has made the process more certain for the creditor while repairing the specific unfairness the courts had last identified. The amendment of 2004, enacted in direct response to Mardia, removed the struck-down deposit regime from Section 17, substituting an application on a prescribed fee, and inserted the Section 13(3A) duty to consider and answer the borrower's representation, initially with a reply period of one week. The Enforcement of Security Interest and Recovery of Debts Laws (Amendment) Act, 2012, brought into force in January 2013, extended the Act's reach to multi-state co-operative banks and lengthened the creditor's reply period under Section 13(3A) from one week to fifteen days. The Enforcement of Security Interest and Recovery of Debts Laws and Miscellaneous Provisions (Amendment) Act, 2016 (Act 44 of 2016) was the largest intervention: it recast Section 13(8) to end redemption at the publication of the auction notice, inserted Chapter IVA with the registration condition in Section 26D and the priority rule in Section 26E, imposed the thirty and sixty day discipline on magistrates acting under Section 14, and added machinery for the tribunal's examination of tenancy claims within Section 17. The extension to non-banking financial companies in 2020 and 2021 was achieved by notification rather than amendment, which is itself consistent with the Act's technique of calibrating the creditor class by delegated decision. A reader tracing the arc will notice what never changes: the sixty-day notice, the absence of prior adjudication, and the tribunal as the single reviewing forum have survived every amendment untouched, because they are not features of the design; they are the design.

## SARFAESI meets the collective regime: the insolvency interface

For its first fourteen years the Act operated in a world of individual enforcement, in which each secured creditor raced its own security. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 superimposed a collective logic, and the two statutes collide at exactly the point where their philosophies differ. [Section 14 of the Code imposes a moratorium](/2012/04/01/insolvency-bankruptcy-code-2016-guide/) once a corporate insolvency resolution process is admitted, and the moratorium's terms expressly prohibit action to foreclose, recover or enforce any security interest created by the corporate debtor, including action under this Act. The Supreme Court has confirmed that a SARFAESI process underway against a corporate debtor cannot continue once the moratorium descends: enforcement begun in the individual mode must halt, and the creditor's claim is remitted to the collective process, where the committee of creditors decides the debtor's fate and the individual security is, for the duration, subordinated to the collective negotiation. The Code's overriding provision, Section 238, resolves textual conflicts in the Code's favour, and Section 26E of this Act acknowledges the subordination in its own Explanation. The boundary is the admission of the application: a SARFAESI sale completed before admission stands on a different footing from one interrupted midway, and the timing of completion, the transfer of the asset, and the state of the process when the insolvency petition is admitted have generated the predictable volume of litigation at the seam.

The reconciliation the law has reached is defensible in both directions. Inside insolvency, collectivity is the point: a single creditor dismantling the debtor's plant mid-negotiation destroys the going-concern value the process exists to preserve, and the secured creditor is compensated by its seniority in the distribution waterfall and by the vote its claim carries. Outside insolvency, the individual logic reasserts itself fully, and it does so even within the Code's own territory, because in liquidation a secured creditor may elect under Section 52 of the Code to stand outside the liquidation estate and realise its security itself, in substance returning to SARFAESI-style self-help, or may relinquish the security and rank in the waterfall. Guarantors occupy a separate seam: the moratorium protects the corporate debtor, not those who guaranteed its debts, and a creditor may pursue the guarantor's assets under this Act while the company's own resolution proceeds. The comparison of what the two regimes actually recover, and for whom, belongs to [the record of the insolvency regime's implementation](/2012/04/15/ibc-implementation-record/), and this article does not duplicate that evidence. What belongs here is the structural verdict: SARFAESI did not fail and get replaced; it was demoted from the system's only answer to its default answer, operative everywhere that a collective process has not been admitted, and displaced, deliberately and temporarily, where one has.

## Choosing the weapon: SARFAESI among the creditor's routes

A secured creditor holding a defaulted loan rarely faces a choice between SARFAESI and nothing; it faces a menu, and the choice among routes is a discipline in itself that borrowers benefit from understanding, because the route chosen predicts the borrower's experience. The oldest route is the original application to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under the Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions Act, 1993, by which the bank proves its debt and obtains a recovery certificate executable against the borrower's assets generally, secured and unsecured alike. That route adjudicates first and enforces afterwards, and it alone among the summary options produces a determination of the debt itself. SARFAESI inverts it: enforce against the security first, adjudicate the compliance afterwards, and take the balance, if any, to the tribunal under Section 13(10) when the sale is done. The civil suit survives at the margins for claims the other regimes cannot reach. The insolvency route, since 2016, offers the financial creditor a collective proceeding against a corporate debtor whose going-concern value exceeds its break-up value, at the price of surrendering individual control to the committee of creditors.

Transcore settled that the routes are cumulative rather than elective: the bank need not abandon a pending tribunal application to invoke SARFAESI, nor abandon SARFAESI to prosecute the application, subject to the statutory discipline governing withdrawal of tribunal applications, because the two proceedings aim at the same debt by different machinery and the creditor must ultimately account for what it recovers under each. The practical calculus that drives the choice is mundane and decisive. SARFAESI is fastest where the security is clean, the title is marketable and the borrower lacks a procedural defence; it is slowest where the asset is occupied, tenanted, fragmentedly owned or likely to attract a tribunal stay. The tribunal route is preferred where the debt itself is disputed on documents the bank would rather have adjudicated once than defended piecemeal. Insolvency is preferred where the borrower's enterprise is worth more alive and the creditor group can be organised. Running through the calculus is an institutional fact borrowers seldom credit: enforcement is expensive for the creditor too, in officer time, legal cost, provisioning consequences and reputational exposure, and most lenders invoke Section 13 not from appetite but after internal escalation has exhausted the recovery department's softer instruments, a reality that gives the borrower's early engagement its leverage and explains why the sixty days are, in practice, the most negotiable period in the entire statute despite the statute's silence about negotiation.

## SARFAESI and the states: land records, rent control and ground friction

A central statute that operates on land is executed inside state systems, and the friction at that joint explains much of the gap between the Act's timetable and its lived pace. Land records, mutation entries and the registration of the sale certificate are state functions, administered through revenue and registration machinery whose records the authorised officer does not control and whose inaccuracies the enforcement inherits: a security created over property whose record of rights is contested, fragmented among heirs, or inconsistent with possession on the ground carries its disputes into the process, and the tribunal's compliance jurisdiction is a poor instrument for resolving what are in substance title questions belonging to the civil courts. The magistrate's assistance under Section 14 is likewise delivered through district administration whose priorities are its own, which is one reason the thirty and sixty day periods were read as directory: the centre legislated the deadline, but the state supplies the force. Tenancy law supplies the sharpest illustration. In Vishal N. Kalsaria v. Bank of India, (2016) 3 SCC 762, the Supreme Court worked through the collision between SARFAESI enforcement and the protection the Maharashtra Rent Control Act gives a tenant, holding in substance that a tenant whose rights arise under the rent control regime cannot be dispossessed by the summary route in disregard of that protection, so that the enforcement must respect the tenancy it finds where the tenancy is genuine and legally protected. Read with Sondagar, the position is coherent: the Act overrides inconsistent law for the enforcement of the security, but it does not manufacture a right to evict persons whose occupation the law independently protects, and the contest between the two propositions is fought property by property. For the reader, the lesson of the state interface is that SARFAESI's uniformity is a uniformity of power, not of experience: the same Section 13 moves at different speeds, and meets different resistance, in different districts, because the ground on which it operates is administered locally.

## The seam in practice: racing the moratorium

The boundary between this Act and the insolvency regime has produced a strategic behaviour on both sides that the texts do not mention and every practitioner recognises. Because a completed enforcement stands on different ground from an interrupted one, a creditor that sees an insolvency application coming has an incentive to accelerate possession and sale so that the transfer is complete before admission, while a corporate debtor that fears enforcement has a corresponding incentive to reach the insolvency forum first, since admission converts the creditor's individual race into a collective negotiation in which the debtor's existing management is displaced but the enterprise survives intact. The moratorium's freeze therefore rewards speed in the corridor before admission, and tribunals and the National Company Law Tribunal alike have had to police the corridor: enforcement steps taken on the eve of insolvency are examined for whether they were genuinely completed, and insolvency applications filed to intercept an imminent sale are examined for whether the collective process is being used as a shield rather than as a resolution. The law's answer, consistent across the seam cases, is that completion is a question of fact at the moment of admission: measures fully executed and a sale concluded before the moratorium are not unwound by it, while anything short of completion is caught. For the individual and the guarantor standing outside the corporate process, none of this applies, and the creditor's SARFAESI track runs on undisturbed, which is why the modern enforcement contest so often proceeds on two fronts at once, collective inside the company and summary against everyone and everything outside it.

## The economic logic the design assumes

Beneath the legal architecture sits an economic argument that should be made explicit, because it is the premise on which the whole statute stands or falls. Credit is priced on expected loss, and expected loss is the product of the probability of default and the loss given default. A recovery regime acts on the second term: the faster and more certain the realisation of security, the smaller the loss given default, and therefore the lower the interest rate at which a lender can profitably lend to a given class of borrower, and the wider the class of borrowers to whom lending is rational at all. This is the sense in which SARFAESI is a statute about borrowers who never default: its intended beneficiaries include every enterprise whose loan became possible, or cheaper, because the lender could look to the security with confidence. The second-order effects cut in both directions and honesty requires both. A lender secured on realisable assets may lend with less attention to the borrower's cash flows, substituting collateral appraisal for credit appraisal, and the system's asset-price cycles are amplified when credit chases property rather than enterprise. Borrowers without assets to pledge gain nothing from the statute and may lose, as lending tilts towards the secured. And the asset that anchors the design, urban and industrial real property, is precisely the asset whose value is most cyclical, so the regime's promise of certain recovery is least reliable in the downturns when defaults cluster. The committees of the 1990s were not naive about these effects; they judged them preferable to a system in which security was fictitious and credit rationed to the connected. Two decades of the Act's operation have not dissolved that judgment, but they have refined it: the statute demonstrably made security real, and the questions that remain open are distributive and cyclical rather than mechanical, about who bears the cost of that reality and when.

## What the recovery record shows, and what it cannot show

Measuring whether the Act worked requires care about what the available evidence can bear, and this article follows the discipline the insolvency cluster applies to its own record. The Reserve Bank's published reporting on the banking system has, across the years since 2002, treated SARFAESI as one of the principal channels through which scheduled commercial banks recover non-performing assets, alongside the tribunals, the civil process and compromise settlements, and after 2016 alongside the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code. The pattern that reporting supports is directional rather than precise: the channel is heavily used, it recovers meaningful amounts against the stock of bad loans, and its relative share of total recovery moves with the composition of the bad loan stock and with the availability of the collective alternative, rising when enforcement against individual security is the practical route and ceding ground for large corporate exposures to the insolvency process, where the going-concern value exceeds what a break-up sale would fetch. What the aggregate figures cannot show is the distribution of the burden: how much of the recovered value came from large industrial security realised in orderly sales, and how much from small borrowers whose assets sold at distress prices; the channel data record amounts recovered, not the fairness of the recoveries, and any argument that reads them as proof of either justice or injustice is reading more than they contain. The secondary evidence on that distributive question is the litigation record itself, in which the recurring grounds of successful borrower challenge, defective notice, unconsidered representation, irregular sale, mark the points where the system's practice falls short of its paper, and the recurring failure of challenges founded on hardship alone marks the boundary Parliament chose. A statute should be judged by both records together: the recovery it achieves, which the banking data attest, and the errors its process generates, which the tribunal record attests, and the assessment that follows is built on exactly that dual reading.

## The honest assessment

The creditor case, at full strength, is the committees' case, and it has not weakened with time. A financial system intermediates other people's savings, and every rupee locked in an unrecoverable loan is a rupee not lent to a creditworthy borrower. Before 2002, the Indian secured lender's remedy was a decade of litigation against a borrower in possession, and the predictable results were evergreened loans, cosmetic restructuring, a premium on credit for honest borrowers, and a banking system whose reported strength concealed rotten assets. SARFAESI made security credible, and credible security is the foundation on which asset-based lending to small and medium enterprises, which lack the audited histories that cash-flow lending demands, can exist at all. The process, moreover, is not lawless: it is codified step by step, its trigger is a classification governed by the Reserve Bank's norms rather than the creditor's whim, it carries a reasoned-reply duty, a tribunal with power to restore possession, and appellate review. Defenders add that the notice itself resolves most cases, because a borrower who knows possession is sixty days away finds money, family support or a settlement that years of adjournments never extracted.

The borrower case, at full strength, begins where the creditor case ends: with the identity of the decision-maker. The creditor classifies the account, computes the amount, rejects the representation, takes possession, fixes the reserve price and conducts the sale, and at every stage until the tribunal is approached, the borrower's counterparty is also the process's administrator. The tribunal remedy, the design's answer, arrives after possession in the ordinary run, is confined to testing compliance rather than rehearing the debt, and leads to an appeal gated by a deposit of half the claimed amount, reducible to a quarter, demanded from a person whose defining characteristic is inability to pay. The borrower who disputes the classification itself, the commonest real grievance, a business whose account slid into default through a delayed receivable or a disputed appropriation of payments, finds that the statute has the least to say to him: his objection is answered by the institution he accuses, and the forum that could examine it opens only after the measures he fears have been taken. Add the distress-sale problem, that property sold under compulsion and time pressure realises less than its market value, leaving the borrower dispossessed and still liable for the balance, and the design's human cost is neither incidental nor rare.

The verdict the record supports is a divided one, and it should be stated as precisely as the evidence allows. As a machine for converting security into recovery, the Act works, and works roughly as its designers intended; the civil-court era it replaced was not a golden age of borrower protection but a subsidy to default paid by performing borrowers and depositors. As a system of justice between the parties, it is deliberately lopsided, and its fairness depends entirely on two load-bearing safeguards: the integrity of the classification trigger, which the Reserve Bank's norms supply on paper, and the willingness of tribunals to set aside enforcement for procedural default, which is the only language the process respects. Where those two hold, the design's bargain, speed for the creditor, a real if retrospective review for the borrower, is honourably kept. Where classification is gamed or tribunals wave defects through, what remains is seizure with paperwork. The statute has also proved something larger about Indian law reform: that the durable way to move a legal system is not to exhort its courts to be faster but to change who must go to court, and for what. SARFAESI reversed the burden of litigation itself, and every recovery and insolvency reform since has been drafted in its shadow.

For the borrower who has received a notice, the practical core of this article reduces to the discipline of the record. The demand notice, the date of its service, the representation made in reply, the creditor's reasoned response, the possession notice and every later document are the case, because the tribunal will decide on the creditor's compliance proved from exactly those papers; keeping them complete, dated and in order, in a file that can be handed to counsel or the tribunal without reconstruction, is the single most valuable step a borrower can take inside the sixty days. ## Reading an enforcement file: the checks a tribunal will make

The most useful way to hold the whole statute in mind is to reconstruct the file the tribunal will read, because the Act converts every dispute into a documentary audit and the party whose papers answer the audit wins. The tribunal begins with the creditor's title to act: the loan and security documents, the creation of the security interest, and, since the amendment of 2016, its registration with the Central Registry, without which Section 26D denies the enforcement rights altogether. It proceeds to the trigger: the classification of the account as a non-performing asset, tested against the Reserve Bank's norms and the ledger said to support it, which is why the statement of account is the most contested document in the proceeding and why errors in appropriation or interest are worth more to the borrower than any rhetoric about hardship. It then examines the demand notice, its contents, the amount it claims, the assets it identifies and the proof of its service, and the running of the full sixty days, because a measure taken inside the period is a measure taken without power. The representation, if one was made, and the creditor's reasoned reply within fifteen days, are read next, and a missing or empty reply is treated as the statute treats it, as a failure of one of the process's few pre-possession duties. Possession is audited against the Rules: the authorised officer's authority, the possession notice, its affixation and publication, the inventory. The sale is audited for valuation, reserve price, the prescribed notice to the borrower, publicity and confirmation. Against that audit the borrower marshals his own documents, and the discipline of this article's practical advice can be stated in its full form: the borrower's case is built in the sixty days, from papers received and sent then, and is merely presented in the tribunal. Readers who work through a live file in this order, testing each stage against the section that governs it, will find that the statute's apparent complexity resolves into a single question asked nine times: was this step, on this record, in accordance with the Act.

## What remains unsettled

A pillar article should end its analysis by marking the ground that is still moving, stated structurally so that the map stays accurate as the litigation proceeds. Four questions stand open in the sense that matters here, that their answers are being worked out case by case rather than declared by the text. The first is the practical content of the classification safeguard: the tribunal's power to test classification against the Reserve Bank's norms is established, but the intensity of that testing, how far a tribunal will go into the ledger of a running business account, varies, and the difference between a tribunal that audits the classification and one that merely notes it is the difference between the safeguard existing and not existing. The second is the price problem: the process disciplines the conduct of the sale elaborately and its outcome hardly at all, and whether a duty to take reasonable care to obtain a proper price should be read into the creditor's position, with consequences for sales that clear the procedural hurdles and fail the economic one, remains an argument borrowers make and courts have not fully taken up. The third is the boundary of the notified creditor class, where the treatment of housing finance companies against the thresholds set for non-banking financial companies has already produced conflicting High Court approaches, and where each extension of the power reopens the question of what scale and supervision justify summary enforcement. The fourth is the newest: the notification of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code's provisions for personal guarantors to corporate debtors has opened a collective front against the same individuals whom this Act reaches individually, and the coordination between a guarantor's personal insolvency and a creditor's pending SARFAESI enforcement against the guarantor's property is being settled in litigation as the two regimes learn each other's boundaries. A fifth and quieter uncertainty concerns the creditor's own conduct between classification and notice, the interval in which a stressed account is nursed, restructured, ever greened or prepared for enforcement under internal policies the borrower never sees. The Act regulates the process from the demand notice onwards and says nothing about the corridor before it, yet the fairness of everything after the notice depends on decisions taken inside that corridor: which accounts are classified promptly and which are carried, which borrowers are offered restructuring and which are moved directly to enforcement. The Reserve Bank's supervisory framework, its inspection of bank portfolios and its directions on stressed assets, is the discipline that governs the corridor, and it operates at the level of the bank's book rather than the individual borrower's rights. A borrower therefore cannot litigate the corridor as such; he can only attack its outputs, the classification and the ledger, when they emerge into the statutory process. Whether an individualised duty at that earlier stage would improve the system or merely relocate the litigation is a genuine policy question on which the committees never pronounced and Parliament has not legislated, and it is the point at which the statute's silence is most consequential.

None of these open questions unsettles the core architecture. The sixty-day notice, enforcement before adjudication, the tribunal as the single reviewing forum and the magistrate as an instrument of possession have survived two decades of challenge, amendment and competition from a collective code, and the reader who has understood why they were built, and what they cost, holds the key to every SARFAESI question the search pages argue about.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the SARFAESI Act allow a lender to do?

The Act allows a secured creditor, a bank, a notified financial institution or an asset reconstruction company that has acquired the loan, to enforce its security interest without approaching a court first. Once the borrower's account is classified as a non-performing asset and a demand notice under Section 13(2) has expired without payment, the creditor may take possession of the secured assets and sell, lease or assign them, take over the management of the borrower's business, appoint a manager for the assets taken, and direct persons who owe the borrower money on account of the secured assets to pay the creditor instead. The creditor may also seek a magistrate's assistance to take possession, and the borrower may challenge the measures afterwards before the Debts Recovery Tribunal.

### Q: What notice must a bank give before taking possession under SARFAESI?

Before taking possession, the secured creditor must serve a written demand notice under Section 13(2) on the borrower. The notice requires the borrower to discharge the full liabilities within sixty days from the date of the notice and must state the amount payable and the secured assets the creditor intends to enforce against if payment is not made. Only after that period expires without discharge of the liability may the creditor take the measures under Section 13(4), including possession. If the borrower files a representation or objection, the creditor must consider it and communicate reasons for rejecting it within fifteen days. Possession itself is then effected through the authorised officer with the notices the Enforcement Rules prescribe, including affixation and publication.

### Q: How can a borrower challenge a SARFAESI action?

The borrower's remedy is an application under Section 17 to the Debts Recovery Tribunal with jurisdiction over the secured asset, filed within forty-five days of the measure complained of, whether that measure is possession, a management takeover or a sale step. The application is an original proceeding in which the tribunal examines whether the creditor's measures comply with the Act and the Rules, and it can declare the measures invalid, restore possession or management to the borrower, and award compensation and costs where possession was taken without entitlement. No deposit is required to file the application. Against the tribunal's order, an appeal under Section 18 lies to the Appellate Tribunal within thirty days, but that appeal requires a deposit of fifty per cent of the debt due, reducible to twenty-five per cent.

### Q: What did the Mardia Chemicals judgment decide?

In Mardia Chemicals Ltd. v. Union of India, decided on 8 April 2004, the Supreme Court upheld the SARFAESI Act as a whole, accepting that Parliament could create an expeditious recovery mechanism for a banking system burdened with non-performing assets and could dispense with prior court intervention. The Court struck down one provision: the original Section 17(2), which required a borrower to deposit seventy-five per cent of the amount claimed before the Debts Recovery Tribunal would entertain the application, a condition the Court held to be oppressive, onerous and arbitrary and therefore ultra vires Article 14 of the Constitution. The Court also held that a secured creditor must consider objections raised by the borrower to the demand notice and communicate reasons for rejecting them, a safeguard Parliament then enacted as Section 13(3A).

### Q: Which loans and assets are outside SARFAESI?

Section 31 keeps several matters outside the Act. It does not apply to a lien on goods, money or security under the Contract Act or the Sale of Goods Act, to a pledge of movables, to security created over an aircraft or a vessel, to the rights of an unpaid seller, or to property that cannot be attached under the Civil Procedure Code, except property specifically charged with the debt. Three exclusions carry the widest effect. The Act does not apply to a security interest created in agricultural land. It does not apply where the financial asset secured does not exceed one lakh rupees. And it does not apply where the amount due is less than twenty per cent of the principal amount and the interest on it, so nearly repaid loans are beyond summary enforcement.

### Q: How does an asset reconstruction company acquire and recover a bad loan?

An asset reconstruction company registered with the Reserve Bank under Section 3 acquires financial assets from banks and financial institutions under Section 5, by agreement or by issuing debentures or bonds, and steps into the lender's position, acquiring the lender's rights against the borrower and in the security. It may fund acquisitions by issuing security receipts to qualified institutional buyers under Section 7 and administer the assets for their benefit. For recovery, Section 9 lets it take over or change the management of the borrower's business, sell or lease the business in whole or part, reschedule the debt, settle the dues, take possession of the secured assets and enforce the security interest under Chapter III of the Act, exactly as the original lender could.

### Q: How does SARFAESI interact with insolvency proceedings?

The two regimes meet at the admission of a corporate insolvency resolution process under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. From the insolvency commencement date, the moratorium under Section 14 of the Code prohibits any action to foreclose, recover or enforce a security interest against the corporate debtor, including action under SARFAESI, and the Supreme Court has held that enforcement underway must stop. The secured creditor's claim then travels through the collective process, where its security counts in the distribution order. Outside a process, SARFAESI operates at full strength, and even inside the Code's liquidation stage a secured creditor may elect under Section 52 to realise its security outside the liquidation estate rather than relinquish it. The moratorium also does not protect guarantors of the corporate debtor.

### Q: Can a District Magistrate be asked to help a lender take possession under SARFAESI?

Yes. Under Section 14, a secured creditor may request in writing the assistance of the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or the District Magistrate within whose jurisdiction the secured asset is situated, for taking possession or control of the asset. The creditor supports the request with an affidavit from its authorised officer confirming the loan, the default and classification, service of the demand notice and compliance with the Act and the Rules. The magistrate's function is ministerial rather than adjudicatory, as the Supreme Court held in the Sondagar case: the magistrate satisfies himself about the affidavit and takes possession, but does not try disputes between creditor and borrower, which belong to the Debts Recovery Tribunal. Since the amendment of 2016, the magistrate is expected to pass orders within thirty days, extendable to sixty days in the aggregate for recorded reasons.


### Q: When can a bank classify a loan account as a non-performing asset?

Classification is not a matter of the bank's free choice. A non-performing asset is defined in Section 2 of the Act as an asset or account of a borrower which has been classified by the bank or financial institution as sub-standard, doubtful or a loss asset in accordance with the directions or guidelines relating to asset classification issued by the Reserve Bank of India. Those norms turn principally on repayment behaviour: an account in which interest or an instalment of principal remains overdue beyond the prescribed period is treated as non-performing, and it then ages through the sub-standard and doubtful categories as the period of default lengthens. Because classification is the trigger for the whole Act, a borrower who says the classification is wrong, because payments were not credited or the norms were misapplied, is raising the foundational question, and it is examined first in the representation to the creditor and later by the tribunal.

### Q: What happens if a borrower objects to a SARFAESI demand notice?

The borrower may make a representation or raise an objection to the secured creditor on receipt of the Section 13(2) notice, and Section 13(3A) obliges the creditor to consider it. If the creditor concludes that the representation or objection is not acceptable or tenable, it must communicate the reasons for non-acceptance to the borrower within fifteen days of receiving it. The reply should engage with the objection, for instance an error in the amount claimed, a payment not appropriated, or a classification contrary to the Reserve Bank's norms, because a failure to reply, or a reply that ignores the objection, is a procedural defect the tribunal can act on when the measures are challenged. The provision is explicit, however, that the communication of reasons does not by itself give the borrower a right to approach the tribunal; that right arises when a measure under Section 13(4) is taken.

### Q: Is a deposit required to appeal to the DRAT against a DRT order under SARFAESI?

Yes, and the amount surprises most borrowers. Under Section 18, no appeal by a borrower to the Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunal shall be entertained unless the borrower has deposited fifty per cent of the amount of debt due from him, as claimed by the secured creditors or as determined by the Debts Recovery Tribunal, whichever is less. The Appellate Tribunal may, for reasons recorded in writing, reduce the deposit, but the statute fixes a floor: not less than twenty-five per cent. The Supreme Court has held that the deposit cannot be waived entirely, because the right of appeal is conferred by the statute on that condition. This deposit applies only at the appeal stage. The original application to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17 requires a prescribed fee, not a deposit of the debt.

### Q: Can a borrower redeem the property after the auction notice is published under SARFAESI?

Under the law as recast by the amendment of 2016, no. Section 13(8) protects the borrower who tenders the entire dues of the secured creditor together with all costs, charges and expenses at any time before the date of publication of the notice for public auction, or the invitation of quotations or tender, or the private treaty, for transfer of the secured assets: on such tender the assets shall not be transferred and no further step towards sale shall be taken. Once that publication happens, the statutory right to redeem is spent, even though the auction itself is still to come. The Supreme Court in Celir LLP v. Bafna Motors upheld the amended position. Before 2016 the text was kinder to the borrower, and the Court in Mathew Varghese had read the right as surviving until the transfer was completed, which is why the date of the enforcement matters to the answer.

### Q: Does SARFAESI apply to a tenant living in the mortgaged property?

A tenancy does not take the property outside the Act, but the tenant is not without protection, and the timing and genuineness of the tenancy decide the matter. In Harshad Govardhan Sondagar v. International Assets Reconstruction Company, the Supreme Court held that a lessee or tenant in possession under a valid tenancy created before the mortgage, particularly one protected by rent control legislation, cannot be dispossessed in the summary process without the claim being examined, while a tenancy created after the demand notice, or in breach of the terms of the mortgage, or unsupported by genuine documentation, will not obstruct enforcement. The magistrate acting under Section 14 may satisfy himself on the threshold genuineness of the claim, disputed tenancies are examined by the Debts Recovery Tribunal, which after the amendment of 2016 has an express remit to scrutinise tenancy claims within the Section 17 proceeding.

### Q: Can a bank use SARFAESI for a home loan?

Yes. Nothing in the Act confines it to business borrowing, and housing loans secured by a mortgage of the house are among the commonest uses of the enforcement chapter. The borrower under a defaulted home loan receives the same Section 13(2) demand notice, has the same sixty days, the same right to make a representation under Section 13(3A), and the same remedy before the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17 as a corporate borrower, on the same forty-five day period. The property specifically charged with the debt is carved out of the protection Section 31 gives to property exempt from attachment, so a mortgaged home is not saved by that route. The exclusions that can matter to a household borrower are the numerical ones: the Act does not apply to a financial asset not exceeding one lakh rupees, or where the amount due has fallen below twenty per cent of the principal and interest.

### Q: What is CERSAI and why does registration matter for SARFAESI enforcement?

CERSAI, the Central Registry of Securitisation Asset Reconstruction and Security Interest of India, is the central registry established under Chapter IV of the Act in which particulars of securitisation and reconstruction transactions and of security interests created over property are registered, so that a lender can discover what encumbrances a property already carries before lending against it. The amendment of 2016 converted registration from a record-keeping duty into a condition of power. Under Section 26D, no secured creditor is entitled to exercise the enforcement rights under Chapter III unless the security interest has been registered with the Central Registry. Registration also carries the benefit declared by Section 26E: after registration, the debts due to the secured creditor are to be paid in priority over all other debts and over revenues, taxes, cesses and other rates payable to the Central Government, a state government or a local authority.

### Q: Who has priority when both a bank and the government claim dues against the same property?

Since the amendment of 2016, the registered secured creditor does, in terms Parliament made difficult to argue with. Section 26E provides that, notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, after the registration of the security interest the debts due to any secured creditor shall be paid in priority over all other debts and all revenues, taxes, cesses and other rates payable to the Central Government or a state government or a local authority. The Supreme Court has given effect to that priority against revenue claims founded on first-charge provisions in taxing statutes. Two qualifications apply. The priority presupposes registration, since Section 26D ties the enforcement rights themselves to it. And Section 26E carries an Explanation subordinating the priority to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code where insolvency proceedings are pending, so the ranking inside an insolvency is governed by the Code's own distribution order.

### Q: Can a borrower file a civil suit against SARFAESI action?

As a rule, no. Section 34 bars a civil court from entertaining any suit or proceeding in respect of a matter which the Debts Recovery Tribunal or the Appellate Tribunal is empowered to determine, and it bars any court or other authority from granting an injunction in respect of action taken under the Act. A borrower who sues in the civil court to stop a possession or sale will ordinarily find the suit rejected and the forty-five day period for the tribunal still running. In Mardia Chemicals the Supreme Court preserved only a very narrow residue of civil jurisdiction, within the limited grounds on which suits are permissible in matters relating to an English mortgage enforceable without the court's intervention, and later decisions have kept that opening narrow. The High Courts' writ jurisdiction exists but is exercised sparingly where the Section 17 remedy is available, which is why the tribunal application is the route that matters.

### Q: Can a bank proceed against a guarantor under SARFAESI?

Yes. The Act's definition of borrower includes a person who has given a guarantee or created any mortgage or pledge as security for the financial assistance granted to the borrower, so a guarantor who has mortgaged property for another's loan stands inside the enforcement chapter in respect of that security. The creditor's rights against a guarantor are co-extensive with its rights against the principal borrower, and the creditor is not required to exhaust its remedies against the borrower before moving against the guarantor's secured assets, although in practice notices are often issued to both together. The guarantor receives the benefit of the same procedure, the demand notice, the representation under Section 13(3A) and the Section 17 remedy, and where the principal debtor is a company in insolvency, the moratorium that protects the company does not protect the guarantor, whose assets may still be proceeded against.

### Q: What did the 2016 amendment change in the SARFAESI Act?

The Enforcement of Security Interest and Recovery of Debts Laws and Miscellaneous Provisions (Amendment) Act, 2016, in force from 1 September 2016, was the largest single revision of the Act. It recast Section 13(8) so that the borrower's right to redeem ends at the publication of the auction notice rather than surviving until the transfer is completed. It inserted Chapter IVA, making registration of the security interest with the Central Registry a condition of enforcement under Section 26D and conferring the priority stated in Section 26E over other debts and government dues. It disciplined assistance proceedings under Section 14 by requiring the magistrate to pass orders within thirty days, extendable to sixty days in the aggregate for reasons recorded. And it strengthened the tribunal's machinery, including express provision for examining tenancy claims raised against enforcement.

### Q: Can an NBFC use SARFAESI to recover its loans?

A specified non-banking financial company can, because the Central Government may notify an NBFC as a financial institution under Section 2(1)(m) of the Act, and notified NBFCs are secured creditors for its purposes. The extension has been calibrated by threshold rather than opened to the whole sector. By notification S.O. 856(E) of February 2020, NBFCs with an asset size of one hundred crore rupees and above became eligible in respect of secured debts of fifty lakh rupees and above, and by notification S.O. 652(E) of February 2021 the minimum secured debt for such NBFCs was reduced to twenty lakh rupees and above. Smaller NBFCs and smaller loans remain outside, to be pursued through the ordinary tribunal and civil routes. Housing finance companies have been brought within the framework through their own notifications on comparable terms.
