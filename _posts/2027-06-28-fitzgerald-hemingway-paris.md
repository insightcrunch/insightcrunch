The friendship started after the book was finished. That one checkable fact should govern everything a reader does with Fitzgerald, Hemingway, and Paris, and almost nobody mentions it. The Great Gatsby was published on April 10, 1925. The two men met in a Montparnasse bar in the weeks that followed, and by then the novel was printed, bound, reviewed, and already selling badly. Whatever the Paris expatriate scene did to Fitzgerald, it did not write his third novel. It arrived too late for that, and knowing exactly how late is the difference between using this context and being used by it.

![Fitzgerald, Hemingway, and Paris: the 1920s expatriate milieu behind The Great Gatsby explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-70.webp)

The temptation runs the other way, and it runs hard. Two of the largest names in American prose, one city, one decade, one shared publisher, one famous mutual antagonism, and a memoir by the survivor that turns the whole thing into an allegory about talent and waste. The pull toward a tidy story is enormous: Paris made them, they made each other, and Gatsby is somehow the product. Every part of that sentence is either wrong or so loose it does no analytical work. The real relationship is stranger, better documented, and considerably more useful, because it lets a reader see what a literary milieu actually does to a writer and what it does not.

This article takes the friendship and the scene apart, dates them precisely, and then argues for a specific and limited use. The Paris milieu cannot explain the composition of The Great Gatsby, because the composition was over. What it can explain is the structure of feeling that a displaced American carried into that book and out the other side of it, the professional world the novel had to survive in, and the interpretive myths that a century of retelling has attached to the novel's author. Those are three real payoffs. None of them requires pretending Hemingway edited a sentence of the book.

## Why Fitzgerald, Hemingway, and Paris Belong in a Reading of The Great Gatsby

Context earns its place in a reading when it changes what a sentence means. Otherwise it is decoration, and a reader can feel the difference immediately. A paragraph explaining that the 1920s were prosperous and that jazz was popular tells nobody anything they could not guess from the novel's own furniture. A paragraph establishing that Fitzgerald wrote a novel about America while living outside it, among other Americans who had also left, and that this condition of voluntary exile is legible in the book's sentences, changes how the closing pages read. The first paragraph is padding. The second is analysis.

The Paris connection sits in an awkward middle position that generates most of the confusion around it. It is not irrelevant, because Fitzgerald spent a substantial portion of the mid-1920s in France, published his masterpiece from there, and formed the defining professional relationship of his later life in that city. It is also not causal for the novel, because the manuscript was complete and the friendship had not begun. Readers who cannot hold both facts at once collapse into one of two errors: they either inflate the Paris scene into the novel's secret engine, or they dismiss the whole context as biographical trivia. Both errors cost real interpretive power.

### Why does the Paris milieu matter for reading The Great Gatsby?

The milieu matters because it names the condition the novel was written from: an American writing about America from outside it, among other Americans who had also left. That distance is legible in Nick's retrospective voice and in the final vision of the continent seen whole. It explains the book's angle of sight, not its plot.

Hold that answer against the alternative and the gain becomes obvious. A reader who knows only that Fitzgerald was a famous drunk who knew Hemingway has a fact. A reader who knows that the novel's American continent is described by a narrator looking backward from a distance, and that its author composed those pages abroad among a colony of Americans professionally engaged in the same act of looking backward, has an argument. The second reader can point at "a fresh, green breast of the new world" and say something specific about why the continent appears as a vision rather than as a place, and why it appears at the exact moment the narrator has decided to leave the East.

The friendship supplies a second and different payoff, which is professional rather than interpretive. Fitzgerald's dealings with Hemingway show a working novelist operating inside a literary economy: recommending a rival to his own editor, line-editing a competitor's manuscript, watching that competitor overtake him, and being written about afterward by the winner. That story tells a reader what it meant to be a novelist in the 1920s, and it explains a great deal about the novel's reception, its author's reputation, and the specific shape of the myth that later readers inherited. The book's first readers are covered in more depth in the account of [how 1920s readers saw The Great Gatsby](/2027/06/21/1920s-readers-gatsby/), and the reception story compounds the biographical one.

There is a third reason, less obvious and more valuable for essay writers. The Fitzgerald and Hemingway relationship is the single best available case study in how biographical legend forms, hardens, and then gets mistaken for evidence. Nearly every widely repeated anecdote about the two men descends from one source, that source is a memoir written decades later by an interested party, and the most famous exchange between them almost certainly never happened in the form everyone repeats. A student who learns to handle this material carefully learns to handle all biographical criticism carefully, which is a transferable skill worth more than any single fact about Montparnasse.

## The City of Exiles: What the 1920s Paris Expatriate Scene Actually Was

Start with money, because money is why the scene existed. After the First World War the French franc collapsed against the dollar and stayed collapsed through the middle of the decade. An American with a modest income in dollars could live in Paris at a standard that the same income would not buy in New York, and could live there while doing work that paid irregularly or not at all. This is the material precondition for the entire expatriate phenomenon. Writers did not go to Paris because Paris was mystical. They went because the exchange rate meant a young writer could eat, drink, rent a room, and buy time to write badly for a year or two before writing well.

### What was the 1920s Paris expatriate scene?

It was a colony of mostly American and British writers, artists, and hangers-on concentrated in Montparnasse and the Latin Quarter, sustained by a weak franc, a permissive cultural climate, and a network of small presses, bookshops, salons, and little magazines that could publish work no commercial American house would touch.

The institutions are worth naming because they did the work. Sylvia Beach ran Shakespeare and Company, a bookshop and lending library on the Left Bank that functioned as post office, bank, clubhouse, and publisher for the English-speaking colony. Beach published James Joyce's Ulysses in 1922 when no house in Britain or America would risk it, which established the scene's central proposition: that the difficult book could exist because it had somewhere to go. Gertrude Stein kept a salon at her apartment where the paintings on the walls were as much the point as the conversation, and where a young writer could be told, at length and without invitation, what was wrong with his sentences. Ezra Pound moved through the city as an agent, editor, promoter, and general accelerant. Ford Madox Ford edited the transatlantic review. Robert McAlmon's Contact Editions printed the writers nobody else would print, including an early Hemingway.

Around these institutions sat the cafés, and the cafés mattered because they were the delivery mechanism. The Dôme, the Rotonde, the Select, and the Closerie des Lilas were not merely places to drink. They were where a manuscript changed hands, where an introduction happened, where a reputation was made or unmade in an evening, and where the day's work got argued about by people qualified to argue about it. A writer in Ohio in 1925 wrote alone. A writer in Montparnasse in 1925 wrote alone and then walked three blocks into a room where fifteen other people were doing the same thing and had opinions.

The permissiveness was real and it was not only about alcohol, though American Prohibition was a genuine push factor and the series covers that pressure in its account of [Prohibition and bootlegging in Gatsby](/2026/10/26/prohibition-bootlegging-gatsby/). The deeper permission was formal. Paris in the 1920s was where a writer could try a book without a plot, a book without punctuation, a book in a language he had invented, and find a reader for it. Modernism did not require Paris, but Paris made modernism cheap to attempt, and cheapness of attempt is how movements happen.

Now the correction, because the romantic version of this scene has swallowed the accurate one. The expatriate colony was small, factional, frequently unpleasant, and shorter-lived than the legend suggests. It ran roughly from the early 1920s to the crash, and it was held together as much by feuds as by fellowship. Stein and Hemingway ended as enemies. Hemingway and McAlmon ended as enemies. Hemingway and Ford ended as enemies, and Hemingway put Ford into a memoir in a way Ford could not answer, being dead. The pattern is not incidental. A scene that concentrates ambitious young people with no money and enormous confidence in a few square blocks produces exactly this. The warmth in the retrospective accounts is largely the warmth of survivors describing a war they won.

### Who was actually in the room, and what did each one mean to Fitzgerald?

Gertrude Stein, Sylvia Beach, Ezra Pound, James Joyce, Ford Madox Ford, and Robert McAlmon anchored the colony. Fitzgerald knew several of them and was formed by none of them. He entered the scene in 1925 as a finished writer with a finished book, which is precisely why the colony's usual effect on a young talent did not operate on him.

That last point deserves developing, because it is where the difference between Fitzgerald's Paris and Hemingway's Paris becomes visible. Hemingway arrived in 1921, at twenty-two, with no books, a newspaper job, and a wife whose small trust fund paid the rent. The colony made him. Stein read his early work and told him to cut the journalism out of it. Pound taught him to distrust the adjective and traded boxing lessons for editing. Beach lent him books he could not buy. McAlmon printed him when nobody else would. Ford put him in the transatlantic review and let him do editorial work. By the time Fitzgerald met him, Hemingway had been assembled by that machine for four years and was two months from beginning the novel that would repay it.

Fitzgerald's relation to the same people was entirely different and mostly cordial. He met Stein and she was gracious about his work, which she rarely was about anyone's. He met Joyce at a dinner and, by the standard account, offered to jump out of the window in tribute, which is either charming or alarming depending on how much he had drunk and how much you trust the reporter. Beach knew him. Pound and he had little to say to each other. None of these encounters changed a line he wrote. He was not a young man looking for a method. He was a famous novelist with a bad year behind him, looking for company and a reason to keep going.

The asymmetry is the point, and it explains why the two men's Paris memories diverge so violently. For Hemingway, Paris was the place where he became himself, and his memoir treats it as a lost paradise of poverty and appetite and work. For Fitzgerald, Paris was a place he lived for a while, between the book that failed and the decade that broke him. He wrote no great account of it. He was passing through, and one of the reasons the legend of Fitzgerald in Paris is so unreliable is that the only person who wrote it down at length was the other man.

Stein is worth one more sentence because of what she supplied to the vocabulary rather than to Fitzgerald. Her remark about a lost generation, delivered to Hemingway in some form and used by him as an epigraph, gave the decade the label that has organized every discussion since. It was a remark, not a theory. It became a category because a novelist put it on a title page, which is a useful reminder of how casually the terms that govern literary history get made.

## How Fitzgerald Got to Paris and What He Brought With Him

The chronology decides the argument, so it has to be exact. Fitzgerald had already published two novels and a pile of well-paid magazine stories when he and Zelda sailed for France in the spring of 1924. They were not poor bohemians looking for a cheap room. They were a celebrity couple with a small daughter, a nanny, and a spending habit that outran a large income, and the move to Europe was partly a budget measure. Fitzgerald went abroad in order to write a serious book without the distractions and the costs of being famous in New York.

He did not go to Paris to write it. He went through Paris and kept going, to the Riviera, where the family settled at Saint-Raphaël for the summer of 1924. The bulk of The Great Gatsby was drafted there, in the heat, near the sea, while Zelda conducted the friendship with the French aviator Edouard Jozan that would poison the marriage and feed the novel's central situation. The full account of that summer belongs to the article on [Fitzgerald and the Riviera years](/2027/04/12/fitzgerald-riviera-years/), which owns the writing locale. What matters here is the negative fact: the drafting happened on the Mediterranean coast, hundreds of miles from Montparnasse, before Fitzgerald had exchanged a word with Hemingway.

The revision happened somewhere else again. The Fitzgeralds wintered in Rome and Capri, and it was from Italy that Fitzgerald sent Maxwell Perkins the manuscript and then, in a burst of correspondence and galley work, performed the restructuring that produced the book as it stands. The Perkins collaboration is the genuine editorial story of this novel, and the series treats it in the account of [Maxwell Perkins and the editing of Gatsby](/2027/03/29/maxwell-perkins-editing-gatsby/). The process itself, draft to final text, is laid out in [how Fitzgerald wrote The Great Gatsby](/2027/03/01/how-fitzgerald-wrote-gatsby/). Both articles will tell a reader the same thing this one does: the shaping intelligence applied to Gatsby from outside was Perkins's, exercised by transatlantic mail from New York, and it had nothing to do with Paris.

### Did Fitzgerald write The Great Gatsby in Paris?

No. Fitzgerald drafted the novel on the French Riviera at Saint-Raphaël in the summer of 1924 and revised it in Rome and Capri that winter, working with his New York editor Maxwell Perkins by mail. He returned to Paris in the spring of 1925, after the book was published, and lived there through 1926.

The Fitzgeralds took an apartment on the rue de Tilsitt, near the Étoile, in the spring of 1925. The address is a small but telling detail: it is on the Right Bank, in a respectable and expensive quarter, nowhere near the Montparnasse the legend places him in. Fitzgerald visited the expatriate scene. He was never quite of it. He arrived as an established commercial author with a Scribner's contract and a Saturday Evening Post income, into a colony whose defining posture was contempt for exactly that kind of success. The tension in every account of Fitzgerald in Paris comes from this mismatch, and it is worth noticing because it repeats the mismatch inside his own novel, where a man with money is never quite admitted by the people who have always had it.

What Fitzgerald brought to Paris, then, was a finished book that was not selling, a marriage that had been damaged the previous summer, a drinking problem that was accelerating, and a reputation as the writer of the Jazz Age at exactly the moment he had written something the Jazz Age audience did not want. The novel's commercial failure, treated at length in [why The Great Gatsby flopped at first](/2027/03/15/why-gatsby-flopped-first/), is the emotional weather of everything that follows. He met Hemingway as a disappointed man.

## The Dingo Bar, Spring 1925: How the Friendship Began

The standard account of the meeting comes from Hemingway, in the chapter of A Moveable Feast that opens his treatment of Fitzgerald. In that telling the two men met at the Dingo Bar on the rue Delambre in Montparnasse, Fitzgerald arrived with a companion, talked at length and embarrassingly about Hemingway's work, asked an intrusive question about Hemingway's sex life, drank too much, changed color, and had to be taken home. Hemingway then spends the rest of the chapter making the point that he had liked the writing before he met the man and could not reconcile the two.

Every element of that account should be handled with tongs, and the reason is straightforward. Hemingway wrote it more than thirty years later, after Fitzgerald had been dead for two decades and could not answer, at a point in Hemingway's life when his own health and work were failing and his instinct for settling scores was at its most active. The memoir was published posthumously in 1964 and was not finished by its author. It is a work of literature about the past, shaped by a novelist at the height of his technique, and it is not a document. Treating its anecdotes as evidence is the single most common error in this whole area.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is the frame: the two met in Paris in the spring of 1925, probably in late April or early May, in a Montparnasse bar, shortly after Gatsby's publication and shortly before The Sun Also Rises was drafted. Fitzgerald was twenty-eight and famous. Hemingway was twenty-five and unknown outside the small circle that had read his short pieces. Within eighteen months the positions would begin to reverse, and neither man ever fully recovered from that reversal.

The second famous anecdote is the trip to Lyon, undertaken shortly after the meeting, to retrieve a car the Fitzgeralds had abandoned there. Hemingway's account is a comic set piece: Fitzgerald misses the train, turns up late, insists he is dying of congestion of the lungs, demands a thermometer, and generally behaves as a burden while the roofless car takes on rain and the two men drink their way north. The car had no roof because Zelda had disliked the top and had it cut away, which is the kind of detail that is either perfect evidence or perfect invention and cannot be distinguished at this distance. What the episode establishes, if anything, is the shape the friendship took from the first weeks: Hemingway competent and irritated, Fitzgerald generous and unreliable, and a running comparison of manhood underneath everything.

Something did happen on that trip, though, and it is the part the comedy obscures. Fitzgerald talked to Hemingway about writing for two days, and Hemingway, whatever he said about it later, was talking to a man who had just published one of the most controlled American novels of the century. Fitzgerald gave him a copy of Gatsby when they got back. Hemingway read it. Hemingway's response, as he recorded it decades afterward, was that if Fitzgerald could write a book that good he could write a better one, and that he decided to be a real friend on the strength of it. That is a genuine reading, and it is one of the few moments in the memoir where the older Hemingway is not obviously performing.

## A Friendship of Rivals: The Arc from 1925 to 1940

The relationship has a shape, and the shape is the argument. It runs in four movements: patronage, collaboration, reversal, and posthumous verdict. Naming the movements is what turns anecdote into analysis.

### Movement one: Fitzgerald as patron

The patronage came first, and it came before the friendship, which is the fact that most complicates the rivalry story. In October 1924, from Italy, months before the two men had met, Fitzgerald wrote to Maxwell Perkins recommending an unknown young American in Paris whose work he had read in the little magazines, and urged Scribner's to sign him. The recommendation is documented in the Perkins correspondence and it is unambiguous. Fitzgerald, at the height of his fame, used his own editor and his own credit at his own house to promote a writer he had never met, on the evidence of a few sketches.

That is not what a rival does. It is what a serious reader does, and it establishes the first and most durable thing about Fitzgerald in this story: his judgment about other people's prose was excellent and his generosity about it was real. He did the same thing repeatedly across his career, and it cost him. When Hemingway broke with his first publisher over the satirical novella The Torrents of Spring in 1926, Scribner's was waiting, because Fitzgerald had already prepared the ground. The Sun Also Rises appeared under the Scribner's imprint in October 1926, edited by Perkins, in a line of succession that runs directly through Fitzgerald.

### Movement two: the edit

The collaboration is the most concrete evidence of the friendship's substance and the least known part of the story. In the summer of 1926 Fitzgerald read the manuscript of The Sun Also Rises and sent Hemingway a long letter of criticism. The central objection was structural: the novel opened with pages of expository backstory about Brett Ashley and Mike Campbell, delivered in a knowing and superior tone, and Fitzgerald told him it was condescending and dead and should go.

Hemingway cut it. The opening chapter and a half went into the bin, and the novel as published begins with the famous flat sentence about Robert Cohn's boxing at Princeton. The book is faster, colder, and better for the cut, and the cut was Fitzgerald's idea. This is the hard fact that the rivalry narrative cannot absorb and that the friendship narrative underrates: the most influential single act of criticism between these two men ran from Fitzgerald to Hemingway, on the book that made Hemingway's career, and it worked.

Hemingway's later account of the episode in A Moveable Feast declines to give Fitzgerald the credit and reframes the decision as his own. That reframing is itself evidence, though not the kind Hemingway intended. A man does not rewrite a debt he does not feel.

### Movement three: the reversal

After 1926 the trajectories cross. The Sun Also Rises made Hemingway, and he compounded it with A Farewell to Arms in 1929. Fitzgerald, having written his best book to no commercial reward, did not finish another novel until Tender Is the Night in 1934, nine years later, and that book was received badly on arrival. He spent those years writing magazine stories for money, drinking, and managing Zelda's collapse into mental illness, a story the series takes up in [Zelda Fitzgerald and The Great Gatsby](/2026/11/16/zelda-fitzgerald-gatsby/).

The friendship did not end. It soured, which is different and more interesting. The letters continue for years, and their tone shifts from collegial to careful to wounded. Hemingway grew contemptuous of what he saw as Fitzgerald's self-indulgence and his willingness to sell his talent to the Post. Fitzgerald grew wary, deferential, and occasionally bitter about Hemingway's ascent. Zelda and Hemingway detested each other from early on, each believing the other was destroying Scott, and that antagonism poisoned whatever domestic ground the friendship might have stood on.

The public rupture came in 1936. Fitzgerald published The Crack-Up essays in Esquire, an unsparing public account of his own breakdown that the series examines in [The Crack-Up and Fitzgerald's decline](/2027/05/03/fitzgerald-crack-up/). Hemingway regarded the essays as a disgrace, a writer whining in print instead of working, and in the same year he published The Snows of Kilimanjaro in the same magazine with a passage that named Fitzgerald directly and pitied him for his romantic awe of the rich. Fitzgerald wrote to him and asked him to leave his name out of it. In the book publication the name was changed to Julian. The damage was done and it was permanent.

### Movement four: the posthumous verdict

Fitzgerald died in December 1940, at forty-four, with The Last Tycoon unfinished and his books effectively out of print. Hemingway lived another twenty years, won the Nobel Prize, and wrote the memoir that would fix Fitzgerald in the public imagination as a gifted child who ruined himself. That memoir appeared after both men were dead. Fitzgerald had no reply and never would.

The image Hemingway made is the one that stuck, and it is worth stating plainly what it consists of. It says that Fitzgerald had a natural gift he did not understand and could not protect, that his gift was as involuntary and as fragile as the pattern on a butterfly's wing, that Zelda destroyed him out of jealousy, and that he could not hold his drink or his nerve. Every part of that portrait is arguable and some of it is demonstrably shaped for effect. All of it has been repeated for sixty years as though it were a coroner's report.

### Was the relationship a friendship or a rivalry?

Both, structurally and at the same time. Fitzgerald promoted Hemingway to his own publisher before they met and improved his breakthrough novel by cutting its opening. Hemingway accepted the help, overtook him, and then wrote the memoir that defined him. The competition was inside the friendship, not opposed to it.

## Two Paris Novels: What Each Writer Made of the Same Material

The clearest way to see what the milieu did and did not do is to set the two books side by side. Hemingway wrote The Sun Also Rises in the summer and autumn of 1925, immediately after meeting Fitzgerald, and it is a Paris novel: the cafés are in it, the expatriates are in it, the exchange rate is in it, and the Montparnasse geography is so exact that a reader can walk it. Fitzgerald wrote The Great Gatsby in 1924 and it contains no Paris at all. One man wrote his milieu. The other wrote his country from inside his milieu and never mentioned the milieu.

That contrast is not a value judgment and it should not be turned into one. It is a difference in the relation between a writer and his surroundings. Hemingway's method requires proximity: the iceberg works because the visible eighth is reported with the accuracy of a man who was there last night. Fitzgerald's method requires distance: the lyricism works because the object is being remembered rather than observed, and remembered by a narrator who has already decided what it meant. Put crudely, Hemingway needed to be in the room and Fitzgerald needed to have left it. Paris gave Hemingway his subject and gave Fitzgerald the altitude to see a subject he had already left behind.

The two novels are compared in full elsewhere in the series, in [Gatsby against The Sun Also Rises](/2028/05/29/gatsby-vs-sun-also-rises/), and the stylistic head-to-head has its own article. What belongs here is the narrower observation that the same city produced two opposite kinds of book from two writers who were drinking together while they wrote them, which is the strongest possible evidence against any simple claim that a milieu determines a text. Milieus offer. Writers take what they can use. Hemingway used Paris as material. Fitzgerald used it as a vantage point, and in the year that mattered he did not even do that, because the book was done.

There is one shared inheritance, and it is real. Both novels are postwar books built on the recognition that the old vocabulary of honor and romance had been discredited, and both handle that recognition through characters who cannot say so directly. Jake Barnes cannot say what happened to him. Nick Carraway cannot say what he wants. The generational condition is genuinely shared, which is why the Lost Generation frame has purchase on both. But it reached the two books by different routes, and Gatsby's route ran through the war and the Middle West and Long Island, not through Montparnasse.

## Zelda, Hemingway, and the Third Party in the Friendship

No account of this relationship is honest without Zelda in it, because she is the reason the friendship could never become domestic and because she is the villain of Hemingway's version.

The dislike was immediate and mutual. Zelda thought Hemingway was a poseur, a man performing masculinity for an audience, and she said so. Hemingway thought Zelda was insane and jealous of her husband's work, and that she deliberately kept him drinking and unproductive because she could not bear him writing well. Each of these judgments contains something and neither is a fair description. Zelda's illness was real and it was catastrophic, and it consumed years of Fitzgerald's working life and most of his money. Hemingway's need to be the most authentic man in any room was also real, and Zelda saw it faster than most.

What matters analytically is that Hemingway's memoir makes Zelda the mechanism of Fitzgerald's destruction, and that this is too convenient to accept. It gives the story a villain who is not the drinking, not the money, not the magazine work, not the market that ignored Gatsby, and not Fitzgerald himself. It also lets Hemingway keep his admiration for the talent while condemning everything around it, which is exactly the position a rival would want to hold. The series takes up Zelda's actual relation to the novel separately, and the marriage's effect on the book is a Riviera-summer question rather than a Paris one, which is another instance of the same discipline: the material that shaped Gatsby was in place before the friendship existed.

The one thing the antagonism does explain is why the friendship stayed a professional and a drinking friendship and never became more. Two couples in a foreign city, in the same trade, at the same publisher, could have made a real circle. They did not, and the reason is that half the four could not be in a room together. Fitzgerald was therefore always alone with Hemingway, which is the condition under which rivalry does its worst work, because there is nobody present to make it ridiculous.

## The Paris Milieu Ledger

The findable artifact for this article is a ledger that separates the four things people routinely fuse. Each row names an element of the milieu, states what it demonstrably did, and states what it did not do. The right-hand column is the operative one, because the errors in this area are almost all errors of overclaim.

| Element of the milieu | What it demonstrably did for Fitzgerald | What it did not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The weak franc and cheap living | Made the 1924 to 1926 European years financially possible on magazine income, buying the time in which Gatsby was drafted and revised | Did not draw him to Paris specifically; the drafting happened on the Riviera and the revising in Italy |
| The expatriate colony (Stein, Beach, Pound, the cafés) | Gave him a professional peer group of serious readers and a sense of the modernist experiment underway around him | Did not shape Gatsby's prose or structure, which were fixed before he entered the colony in 1925 |
| The condition of exile itself | Supplied the retrospective, from-a-distance angle of vision that the novel's narrator uses on America | Did not supply the plot, the characters, or the Long Island material, all drawn from 1922 and 1923 in New York |
| The meeting with Hemingway (spring 1925) | Began the defining professional relationship of Fitzgerald's later life and produced his best documented act of literary criticism | Did not touch The Great Gatsby, which was published weeks earlier and never revised afterward |
| Fitzgerald's patronage of Hemingway | Placed Hemingway at Scribner's under Perkins and removed the opening of The Sun Also Rises | Did not earn reciprocal generosity; the debt was reframed as Hemingway's own decision |
| A Moveable Feast (written 1957 to 1960, published 1964) | Created the durable public image of Fitzgerald as a ruined natural talent | Did not record the friendship; it composed a retrospective verdict, unanswerable and shaped for effect |
| The Snows of Kilimanjaro (1936) | Made the antagonism public and fixed the awe-of-the-rich charge to Fitzgerald's name | Did not report a real conversation; the famous exchange it seeds is a documented misattribution |

Read the right-hand column top to bottom and the shape of the correct claim appears. Every genuine effect of the milieu is either financial, professional, or perspectival. Not one of them is textual. That distinction is the article's namable claim, and it deserves a name.

## The April 1925 Line: What the Milieu Can and Cannot Explain

Call it the April 1925 line. The Great Gatsby was published on April 10, 1925, and Fitzgerald met Hemingway after that date. Everything on the far side of the line belongs to the novel's making. Everything on the near side belongs to its author's life and reputation but cannot have touched the text. The line is not a technicality. It is the discipline that converts this context from an anecdote farm into a usable analytical instrument, and applying it kills roughly half of what gets written about Fitzgerald, Hemingway, and Paris.

What falls on the wrong side of the line, and therefore cannot be claimed? Any argument that Hemingway's compression taught Fitzgerald economy. Any argument that the modernist experiment in Paris pushed Gatsby toward its symbolist method. Any argument that Stein's theories about repetition reached the novel's cadences. Any argument that the cafés supplied the party scenes. Each of these gets asserted somewhere, and each is chronologically impossible. Gatsby was drafted in a French seaside town in 1924 by a man reading his own Conrad and arguing with his own editor, and it was fixed in Rome that winter. The Paris colony met the finished object.

What survives the line, then? Three things, and they are worth more than what they replace.

### The condition of exile and the novel's angle of sight

The first survivor is the deepest. Fitzgerald wrote The Great Gatsby about America while living outside America, and the novel is built on exactly that relation. Nick Carraway narrates the summer of 1922 from an unspecified later vantage, back in the Middle West, having withdrawn. The whole book is a man looking at a place he has left. That is the expatriate's structure of feeling rendered as narrative technique, and it does not require Hemingway or the Dingo Bar to be legible. It requires only the recognition that distance was the author's actual condition when the pages were made.

The evidence is in the diction. Nick's opening establishes the retrospective frame at once, in "In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice," where the tense arrangement puts the narrator somewhere else and later, looking back at a self he has outgrown. It is confirmed in the closing pages, where the narrator says "I see now that this has been a story of the West, after all," a sentence that reclassifies the entire novel from the position of someone who has gone home and can finally see the shape. And it is completed in the final vision, where the land itself appears as "a fresh, green breast of the new world," a continent perceived not by anyone standing on it but by an imagined sailor arriving, or by a writer remembering. America in this novel is never simply present. It is always being looked at across a gap.

That gap is what exile does to perception, and it is the one thing about Fitzgerald's European years that reaches into the text. A writer in Saint Paul writes about the Middle West. A writer in Saint-Raphaël writes about the Middle West as a lost thing. The novel's closing paragraph, with its "boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past," is not merely a statement about Gatsby's psychology. It is a formal description of what the whole book has been doing, and it was written by a man who had physically borne himself away and was looking back.

### The double vision of the outsider inside

The second survivor is more specific. Fitzgerald's position in the Paris colony reproduced, almost exactly, the position he had given his protagonist a year earlier. He was a man with money and success trying to be admitted by a group that regarded his kind of money and success as disqualifying. Montparnasse's serious young modernists looked at a Saturday Evening Post author living near the Étoile the way East Egg looked at West Egg. He was inside the room and outside the circle.

The novel had already named this condition in Nick's most famous self-description: "I was within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled by the inexhaustible variety of life." Nick says it in a New York apartment in Chapter Two, watching a party he is attending and observing. It describes Fitzgerald in Montparnasse in 1925 with a precision that is uncanny and, being chronologically backward, cannot be an influence. It is a coincidence, which makes it useful rather than suspicious. It suggests that the double vision was already Fitzgerald's, that he had it before Paris confirmed it, and that Paris is therefore evidence for a reading of the novel rather than a cause of it. Context can corroborate without causing. That distinction is where most biographical criticism goes wrong, and the series treats the method itself under the critical-lens block.

The same doubleness runs through the novel's treatment of class, which is why the milieu material connects to the theme work rather than sitting beside it. Nick's small rented house sits between mansions and gives him "the consoling proximity of millionaires," a phrase with a sneer inside the comfort. Gatsby's parties are described by a man who goes to them. The novel never resolves whether wealth is enchanting or repellent because its author never resolved it, and Paris put him in a room where he had to feel both again, on the other side of the equation, as the vulgar success among the pure.

### The professional world the novel had to survive

The third survivor is the least literary and the most practical. The friendship and the colony show a reader what the literary economy of the 1920s actually was: a small number of houses, a smaller number of editors who mattered, a network of little magazines that made reputations, and a set of personal relationships that decided which manuscripts got read. Fitzgerald's letter to Perkins about Hemingway is a piece of that machine working. So is Beach printing Ulysses. So is Perkins fighting for a novel that did not sell.

This matters for a reader of Gatsby because the novel's early failure was not a verdict on the book. It was an outcome of that machine, in which a novel too compressed and too bleak for the audience that had bought Fitzgerald's earlier work simply had no path to the readers who would eventually want it. Knowing how the machine worked makes the reception story legible instead of mysterious, and the [revival after the Second World War](/2027/03/22/gatsby-revival-after-wwii/) is not a miracle of taste but a change in the machine's distribution. Understanding the milieu is understanding the conditions of publication, and those conditions bear on the novel's life even when they do not bear on its sentences.

## The Passages the Milieu Illuminates

Context becomes analysis at the level of the sentence or not at all. Four passages carry the weight here, and each one gains something specific from the expatriate frame.

Take Nick's account of his own honesty first. "I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known" is placed at the end of Chapter Three, immediately after a sequence in which Nick has been evasive with Jordan, has enjoyed a party he claims to disdain, and has narrated a scene he has already told the reader he found repellent. The sentence is a trap and the reader is meant to catch it. Read through the expatriate frame, the trap acquires a second floor: this is the self-account of a man who has removed himself from a scene and is now describing it, and removal is exactly the posture that produces false confidence about one's own detachment. Every expatriate memoir of the period, Hemingway's very much included, performs this same move. The narrator withdraws, and the withdrawal is offered as a guarantee of clear sight. Nick's sentence is the novel diagnosing a habit that its author was about to spend two years living among.

Take the green light next. "A single green light, minute and far away" closes Chapter One with Gatsby reaching toward something across water. The image is about distance as the precondition of desire, and it is reinforced at the moment of contact, when Nick observes that "his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one." An object seen across water is enchanted. An object in the room is inventory. The novel's whole engine is the argument that proximity destroys, and a reader who knows the author spent the years around composition and publication looking at America across an ocean has a genuine reason to press on that structure. The green light article owns the symbol itself; the milieu adds the fact that its author had arranged his own life into the same geometry. The novel's full symbolic architecture is mapped in [The Great Gatsby: the complete analytical guide](/2022/01/03/great-gatsby-complete-analytical-guide/).

Take the passage about Gatsby's origins. "The truth was that Jay Gatsby of West Egg, Long Island, sprang from his Platonic conception of himself" is the novel's clearest statement about self-invention, and it stands over a man whose "parents were shiftless and unsuccessful farm people." The expatriate colony was full of Americans performing invented selves at a safe distance from the people who knew their fathers. That is what an ocean is for. The novel does not need this context to be understood, but it sharpens the reading, because it shows that Fitzgerald was writing about the mechanics of geographic self-reinvention immediately before enacting a mild version of it, and that his judgment on the practice was already merciless.

Take, finally, the last sentence. "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past" was written by a man in the process of leaving. Its verbs are all about being carried backward against intention. That is not a description of the American Dream in the abstract. It is a description of what happens to a person who tries to move forward geographically and finds the past traveling with him, which is the actual experience of every expatriate who ever went abroad to escape himself. The sentence works without this reading. It gets an extra floor with it.

Notice what has and has not been claimed in those four readings. In none of them does the milieu explain how the sentence was made. In each of them the milieu supplies a corroborating pressure that lets a reader argue for a reading with more confidence. That is the correct use, and it is available to anyone who applies the April 1925 line honestly. ## Myths and Oversimplifications to Avoid

Five errors dominate this territory. Each one is repeated in classrooms, in study guides, and in a good deal of popular biography, and each one is correctable from the record.

### The myth that Hemingway influenced Gatsby's prose

This is the April 1925 line again, and it is worth stating in its strongest form because the temptation is real. The Great Gatsby is a compressed novel. It runs to roughly fifty thousand words and does in that space what other novelists of the period needed three hundred pages for. Hemingway is the century's great theorist of compression, and his iceberg principle, articulated later in Death in the Afternoon, holds that what is left out strengthens what remains. The two things look related. They are not.

Gatsby's compression was achieved in 1924 and refined on galleys in early 1925 through Fitzgerald's work with Perkins, principally by moving Gatsby's biography out of a lump and distributing it through the book. That is a structural solution Fitzgerald arrived at by argument with his editor and by reading Conrad, whose framed retrospective narration is a demonstrable and acknowledged model. The Sun Also Rises did not exist. Death in the Afternoon was seven years away. The compression in Gatsby has a documented origin and it is not in Montparnasse.

The genuine comparison between the two writers' methods is a separate and legitimate subject, and it belongs to the article on [Fitzgerald and Hemingway as stylists](/2028/10/23/fitzgerald-vs-hemingway-style/), which handles the head-to-head properly. What that comparison cannot be is a causal story running from one man to the other in the direction people assume. If there is any documented directional influence between them, it runs the other way: Fitzgerald cut the front off Hemingway's first novel.

### The myth of the rich-are-different exchange

The most quoted exchange between the two men is almost certainly not an exchange. In the standard version, Fitzgerald says something reverent about the rich being different from you and me, and Hemingway flattens him with the reply that yes, they have more money. It is a perfect anecdote: it dramatizes Hemingway's toughness against Fitzgerald's snobbery in two lines, and it is repeated everywhere.

The record does not support it. Fitzgerald wrote a line about the very rich being different from you and me at the opening of his 1926 story The Rich Boy, and it is a narrator's line in a work of fiction, offered as the beginning of a critical analysis rather than as an expression of awe. Hemingway attached the retort to Fitzgerald's name in The Snows of Kilimanjaro in 1936, ten years later, in a passage that names him and pities him. Accounts from people around Perkins indicate that the actual squelching, if it happened at all, involved Hemingway and the critic Mary Colum, with Colum delivering the line at Hemingway's expense rather than the reverse. Hemingway then reassigned the exchange, cast himself in the winning role, and gave the losing role to a living friend in a national magazine.

So the anecdote is doing the opposite of what it appears to do. It does not show that Fitzgerald worshipped the rich. It shows that Hemingway was willing to construct a scene that had not occurred, put a friend in it, and print it. And the charge itself is refuted by the novel under discussion. A writer in awe of the rich does not write "They were careless people, Tom and Daisy," or arrange his entire plot so that the wealthy destroy people and retreat behind their money. Fitzgerald's relation to wealth was analytical and it was hostile. The line about the rich being different is the opening of an autopsy, not a hymn.

### The myth of A Moveable Feast as a record

The memoir is a beautiful book and a bad witness. Hemingway wrote it in the late 1950s, decades after the events, working partly from notebooks recovered from a Paris hotel, and he did not finish or authorize the version published in 1964. It is shaped, selective, and settling accounts on every page: with Stein, with Ford, with McAlmon, with his own first wife, and above all with Fitzgerald, who is given three chapters and no defense.

The methodological point matters more than any individual correction. When a text is the sole source for an anecdote, and the author of that text has an obvious interest in the anecdote's outcome, and the subject of the anecdote is dead, the anecdote is not evidence. It is testimony from a party to the dispute. That does not make it false. It makes it unverified, and unverified is where it should stay in any serious argument. The proper treatment of the memoir is as a primary source for Hemingway's view of Fitzgerald in 1958, which is a real and interesting thing to know about, and not as a primary source for Fitzgerald in 1925.

### The myth that it was rivalry, or that it was friendship

Both simplifications fail against the same evidence, and the failure is instructive. Read the relationship as pure rivalry and you cannot explain the October 1924 letter to Perkins, written before the men had met, on behalf of a writer who could only ever be competition. Read it as pure friendship and you cannot explain The Snows of Kilimanjaro, or the memoir, or the systematic refusal to credit the edit that made The Sun Also Rises publishable.

The accurate account is that the two things were the same thing. In a small profession, at the same publishing house, with the same editor, in the same generation, working the same national material, admiration and competition are not opposites and cannot be separated. Fitzgerald admired Hemingway's work enough to promote it and improve it, and that admiration was inseparable from the knowledge that Hemingway would be measured against him. Hemingway was drawn to Fitzgerald's gift and repelled by his need, and the repulsion grew as his own position improved. A friendship of rivals in a city of exiles is not a paradox to be resolved. It is the ordinary condition of writers who matter to one another.

### The myth that Paris equals the Lost Generation equals Gatsby

The last error is the flattening one, and it is the most common in student essays. Paris in the 1920s, the Lost Generation, and The Great Gatsby get treated as three names for the same thing, and the essay then moves freely between them as though any fact about one licenses a claim about another. It does not. The Lost Generation is a term Stein applied and Hemingway made famous as the epigraph to The Sun Also Rises, and it names a postwar structure of disillusion that the series treats fully in [the Lost Generation and The Great Gatsby](/2026/12/21/lost-generation-great-gatsby/). Gatsby participates in that structure and was not produced by the Paris colony that carried its name. The overlap is real and partial, and the essay writer's job is to say which part.

Here is the discipline. If a claim about Paris cannot be tied to a sentence in the novel or to a documented event before April 1925, it is not a claim about The Great Gatsby. It is a claim about F. Scott Fitzgerald, which is a different and legitimate subject, covered in the series' account of [F. Scott Fitzgerald's life for readers](/2026/11/09/f-scott-fitzgerald-biography/). Keeping the two separate is not pedantry. It is what makes either of them worth writing about.

## Using the Paris Milieu as Analysis Rather Than Padding in an Essay

Examiners and supervisors can smell context that has been bolted on. The tell is structural: a paragraph of background sits between two paragraphs of analysis and nothing in the analysis would change if the background were deleted. That paragraph is costing marks even when every fact in it is correct. Context earns its place only when the reading downstream of it is different from the reading that would have happened without it.

The Paris material is unusually easy to bolt on and unusually rewarding when it is integrated, so it repays a method. The method has four steps and they are worth working through in order.

Begin with the sentence, never with the biography. Choose the passage you intend to argue about, do the close reading first, and reach a claim that stands on the text alone. If the claim collapses when the context is removed, the claim was never textual and the essay is in trouble. Nick's "within and without" holds up on its own: the sentence stages a contradiction the narrator does not notice, and that is an argument about the novel's method of characterization. It needs nothing from France.

Then ask what pressure the context puts on the claim. This is where the milieu does work. The expatriate condition does not prove that Nick's detachment is compromised; the sentence proves that. What the milieu supplies is a reason to think the compromise is deliberate and thematic rather than accidental, because it shows the author living inside the same posture and writing about it with a cold eye. That is corroboration, and corroboration is a real move in an argument. Write it as one: the reading holds, and the biographical record makes the reading more likely to be intended than incidental.

Third, use the April 1925 line as a filter on every context sentence you are about to write. Ask whether the fact you are about to deploy predates the finished book. If it does, it may bear on composition. If it does not, it can corroborate a reading of the finished text but cannot explain how the text got that way. This single check will remove most of the errors that cap grades in context-heavy essays. It also gives you something valuable to say, because an examiner who has read forty essays claiming Hemingway taught Fitzgerald economy will notice the one that explains why that is chronologically impossible.

Fourth, name the limit. The strongest context paragraph in a student essay usually ends by conceding what the context cannot do. "The expatriate frame corroborates the reading of Nick's detachment as compromised, though it cannot account for a technique Fitzgerald had already used in the Riviera drafts" is a better sentence than any confident overclaim, because it demonstrates control of the evidence. Markers reward that. The general skill of embedding context without padding is covered in the essay-strategy block, and the principle there is identical: the context must change the reading or it must go.

A worked example is worth more than the rules. Suppose the essay question asks how the novel presents the American Dream. A weak context paragraph says that Fitzgerald lived in Paris among the Lost Generation and was therefore disillusioned. Nothing follows from that. A strong one says that the novel's final vision of the continent arrives as "a fresh, green breast of the new world," a phrase that places America in the past and in the eye of an arriving stranger rather than in the present of anyone living there, and that this is the vision of a writer who had physically removed himself from the country and could only see it whole from outside; the Dream in this novel is therefore never available as a present possibility but only as a retrospective object, and that structural fact, not any character's failure, is the novel's argument. The context is load-bearing in the second version. Delete it and the sentence about retrospective objects loses its warrant.

One further caution for the essay writer. Do not put the anecdotes in. The Dingo Bar, the thermometer at Lyon, the measurement chapter, and the exchange about the rich are irresistible and they are all either unverified or misattributed, and an examiner who knows the field will read their presence as a signal that the candidate has been reading popular biography rather than the novel. If the friendship must appear, let it appear as the Perkins letter and the cut opening of The Sun Also Rises, which are documented, dull, and devastating.

## The Verdict

The friendship of rivals in a city of exiles is a real subject and it is not a subject about The Great Gatsby's composition. That is the finding, and everything in this article has been arranged to make it hold.

Fitzgerald and Hemingway met after the novel was published, and the relationship that followed was neither the tragic mentorship of the popular story nor the simple rivalry of the corrective version. It was both at once, and it ran the wrong way from the direction everyone assumes: the established writer promoted the unknown one to his own editor before they had met, then cut the front off his breakthrough novel and made it work, then watched him take the decade, then read himself pitied by name in a national magazine, then died at forty-four and was buried a second time by a memoir he could not answer. Hemingway won the argument because he outlived it. That is the whole mechanism of literary reputation compressed into one relationship, and it is worth knowing for its own sake.

What the Paris colony gave Fitzgerald was money that stretched, a peer group that took prose seriously, and a position that reproduced his own protagonist's: inside the room, outside the circle, holding the wrong kind of success. What it did not give him was The Great Gatsby, which he had already written, in a seaside town in 1924, arguing by mail with a man in New York.

The gain from getting this right is not merely accuracy. It is a working model of what context does. The milieu supplies the condition of the writing and the corroboration of the reading, and it never supplies the reading itself. Apply the April 1925 line, keep the anecdotes out, use the documented acts instead, and the Paris years stop being a picturesque backdrop and start being evidence. A reader who can do that with Fitzgerald in Montparnasse can do it with any writer anywhere, which is finally why the case is worth the trouble. The novel was made by a man alone with a manuscript. The city only told him what it had cost.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do Fitzgerald, Hemingway, and Paris relate to The Great Gatsby?

They relate as condition and aftermath, not as cause. Fitzgerald drafted the novel on the French Riviera in 1924 and revised it in Italy that winter, so the Paris expatriate scene and the Hemingway friendship both arrived after the text was fixed. The novel was published on April 10, 1925, and the two men met in Montparnasse in the weeks that followed. What the milieu genuinely supplies is the condition Fitzgerald was writing from, which was voluntary exile, an American describing America from outside it among other Americans who had also left. That condition is legible in the novel's retrospective narration and in its final vision of the continent seen whole from a distance. The friendship, by contrast, belongs to the author's later life and reputation rather than to the book, and treating it as an influence on the prose is a chronological impossibility that a great deal of popular writing repeats anyway.

### Q: What was the Fitzgerald-Hemingway relationship?

It was a friendship of rivals that ran from the spring of 1925 to Fitzgerald's death in 1940 and then continued, one-sidedly, in Hemingway's posthumous memoir. It has four phases. Fitzgerald acted as patron first, recommending Hemingway to his own editor Maxwell Perkins in October 1924, before the men had met. He then acted as critic, reading the manuscript of The Sun Also Rises in 1926 and telling Hemingway to cut its expository opening, which Hemingway did. The trajectories then crossed: Hemingway's career took the decade while Fitzgerald's stalled. Finally came the verdict, delivered in The Snows of Kilimanjaro in 1936 and completed in A Moveable Feast, published in 1964, which fixed Fitzgerald in public memory as a ruined natural talent. The relationship was never purely warm and never purely hostile. The competition lived inside the friendship rather than replacing it.

### Q: What was the 1920s Paris expatriate scene?

It was a small colony of mainly American and British writers and artists concentrated in Montparnasse and the Latin Quarter through the 1920s, held together by a weak franc, a permissive cultural climate, and an infrastructure of bookshops, salons, small presses, and little magazines. Sylvia Beach's Shakespeare and Company acted as bookshop, lending library, post office, and publisher. Gertrude Stein kept a salon where a young writer could be told what was wrong with his sentences. Ezra Pound worked as agent and accelerant, Ford Madox Ford edited the transatlantic review, and Robert McAlmon's Contact Editions printed what nobody else would. The cafés were the delivery mechanism, where manuscripts changed hands and reputations were made. The colony was also factional and frequently unpleasant, and it ran on feuds as much as fellowship. The warmth in the retrospective accounts is largely the warmth of the survivors.

### Q: How did the writers influence each other?

The documented influence runs from Fitzgerald to Hemingway, which reverses the direction most readers assume. Fitzgerald recommended Hemingway to Perkins at Scribner's before meeting him, helping place him at the house and with the editor who would shape his career. He then read The Sun Also Rises in manuscript and objected to its opening chapter and a half of expository backstory about Brett Ashley and Mike Campbell, calling it condescending. Hemingway cut it, and the novel as published begins with the flat sentence about Robert Cohn. That is the single most consequential act of criticism between them and it went one way. The reverse claim, that Hemingway taught Fitzgerald compression, is chronologically impossible for The Great Gatsby, which was complete before the two met. Any influence Hemingway had on Fitzgerald's later work is a matter of pressure and example rather than of documented intervention.

### Q: How did the Paris scene shape Fitzgerald's work?

It shaped his conditions and corroborated his perspective without shaping his best novel. The weak franc made the European years affordable on magazine income, which bought the time in which he drafted and revised The Great Gatsby, though that work happened on the Riviera and in Italy rather than in Paris. The colony gave him a peer group of serious readers at a moment when his own book was failing commercially. Most usefully for a reader, it placed him in a position that reproduced his own protagonist's: a man with the wrong kind of success trying to be admitted by people who regarded that success as disqualifying. That is corroboration for the novel's double vision, not a source for it. His later work, and the long stall between Gatsby in 1925 and Tender Is the Night in 1934, owe more to Zelda's illness, the drinking, and the money than to Montparnasse.

### Q: Was the relationship rivalry or friendship?

It was structurally both, and the insistence on choosing is what produces bad accounts of it. The pure-rivalry reading cannot explain the October 1924 letter to Perkins, written before the men had met, promoting a writer who could only ever become competition. The pure-friendship reading cannot explain The Snows of Kilimanjaro, in which Hemingway named a living friend and pitied him in a national magazine, or the memoir that refused Fitzgerald credit for the edit that made The Sun Also Rises publishable. In a small profession, at the same publishing house, with the same editor, in the same generation, working the same national material, admiration and competition are not opposites. Fitzgerald admired the work enough to promote and improve it, knowing he would be measured against it. Hemingway was drawn to the gift and repelled by the need, and the repulsion grew as his own position improved.

### Q: When and where did Fitzgerald first meet Hemingway?

In Paris in the spring of 1925, most probably late April or early May, at the Dingo Bar on the rue Delambre in Montparnasse. The date is the important part, because The Great Gatsby had been published on April 10 of that year, which places the meeting after the novel was printed, bound, and reviewed. Fitzgerald was twenty-eight and famous. Hemingway was twenty-five and known only to the small circle that had read his sketches in the little magazines. The detailed account of the evening comes from Hemingway's A Moveable Feast, written more than thirty years later and published posthumously, and it should be handled as testimony from an interested party rather than as a record. The frame is reliable. The dialogue, the color changes, and the intrusive questions are Hemingway's construction and cannot be checked.

### Q: Did Hemingway help Fitzgerald get published?

No, and the question has the arrow backward, which is exactly why it is worth asking. Fitzgerald was already an established Scribner's author with two novels and a substantial magazine income when the two men met in 1925. It was Fitzgerald who helped Hemingway, writing to Maxwell Perkins from Italy in October 1924 to recommend an unknown young American in Paris on the strength of a few pieces in little magazines, months before they had met. When Hemingway broke with his first publisher over The Torrents of Spring in 1926, Scribner's was ready for him because Fitzgerald had prepared the ground. The Sun Also Rises appeared under that imprint in October 1926, edited by Perkins, in a line of succession that runs directly through Fitzgerald. Hemingway never returned the favor in any comparable form.

### Q: Why did Hemingway mock Fitzgerald in A Moveable Feast?

The honest answer combines several motives and no single one accounts for it. Hemingway wrote the memoir in the late 1950s, when his own health and work were failing and his appetite for settling scores was at its sharpest, and Fitzgerald is not the only casualty in the book: Stein, Ford, McAlmon, and others receive similar treatment. Fitzgerald had been dead for nearly two decades and could not answer. There was also an unpaid debt in the background, since Fitzgerald had promoted Hemingway to Perkins and had cut the opening of The Sun Also Rises, and the memoir declines to credit either. A man does not rewrite a debt he does not feel. The result is a portrait of a gifted child who ruined himself, delivered with enormous technical skill, unanswerable, and repeated ever since as though it were a coroner's report.

### Q: What did Gertrude Stein contribute to the expatriate circle?

Stein supplied the salon, the authority, and, almost accidentally, the vocabulary. Her apartment on the rue de Fleurus functioned as a gathering point where the paintings on the walls were as much the argument as the conversation, and where a young writer could be told at length what was wrong with his sentences. Her effect on Hemingway was real and formative: she read his early work and pushed him away from journalistic habits. Her effect on Fitzgerald was negligible, because he arrived in 1925 as a finished writer with a finished book rather than as an apprentice looking for a method. Her most durable contribution was a remark rather than a theory. Her comment about a lost generation, taken up by Hemingway as the epigraph to The Sun Also Rises, gave the decade the label that has organized every discussion of it since, which shows how casually the terms of literary history get made.

### Q: Why did American writers move to Paris in the 1920s?

Money first, then permission. The French franc collapsed against the dollar after the First World War and stayed weak through the middle of the decade, so an American with a modest dollar income could live in Paris at a standard the same income would not buy in New York, and could do it while producing work that paid irregularly or not at all. That exchange rate is the material precondition for the entire phenomenon. Prohibition was a genuine push factor, though the deeper permission was formal rather than alcoholic. Paris was where a writer could attempt a book without a plot or without punctuation and find a press, a bookshop, and a magazine willing to carry it. Sylvia Beach publishing Ulysses in 1922, when no British or American house would risk it, established the proposition the colony ran on. Modernism did not need Paris, but Paris made attempting it cheap.

### Q: Did Hemingway have any effect on the text of The Great Gatsby?

None whatever, and the impossibility is worth stating precisely because the claim is so widely repeated. The novel was drafted at Saint-Raphaël in the summer of 1924, revised in Rome and Capri that winter, worked over on galleys with Maxwell Perkins by transatlantic mail, and published on April 10, 1925. Fitzgerald met Hemingway after that date and never revised the book afterward. The compression readers notice, and then attribute to Hemingway's example, has a documented origin: it came from Fitzgerald's structural work with Perkins, principally the redistribution of Gatsby's biography through the novel, and from Conrad's framed retrospective narration, which Fitzgerald acknowledged as a model. The Sun Also Rises did not yet exist. Death in the Afternoon, where the iceberg principle is stated, was seven years away. The resemblance is real and the causation is backward.

### Q: What did Hemingway think of Zelda Fitzgerald?

He thought she was insane, jealous of her husband's talent, and deliberately destructive of his work, and he said so in the memoir at length. The dislike was mutual and immediate. Zelda regarded Hemingway as a poseur performing masculinity for an audience, and she said that too. Neither judgment is a fair description and each contains something. Her illness was real and catastrophic and it consumed years of Fitzgerald's working life and most of his money. Hemingway's need to be the most authentic man in any room was also real, and she identified it faster than most people did. Analytically, the important point is that the memoir makes Zelda the mechanism of Fitzgerald's destruction, which is too convenient to accept, since it supplies a villain who is not the drinking, the money, the magazine work, or the market that ignored Gatsby.

### Q: Was Fitzgerald jealous of Hemingway's success?

The record shows something more complicated and more creditable than jealousy. Fitzgerald promoted Hemingway before they met, improved his breakthrough novel by telling him to cut its opening, and continued to praise the work publicly and privately after Hemingway had overtaken him. That is not the behavior of a jealous man, and any account that starts from jealousy has to explain it away. What the letters do show, especially through the 1930s, is wariness, deference, and occasional bitterness, which is not the same thing. He knew what had happened to their relative positions and he knew it was partly his own doing. The wound was less about Hemingway's success than about his own stall: nine years between Gatsby and Tender Is the Night, a book that failed on arrival, and a growing sense that he had spent his gift on magazine stories to pay for a catastrophe he could not fix.

### Q: What role did Sylvia Beach and Shakespeare and Company play?

Beach ran the colony's central institution. Her bookshop on the Left Bank worked simultaneously as a lending library for people who could not buy books, a post office and bank for writers with no fixed address, a clubhouse where introductions happened, and, when necessary, a publisher. The decisive act was publishing James Joyce's Ulysses in 1922, when no house in Britain or America would touch it. That single decision established the proposition the whole scene depended on: that the difficult and unprofitable book could exist because there was somewhere for it to go. Hemingway borrowed from her shelves in the years when he could not afford to buy. Fitzgerald knew her and used the shop, but by the time he arrived in 1925 he had a Scribner's contract and a Saturday Evening Post income and needed nothing she provided. The asymmetry is characteristic of his whole relation to the colony.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald actually live in Paris while working on the novel?

No, and the distinction between living in Paris and working on the novel is the whole point. The Fitzgeralds sailed for France in the spring of 1924, passed through Paris, and settled at Saint-Raphaël on the Riviera, where the bulk of The Great Gatsby was drafted that summer. They wintered in Rome and Capri, where the revision was done in correspondence with Maxwell Perkins in New York. Only in the spring of 1925, after publication, did they take an apartment in Paris, on the rue de Tilsitt near the Étoile, and they lived there through 1926. The address matters as evidence: it is on the Right Bank, in an expensive and respectable quarter, nowhere near the Montparnasse where the legend places him. He visited the expatriate scene. He was never of it, and he never wrote his best book there.

### Q: How did the expatriate exchange rate make Paris affordable?

The franc's collapse against the dollar after the First World War meant that American money bought several times more in France than at home, and it stayed that way through the middle of the decade. Practically, that converted a modest and irregular dollar income into a comfortable French one. A young writer could rent a room, eat, drink, and buy a year or two of unproductive time in which to learn the trade, which is a purchase that was simply unavailable in New York on the same money. Hemingway lived on his wife's small trust income and newspaper work and still found the city cheap. Fitzgerald, on a much larger income, went partly as a budget measure, because being famous in New York was costing him more than he earned. Remove the exchange rate and the colony does not form. The romance of the scene is downstream of an accounting fact.

### Q: What can students safely claim about the Hemingway connection in an essay?

Claim the documented acts and nothing else. It is safe to say that Fitzgerald recommended Hemingway to Perkins in October 1924, before they had met, and that in 1926 he told Hemingway to cut the expository opening of The Sun Also Rises and Hemingway did. Both are supported by the correspondence and both are devastating to the usual story. It is safe to note that the two met after The Great Gatsby was published and that Hemingway therefore cannot have influenced it. What is not safe is the anecdote layer: the Dingo Bar evening, the thermometer at Lyon, the chapter about measurements, and the exchange about the rich are unverified, misattributed, or both, and their appearance in an essay signals popular biography rather than research. An examiner who knows the field will notice which layer a candidate is working from.

### Q: How reliable is A Moveable Feast as a record of the friendship?

As a record, poorly. As a source for Hemingway's late view of Fitzgerald, excellent, and that is how it should be used. The memoir was composed in the late 1950s, roughly three decades after the events, partly from notebooks recovered from a Paris hotel, and Hemingway neither finished nor authorized the text published in 1964. It is shaped, selective, and settling accounts with almost everyone in it. The methodological rule applies with unusual force: when a text is the sole source for an anecdote, its author has an obvious interest in the outcome, and the subject is dead and cannot answer, the anecdote is testimony rather than evidence. That does not make it false. It makes it unverified, and unverified is where it should stay in an argument. Read it as a primary source for 1958, not for 1925.

### Q: Did the friendship survive the 1930s?

It survived in a damaged form and never recovered. The letters continue for years and their tone moves from collegial to careful to wounded rather than stopping. Hemingway grew contemptuous of what he saw as Fitzgerald's self-indulgence and his willingness to sell his talent to the Saturday Evening Post. Fitzgerald grew wary and occasionally bitter. The public rupture came in 1936, when Fitzgerald published The Crack-Up essays in Esquire and Hemingway regarded them as a writer whining in print instead of working. In the same year and the same magazine, Hemingway published The Snows of Kilimanjaro with a passage naming Fitzgerald and pitying his romantic awe of the rich. Fitzgerald wrote and asked him to remove the name, and in book publication it was changed to Julian. Fitzgerald died in December 1940 at forty-four. Hemingway lived another twenty years and wrote the memoir.
