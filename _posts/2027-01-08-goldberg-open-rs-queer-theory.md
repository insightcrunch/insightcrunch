## The Verdict in Brief: A Landmark of the Question, Not of the Proof

Jonathan Goldberg's "Romeo and Juliet's Open Rs," published in *Queering the Renaissance* (Duke University Press, 1994), the collection Goldberg himself edited, is the most consequential piece of queer criticism ever written on the play, and its consequence survives the damage its evidence has taken. The verdict defended across this article is double. As diagnosis, the essay is permanent: Goldberg demonstrated that the play's reign as the culture's icon of one unique heterosexual love is an achievement of reading, not a property of the text, and that desire inside the play runs in substitutions that no doctrine of the couple can contain. As demonstration, the essay is vulnerable at exactly the points where it performs hardest: a word no early edition prints, a sentence the Nurse never finishes, a rose made to carry a predecessor's name. Joseph Pequigney, the most damaging of its critics, wins most of the skirmishes over the letter R. Goldberg wins the war over what the play's desire is doing. An honest account of the landmark has to hold both halves at once, because the essay's own method, a philology that treats letters as evidence, invites judgment by the standard it sets for itself.

![Jonathan Goldberg and the letter R in Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-101.webp)

The essay's materials are small and famous. A joke in Act 2, Scene 4, in which the Nurse fails to finish a sentence about the letter R. Six lines in Act 2, Scene 1, in which Mercutio wishes Rosaline were a fruit whose dialect name no early text of the play actually prints. A rose in the orchard scene of Act 2, Scene 2. A sun in the same scene. An adjective, "effeminate," in Act 3, Scene 1. From these Goldberg builds the claim that "the sexual field in which desire operates is the forbidden desire named sodomy" (p. 228), and that "the coupling of Romeo and Juliet is not a unique moment of heterosexual perfection and privacy but part of a series whose substitutions do not respect either the uniqueness of individuals or the boundaries of gender difference" (p. 222). Every quotation from the play in this article follows the Folger Shakespeare, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, cited by act, scene, and line; every quotation from Goldberg carries the page of *Queering the Renaissance* where a reader can test it. The essay is paraphrased rather than quoted at length, as its argument deserves and its difficulty requires.

### What kind of evidence is a letter?

A letter is evidence of the weakest kind when it stands alone and of a peculiar kind when it clusters: it cannot prove an intention, but it can register the pressure a text exerts on its own vocabulary, the charge a word or a name accumulates by recurrence. Goldberg's wager is that the cluster matters more than the case.

Where a play keeps returning to the same letter at the same charged moments, the recurrence is a fact about the text even if no single instance compels his gloss. That wager is the essay's method in miniature, and it is why the argument can be damaged at every point and still stand as a landmark: the points were never the whole of it.

## The Icon Under Attack: Idealization as Ideology

Goldberg begins not with the play but with its reputation, and the order is the argument. *Romeo and Juliet* functions in the wider culture as the standing proof that heterosexual love is natural, unique, and self-evidently good: two young people, one love, a feud too stupid to survive it. Goldberg's opening move is to treat that reputation as a reading with interests. Idealizing accounts of the couple, he writes, provide the bedrock of the heterosexual order (p. 219), a sentence whose geology is exact: the idealized couple is not one interpretation among many but the foundation on which a whole arrangement of sex and gender rests its claim to be nature rather than custom. To read the play as the monument of the couple is therefore, in his account, to perform political work while believing one is merely admiring a poem.

The force of the opening is easy to underestimate because its target has since become familiar. In 1994 the play's classroom identity was still overwhelmingly the monument: the lovers as the type of pure young love, the bawdy quarantined as comic relief, Mercutio's filth treated as local colour, Rosaline forgotten as a false start on the road to the true beloved. Goldberg's counter-claim is that each of those pieties requires suppression. The bawdy is not decoration around the love plot; it is one of the places the love plot is thought. Rosaline is not discarded; she is replaced, and the replacement follows a pattern the play repeats at every level. The couple is not private; their desire is routed through go-betweens, overheard by friends, joked about by servants, and finally displayed as corpses and statues for the city's use. The essay's first service is simply to make the idealization visible as an interpretation, with a history and a cost, rather than as the play's transparent meaning.

That service has been absorbed so completely that its origin is now easy to miss. When later criticism of the play, including criticism with quite different agendas, treats the golden statues of the ending as the city's appropriation of the lovers rather than as their apotheosis, it is walking through a door Goldberg opened: the insistence that what a culture does with this play is evidence about the culture, and that the text supplies materials the monument cannot use. The idealization thesis is also the part of the essay its fiercest critic never really engages. Pequigney's prosecution is aimed at the readings of passages, and on passages he often scores; the claim that the couple's icon status does ideological work is left standing, because it does not depend on any letter, any pun, or any single line. It depends on the observable fact of the play's reception, which no collation can overturn.

There is a second, quieter point in the opening. Goldberg does not argue that the play is secretly a homosexual love story, that Romeo desires Mercutio instead of Juliet, or that any character carries a sexual identity a modern reader would recognize. The refusal is categorical and repeated: "hetero- and homosexuality are profound misnomers for the organization of sex in Shakespeare's time" (p. 227). The essay's claim concerns the organization of desire in a text, the routes it travels and the substitutions it performs, not the orientation of persons. Readers who meet the essay expecting an outing will misread it, and much of the polemic around it, friendly and hostile, has proceeded from exactly that misreading. What Goldberg queers is not Romeo. It is the couple form itself, the assumption that desire in the play gathers into one sanctioned pair and stays there.

## The Volume and the Moment: Queering the Renaissance in 1994

The essay appeared as the twelfth piece in a collection of sixteen, and its neighbours explain its method. *Queering the Renaissance* was a volume of the Series Q project at Duke University Press, gathering critics working from gay and lesbian studies to redraw the map of Renaissance sexuality. Janet Halley's opening essay reads the Renaissance precedents inside the United States Supreme Court's 1986 decision in *Bowers v. Hardwick*, which had upheld the criminalization of sodomy; the volume's present tense was juridical before it was literary. Alan Bray's "Homosexuality and the Signs of Male Friendship in Elizabethan England" supplied the historical ground Goldberg builds on: in the period, intense male friendship was publicly honoured, kissed, bed-shared, and mourned, while "sodomy" named a monstrous, confusedly imagined crime attached to heresy, treason, and foreignness, a charge so incoherent that men could practice what the law condemned without recognizing themselves in the condemnation. Valerie Traub's essay on the (in)significance of lesbian desire and Carla Freccero's "Practicing Queer Philology with Marguerite de Navarre" complete the local context: Freccero's title gave the volume, and the decade, a method name. Queer philology is attention to the letter, to etymology, pun, and spelling, practised against the grain of what words are supposed to mean. Goldberg's essay is that method turned on the most famous love story in English.

The volume's order is itself an argument about where the play essay belongs. Halley's opening piece examines the uses of Renaissance precedents inside *Bowers v. Hardwick*, the 1986 decision in which the United States Supreme Court upheld a state sodomy statute against a privacy challenge, treating the history of the prohibition as a reason in law. A collection that begins in a modern courtroom announces that the categories governing desire are juridical before they are literary, that what a culture calls sodomy is decided in statutes and judgments and only afterward in poems. Goldberg's essay, arriving twelfth, inherits the announcement: when it names sodomy as the play's sexual field, it is borrowing the law's word with the law's incoherence attached, and its claim that the lovers' lawful marriage leaves their desire in the forbidden territory is the literary form of the volume's legal premise, that the official map and the lived territory are different countries.

Behind the volume stands Goldberg's own *Sodometries*: Renaissance Texts, Modern Sexualities* (Stanford University Press, 1992), whose introduction bears the title "That Utterly Confused Category," Michel Foucault's description of sodomy in the first volume of *The History of Sexuality*. Foucault's famous distinction, between sodomy as a category of forbidden acts whose perpetrator was merely a juridical subject and the nineteenth-century homosexual as a species of person, is the essay's licence and its limit. Goldberg radicalized the point in *Sodometries*, where sodomy, in Pequigney's quotation of the claim, "functions neither solely to designate sex between men, nor is it only (perhaps not even primarily) a sexual term" (p. 70). The *Open Rs* essay applies that expansion to a single play: if sodomy in the period named not an identity but a field of forbidden, disorderly, non-procreative desire, then a play's desires can be mapped onto that field without any character being assigned an orientation.

The historical stakes of the category deserve one plain paragraph, because the essay assumes them. Sodomy entered theology as a sin against nature, codified by Peter Damian in the eleventh century and taxonomized by Thomas Aquinas among the vices that preclude generation; in England the Buggery Act of 1533 made it a capital felony, and Coke would call it a sin "amongst Christians not to be named." Yet, as Bray showed, prosecutions were rare and the culture's imagination of the sodomite was of a monster at the margins, a figure of religious and political disorder, not of the school friend or the bedfellow. A term at once capital and unrecognizable is a strange instrument, and Goldberg's essay is an attempt to hear what a play does inside such a culture's categories rather than inside modern ones. Whether the instrument can bear the weight he loads on it is the question the prosecution later presses, and it is a fair question. What cannot be denied is that the question itself, asked in this form, reorganized the study of sexuality in the play: after 1994, the bawdy, the friendships, the names, and the substitutions were evidence in one inquiry, where before they had been four separate footnotes.


## The Critic's Instruments: A Spenserian Among the Letters

Goldberg came to the play from Spenser, and the formation shows. His first books, *Endlesse Worke* (1981) and *Voice Terminal Echo* (1986), are studies of *The Faerie Queene* and of the poststructuralist Spenser, works preoccupied with allegory's letters, with echo, repetition, and the way a text's smallest units refuse to stay beneath its meanings. A critic trained on Spenser's letter-play, on names that anagrammatize and episodes that repeat with a difference, brings a distinctive sensorium to Shakespeare: he hears the recurrence before he parses the argument, and he trusts the recurrence as data. The *Open Rs* essay is unthinkable from a critic formed by character criticism or by the history of ideas. Its basic unit is not the person, the theme, or the image cluster. It is the grapheme, the letter as it travels through names, jokes, and suspended sentences, accreting charge.

The formation is also the liability, and naming it clarifies the quarrel with Pequigney into a quarrel between two philologies. Pequigney's philology is lexical and judicial: a word means what its contexts, its dictionary, and its period attest, and a reading is a claim that can be convicted of error. Goldberg's philology is distributive and poetic: a letter or syllable means, in part, whatever the text's pattern of recurrence lets it mean, and a reading is a performance that organizes attention. The first philology asks of the R passage: what did the Nurse not say, and may a critic say it for her? The second asks: why does the play keep staging this letter at its erotic hinges, and what does the staging let a hearer feel? Both questions are legitimate objects of scholarship. They become enemies only when either claims the other's jurisdiction: when the performance presents its organizations as demonstrations, or when the tribunal denies that availability, recurrence, and half-heard punning are constituents of a play's meaning in the theatre. The adjudication offered in this article assigns each philology its province and judges the essay to have trespassed, repeatedly and sometimes gloriously, from the second province into the first. Trespass explains both its landmark status and its rap sheet: the readings that fail judicial review are the same performances that reorganized what audiences of scholars notice. A purely judicial criticism of this play existed for centuries and noticed, in the end, very little of its bawdy, its substitutions, or its letters. It took a trespasser to put them on the record.

## A Play That Teaches Double Hearing

Goldberg's method has one warrant stronger than any theory, and it is the play's own first scene. *Romeo and Juliet* opens with two servants turning every noun into a sexual instrument: Sampson will "stand" or fall, Gregory plays on maidenheads and the tool and the weapon, and the audience learns in forty lines that in this text the second sense is always loaded. The training continues at every level of the society. Mercutio informs Romeo that "the bawdy hand of the dial is now upon the prick of noon" (2.4), a sentence whose timekeeping is inseparable from its anatomy; the wit-combat that follows runs on pumps, pinks, and cases, words whose respectable senses are the cover for their others. The Nurse's whole idiolect is a double register, her fondest memory a joke about a toddler who will fall backward when she has more wit. Even the Friar's botany, the play's most respectable discourse, speaks of the earth as womb and tomb and of plants whose virtues invert into poisons, a natural philosophy of doubleness. A first audience did not need a seminar to hear this play twice. It needed only to keep up.

This matters to the adjudication because it relocates the burden of proof. Pequigney's tribunal treats each of Goldberg's hearings as an isolated conjecture requiring isolated proof, and in isolation several fail. But the play demonstrably operates a general economy of the pun, in which sexual second senses are not ornaments but the vernacular of Verona, spoken by servants, wits, nurses, and, in their figures, lovers. Within such an economy, the question about any given instance is not whether double hearing is permitted; the play permits nothing else. The question is whether the critic's particular hearing is among the senses the economy makes available, and that is a question of attestation and pattern, the medlar's dialect name on one side, the critic's imported vocabulary on the other. Where the attestation is period and the pattern is the text's, Goldberg's hearings stand in the same relation to the play as any gloss in an edition's notes, conjectural in the way of all glossing and answerable to the same tests. Where the attestation is the critic's century and the pattern is the critic's desire, the hearing is a performance about the critic. The essay contains both kinds, sometimes within a page, and the failure of its hostile and friendly readers alike has been to grade it wholesale. The play's own training of its audience is the reason wholesale rejection is as unsustainable as wholesale assent: a text that teaches double hearing cannot complain when a reader, however unruly, hears double in its letters too.

There is a refinement worth making, because it distinguishes the essay from mere bawd-hunting. Most double senses in the play are local: a word flashes its second meaning and the scene moves on. Goldberg's claim is that in this play the flashes accumulate around a single complex, opening, substitution, and non-generative desire, until the local puns compose a climate. The claim is about weather, not about any one cloud. Criticism that refutes clouds has its uses, and Pequigney's are real; but weather is refuted only by a different climate-reading, and none of the essay's opponents has produced one that accounts for the same range: the servants' opening, the conjuration, the medlar, the letter joke, the sun and moon, the sheath and the vault. The monument's climate, pure love in a coarse world, is precisely such a rival reading, and its inadequacy to the range is the vacancy the essay filled. That is why the essay survives its refutations. It is not the best-attested reading of the play's desire. It is the only comprehensive one that treats the play's dirt as thought.

## The Dog's Letter: The Joke in Act 2, Scene 4

The essay's title rests first on a piece of clowning so small that most readings step over it. In Act 2, Scene 4, the Nurse, prattling before she delivers her message, reports a saying of Juliet's and checks it with Romeo: "Doth not rosemary and Romeo begin both with a letter?" Romeo answers, "Ay, Nurse, what of that? Both with an R." The Nurse, suspecting mockery, replies: "Ah, mocker! That's the dog's name. R is for the" and breaks off: "No, I know it begins with some other letter, and she hath the prettiest sententious of it, of you and rosemary, that it would do you good to hear it" (2.4). The exchange is a malapropism machine: the Nurse confuses letters with names, "sententious" with sentences, and R, the grammarians' dog letter, with a dog's name. R had been the dog's letter since Persius called it the *littera canina*, because the trilled sound growls. The joke, at its surface, is that the unlettered Nurse has hold of a scholar's tag and drops it.

Goldberg stops at the dropped sentence. "R is for the" expects a completion, and the completion the scene's bawdy atmosphere supplies, the completion the growling letter itself all but pronounces, since a rolled R is the closest the alphabet comes to sounding the word, is "arse." On that suspended word the title turns: the play's Rs are open Rs, apertures, the letter of the body's opening, and they cluster exactly where desire is being transacted. Rosemary and Romeo share the letter at the moment the marriage is being arranged; the scene is the play's great market of substitutions, Mercutio performing his friendship in bawdy wordplay, the Nurse buying and selling access to Juliet, Romeo passing in an hour from the friend group to the wedding. The letter that begins the lover's name is made, by an illiterate go-between's unfinished joke, to name the orifice the culture's law called unnameable. That is the essay in embryo: the sign of idealized love and the sign of forbidden desire are the same sign, and the play itself, in a servant's mouth, nearly says so.

The cross-examination of this passage belongs to the prosecution section, but the textual facts can be settled here. The dash is the text: no early edition completes the Nurse's sentence, and no responsible editor has supplied a word. The Nurse may be trailing into nonsense rather than into obscenity; her error about the letter may be the whole joke. What Goldberg adds is an inference from sound and situation, and he presents it with a performative confidence that has always divided his readers. Sympathetic readers hear the inference as the recovery of a joke an audience of groundlings, quicker with bawdy than any seminar, would have caught in the air. Sceptical readers note that an unfinished sentence can be finished in many ways and that the critic has chosen the one his thesis needs. Both are right about the evidence. The disagreement is about what a pun is allowed to prove, and it recurs at every stage of the essay.

Note what the passage is asked to do and what it is not. The R exchange does not show any character desiring any other. It shows the play's language, at a hinge of the plot, letting the hero's name slip toward the body's forbidden opening in a joke about letters. Goldberg's claim is about the slipperiness of the sign, and the scene is perfectly chosen for that narrower claim: it is a scene about messages miscarrying, words mistaken, names confused, in which a wedding is arranged through an intermediary who cannot keep a letter straight. The essay's larger architecture, the substitutions and the sexual field, does not stand on this dash. But the title advertises that it does, and the advertisement has cost the argument dearly, because it lets a critic of Pequigney's skill treat the whole essay as a pun with footnotes. The sections that follow separate the load-bearing claims from the ornamental ones, which is the separation the essay's own rhetoric declines to make.

## The Medlar Speech: Mercutio's Wish in Act 2, Scene 1

The second hinge is the most famous bawdy in the play. Romeo has slipped away after the feast; Mercutio, not knowing of Juliet, calls him back with a conjuration in Rosaline's name, itemizing her body as a spell: "I conjure thee by Rosaline's bright eyes, / By her high forehead and her scarlet lip, / By her fine foot, straight leg and quivering thigh / And the demesnes that there adjacent lie" (2.1). Then, giving up, he imagines Romeo's night: "If love be blind, love cannot hit the mark. / Now will he sit under a medlar tree, / And wish his mistress were that kind of fruit / As maids call medlars when they laugh alone. / O Romeo, that she were, O that she were / An open-arse, thou a pop'rin pear!" (2.1.33 to 38). The medlar, a fruit eaten only when rotted soft and split, bore the dialect name "open-arse"; the poperin pear, from Poperinghe in Flanders, carried its shape and a pun on popping her in. Mercutio's farewell imagines the sexual act entirely in fruit, and imagines it as a wish: optative, unconsummated, spoken by one man about another man and an absent woman.

Goldberg's attention fastens on the geometry of the scene. Romeo is not present; he is being spoken, conjured, positioned by his friend's voice. Rosaline is not present; she exists as a catalogue of parts and then as a fruit. The desire on stage is a transaction between the speaker and the spoken of, conducted across a woman's imagined and opened body. This is the triangular structure that Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick's *Between Men* (1985) had taught the period's critics to see: male bonds routed through female objects, the woman as the conduit of a relation between men. Goldberg's version is textual rather than sociological. The conjuration raises Romeo, in Mercutio's fantasy, by means of Rosaline; the parts catalogued are a spell for summoning a man. When the wish turns to the medlar, the woman's body is opened by the friend's sentence so that the friend's sentence can be about Romeo. The scene's erotics are real, and none of them belongs to the couple: the couple, at this moment, does not know it exists.

The fence agreed for this article keeps Mercutio himself at the length the essay gives him, and the queer reading of the character, his love, his death, and his afterlife belongs to a companion study, [Was Mercutio in Love With Romeo? Queer Readings](/2022/09/23/mercutio-queer-reading-romeo/). What belongs here is narrower: the speech as the essay's evidence. And as evidence it is double-edged in a way Goldberg's rhetoric acknowledges only by outrunning it. The fantasy is Mercutio's joke, in the optative mood, about a chaste and refusing woman; nothing in it is ascribed by the speaker to Romeo's actual desires, and the woman imagined opened is the woman Romeo is about to forget. Pequigney presses exactly there: anal intercourse is adverted to once, in a joke, in a wish, about Rosaline, and never again attached to the protagonists. The observation is accurate and it does not dispose of the passage, because Goldberg's claim was never that the play depicts the act. His claim is that the play's bawdy imagination, at its most inventive, pictures desire as opening, substitution, and indirection, and that this imagination is the medium in which the celebrated love must also swim. The medlar speech proves that the medium exists. How far the medium determines the meaning of the love scenes is the genuine question, and it cannot be settled by counting the speech's appearances in the essay.

One more feature of the passage matters for what follows: its editorial history is a scandal in miniature, and Goldberg's title depends on a word that the play's early printers would not print. The next section takes that history on its own terms, because an argument about suppression ought to be tested against the one place suppression demonstrably happened.

## One Word, Three Centuries of Editors

The textual record, settled by collation, is this. The word "arse" does not stand in any early text of the play at this line. The first quarto of 1597 veils the fruit as an "open Et caetera," a compositor's or a company's euphemism, an early and literal act of censorship. The second quarto of 1599, the good text on which modern editions rest, prints "open, or," a garble generally understood as a misreading of "open ars" in the manuscript copy; the First Folio of 1623 repeats "open, or." There the matter slept. Editors of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries printed the euphemism or the garble, glossed the medlar as a fruit resembling what Eric Partridge, in *Shakespeare's Bawdy*, finally said outright, and left the joke half-told. Not until Richard Hosley's edition of 1957 was the reading "open-arse" restored to the text as what Stanley Wells calls the true reading, on the convergent evidence of the dialect name, the metre, the poperin pear's answering shape, and the joke's structure. Partridge's own conversion survives as the emblem of the episode: on a second reading, prompted by reproaches, he concluded that the pun is "so forcibly obvious" that the et cetera must mean the arse. Modern scholarly editions, the Arden under Brian Gibbons and then René Weis, the Oxford under Jill L. Levenson, the Folger of Mowat and Werstine, print "open-arse" and gloss it, some of them noting plainly that the fruit's name was slang for the body's opening and that Mercutio's wish is anal in its geometry.

Adjudicated as an editorial question, the restoration is sound, and the soundness matters to the larger quarrel. Hosley was not inventing a bawdy reading; he was repairing a transmission that had demonstrably been bowdlerized at its first step, in 1597, before the good quarto's compositor garbled what remained. Pequigney, no friend of the essay, concedes the emendation is convincing. An editor's duty to the copy-text, to the metre, and to the dialect record all point one way. The word Goldberg builds on is an editorial restoration, but it is a restoration the way a scraped palimpsest's underwriting is a restoration: inferred, argued, and as certain as such things get.

Adjudicated as evidence for the essay, the history cuts both ways, and the honest verdict gives each side its due. For Goldberg: the tradition's behaviour around this word is his opening thesis in miniature. For three and a half centuries the play's editors protected its readers from its own bawdiest syllable, and the protection was invisible, exactly the mechanism by which an idealized text is manufactured out of a dirtier one. The et cetera of 1597 is the heterosexual order's bedrock being laid in real time. For the prosecution: an argument whose title-word had to be restored by conjecture in 1957 is an argument about a text its first audiences may never have heard in that form, and the Folio audience read "open, or" and, presumably, puzzled or passed on. The inference Goldberg draws is from the play as edited in the late twentieth century, not from the play as printed in the sixteenth. The reply, and it is sufficient, is that editors restore what transmission damaged; the pun's presence in the copy behind the garble is the best explanation of the garble, and the medlar's dialect name is a fact of the language, not of the edition. The word was in the play before it was in the editions. What the editions did to it remains the cleanest evidence in the whole dispute that the play's sexual frankness has been managed, tidied, and hidden by the very tradition that celebrates its love.

### Did any early edition print the word the title leans on?

No early edition prints it. The 1597 quarto substitutes an "open Et caetera," the 1599 quarto and the 1623 Folio print the garble "open, or," and the word "open-arse" enters the text only as an editorial restoration, generally credited to Richard Hosley's edition of 1957 and accepted since on the evidence of the medlar's dialect name and the metre.

The answering pun on the poperin pear completes the case. The restoration is convincing even to the essay's fiercest critic; the dependence remains a fact a careful reader of Goldberg should know, because it shows the essay arguing from the play as modern editing recovered it, not from any page an Elizabethan reader held.

## The Plot of Replacement: Substitutions Without Respect

Strip the letters away and the essay's central claim is about the plot, stated in its most quoted sentence: "the coupling of Romeo and Juliet is not a unique moment of heterosexual perfection and privacy but part of a series whose substitutions do not respect either the uniqueness of individuals or the boundaries of gender difference" (p. 222). Goldberg calls the pattern a plot of replacement, and the play obliges at every joint. Romeo enters the tragedy in love, fluent, Petrarchan, sighing in oxymorons: "O brawling love, O loving hate, / O any thing of nothing first create!" (1.1). The object of that fully formed desire is Rosaline, who never appears. At the feast the desire transfers, whole and unaltered in its rhetoric, to Juliet: "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight! / For I ne'er saw true beauty till this night" (1.5). The couplet performs the substitution it announces; nothing in the lover changes but the name of the beloved. A companion study of the play's names traces how designations govern identity across the tragedy, [Names and Identity: Becoming and Unbecoming](/2024/04/12/names-and-identity-romeo-and-juliet/); Goldberg's point is the darker one underneath, that the desire precedes and survives its objects, and that the play knows it.

The essay's most virtuosic page turns on the orchard scene's rose. Juliet, reasoning that a name is a convention, argues: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2.43 to 44). Her target is "Montague," the surname that makes Romeo her enemy; her logic is that the thing exceeds its label. Goldberg hears the predecessor inside the flower: "rose" is the opening of "Rosaline," the earlier beloved's name with its syllables dropped, a misspelling that keeps the first beloved present inside the speech that is supposed to free the lover from names. Juliet becomes, in his phrase-structure, that rose, and thereby Rosaline renamed; and in the exchange of places the figure also locates Romeo in the rose's place, and thus in Rosaline's, the lover feminized into the beloved's position. The "infinite replaceability of the rose" becomes the essay's emblem for a desire whose objects are positions in a pattern, exchangeable without remainder.

Pequigney's correction of this page is the prosecution's cleanest hit, and it should be given in full. Juliet does not compare Romeo to a flower. She analogizes two pairs: as the word "rose" stands to the flower, so the name "Montague" stands to the man; the argument is about labels and things, and its grammar is precise. To load the rose with Rosaline requires the critic to hear a truncated name inside a common noun, an operation no more licensed here than it would be anywhere else in the language. The hit lands. And yet the larger claim survives its emblem, because the substitution series does not need the rose. Rosaline's structural function is documented by the plot itself: she is the unattainable, chaste, Dian-committed beloved of the Petrarchan script, and readers of the essay have long noted that she occupies, in the tragedy's design, the position the fair young man occupies in the Sonnets, the beautiful object of a one-sided, versified, unconsummable desire that exists to display the desiring subject. When Juliet replaces her, the script continues with a partner; the replacement is the event the tradition reads as destiny and Goldberg reads as mechanism. His further flourish, that energies flow through the figure of Rosaline out of this play into the sonnets and into the Rosalines and Rosalinds of other plays, is the least disciplined part of the essay, a claim about literary history with no stated test. The series inside the play is enough. It shows a desire that behaves like a form looking for occupants, and a criticism committed to the uniqueness of the couple has to explain away the play's own first act, in which the unique beloved is the second one.

The replacements do not stop at the beloved. Romeo takes Juliet's name into his mouth and offers to be renamed, baptized, anything but Montague; Juliet does the allocating of times, places, and messengers, and Romeo receives them. In the tomb, places exchange again: Romeo finds Juliet laid in her ancestors' monument and speaks to her as though she were still the living partner, while Death, in his conceit, has taken her as a paramour, a rival male keeping her in the dark. The Prince's closing sentence distributes the lovers into the city's keeping, and the fathers convert them into statues, gold replacing flesh. A reading organized by substitution does not have to invent its pattern; it has to decline to forget it. That is the essay's strongest ground, and on this ground the prosecution's passage-by-passage method cannot follow, because the pattern is not a passage. It is the shape of the whole.

### Is Rosaline a character in the essay, or a function?

A function, and Goldberg is explicit in effect if not in term: Rosaline never appears, never speaks, and exists in the play only as the prior occupant of the beloved's place, the name inside the rose, the chaste refuser whose structural twin is the fair youth of the Sonnets.

Pequigney objects that the essay promotes a nonentity into a major role and a principal conductor of energies flowing between plays. The objection is fair as far as the intertextual currents go. Inside this play, though, the function is the fact: the plot itself treats Rosaline as a slot to be refilled, and the essay's reading merely refuses to call the refilling fate.

## The Sonnet Script: Rosaline and the Empty Chair

The substitution argument gains its historical depth from a comparison the essay presses and its readers have amplified: the play's first movement restages the situation of Shakespeare's Sonnets with the sexes rearranged. In the Sonnets, a male speaker addresses a beautiful, withholding young man whose refusal organizes more than a hundred poems; the desire is one-sided, versified, and structurally incapable of consummation, and its literary mode is petition. In the play's first act, Romeo occupies the speaker's position with the sexes of the object reversed: Rosaline has "forsworn to love," lives chaste, and is besieged in the vocabulary of warfare and economics, "she will not stay the siege of loving terms, / Nor bide th' encounter of assailing eyes" (1.1). The posture is identical because the script is identical: an unattainable object, a fluent subject, and a desire whose real product is language. Goldberg's point, developed through his claim that energies pass between the play and the sonnet sequence, is that the script does not know the sex of its object. It knows a position, refuser, and a position, petitioner, and it will run on any occupants.

The play then performs the experiment the Sonnets never perform: it empties the refuser's chair and seats a reciprocator in it. Juliet answers, proposes, sets the terms, and the petitionary script collapses into dialogue. What the tradition calls the miracle of the mutual love is, in the essay's accounting, the moment the machine jams: the sonnet script running on two voices instead of one, generating the shared sonnet of the first meeting, in which the lovers divide a form between them line by line, four lines each and a couplet traded, the Petrarchan monologue socialized into antiphon. The reading does not deny the meeting's beauty. It redescribes its mechanism, and the redescription has teeth: if the form survives the exchange of its object and the doubling of its voice, then the form, not the person, was the desire's first object all along. Romeo's own testimony convicts him of the pattern: "Did my heart love till now?" (1.5) is the question of a man discovering that his heart's history is a series, asked at the exact moment he insists the series has ended.

The prosecution's answer, that a young man's transfer of affection from a chaste girl who refuses him to a girl who returns it is the most ordinary event in comedy, is true and insufficient. Ordinariness is not the opposite of structure; it is structure wearing the mask of nature, which is the essay's whole thesis about the couple. What Goldberg adds to the ordinary event is the question the tradition declines to ask: why the play bothers to give the discarded beloved a name that contains the emblem of the succeeding love, a vow of Diana's chastity that mirrors the Sonnets' youth in his self-sufficiency, and a first act written entirely in the discarded script's idiom. Coincidence is possible at each point. The essay's claim is that the points compose a pattern, and patterns in a work of this verbal density are the critic's proper evidence. On the sonnet parallel, the judgment of this article is that Goldberg identifies a genuine structural rhyme, the strongest of his large claims after the idealization thesis, and that its force is independent of every letter in the alphabet.

## By the Book: The Shared Sonnet and Desire as Citation

The first meeting is the monument's favourite exhibit, and it is the essay's quietest triumph that the exhibit testifies for the prosecution of the monument. Romeo approaches Juliet at the feast and the two of them, strangers, compose a sonnet together, alternating quatrains in the pilgrim conceit: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5). Juliet takes the figure up without missing the form, corrects its theology, grants the prayer, and when the kiss is done delivers the scene's verdict on itself: "You kiss by th' book" (1.5). The line is usually played as a compliment. It is also a stage direction for reading the scene: this love is performed out of a book, out of the inherited script of pilgrims, saints, and sonnets, and both parties know the steps because the steps exist before the dancers.

For the essay's argument the meeting is the substitution machine caught in the act of producing the unique. The tradition reads the shared sonnet as the miracle of two subjectivities improvising in perfect accord. Goldberg's framework reads the same perfection as evidence of the opposite: the accord is possible because neither party is improvising. The form is prior, shared, and public, a liturgy any two competent speakers could perform, and the lovers' distinction is not that they escape the script but that they perform it flawlessly, two voices where the sonnet tradition has one. Rosaline's absence from the scene is the point: the script Romeo rehearsed on her in soliloquy is the script that now, with a partner, becomes the couple. What changes at the feast is not the desire but its staffing. The essay does not need to deny the scene's electricity; it relocates the generator, from the souls of the lovers to the culture's stock of forms, and in doing so it explains something the monument cannot, which is why the meeting feels destined and conventional in the same instant, a first encounter conducted entirely in quotations.

The bookishness runs deeper than the sonnet. Juliet is introduced as a volume in her mother's figure, Paris as a book to be read in the same scene, and the lovers keep meeting inside texts: a shared sonnet, an overheard soliloquy mistaken for address, a message carried by a go-between, a letter that fails to arrive, an epitaph spoken over bodies. Desire in this play is mediated by writing at every step, which is why a philological criticism, a criticism of letters, has a purchase on it that character criticism lacks. Goldberg's wager on the letter is, at this depth, a wager that the play's own medium of desire is textual: names, mottoes, sentences, sententious sayings, suspended words. The Nurse's report that Juliet has "the prettiest sententious" of Romeo and rosemary (2.4) is the play telling its audience that the heroine, offstage, composes sentences out of her lover's name, plays with his letters as the essay does. Whether the play endorses the play is the question the prosecution presses. That the play depicts it, in its heroine, in its plot, and in its most famous love scene, is not conjecture. It is the text.

## Sodomy as the Sexual Field

The essay's largest claim is also its most misunderstood. When Goldberg writes that "the sexual field in which desire operates is the forbidden desire named sodomy" (p. 228), he is not accusing the characters. He is naming the period's own map. In the map drawn by the theologians and inherited by the law, desire was sorted not by the sex of its object but by its relation to generation: acts open to procreation stood within nature, and a confused penumbra of non-procreative acts, solitary, interfemoral, anal, bestial, stood outside it under the sign of the sin against nature, whose English legal name was buggery and whose theological name was sodomy. Shakespeare, as Pequigney himself notes with approval, nowhere uses the word sodomy or any of its siblings in the plays. The absence is the point of contact between the antagonists: both agree the term is the culture's, not the text's vocabulary. They disagree about whether a critic may use the culture's map to chart the text's weather.

Goldberg's charting runs as follows. The lovers' union is celebrated by the tradition as nature's own act, the procreative pair blessed by the Friar's earth-and-grace botany. But the play gives that union no issue and no future: it is consummated once, in secret, between a wedding and a death, and its only offspring are two corpses and two statues. Its erotics, moreover, keep sliding off the procreative track in the text's own figures: desire figured as opening and being opened, as cutting and being cut, as taking from behind the veil of night, as a sheath receiving a dagger. Even legitimated by marriage, Goldberg writes, the union "continues to summon its allure from the unspeakable terrain of sodomy" (p. 228), because the allure the play builds for it is the allure of the forbidden, the nocturnal, the non-generative. Juliet herself is, for Romeo, a dangerous flower to pluck, dangerous "because the desires she represents are closely allied to the forbidden sexual acts more usually thought of as taking place between men" (p. 227). On the period's map, a sterile, secret, death-bound, figure-crossing desire does not sit in the territory of nature, whatever the sexes of the desirers. It sits in the confused category. The wider map of the play's erotic life, its servants' opening boasts, its Nurse's memories, its Mercutio, is drawn in the companion study [Sex and Sexuality in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/03/08/sex-and-sexuality-romeo-and-juliet/); the essay's contribution is to name the territory those materials share.

The play's own officiant supplies the field claim's best single witness. Friar Laurence, at the moment of performing the marriage that is supposed to legitimate the desire, warns the bridegroom in the desire's own physics: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6). The image is of an explosion, a consummation that annihilates its agents in the instant of contact and generates nothing beyond its own flash; it is spoken not by an enemy of the marriage but by its priest, inside the rite, as a description of what is being blessed. A desire whose triumph is figured by its own celebrant as gunpowder kissing fire belongs, in the period's taxonomy, to the economy of sterile and self consuming acts, however lawful its licence. Goldberg's field claim does little more than take the Friar's metaphor as seriously as the Friar does.

The claim's strength is that it explains the play's temperature, the way its love feels illicit from inside and not only because two fathers forbid it. The feud supplies an external prohibition, but the text's own imagery eroticizes prohibition itself: night as the element of desire, masks, ladders, potions, a borrowed tomb for a bed. The claim's weakness is the expansiveness of the category it borrows. If sodomy can mean any non-procreative, disorderly, or secret desire, then finding it everywhere in a tragedy of secret desire risks circularity: the category was confused, and a confused category confirms everything. Goldberg knew the objection and embraced the confusion as historically real; the category's incoherence, in *Sodometries*, is what allowed it to float free of acts and attach to representations, foreigners, heretics, and texts. Critics who want the term kept to its legal-theological core, and Pequigney is their ablest voice here, will call the embrace a licence. The adjudication reached in this article is that the field claim stands as a claim about the period's imagination, which is the claim Goldberg's sentences actually make, and falls if it is read as a claim about what the lovers do, which is the claim his rhetoric sometimes invites. Precision about that distinction is the difference between the landmark and the polemic, and Goldberg is not always precise. His readers have to be.

## Gender Coming Loose: Suns, Effeminacy, and Little Stars

The gender argument is the field claim at the level of the person. Its exhibits are three. In the orchard, Romeo casts Juliet as the sun: "It is the east, and Juliet is the sun. / Arise, fair sun, and kill the envious moon" (2.2.2 to 3). The moon is Diana's, the virgin goddess to whom Rosaline had sworn herself; the sun, in the period's cosmology and mythology, is Apollo's, male. Goldberg reads the compliment as a crossing: the beloved is masculinized by her own apotheosis, raised out of the feminine lunar register into the solar male one (p. 225). Second exhibit: Romeo, after the killing of Tybalt, accuses himself in Juliet's name: "O sweet Juliet, / Thy beauty hath made me effeminate / And in my temper softened valour's steel" (3.1). The lover reports that love has unsexed him, melted the masculine metal of his temper. Third exhibit: Juliet's invocation to night, in which she imagines Romeo after death: "Take him and cut him out in little stars, / And he will make the face of heaven so fine / That all the world will be in love with night" (3.2.21 to 23). Goldberg hears the erotic imagination itself at work in the dismemberment: when Juliet thinks about possessing Romeo, the essay says, she imagines cutting him up (p. 226). From these crossings the essay draws its conclusion about positions: within the couple's rhetoric, either member could assume either position (p. 232), the masculine and feminine places circulate as freely as the beloved's.

The prosecution's replies are uneven, and grading them is instructive. On "effeminate," Pequigney is simply right about the word's local sense: Romeo is lamenting a failure of valour, the softening of steel, and the adjective in context measures courage, not anatomy; the essay's slide from a moral sense to a sexual one is the kind of equivocation it elsewhere deplores in the tradition. On the sun, the prosecution is right about the compliment's conventionality and wrong to think conventionality settles it: lovers have always called beloveds suns, but the essay's point is comparative, that this play sets its sun against a moon explicitly gendered and chaste, and moves its heroine from Diana's party to Apollo's within a single image system that elsewhere distributes night, stars, and torches between the lovers without respect to sex. The point is suggestive, not probative, and should be weighed as such. On the stars, Pequigney's ridicule, that Goldberg makes Juliet a sadist, mistakes a figure of erotic imagination for a diagnosis; the image is Juliet's own, spoken in the play's most desirous speech, the "Gallop apace" invocation (3.2.1 to 2) in which she commands the night like a sovereign. That speech, the play's fullest statement of female wanting, is examined in the companion study [Female Desire in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/03/01/female-desire-romeo-and-juliet/); within the essay's argument it functions as proof that the heroine's desire is as active, as figuratively violent, and as little contained by the feminine place as the hero's. The honest summary of the three exhibits is that they show the play's gendered imagery to be mobile under pressure, which the tradition's monument cannot easily admit, and that Goldberg repeatedly promotes mobility into exchange, exchange into identity of structure. The promotions are where the quarrel lives.

### Why does the essay refuse the words "homosexual" and "heterosexual"?

Because both words name identities coined in the nineteenth century, and the essay's whole historical wager is that the sixteenth century organized sex by acts, relations, and their likeness to or distance from generation, not by orientations inhering in persons or by identities at all.

To call Romeo heterosexual, Goldberg holds, is as anachronistic as any other modern sorting, and it performs the idealization he is attacking: it certifies the couple as nature's norm before the evidence is heard. The refusal is the essay's discipline, and its cost: readers who want to know what the essay says anyone in the play is will find that it declines, on principle, to say.

## The Last Opening: The Tomb and the Sheath

An argument about a sexual field should be tested at the place the play's desire ends, the Capel vault, and the essay's logic proves unnervingly at home there. The play has prepared the tomb as a bed from the Friar's first speech, where the earth that is nature's mother is also her tomb, and the burying grave is a womb (2.3): generation and burial share one opening in the play's founding metaphor. Romeo, entering the vault, addresses the place as a body, "thou detestable maw, thou womb of death, / Gorged with the dearest morsel of the earth" (5.3), and forces its jaws: he will cram it with more food. Within, he finds Juliet and immediately sexualizes her keeper: "Shall I believe / That unsubstantial death is amorous, / And that the lean abhorred monster keeps / Thee here in dark to be his paramour?" (5.3). The bridegroom in the tomb is displaced by a rival, Death, who holds the bride in the dark for sexual use; Romeo's suicide is staged as a competition for possession of an opened body in an opened grave. Juliet, waking, completes the circuit with the play's most famous piece of eroticized anatomy: "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3). The sheath is her body, the dagger his weapon become her instrument, and the consummation the marriage bed never got to signify is performed as penetration into death, in the feminine grammatical place, by the woman's own hand.

None of this requires Goldberg to be believed in advance; the passages are the play's, and their convergence of opening, body, weapon, womb, and grave is a textual fact prior to any theory. What the essay's field claim predicts, and what a monument reading must minimize, is that the play's imagination of consummation would be indistinguishable from its imagination of burial, that desire's final opening would be a grave's mouth. The tradition handles the convergence by calling it poetry, as though that were an alternative to meaning. Goldberg's framework gives the convergence a period name and a period logic: a desire whose satisfactions are all nocturnal, secret, substitutional, and sterile ends, with a kind of taxonomic honesty, in the anti-generative place, the womb that is a tomb. Pequigney's demand for literal detail is satisfied by these passages more fully than by any R; they are spoken, enacted, and central. If the essay had built its case from the vault outward, from the maw and the sheath to the medlar and the letter, its rhetoric would have matched its evidence, and the prosecution would have had a harder brief. That Goldberg built from the letter outward is the essay's strategic mystery and its author's signature: the Spenserian's faith that the smallest unit, rightly heard, sounds the whole. The vault suggests the faith was not misplaced about the whole, whatever its fortunes at the level of the unit.

## The Prosecution: Joseph Pequigney Reads the Reader

The essay's most formidable answer came a decade later, from inside the house. Joseph Pequigney was not a hostile outsider to gay Shakespeare studies; he was one of its founders. His *Such Is My Love: A Study of Shakespeare's Sonnets* (1985) had called the Sonnets the grand masterpiece of homoerotic poetry and defended their same-sex desire against two centuries of editorial embarrassment, and his "The Two Antonios" (1992) remains a standard reading of same-sex love in *Twelfth Night* and *The Merchant of Venice*. When Pequigney published "The (In)Significance: 'What the Age Might Call Sodomy' and Homosexuality in Certain Recent Studies of Shakespeare's Plays" in *Intertexts* in 2004, the Goldberg essay was among his principal exhibits, and the charge was not prudery. It was professional: that the dominant queer criticism of the period, Goldberg's included, was methodologically and hermeneutically flawed, deficient in the exegetical skills it contemned as formalism, and careless with the historical record it claimed as its authority. A prosecutor with those credentials cannot be waved off as a defender of the monument. He has to be answered point by point, and the points are these.

First, the foundation. The title, Pequigney argues, is precariously based on two passages: a monologue of Mercutio's and a dialogue between Romeo and the Nurse. Of the two Rs, neither is Juliet's; one is Romeo's initial, the other begins "rosemary"; the Nurse's unuttered word is conjecture, "no one's in particular." A thesis announced by a pun on an editor's restoration and a servant's unfinished sentence has advertised a weakness it never repairs. Second, the distribution. Sodomy in the play, in the sense of anal sexuality, is adverted to exactly once, in Mercutio's optative joke, and is ascribed there to a fantasy about Rosaline, never to the protagonists or to any enacted desire; the essay's scattered sightings of the act elsewhere, in beds demanded, in Cupid's darts, in Juliet's stars, are sightings only in the sense that a determined reader can find a shape in clouds. Third, the equivocations: "effeminate" read as a sex change when its context is valour; the rose made to bear a predecessor's name when its grammar is an analogy about labels. Fourth, the politics of the essay's own page 227, where readings that enforce compulsory heterosexuality are declared complicit with the domestication of women and the scapegoating of gay men. Pequigney names the move coercion: agree that the lovers have no fixed genders, or stand convicted of oppression. An argument that arranges for disagreement to incriminate the disagreeing reader has left scholarship for discipline. Fifth, the unexamined machinery: the energies that are said to flow through Rosaline out of the play and into other plays, currents no instrument is offered to detect. Insofar as theory means the elucidation of method, he concludes, this criticism is untheorized, and the discrepancy between its account of the drama and the drama's literal detail is radical.

Two features of the prosecution deserve emphasis before judgment. Pequigney's own positive position is not the monument's. He holds that "homosexual" is a usable word for early modern persons and desires, that Shakespeare did write affirmatively about same-sex love where he chose to, in the Sonnets above all, and that the acts-versus-identity doctrine, as popularized from Foucault, is itself a misreading, a point on which he enlists David Halperin's "Forgetting Foucault" against the queer-theoretical consensus. His quarrel with Goldberg is therefore a quarrel within the project of taking the period's same-sex desires seriously, between an exegete who wants the evidence line by line and a philologist-performer who wants the letter to testify. And his exegesis is not uniformly victorious: his own summary of the sodomy tradition, Damian, Aquinas, Coke, the word's career through the statute book, supplies the very map on which Goldberg's field claim depends, and his concession that Hosley's restoration is convincing hands the essay its title word with an editor's blessing. The prosecution is at its weakest exactly where it is most contemptuous, in the suggestion that the whole performance is a falsification of the play. An essay cannot falsify a play whose bawdy it quotes accurately, whose plot substitutions it describes correctly, and whose reception it documents. It can over-read, equivocate, and coerce, and on those counts the verdicts are mixed, which is the subject of the next section.

## The Wider Indictment: The Method on Trial in Coriolanus

Pequigney's case against the essay does not rest on this play alone, and its wider count explains the essay's particular vulnerability. Goldberg returned to the territory in "The Anus in Coriolanus," a later reading of Aufidius's welcome to his enemy, in which the Roman recalls dreaming of encounters, of being down together in sleep, "fisting each other's throats" (4.5). Where the dictionaries take "fisting" as grasping, Goldberg's reading displaces anal eroticism to the mouth and throat, supported, at a 1994 conference performance, by a distributed image making the visceral connection explicit. Pequigney's contempt for the performance is total: a practice named and invented in the San Francisco of the 1970s is projected onto a Jacobean stage and an ancient Roman one, a prochronism so flagrant that it discredits the instrument. The count matters for the *Romeo and Juliet* essay because it identifies the method's failure mode with precision. Goldberg's philology works by licensed displacement: a word's buried sense is allowed to migrate across a text and charge distant passages. Where the buried sense is historically attested, the medlar's dialect name, the dog letter's growl, the migration recovers something real. Where the sense is imported from the critic's own century, the migration is projection wearing philology's clothes. The Coriolanus reading is Pequigney's control experiment: the same instrument, the same confidence, and a result that collapses on inspection. A defender of the earlier essay has to show why its displacements differ in kind, and the showing can be done: the R's growl is Persius's, the medlar's name is the period's dialect, the substitutions are the plot's own. But the control experiment stands as a permanent warning label on the method, and any honest adjudication has to record that the essay's author later supplied the prosecution with its best corroborating evidence.

## Adjudication: What Survives the Cross-Examination

The counts, taken in order. On the title's evidence, judgment for Pequigney. Two passages, one garbled by transmission and one unfinished by its speaker, cannot demonstrate a thesis about a whole play, and the essay's rhetoric, which lets the pun stand as the argument's emblem and advertisement, invites the standard it fails. A letter is corroboration, not proof, and on the occasions when Goldberg's prose forgets the difference, the prosecution's ridicule is earned. On "effeminate," judgment for Pequigney without qualification: the word's sense in its sentence is valour softened, and the essay's use of it is an equivocation. On the rose's grammar, judgment for Pequigney on the sentence and for Goldberg on the structure: Juliet's analogy is about names and things, and no misspelling is required to see that the play replaces Rosaline with Juliet while keeping the desiring script, a fact of the plot that no grammatical correction touches. On the coercion of page 227, judgment for Pequigney as a matter of scholarly ethics: complicity charges are not arguments, and the essay's weakest paragraph is the one that prices disagreement. On the energies between plays, judgment for Pequigney: an undetectable current is not a critical instrument.

On the counts that decide the essay's standing, judgment for Goldberg. The idealization thesis, that the couple's monument does ideological work and rests on suppressions a reader can document, editions that veiled the medlar line for three centuries among them, has not been answered and is no longer seriously deniable. The substitution thesis, that the play's desire is structured by replacement rather than by unique election, is a description of the plot before it is a theory of it, and it permanently changed what an account of the love has to explain: not why Romeo's love is true, but why its truth is indistinguishable in form from its discarded rehearsal. The field thesis, disciplined to its proper scope, that the period organized desire by generation and its failures rather than by object-choice, and that the play's sterile, secret, image-crossing union belongs on that map to the confused category rather than to nature, survives as the essay's most fertile idea, the one its successors actually use. The method, queer philology, survives with a health warning: attention to letters, puns, and suspended words is a way of noticing, not a way of proving, and its findings are hypotheses whose strength is the pattern they organize, never the instance they start from. Read under those disciplines, the essay remains what its citation history says it is, the landmark queer reading of the play. Read as its own rhetoric sometimes demands, as a demonstration that the tragedy is an orgy of sodomy, it collapses into the performance Pequigney describes. The difference between the landmark and the polemic is not in the essay. It is in the care of the reading, which is why the essay repays adjudication rather than allegiance.

There is a final, quieter ground on which Goldberg prevails, and it concerns the kind of object a play is. Pequigney's tribunal assumes that a reading is answerable to the literal detail of the text the way a paraphrase is answerable to a sentence, and much of the time he is right to insist on it. But a play is also a machine for producing effects in audiences across centuries, and its meanings include the ones its language makes available under pressure, in puns heard half-consciously, in jokes a servant drops, in a fruit's slang name. Goldberg's philology, at its best, is a claim about availability: that these senses were in the air of the first performances, breathed by an audience raised on bawdy, and that a criticism which filters them out in the name of literal detail is performing its own suppression. The et cetera of 1597 shows the filtering beginning in the play's first printing. Against that record, the burden of proof is not all on one side.

One further voice belongs in the adjudication, because it disciplines both parties. David Halperin's "Forgetting Foucault" argues that the acts-versus-identity distinction, as queer scholarship popularized it, is a dogmatic misreading of Foucault, whose point was discursive and strategic rather than a false historical law; Aquinas and Dante, as Pequigney adds, could already conceive the sodomite as a kind of person with a settled inclination. If Halperin is right, Goldberg's licence to discard the identity words is weaker than the essay assumes, and the prosecution is right to smell doctrine. But the discipline cuts the other way as well: if the period could hold acts and inclinations, fields and proto-identities, in unresolved suspension, then a criticism that maps the suspension, rather than resolving it into either modern term, is closer to the record than a criticism that polices the vocabulary. Goldberg's essay, shorn of its Foucauldian certainty, emerges from Halperin's correction less as the application of a theory than as an improvisation on a genuinely confused archive, which is what the archive, on the best historical evidence, was.

### What would count as evidence against the essay's thesis?

Three things, none of which has been produced. A demonstration that the play's substitutions are uniquely determined by its sources, so that the replacement pattern is inherited machinery rather than the text's own emphasis, would weaken the plot of replacement and with it the essay's centre.

A showing that the period's map of desire sorted by object-choice after all, against Bray and the statute book, would remove the field the essay charts. And an account of the idealization tradition that explained the editions' three-century veil over one syllable without invoking protection of the couple's purity would answer the opening gambit. Pequigney establishes none of these; his victories are over the essay's instances, and an argument whose instances are damaged but whose tests go unmet remains, on balance, standing.

## The Limits of the Landmark: What the Essay Cannot See

A landmark's limits are part of its survey, and this essay has three that its successors have had to correct. The first is race. The play's desire is shadowed from its first scenes by a language of darkness and fairness, Ethiop's ear and swan among crows, that the essay's sexual map does not chart; later criticism of the play's racial imagination has had to note, even while building directly on Goldberg's account of idealization, that he does not discuss race in the play at all. The anti-idealization instrument proved transferable to an axis its maker left untouched, which is a tribute to the instrument and a mark against the essay's completeness. A map of a field that omits one of its forces is a partial map, and the essay's field is partial in exactly the way its period's categories were not: Verona's imagination of desire is racialized in its metaphors from the first act, and a reading organized by the period's own confusions should have heard them.

The second limit is the audience in the theatre. The essay is a reader's performance, built from the page's letters, spellings, and suspensions; it has almost nothing to say about bodies on a stage, about what an actor's Mercutio does to the conjuration's geometry, or about the boy actor beneath Juliet's part, the one material fact of the early modern stage that a queer reading might be expected to prize. Goldberg's Verona is a text, and his sodomy a relation among words. Performance-oriented successors found the essay suggestive and unusable in roughly equal measure, an irony for a reading of drama: the substitutions it tracks are, in the theatre, visible events, entrances, exits, a corpse carried, a statue unveiled, and the essay's page-bound method consistently undervalues its own best evidence. The third limit is Juliet. In the essay's pattern she is a position, a rose, a sun, a dangerous flower, the second occupant of a chair; her wanting, the play's most articulate desire, is read as structure. Feminist criticism of the play, which had spent a decade recovering Juliet's desire as a woman's own, could reasonably see the landmark as one more system in which the heroine is a function of an argument. The essay queers the couple at the price of abstracting both its members, and the price is real: a reading in which nobody wants anything in particular has explained the pattern of desire by dissolving the desirers. The adjudication of this article has sided with the pattern against its prosecutors. The pattern's cost should stay on the record.

The Nurse concentrates the third limit in a single figure. The essay uses her as a phonetic instrument, the speaker of its founding joke, and passes over the desire she herself narrates: the dead husband's jest she retells with such relish in Act 1, Scene 3, that the child who fell on her brow will fall backward when she has more wit, is the play's earthiest piece of female sexual autobiography, and it belongs to a servant, a widow, and a paid go-between. A map of the play's sexual field that hears the Nurse's letter but not her life repeats, at the level of class and age, the abstraction it performs on Juliet: the women of the play become positions in an argument whose desiring subjects, once again, are elsewhere. Goldberg's Verona has no old women in it, though the play's Verona cannot move a message without one.

## The Afterlife: From Landmark to Infrastructure

The measure of a landmark is what gets built on it. Mario DiGangi's *The Homoerotics of Early Modern Drama* (1997), the next substantial mapping of the territory, works the ground Goldberg and Bray opened, distinguishing orderly from disorderly sodomitical relations and treating "sodomite" as a term that could mark a man as treasonous, monstrous, and heretical as well as sexual: the field model, disciplined into a taxonomy. The essay travelled beyond Renaissance studies into performance and film criticism; Timothy Murray's *Drama Trauma* (1997), reading Baz Luhrmann's film of the play, cites Goldberg for the proposition that Mercutio signifies the imprint of the play's sexual field in sodomy and forbidden desire, a sentence that had become, within three years, a portable instrument for reading a pop film. In the classroom the essay became the standard exhibit for the queer approach to the play, the reading a syllabus assigns when it wants students to see that the monument is made, not found. Seminar leaders assembling the essay's two hinge passages and its page of Pequigney's reply for presentation can lay the parallel texts out with the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), and the exercise the layout invites, claim against text, is the one this article has performed at length.

Goldberg himself drew the essay's moral a decade later, with Madhavi Menon, in "Queering History" (*PMLA*, 2005), the manifesto of what they called homohistory: a refusal to organize the past by the identity categories of the present, and a defence of unhistoricizing as a way of thinking relations between periods neither of which is self-identical. The *Open Rs* essay is that programme in miniature and avant la lettre: its refusal to call anyone in Verona homosexual or heterosexual, its preference for the period's confused category over the modern clinic's clean ones, its trust in the letter over the identity. Readers who find the later manifesto exasperating will find the seed of the exasperation here; readers who find it liberating will find the same. Either way, the essay is the place where the programme first proved it could take the most canonical love story in the culture and make it strange without making it unrecognizable.

What the essay did not do is also part of its record. It did not displace the monument in the culture at large, where the couple's statues still stand on a million Valentine's cards. It did not settle the queer reading of Mercutio, which continued on other evidence and other terms and is treated in its own right elsewhere in this series. And it did not persuade its ablest prosecutor, whose objections remain the necessary companion reading, the grit without which the landmark hardens into dogma. The essay's final standing is therefore best stated as a division of the house. As philology, it is a performance whose boldest exhibits are a restored word and an unfinished sentence, and whose local readings are right perhaps half the time. As criticism, it is the essay that made the play's desire a problem again, that refused the alibi of the monument, and that taught two generations of readers to watch what a text's language does when its doctrine is looking elsewhere. Landmarks are not required to be right in every particular. They are required to make return impossible. After "Open Rs," no account of the play's love can proceed as if the substitutions were not there, the bawdy were not thinking, and the couple's purity were a fact of nature. Return is impossible. That is the verdict, and it is enough. The essay also leaves its readers an instrument they did not have before it: a way of asking, about any celebrated representation of desire, whose substitutions the celebration requires them to forget. Applied to this play the question reorganized a field; applied beyond it, the question has become one of the period's standard instruments, carried into work on race, on performance, and on the other plays. Instruments outlive the hands that forged them and the occasions that proved them. That is the deepest sense in which the essay, whatever its rap sheet, is a landmark.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Goldberg summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Goldberg summary is the table in this article collecting the claims of Jonathan Goldberg's "Romeo and Juliet's Open Rs" (1994), the play passages each claim rests on, the essay pages where the claims can be checked, and their standing after cross-examination. The pattern it exposes is a division inside the landmark itself: the claims that describe the play's structures, its substitutions, its editorial suppression, its sterile union, survive every test applied to them, while the claims that rest on the letter R, on a word an editor restored in 1957, and on a sentence the Nurse never finishes remain performances rather than proofs. The summary therefore teaches the only responsible way to use the essay, which is to cite its architecture with confidence and its philology with the caution its own method demands.

### Q: Where was "Romeo and Juliet's Open Rs" first published, and who edited the volume it appeared in?

The essay was first published in *Queering the Renaissance*, issued by Duke University Press in 1994 in its Series Q, at pages 218 to 235. Jonathan Goldberg edited the volume himself and contributed the essay as its twelfth piece, alongside Alan Bray on male friendship, Valerie Traub on lesbian desire, and Carla Freccero on queer philology. The double role matters to the essay's standing: Goldberg was simultaneously the collection's architect and its most provocative contributor, so the volume's programme, locating forms of desire not organized by modern sexual identities, and the essay's performance of that programme on the most famous love story in English, were designed together. The essay has been reprinted, anthologized, and assigned continuously since, which is the publishing history its landmark status rests on. Its pages, 218 to 235, are the most cited in the volume.

### Q: Which two passages does the essay's title pun rest on?

The title rests on the Nurse's unfinished sentence in Act 2, Scene 4 and on Mercutio's medlar speech in Act 2, Scene 1. In the first, the Nurse asks whether rosemary and Romeo begin with a letter, hears that both begin with an R, calls R the dog's name, and breaks off at "R is for the," a suspension Goldberg completes with the word the growling letter suggests. In the second, Mercutio wishes Rosaline were an "open-arse," the dialect name of the medlar fruit, and Romeo a poperin pear. The pun of the title fuses the two: the play's Rs are open, apertures, the letter of the body's forbidden opening. Joseph Pequigney's prosecution begins by noting how precarious the foundation is, one restored word and one unfinished sentence, and the observation has never been answered on its own terms.

### Q: What does Goldberg accuse the play's admiring tradition of building?

Goldberg accuses the admiring tradition of building the bedrock of the heterosexual order. In the essay's opening movement (p. 219), readings that idealize Romeo and Juliet as the type of unique, natural, self evident heterosexual love are charged with foundational work: they make one arrangement of sex and gender appear to be nature itself, ratified by the culture's most beloved love story. The accusation is about function, not sincerity. Generations of editors, teachers, and critics who veiled the play's bawdy, forgot Rosaline, and quarantined Mercutio were, in the essay's account, manufacturing the monument they claimed to find. The charge is the part of the essay its critics have never answered, because it rests on the documented record of the play's reception, the expurgated editions among it, rather than on any contested reading of a line.

### Q: What does Goldberg mean by calling sodomy the sexual field of the play?

Goldberg means that the period's own category for forbidden, non-procreative desire is the territory in which the play's desires move, whatever the sexes of the desirers. The sentence stands at page 228: the sexual field in which desire operates is the forbidden desire named sodomy. In the theological and legal map of the sixteenth century, sodomy named a confused class of acts against nature, sorted by their distance from generation rather than by the sex of their object. Goldberg's claim is that the lovers' union belongs on that map's forbidden side: secret, nocturnal, sterile, consummated between a wedding and a death, and figured throughout as opening, cutting, and taking. The claim concerns the organization of desire in the text. It accuses no character of any act, and it explicitly refuses the modern identity words. Readers who want the play's desire sorted by orientation will find the essay declines the sorting on principle, because the sorting is the monument's own.

### Q: What substitutions make up the plot of replacement Goldberg traces?

The substitutions begin with the beloved: Rosaline, the chaste refuser of the first act, is replaced by Juliet while Romeo's desiring script, its Petrarchan rhetoric, its postures and oxymorons, continues unchanged. They continue in the name speech, where Goldberg hears Rosaline inside the rose and Juliet installed as Rosaline renamed, and in the orchard, where the lovers exchange the masculine and feminine positions of the image system, Juliet raised to the sun, Romeo unsexed by beauty. They end in the tomb, where Death replaces Romeo as Juliet's keeper, the fathers replace the lovers with golden statues, and the city replaces the couple with a story. Goldberg's sentence for the whole pattern (p. 222) is that the substitutions respect neither the uniqueness of individuals nor the boundaries of gender difference. The plot itself, before any theory, is a chain of replacements; the essay's contribution is to refuse to call the chain fate.

### Q: Why does Goldberg hear Rosaline inside Juliet's rose speech?

Goldberg hears Rosaline inside the speech because "rose" is the opening of her name, the earlier beloved's name with its remaining syllables dropped, surfacing in the mouth of her replacement at the very moment Juliet argues that names do not matter. The irony is the argument: Juliet's thesis is that the thing exceeds the label, that Romeo would retain his dear perfection were he not called Romeo, yet the speech that makes the case cannot keep the predecessor's name out of its central flower. Goldberg develops the hearing into an exchange of places, Juliet as that rose and thereby Rosaline renamed, Romeo located in the rose's place and thus in Rosaline's. Pequigney's correction, that Juliet analogizes the word "rose" with the name "Montague" and compares Romeo to no flower, is grammatically sound. The hearing survives, where it survives, as an account of the name's persistence in the text's ear rather than of the sentence's logic.

### Q: How does the sun image in the orchard scene cross Juliet's gender for Goldberg?

The image crosses her gender by promotion: Romeo declares "It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2 to 3), and the sun, in the period's mythology, is Apollo's, a male god's emblem, while the moon the sun is told to kill is Diana's, the virgin goddess to whom Rosaline had consecrated herself. Goldberg reads the compliment as an apotheosis that changes the beloved's register, lifting Juliet out of the feminine lunar economy of chastity and reflection into the solar, masculine economy of source and radiance (p. 225). The reading is comparative rather than inevitable: lovers had always called beloveds suns, and the prosecution is entitled to the convention. Its force comes from the play's own distribution, in which moon, stars, night, and torches circulate between the lovers with so little respect for the period's gendered astronomy that the compliment lands as one crossing among many.

### Q: Why does Rosaline matter to the essay if she never speaks or appears?

Rosaline matters as the proof that the play's desire precedes its celebrated object. She never appears, never speaks, and is defined entirely by refusal, Diana's vow, and Romeo's rhetoric; yet the first act runs on her, and the rhetoric that runs on her is indistinguishable in form from the rhetoric that will be consecrated as unique when Juliet replaces her. For Goldberg she is therefore the control case of the whole argument: if the same desire, in the same language, attaches first to an absent refuser and then to the monument's heroine, the desire's truth cannot consist in the uniqueness of its second object. He extends her into a structural parallel with the fair youth of the Sonnets, the beautiful refuser who organizes a male speaker's verse, and it is this extension, more than her presence in the plot, that Pequigney contests, objecting that the essay promotes a nonentity into a principal conductor of energies between plays.

### Q: Which editor restored the reading "open-arse" in 1957, and what stood in the early editions instead?

Richard Hosley's edition of 1957 restored "open-arse" to Mercutio's medlar line, and the restoration has been generally accepted since. What stood in the early editions was evasion and damage: the first quarto of 1597 prints an "open Et caetera," substituting a decorous Latin shrug for the fruit's name, and the second quarto of 1599 prints "open, or," a garble usually explained as a misreading of "open ars" in the copy, which the First Folio of 1623 repeats. The dialect name of the medlar, the metre, and the answering shape of the poperin pear converge on Hosley's word, and even Joseph Pequigney, the essay's fiercest critic, calls the emendation convincing. The episode matters beyond the line, because the play's most famous queer reading takes its title from a word its first printers would not print and its editors needed three centuries to restore.

### Q: What word does the Nurse leave unsaid after "R is for the," and what does Goldberg do with the silence?

The Nurse leaves unsaid whatever word her grammar was reaching for, and the text gives a dash where the word should be: "Ah, mocker! That's the dog's name. R is for the" (2.4), after which she corrects herself and chatters on. Goldberg supplies the completion the scene's bawdy and the letter's growl suggest, the word "arse," R having been the dog's letter since Persius because the trilled sound snarls, and a rolled R being the nearest the alphabet comes to pronouncing the completion. With the supplied word, the hero's initial becomes the sign of the body's forbidden opening, and the essay's title is minted. The prosecution's answer is that the completion is conjecture, the silence is the text, and the word the critic supplies is, in Pequigney's phrase, no one's in particular. Both accounts agree on the dash. They disagree about what a dash in a bawdy scene, in a play of puns, is evidence of. The question is a fair emblem for the whole quarrel, since the essay and its prosecution divide, finally, over the evidentiary status of everything a text almost says.

### Q: What are Joseph Pequigney's main objections to the essay?

Pequigney's objections, published in *Intertexts* in 2004, are exegetical before they are ideological. The title rests precariously on two passages, one a garbled line and the other an unfinished sentence, and neither of its Rs belongs to Juliet. Anal desire is adverted to once in the play, in Mercutio's optative joke about Rosaline, and is never ascribed to the protagonists whose union the essay claims for the sodomitical field. The essay equivocates on "effeminate," which in its sentence measures valour, and mishandles the rose, which in Juliet's grammar analogizes a word with a name. Its page 227, where readings enforcing compulsory heterosexuality are declared complicit with oppression, substitutes coercion for argument. And its machinery of energies flowing between plays is asserted without any method for detecting them. The cumulative charge is that the discrepancy between the essay's account and the play's literal detail is radical.

### Q: Why does Pequigney's own career give his objections their particular force?

Pequigney's objections carry force because he cannot be mistaken for a defender of the monument he is accused of protecting. His *Such Is My Love* (1985) had named the Sonnets the grand masterpiece of homoerotic poetry and defended their same-sex desire against centuries of editorial embarrassment; his "Two Antonios" (1992) is a standard account of same-sex love in two other plays. When such a critic charges the dominant queer reading of *Romeo and Juliet* with exegetical deficiency, the charge is a professional's, from inside the project of taking the period's same-sex desires seriously. His positive positions sharpen the point: he holds that "homosexual" remains a usable word for early modern desire, against the acts-versus-identity orthodoxy, and that Shakespeare wrote affirmatively about same-sex love where he chose to, which makes his refusal of Goldberg's findings a refusal of the evidence offered, not of the subject. Pequigney's own positive programme, careful exegesis in the service of the period's same-sex desires, is the standing alternative the essay's readers are asked to choose between, and the choice is a real one.

### Q: What does Goldberg claim marriage fails to change about the lovers' desire?

Goldberg claims that marriage fails to move the lovers' desire out of the forbidden territory in which the play has placed it. The union, he writes, even legitimated by marriage, "continues to summon its allure from the unspeakable terrain of sodomy" (p. 228). The Friar's rite makes the couple lawful in the church's register; it does not make their desire procreative, public, or safe, and the play underlines the failure by setting the wedding between a street killing and a death, the consummation in a house of mourning, and the marriage's only issue in a tomb. The allure the text builds for the union is the allure of the nocturnal and the prohibited, and a ceremony conducted in a cell at dawn does not alter the figures in which the desire speaks. For Goldberg, that persistence is the proof that the field, not the licence, governs the play's erotics.

### Q: What does Romeo's word "effeminate" in Act 3, Scene 1 become in the quarrel over the essay?

The word becomes the quarrel's cleanest test case, because its context is short enough to judge. Romeo, having stood aside while Mercutio fell, cries: "O sweet Juliet, / Thy beauty hath made me effeminate / And in my temper softened valour's steel" (3.1). Goldberg takes the sentence as a report of gender destabilization, love unsexing the lover, and builds it into the essay's account of positions exchanged within the couple. Pequigney reads the sentence in its place: Romeo is reproaching himself for a failure of courage at the moment courage was required, the adjective measures the softening of valour's steel, and no sex or gender change is suggested. On this count the prosecution's local reading is the right one, and the essay's use of the word is an equivocation. The case matters because it is decidable, and its decidability is the standard the essay's larger, looser claims cannot meet in the same way.

### Q: How does Goldberg's essay keep company with Alan Bray's essay in the same volume?

The two essays are the volume's hinge, one historical and one literary, and each supplies what the other needs. Bray's "Homosexuality and the Signs of Male Friendship in Elizabethan England" reconstructs a culture in which men kissed, shared beds, and protested devotion in terms a modern reader is trained to call erotic, while sodomy named a monstrous crime of heretics and foreigners that no ordinary friend could recognize in his own conduct. Goldberg's essay presupposes that reconstruction at every step: only in a culture where the signs of friendship and the signs of the capital sin could occupy the same gestures does the play's triangular bawdy, its bed sharing friendships, and its unspeakable field become historically legible rather than anachronistic. Bray, for his part, warned that homosexual themes in the period's literature need extreme caution from the historian. The volume holds the warning and the performance side by side, and much of the subsequent quarrel, Pequigney's included, is a replay of that internal tension. Goldberg, as editor, printed the caution his own essay would spend itself against, which suggests the performance knew its risk from the start.

### Q: What did Goldberg and Madhavi Menon argue in "Queering History" a decade later?

In "Queering History" (*PMLA*, 2005), Goldberg and Madhavi Menon argued for what they called homohistory: a study of the sexual past that refuses to organize it by the identity categories of the present and that deliberately unhistoricizes, thinking the relations between periods neither of which is self-identical. The manifesto generalizes the *Open Rs* essay's founding refusals. The essay had declined to call anyone in Verona homosexual or heterosexual, had preferred the period's confused category to the modern clinic's clean ones, and had trusted letters and substitutions over identities; the later essay turns those local decisions into a programme for the field. Critics who found the programme exasperating, a licence for anachronism in the act of denouncing it, and critics who found it liberating are replaying, at the scale of a discipline, the quarrel Pequigney staged over a single play. The line from the essay to the manifesto is the clearest evidence that the landmark's method, not merely its findings, is what endured.

### Q: How did the essay travel into writing on Baz Luhrmann's film?

The essay travelled into film writing within three years, when Timothy Murray's *Drama Trauma* (1997) read Luhrmann's film of the play with Goldberg as the literary warrant: Mercutio signifies, in Goldberg's phrase as Murray cites it, the imprint of the play's sexual field in sodomy and forbidden desire. The borrowing is diagnostic of the essay's afterlife. Performance criticism needed a vocabulary for what the film made visible, the erotic charge circulating among the men around the couple, and the essay supplied it in portable form, one sentence detachable from the philology that produced it. The pattern repeats across the essay's reception: its field claim and its idealization charge travel everywhere, while its letter evidence stays home, too local and too contested to pack. Landmarks often survive as their two or three most portable sentences. The film critics who borrowed the field sentence rarely mention the Nurse's dash or the medlar's textual history, and the sentence does its work for them anyway, which is either the mark of a real insight or of a very successful slogan; this article's adjudication has treated it as the former travelling in the latter's clothes.

### Q: Why does Goldberg's argument not depend on calling any character homosexual?

The argument does not depend on it because its subject is the organization of desire in a text, not the orientation of persons, and because its historical premise is that the identity the word names did not exist to be attributed. Goldberg's refusal is stated as a principle: hetero- and homosexuality are profound misnomers for the organization of sex in Shakespeare's time (p. 227). What the essay claims is that the play's desires are routed through substitutions, triangulations, and figures that the period's own map sorted into the forbidden category of sodomy, a claim about routes and categories that can be tested against the text's language and the period's law. Readers who ask whether the essay proves Romeo gay, or Mercutio, are asking a question the essay exists to dissolve. Its queerness is a property it assigns to structures, a field, a plot of replacement, not to any character's heart. That refusal frustrates readers who want the play's persons sorted, and it is the refusal, more than any single reading, that separates the essay from the character criticism that preceded it and from the identity criticism that surrounded it.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Goldberg summary to argue about desire in the play?

An essay should use the InsightCrunch Goldberg summary as a map of burdens, assigning each claim the weight its row shows it can bear. Open with the rows marked upheld: the idealization charge, which frames the couple's monument as a reading with interests, and the substitution series, which the plot itself documents, and let those carry the thesis that the play's desire is a structure of replacements rather than a unique election. Bring in the field claim next, scoped precisely as a claim about the period's categories, with the statute book and Bray behind it. Use the contested rows last and honestly: the letter, the suspended word, and the restored "open-arse" are evidence of the play's verbal climate, corroboration for a pattern established on other ground, and an essay that presents them as demonstrations will be convicted by Pequigney's questions. The summary's discipline, confidence in the architecture and candour about the philology, is the difference between citing a landmark and repeating a polemic.
