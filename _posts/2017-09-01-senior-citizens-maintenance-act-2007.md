The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 is named for maintenance, and maintenance is the smaller half of what it does in practice. A tribunal constituted under the Senior Citizens Act can order a son, a daughter, a grandson or a granddaughter to pay a parent a monthly allowance, and the amount it can order is capped by a figure Parliament fixed in 2007 and has never revised: ten thousand rupees a month at the very most, and only such lesser sum as the state government has prescribed. The same tribunal can also declare void a transfer of property worth many times that annual figure, unwind a registered gift deed, and, on the Supreme Court's reading, restore possession of a house to the senior citizen who gave it away. The first power is limited, hedged and denominated in a sum that inflation has steadily hollowed out. The second is unlimited in value. Anyone who wants to understand this statute has to hold those two facts together, because the relationship between them explains the Act's caseload, its case law, its critics and its defenders.

The One Test answer for this pillar is the one the brief fixes. A senior citizen, or a member of a senior citizen's family, who finishes this article should know how the tribunal works, what it can order and what it cannot, and should understand that the provision which has become the statute's principal use is not the maintenance provision at all but the power in Section 23 to undo a transfer of property. That inversion is not an accident of litigation fashion. It is built into the design. Parliament capped the money remedy and left the property remedy uncapped, barred lawyers from the forum it created, staffed that forum with revenue officers rather than judges, and set the whole machine to run in ninety days. Each of those choices has a defensible reason behind it, and each has a cost that the courts have spent years managing. A statute pillar owes the reader both the reasons and the costs, stated at full strength, before it reaches a verdict.

## The problem the Act was built to solve

The Statement of Objects and Reasons that accompanied the Bill of 2007 describes the social change Parliament believed it was answering. Traditional norms and values of Indian society, it said, laid stress on providing care for the elderly, but the withering of the joint family system had left a large number of elderly persons without family care, and many older persons, particularly widowed women, were forced to spend their later years alone, exposed to emotional neglect and to a lack of physical and financial support. Ageing, the statement concluded, had become a major social challenge requiring more attention to the care and protection of older persons. That framing matters because it identifies the harm the Act targets. The harm is not poverty in the abstract, which is the business of pension and assistance programmes. The harm is the withdrawal of support by the specific people the statute can name: children, grandchildren and the relatives who stand to inherit. The Act is a family obligation statute dressed as a welfare statute, and almost everything distinctive about it follows from that choice.

The constitutional footing was equally specific. Article 41 of the Constitution, a Directive Principle of State Policy, directs the state, within the limits of its economic capacity and development, to make effective provision for securing the right to public assistance in cases of old age, sickness and disablement and in other cases of undeserved want. A Directive Principle does not create an enforceable claim, and the Act does not pretend to convert Article 41 into one against the state. What it does instead is legislate the family side of the obligation, which Parliament could do directly, while assigning the state the supporting roles of constituting tribunals, building old age homes in a phased manner and ensuring medical support in government hospitals. Readers who want the wider field of rights-based welfare legislation, and the way statutes distribute duties between family, employer and state, will find it mapped in the [complete guide to welfare legislation in India](/2017/07/01/welfare-laws-india-complete-guide/), which owns that connective account; this pillar stays with the one statute and its machinery.

What existed before 2007, and why Parliament judged it insufficient, is the necessary next step, because the Act's summary character is unintelligible except as a reaction against the route that preceded it.

## What existed before, and why it failed the elderly applicant

A parent who could not maintain himself or herself was not remediless before this Act. The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 carried in its Chapter IX a summary maintenance jurisdiction under which a Magistrate could order a person having sufficient means, who neglected or refused to maintain a parent unable to maintain himself or herself, to pay a monthly allowance. Personal laws supplied parallel duties. Hindu law recognised an obligation to maintain aged and infirm parents, and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 states that obligation as a statutory duty of sons and daughters. Muslim law, on the account given in the standard commentaries, likewise obliges children and grandchildren with means to maintain parents and grandparents in indigent circumstances. Christian and Parsi parents had no equivalent personal-law provision and depended on the criminal procedure route. The patchwork was real, but it shared three defects from the elderly applicant's side of the counter. The criminal procedure route required the applicant to litigate in a Magistrate's court, with the pace, the expense and the adversarial atmosphere of criminal process. The personal-law route required a civil suit. And neither route offered anything at all for the two problems that dominate old-age insecurity in practice: the house that has been signed away, and the absence of any institution charged with the applicant's welfare rather than with deciding a dispute.

The Statement of Objects and Reasons says the quiet part aloud. Parents could claim maintenance under the Code of Criminal Procedure, it observed, but the procedure was both time consuming and expensive, and there was therefore a need for simple, inexpensive and speedy provisions to claim maintenance for parents. Every design feature of the 2007 Act is that sentence translated into machinery. A tribunal rather than a court. A sub-divisional officer rather than a judge. A summary procedure rather than a trial. A ninety day endeavour rather than an open-ended list. No lawyers, because lawyers were identified, rightly or wrongly, with the delay and cost the new forum was built to escape. Whether that diagnosis was fair to the legal profession is a question this article takes up in its own section below, because the bar on representation cuts in both directions and the respondent's side of it deserves a full hearing. The diagnosis itself, that the old routes were too slow and too costly for an applicant whose need is monthly and immediate, was widely shared across the political spectrum, and the Bill passed without the kind of partisan contest that marks most social legislation.

One state had legislated earlier, and its experiment is part of the record. The Himachal Pradesh Maintenance of Parents and Dependants Act, 2001 created a state maintenance regime for parents and dependants, resting expressly on Articles 38 and 41 of the Constitution, with a ceiling of five thousand rupees a month in its original design and an eligibility frame initially tied to income below the poverty line, a restriction the state removed by amendment in 2007. The central Act is broader in every dimension: it applies irrespective of religion, it is not confined to applicants below a poverty line, it carries the higher ceiling of ten thousand rupees, and it adds the welfare chapters and the property chapter that the state statute lacked. The comparison is worth holding because it shows the central Act as a considered expansion of a tested idea rather than an invention, and because the state experience already contained the central Act's central weakness in miniature: a ceiling fixed in nominal rupees, in a statute with no mechanism for revising it, begins to shrink from the day it is enacted.

### How did the Himachal Pradesh statute anticipate the central Act?

The Himachal Pradesh Maintenance of Parents and Dependants Act, 2001 anticipated the central Act by six years: a state tribunal route to monthly maintenance for parents and dependants, justified by Articles 38 and 41, with a five thousand rupee ceiling and, at first, a poverty line eligibility condition that the state later removed.

The state statute also demonstrates the limits of anticipation. It was a maintenance statute and nothing more. It had no answer to the transferred house, no old age home chapter with a district floor, no geriatric care duties, and no offence of abandonment. The central Parliament of 2007 took the tribunal idea and wrapped around it a welfare and protection code, and it is the additions, above all the protection of life and property chapter, that generated the jurisprudence. A reader tracing only the maintenance line from 2001 to 2007 would miss the point of the later Act entirely. The maintenance line is the inheritance. The property line is the innovation, and innovations in a statute are where litigation concentrates.

## The Senior Citizens Act among its neighbours: two older routes

Because Section 12 forces an election between this Act and Chapter IX of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, the comparison between the two routes is not academic; it is a choice real applicants make, and it deserves to be drawn precisely. Under the criminal procedure route, a parent unable to maintain himself or herself applied to a Magistrate against a son or daughter with sufficient means who had neglected or refused to maintain. The Magistrate could order a monthly allowance, enforce it by warrant and by imprisonment for default, and the order was subject to the ordinary appellate and revisional structure of criminal process, with lawyers on both sides as a matter of course. The route's strengths were its age, its familiarity to every district bar, and the absence of any statutory ceiling on the allowance, which the Magistrate fixed on means and needs. Its weaknesses were the ones the Statement of Objects and Reasons named: time and expense, to which two more may be added from the applicant's side. The proceeding carried the atmosphere of a criminal court, in which a parent prosecuted, in substance if not in form, his or her own child. And the route offered nothing beyond money: no conciliation stage designed for families, no officer charged with representing the parent, no welfare chapters, and no answer at all to the transferred property.

The personal law route differs again. A civil suit for maintenance under personal law could engage wider heads of obligation, interim relief, and the full apparatus of pleadings, evidence and appeal, and for a claimant with a substantial claim against a respondent of substance it remained, and remains, a rational choice. But it is the slowest and most expensive of the three routes, it depends on the claimant's personal law recognising the duty in the claimant's situation, and it exposes the elderly plaintiff to the ordinary attrition of civil litigation, in which time itself is the respondent's best defence. The 2007 Act was not designed to beat the civil suit at its own game. It was designed for the paradigm case the other routes served worst: the parent of modest means, the respondent of ordinary means, the need that is monthly and immediate, and the dispute whose real subject is as likely to be a house as an income.

Against those neighbours, the distinctive contributions of this Act can be isolated, and a pillar should isolate them rather than repeat the general claim of speed. It created a forum below the district court, at the sub-division where the applicant lives. It allowed the forum to begin a case itself. It wrote conciliation into the procedure before hearing. It created a public officer whose statutory function includes representing the parent. It capped the money remedy, which the criminal procedure route does not, and in exchange it created a property remedy, which neither neighbour possesses in summary form. And it attached to one statute a welfare and protection code, old age homes, medical support, publicity, district action plans and an offence of abandonment, converting a maintenance claim into the visible tip of a general elderly welfare scheme. Whether the exchange was well judged is the assessment question. That these are the terms of the exchange is the analytical point, and any account of the Act that praises or condemns it without naming them is trading in impressions.

One further neighbour belongs in the map, because tribunals meet it in practice: the succession and property law that determines who holds what when the application is filed. A maintenance claim against a relative turns on inheritance shares. A Section 23 claim turns on a deed. A residence dispute turns on title and on the domestic violence legislation together. The tribunal does not decide those bodies of law; it applies its own Act across their surface, and the quality of a tribunal's work is often the quality of its awareness of where its Act ends and the neighbour begins. Section 10's duty to cancel or vary an order where a competent civil court's decision requires it, and Section 27's bar on civil court jurisdiction over matters the Act covers, are the two hinges on which that boundary swings, and they pull in opposite directions by design: the tribunal is exclusive inside its field and subordinate, on the same subject, to a civil court's adjudication where one competently exists.

## The architecture of the Act

The Act, Act 56 of 2007, received the assent of the President on 29 December 2007 and was published in the Gazette on 31 December 2007. It has thirty-two sections arranged in seven chapters, and the chapter map is the fastest accurate guide to what Parliament thought it was doing. Chapter I carries the preliminary provisions: the short title, extent and commencement in Section 1, the definitions in Section 2, and an overriding effect clause in Section 3. Chapter II, Sections 4 to 18, is the maintenance code proper: the entitlement, the application, jurisdiction and procedure, the constitution of tribunals, the order, its alteration and enforcement, the option against the criminal procedure route, deposit, interest, the appellate tribunal and appeals, the bar on legal practitioners, and the Maintenance Officer. Chapter III, Section 19 alone, deals with the establishment of old age homes. Chapter IV, Section 20 alone, deals with medical support. Chapter V, Sections 21 to 23, is headed protection of life and property of senior citizens, and contains the publicity and coordination duties, the implementing authorities, and the transfer provision. Chapter VI, Sections 24 and 25, creates the single offence and prescribes its trial. Chapter VII, Sections 26 to 32, is miscellaneous: public servant status, the bar on civil court jurisdiction, good faith protection, removal of difficulties, central directions and review, and state rule making power.

Two features of that architecture deserve emphasis before any provision is examined. The first is proportion. Eighteen of the thirty-two sections serve the maintenance machinery, and one section, Section 23, serves the property remedy that dominates the reported case law. A statute's centre of gravity in litigation is not set by the space its drafters gave a subject but by the value of what a provision can deliver and the absence of alternative routes to the same relief. Section 23 delivers a registered deed undone and a house recovered, in a summary forum, without court fees of the order a civil suit would attract and without the years a title suit consumes. No other provision in the Act, and no other statute on the book, offers that combination, and so the litigation gathered there. The second feature is the federal skeleton. The Act is a central statute that does almost nothing by its own force. Its commencement, its tribunals, its prescribed ceiling within the statutory maximum, its conciliation and maintenance officers, its old age homes, its action plan for protection of life and property, and its rules are all state acts of will. Section 1, sub-section (3) provides that the Act shall come into force in a State on such date as the State Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, appoint. There is therefore no single national commencement date for this Act, and any account that gives one is wrong. States brought the Act into force by their own notifications at different times, constituted tribunals within the six months Section 7 allows from the commencement applicable to them, and prescribed their own ceilings up to the ten thousand rupee maximum. The Act a citizen meets is, in its operative detail, the Act as the citizen's state has switched it on and filled it in.

The extent clause carries a period detail that dates the text. As enacted, Section 1, sub-section (2) extended the Act to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, and applied it also to citizens of India outside India. The exception was the standard consequence of the constitutional position Jammu and Kashmir then held, and the reorganisation legislation of 2019, which recast the state into two Union territories and extended the central statute book to them, removed the basis of the exception. The durable point for a reader of the Act is the second limb: the Act follows the Indian citizen abroad, a reach that connects with the service provisions for respondents outside India examined below.

Section 3 gives the Act overriding effect: its provisions have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent contained in any other enactment or in any instrument having effect by virtue of another enactment. That clause looks like a trump, and respondents in the domestic violence cases treated it as one. The Supreme Court's treatment of the clause in S. Vanitha, examined in the section on the domestic violence balance below, is the necessary corrective: an overriding clause in each of two welfare statutes does not licence a court to let the later one erase the earlier, and the enquiry becomes one of dominant purpose and harmonious construction. Section 3 settles conflicts between this Act and inconsistent instruments in the ordinary run of cases. It does not settle, by itself, a collision between two protective codes, each with its own non-obstante armour.

## Who the Act binds: the definitions

Section 2 carries eleven definitions, and four of them decide most applications before any tribunal reaches the merits. A senior citizen, by clause (h), means any person being a citizen of India who has attained the age of sixty years or above. Three elements are packed into that short clause. Citizenship is required; the Act does not extend its senior citizen category to a foreign national resident in India, however long the residence. The age threshold is sixty, not the sixty-five or seventy that pension discourse sometimes uses, and it is attained age, so a person of fifty-nine years and eleven months is outside the definition however infirm. And the definition is indifferent to means, sex, marital status and parenthood: a childless citizen of sixty-one with substantial property is a senior citizen for the Act's purposes, entitled to invoke Section 23 and the protection chapter, even though the maintenance entitlement in Section 4 has its own separate condition of inability to maintain oneself.

A parent, by clause (d), means a father or mother, whether biological, adoptive or a step father or step mother, as the case may be, whether or not the father or the mother is a senior citizen. The closing words do real work. A parent of fifty-five with a major son is a parent under the Act and can claim maintenance through its tribunal, even though that parent is not a senior citizen and could not invoke Section 23, which is confined to senior citizens. The Act's maintenance chapter thus protects two overlapping classes: senior citizens, including parents among them, and parents as such. The 2019 amendment Bill proposed to widen this definition to include parents in law and grandparents expressly, and its lapse leaves the enacted text where it stood: grandparents claim, if at all, in their capacity as parents of their own children or as senior citizens against the relatives the Act specifies, and the definitional gap the Bill identified remains a gap in the law.

Children, by clause (a), includes a son, a daughter, a grandson and a granddaughter, but does not include a minor. The inclusion of grandchildren is the feature lay accounts most often miss, and it reflects the demographics of the obligation: the person with means in a three-generation household may be the grandson, and the Act follows the means and the descent line together. The exclusion of minors is symmetrical with the rest of the scheme, since a minor cannot be ordered to pay a monthly allowance out of means the law treats the minor as not fully controlling. What the definition does not include is as significant as what it does. A son in law and a daughter in law are not children under the enacted Act, however central they may be to the household's actual arrangements and however often they appear as respondents in the property litigation, where they are reached, when they are reached, through the transfer provisions or through their spouse's obligation rather than through the maintenance definition. The 2019 Bill proposed to add adoptive children, step children and children in law; Parliament did not enact the addition, and a tribunal that ordered a daughter in law, as such, to pay maintenance under Section 9 would be acting outside the definition the Act gives it.

A relative, by clause (g), means any legal heir of the childless senior citizen who is not a minor and is in possession of the property of the senior citizen or would inherit that property after the senior citizen's death. The relative is the Act's most transactional figure. The maintenance duty is not cast on relatives at large, nor on nephews and nieces as a class, but on the legal heir whose connection to the senior citizen is proprietary: the person who has the property already, or who stands to take it. Obligation follows inheritance with unusual literalness in this statute, and Section 4, sub-section (4) completes the logic by requiring that a relative with sufficient means maintain the senior citizen provided the relative is in possession of the property or would inherit it, and by apportioning the duty among several heirs in the proportion in which they would inherit. The succession question, who inherits and in what shares, is therefore not background colour in a relative case; it is an element of the claim, and it is governed by the succession law applicable to the parties, including, for Hindus, the scheme recast by the [Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005](/2016/10/15/hindu-succession-amendment-2005/), which owns the account of how those shares are computed.

### What counts as maintenance under the Act?

Maintenance, by Section 2, clause (b), includes provision for food, clothing, residence and medical attendance and treatment. The definition is inclusive rather than exhaustive, and its four named heads fix the character of the entitlement: subsistence, shelter and care, measured monthly, and not education, travel, ceremony or any of the wider heads a civil court might entertain.

The remaining definitions are machinery. Minor takes its meaning from the Majority Act, 1875. Property, by clause (f), means property of any kind, movable or immovable, ancestral or self-acquired, tangible or intangible, and includes rights or interests in such property; the breadth matters for Section 23, which is not confined to houses even though houses dominate its use. Welfare, by clause (k), means provision for food, health care, recreation centres and other amenities necessary for senior citizens, a definition that serves the welfare chapters rather than the maintenance claim. Tribunal means the Maintenance Tribunal constituted under Section 7. Prescribed means prescribed by state rules, which is the hinge on which the ceiling, the procedure and much else swings from state to state.

One structural observation completes the definitions. The Act defines its people by relationship and by property, never by desert. There is no definition of neglect, no disqualification for a parent whose own conduct is alleged to have been wanting, and no defence written into the entertainment of the claim that a child's resources are committed elsewhere. Tribunals have had to work out, case by case, how a respondent's own dependants, illnesses and obligations bear on quantum, because the Act's only textual measure is the one Section 4 supplies: the needs of the senior citizen so that the citizen may lead a normal life, set against inability to maintain from the citizen's own earning or property. That measure is the subject of the next section.

## The maintenance entitlement in Section 4

Section 4 is the entitlement section, and it is built from a condition, a respondent class and a standard. The condition, in sub-section (1), is inability: a senior citizen including a parent who is unable to maintain himself from his own earning or out of the property owned by him is entitled to make an application under Section 5. Inability is a question of fact and degree. A senior citizen may own a house and be unable to maintain himself out of it, because a self-occupied house yields no income; the text says out of the property owned, and tribunals have had to decide whether ownership of an unrealisable or unproductive asset defeats the condition. The better reading, and the one consistent with the Act's purpose, treats inability as a practical test of whether the applicant's earning and property together yield the means of a normal life, not a test of whether the applicant owns anything at all. A contrary reading would produce the absurd result that the Act's own paradigm applicant, the parent who transferred the house to a child and kept nothing, is disentitled by the very transfer Section 23 exists to scrutinise. The two provisions read together resolve the point: property transferred away is not property out of which the transferor can maintain himself, and property retained but barren does not feed its owner.

The respondent class is stated in two limbs. In the case of a parent or grandparent, the application lies against one or more of his children, not being a minor. In the case of a childless senior citizen, it lies against such of his relatives as clause (g) of Section 2 identifies. Note what the first limb does not require: it does not require the child to be the applicant's heir, to live with the applicant, to have taken any property, or to be on speaking terms with the applicant. The duty of a child under this Act is a duty of status, arising from the relationship and the child's majority, and it is the purest expression of the family obligation theory on which the statute rests. The relative's duty, by contrast, is a duty of succession, arising only where property is held or will be inherited, and apportioned by inheritance shares where several relatives qualify. The Act thus runs two different moral logics side by side and assigns each to its class: children owe because they are children; relatives owe because they take, or will take, the property.

The standard, in sub-sections (2) and (3), is that the obligation extends to the needs of the senior citizen or parent so that the citizen or parent may lead a normal life. Normal life is a modest phrase with a deliberate edge. It is not bare survival, the standard of animal subsistence that maintenance jurisprudence under the criminal procedure code was sometimes accused of applying. Nor is it the standard of the applicant's former household or the respondent's present one. It is a needs standard indexed to ordinary dignified living: food, clothing, residence, medical attendance and treatment, the four heads of the definition, provided at a level that lets the applicant live as a member of the community rather than as its dependent embarrassment. When the Karnataka High Court, in proceedings brought by children resisting their parents' maintenance order, described the statutory question as whether a citizen can secure dignity, subsistence and medical aid within the confines of Section 9, it was this standard that the court was measuring the ceiling against. The standard is generous in words. The ceiling, examined below, is where generosity stops.

### What standard of life does the Act promise a parent?

The Act promises a normal life. Sections 4, sub-sections (2) and (3) extend the obligation of children and relatives to the needs of the senior citizen or parent so that the parent may lead a normal life, a standard above bare subsistence and below the household standard of either generation.

Two further features of Section 4 reward attention. The first is that the entitlement is to make an application. The Act does not itself quantify or impose the duty in the abstract; it creates a forum and a procedure, and the duty becomes an enforceable monthly sum only when a tribunal orders it. That is why the machinery sections, which a summary account treats as procedure and skips, are in truth half the statute's substance: an entitlement that depends on a tribunal existing, sitting and deciding within ninety days is only as strong as the state's performance in constituting and staffing that tribunal. The second feature is the absence of any corresponding duty on the state to pay where children cannot or will not. If the respondents lack means, the tribunal's order is worth what their means are worth, and the applicant's fallback is the welfare chapters: the old age home for the indigent under Section 19 and the pension architecture that lives outside this Act altogether in the social assistance programmes. The Act assumes a family with means and legislates for its failure of will. For failure of capacity it has sympathy, machinery and very little money. That asymmetry is the deepest structural fact about the statute, and it returns in the assessment below.

## How a maintenance application moves

Section 5 governs the application, and its design choices are the Act's answer to the complaint that the old procedure was slow and expensive. An application may be made by the senior citizen or parent; if the applicant is incapable, by any other person or by an organisation authorised by the applicant, where organisation means a voluntary association registered under the Societies Registration Act, 1860 or any other law in force; and the tribunal may take cognizance suo motu. The suo motu limb deserves emphasis, because it converts the tribunal from a passive forum into an institution that can begin a case on information received, a design suited to applicants who are infirm, illiterate, confined to a house they no longer control, or unaware that the forum exists. The authorised organisation limb does complementary work: a registered voluntary association can carry the application of an incapable applicant, which gives the senior citizens' organisations that campaigned for this statute a formal place inside its procedure.

Pendency is not supposed to mean destitution. Section 5, sub-section (2) empowers the tribunal, during the pendency of the proceeding, to order the children or relative to make a monthly allowance for the interim maintenance of the applicant and to pay it as the tribunal directs. Interim maintenance is the provision that determines whether the ninety day period is a bridge or a siege. An applicant who must fund three months of survival while the tribunal inquires is an applicant the delay itself defeats, whatever the final order says. Tribunals that use the interim power early make the Act work; the power is discretionary in text, and the discipline of using it is one of the clearest measures of a tribunal's quality.

On receipt of the application, Section 5, sub-section (3) requires the tribunal to give notice to the children or relative, give the parties an opportunity of being heard, and hold an inquiry for determining the amount of maintenance. Sub-section (4) then sets the clock: an application for the monthly allowance shall be disposed of within ninety days from the date of service of notice on the respondent, provided that the tribunal may extend the period once, for a maximum of thirty days, in exceptional circumstances and for reasons recorded in writing. The clock runs from service, not from filing, which protects respondents from orders built on unserved applications and gives applicants a reason to press for prompt service. The single extension, capped and reasoned, is the Act's discipline against the adjournment culture of the courts it was designed to bypass. Whether the discipline holds in a given district is an empirical question on which published figures vary and are not asserted here; the design, at least, is unambiguous about what delay is permissible.

The remaining limbs of Section 5 complete the procedural picture. An application may be filed against one or more persons, and a respondent child or relative may implead another person liable to maintain the applicant, so that the duty can be spread across the whole liable class in one proceeding rather than serially. Where an order is made against more than one person, the death of one does not affect the liability of the others to continue paying. The allowance is payable from the date of the order or, if the tribunal so orders, from the date of the application, a retrospection limb that prevents a respondent from profiting by procedural delay. And sub-section (8) carries the teeth that are more fully examined with enforcement below: a respondent who fails without sufficient cause to comply may face a warrant for levying the amount as a fine and imprisonment for up to one month for each month's unpaid allowance, subject to the condition that no warrant issues unless application for levy is made within three months of the amount falling due.

Section 6 supplies jurisdiction and the coercive procedure. Proceedings may be taken against a child or relative in any district where the applicant resides or last resided, or where the child or relative resides. The applicant's district is thus an available forum, sparing an elderly person the journey to a respondent who has moved away, and the respondent's district is available where the applicant prefers it. The tribunal issues process for procuring the respondent's presence and, for securing attendance, has the power of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class under the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. Evidence is taken in the respondent's presence and recorded in the manner prescribed for summons cases, with an ex parte limb where the tribunal is satisfied the respondent is wilfully avoiding service or wilfully neglecting to attend. Where the respondent resides outside India, summons is served through the authority the Central Government specifies by notification, a provision that speaks directly to the demographic reality of elderly parents whose children have emigrated. Finally, Section 6, sub-section (6) inserts conciliation into the heart of the process: before hearing the application, the tribunal may refer it to a Conciliation Officer, who must submit findings within one month, and if an amicable settlement is arrived at, the tribunal passes an order to that effect. The Conciliation Officer may be a person or an organisation's representative of the kind Section 5 contemplates, a Maintenance Officer designated under Section 18, or any other person the tribunal nominates. The conciliation stage is not decoration. In a jurisdiction where the parties must often continue to share a house, a staircase or a family after the order, a settlement the parties have made is worth more than an order one of them resents, and the Act's drafters built that preference into the procedure rather than leaving it to chance.

### How does the tribunal compel attendance and take evidence?

The tribunal holds the powers of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class for securing attendance, and all the powers of a civil court for taking evidence on oath, enforcing the attendance of witnesses and compelling the production of documents, with an ex parte power where a respondent wilfully avoids service or attendance.

The general information in this article is legal information and analysis, and it does not substitute for advice on an individual case, in which limitation periods, state rules and the facts of a particular transfer or household will decide the outcome. That sentence belongs here, at the point where procedure has been described and a reader might be tempted to treat a general map as a personal route.

## Two cases through the machinery

The machinery is best understood by watching it work on the two paradigm cases, stated as illustrations of the provisions already examined and not as accounts of decided litigation.

Take first a maintenance case in its commonest form. A widow of seventy one lives in the house she and her husband occupied, in a sub-division town. Her husband's pension died with him. She has two sons and a daughter, all major, all earning, none contributing. Her own property is the house, which yields nothing. She applies under Section 5 in the district where she resides, which Section 6 permits. The tribunal issues process, and before the hearing it refers the application to a Conciliation Officer, who reports within the month that the daughter is willing to pay a monthly sum and the sons are not willing to pay anything, each pointing to the other. Conciliation having failed as a complete settlement, the tribunal proceeds to inquiry. It may order interim maintenance under Section 5, sub-section (2) at this point, and a tribunal alert to the applicant's position will do so, because the ninety day clock runs from service and the applicant's need does not wait for findings. At the inquiry the questions are the two the Act poses: her inability, tested against her earning and the yield of her property, on which the unproductive house does not defeat her; and the respondents' means, on which each respondent may implead the others under Section 5, sub-section (5) so that the burden is spread in the single proceeding. The tribunal fixes a monthly rate for each respondent within the state prescribed maximum and the Act's ten thousand rupee ceiling, directs payment, orders the amounts deposited within thirty days of the order as Section 13 requires, and may add interest where non-payment has already caused loss. If a son defaults, the widow's remedy is the levy warrant and, behind it, imprisonment of up to one month for each month's default, provided she applies for levy within three months of the amount falling due. If she is dissatisfied with the rate, she may appeal within sixty days to the Appellate Tribunal presided over by the District Magistrate, while the respondents, who must keep paying meanwhile, have no statutory appeal and must look to the High Court's constitutional review if they say the order is unlawful. Every step in that sequence is a provision examined above, doing exactly the work it was designed for.

Take secondly the property case, which is the same family's other face. A father of sixty eight, persuaded that his son will look after him, executes a registered gift deed of the family house in the son's favour. A document recording the son's promise to maintain him is signed the same week. Within two years the father is living in one room of the house he gave away, his food and medicine are grudged, and he is told to leave. His application is not, in substance, for a monthly allowance, since no allowance within the ceiling would restore his position, and his advisers, meaning in this forum the Maintenance Officer and any organisation acting for him rather than a lawyer, frame the application under Section 23. The tribunal's inquiry has the two Sudesh Chhikara questions at its centre. Was the transfer made subject to the condition that the transferee provide the basic amenities and basic physical needs of the transferor? The deed and the promise document are read together, on the method Urmila Dixit approved, and the tribunal must record a finding, not an assumption. Has the transferee refused or failed to provide them? The father's evidence of his present circumstances, taken in the respondent's presence as Section 6 requires, either establishes the failure or it does not. If both findings are made, the tribunal declares the transfer void at the father's option, the transfer being deemed by the statute to have been made by fraud, coercion or undue influence, and, on Urmila Dixit's holding, it may direct that possession be restored to him. If another occupant of the house, a daughter in law, asserts a right to reside in a shared household under the domestic violence legislation, the tribunal must then do the Vanitha work: advert to that claim, hear it, and mould its relief so that the father's protection does not simply erase her statutory right. The sequence shows why this provision, and not the maintenance code around it, draws the litigation. It is the only route in Indian law by which that father recovers that house in that time, and everybody in the district, including the son, knows it.

The two illustrations also mark the machinery's limits. In the first, the ceiling compresses a three respondent family's combined duty into a sum the state has prescribed below ten thousand rupees, whatever the inquiry finds the need and the means to be. In the second, everything depends on a documentary condition and on findings recorded by an officer whose legal training the Act does not guarantee. The Act's strengths and its weaknesses are not distributed between good states and bad states alone. They are present, in embryo, inside its two paradigm cases.

## The Maintenance Tribunal and the Appellate Tribunal

Section 7 obliges the State Government, within six months of the commencement of the Act in that state, to constitute by notification, for each sub-division, one or more Maintenance Tribunals to adjudicate and decide upon orders for maintenance under Section 5. The tribunal is presided over by an officer not below the rank of a Sub-Divisional Officer of the state. Where two or more tribunals are constituted for an area, the state may regulate the distribution of business among them. The choice of the sub-divisional officer, the revenue administrator who also exercises magisterial functions in the sub-division, is one of the Act's most consequential and most debated decisions. Its rationale is accessibility and local knowledge: the sub-divisional officer is the state's most senior generalist officer at the level where the applicant lives, commands the local administrative machinery, and can act with a speed the district judiciary's list rarely permits. Its cost is equally plain. The officer is an executive administrator carrying a heavy general charge, for whom tribunal work is one duty among many, without the legal training, the tenure protections or the adjudicatory specialisation of a judge, and the quality of tribunal justice therefore varies with the officer in a way that court justice, for all its delays, is structured to restrain. The courts reviewing tribunal orders have had to hold both truths at once, insisting on natural justice and reasoned findings from a forum Parliament deliberately kept informal.

Section 8 prescribes the procedure of the inquiry: the tribunal may, subject to state rules, follow such summary procedure as it deems fit. It has all the powers of a civil court for taking evidence on oath, enforcing attendance of witnesses, compelling discovery and production of documents and material objects, and such other purposes as may be prescribed, and it is deemed a civil court for the purposes of Section 195 and Chapter XXVI of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, the provisions on offences against public justice. It may also choose one or more persons possessing special knowledge of a relevant matter to assist it in the inquiry. Summary procedure armed with civil court powers is the Act's procedural signature: the form of a conversation, the compulsion of a court. The signature works when the presiding officer understands that summary describes the route, not the standard of fairness. Notice, hearing, evidence in the respondent's presence and a reasoned order are all written into Sections 5 and 6, and an order that skips them is vulnerable on review however summary the tribunal's mandate.

Section 15 constitutes the appellate tier: the State Government may constitute one Appellate Tribunal for each district to hear appeals against the tribunal's orders, presided over by an officer not below the rank of District Magistrate. Section 16 then makes a choice that surprises every first reader and that the 2019 Bill proposed to reverse. As enacted, the right of appeal is given to a senior citizen or a parent aggrieved by an order of the tribunal, who may appeal within sixty days of the order, with a condonation limb where the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause. The text gives the appeal to the applicant side. A child or relative against whom an order has been made is not, in terms, given an appeal by Section 16, and must find a remedy in constitutional review by the High Court. The asymmetry was defended as consistent with the Act's protective design, and criticised as a denial of an ordinary incident of adjudication to the party who bears the order's burden; the criticism has force precisely because maintenance orders, and still more Section 23 orders, can determine substantial rights. The appeal that does lie carries its own disciplines. The respondent must continue to pay the amount ordered while the appeal is pending, as the Appellate Tribunal directs. The Appellate Tribunal serves notice on the respondent, may call for the record, must not reject an appeal without hearing both parties in person or through a duly authorised representative, and must endeavour to pronounce its order in writing within one month of receipt of the appeal. Its order is final, and a copy goes to both parties free of cost.

Section 17 is short enough to quote in substance entire: notwithstanding anything contained in any law, no party to a proceeding before a tribunal or an Appellate Tribunal shall be represented by a legal practitioner. Section 18 supplies the partial counterweight: the State Government designates the District Social Welfare Officer, or an officer not below that rank, as Maintenance Officer, and the Maintenance Officer shall represent a parent if the parent so desires during the proceedings before the tribunal or the Appellate Tribunal. The representation the Act forbids is professional representation for either side. The representation it provides is official representation for one side, the parent's, at the parent's choice. A senior citizen who is not a parent, claiming against a relative, is not named in Section 18's representation limb, a small asymmetry inside the larger one that the 2019 Bill's proposal to place maintenance officers on a firmer statutory footing would partly have answered. Where a party needs help that is neither a lawyer's advocacy nor an officer's representation, the legal services architecture exists outside the Act: the district legal services authorities provide free legal services and advice to those who qualify, under the framework of the [Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987](/2021/06/15/legal-services-authorities-act-1987/), which owns the account of that assistance and its eligibility rules. Advice before a proceeding, and help in preparing papers, are not representation in the proceeding, and the distinction is the practical channel through which legally informed help lawfully reaches parties in a forum lawyers may not address.

The bar on legal practitioners receives its full two-sided treatment in the assessment section below. Its machinery context belongs here: Parliament did not bar lawyers from a court, where their presence is an incident of the forum, but from a tribunal it designed as an administrative conversation about a family's means and needs. The same Parliament gave the tribunal a magistrate's coercive powers and a civil court's evidentiary powers, and gave its orders the force of orders under Chapter IX of the criminal procedure code. The forum is thus informal in advocacy and formal in effect, and that combination, rather than either feature alone, is what the fairness debate is actually about.

## The order, the ceiling, and enforcement

Section 9 is the order-making power, and its two sub-sections contain the whole maintenance story in miniature. Sub-section (1) provides that if children or relatives neglect or refuse to maintain a senior citizen who is unable to maintain himself, the tribunal may, on being satisfied of the neglect or refusal, order them to make a monthly allowance at such monthly rate as the tribunal deems fit, payable as the tribunal directs. The discretion as to rate, within the ceiling, is the tribunal's, exercised on the inquiry into means and needs. Sub-section (2) then fixes the boundary: the maximum maintenance allowance which may be ordered shall be such as may be prescribed by the State Government, which shall not exceed ten thousand rupees per month. Two ceilings are stacked. The state prescribes a maximum by rule, and the Act forbids that maximum to exceed ten thousand rupees. A state may prescribe less; several prescribed less in the Act's early years; none may prescribe more. The figure is a ceiling on the aggregate order a tribunal makes on an application, expressed as a monthly rate, and it has stood unrevised in the Act since 2007. The only amending attention the Act has received on this point is the 2019 Bill, which proposed to delete the ceiling altogether and lapsed unpassed, and the periodic judicial exhortation, of which the Karnataka High Court's is the most fully reasoned, that Parliament revisit a figure the cost of living has left behind.

Around the order, Chapter II builds a complete maintenance code in miniature. Section 10 empowers the tribunal to alter the allowance on proof of misrepresentation or mistake of fact or a change in the circumstances of the person receiving or paying, and obliges it to cancel or vary an order where a competent civil court's decision requires that course. Section 11 gives the applicant a copy of the order free of fee, makes the order enforceable by any tribunal where the respondent is found, on satisfaction as to identity and non-payment, and gives a maintenance order the same force and effect as an order under Chapter IX of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, executable in the manner prescribed for such orders. Section 12 resolves the overlap with that code: a senior citizen or parent entitled under both regimes may claim under either, but not under both. The election rule prevents double recovery for the same need while preserving the applicant's choice of forum, a choice that is real, since the criminal procedure route has no ten thousand rupee ceiling and no maintenance tribunal route offers its combination of suo motu cognizance, conciliation and a parent side Maintenance Officer. Section 13 requires the respondent to deposit the entire amount ordered within thirty days of the announcement of the order, in the manner the tribunal directs. Section 14 empowers the tribunal to award simple interest on the maintenance allowed, at a rate it determines between five per cent and eighteen per cent, from a date not earlier than the application. Interest between a floor and a ceiling is a distinctive touch: it compensates the applicant for the period of non-payment without converting the tribunal into a commercial forum, and its presence in the Act is a reminder that the drafters understood delay as a form of refusal.

Enforcement for breach sits in Section 5, sub-section (8), read with Section 11. Where the respondent fails without sufficient cause to comply, the tribunal may, for every breach, issue a warrant for levying the amount due in the manner provided for levying fines, and may sentence the defaulter to imprisonment for a term which may extend to one month, or until payment if sooner made, for the whole or any part of each month's allowance remaining unpaid after execution of the warrant. The proviso disciplines the applicant as well: no warrant issues for recovery unless application to levy is made within three months of the amount falling due, so arrears cannot be banked indefinitely and then detonated. The enforcement design is borrowed from the criminal procedure maintenance model and shares its character: coercive rather than compensatory, aimed at making payment the easier course, and dependent for its effect on the tribunal's willingness to use it against respondents who are, after all, the applicant's children. Many tribunals hesitate at the prison door of a family case, and the Act's answer to hesitation is the warrant for levy, which reaches money without reaching the person. A maintenance order under this Act is therefore neither a decree nor a mere recommendation. It is an executive judicial hybrid, summarily made, freely copied, portable across districts, backed by levy and by short imprisonment, and capped at a figure that no amount of enforcement ingenuity can raise.

## Means and needs: how the figure is actually reached

Between the entitlement and the ceiling sits the inquiry itself, and a pillar that omitted it would leave the reader knowing the law of the order but not its arithmetic. The tribunal's inquiry under Section 5, sub-section (3) is directed to determining the amount of maintenance, and the amount is a function of two findings: what the applicant needs in order to lead a normal life, and what each respondent can pay without the order becoming an instrument of a different injustice.

On the needs side, the heads are given by the definition of maintenance: food, clothing, residence and medical attendance and treatment. A tribunal working properly prices them locally rather than abstractly. Residence is the head that decides most contested cases, because an applicant who lives in a house owned by a respondent, or by the applicant, has a residence cost of a different order from an applicant who must rent in the same town, and an order that ignores the distinction either overpays the housed applicant against the ceiling or underpays the renting applicant against the standard. Medical need is the head most likely to be documented, through prescriptions, hospital records and the recurring cost of treatment for chronic conditions, and it is the head that grows during the life of an order, which is why Section 10's alteration power is most often invoked, in substance, by applicants whose treatment has become more expensive. Food and clothing are ordinarily taken at a standard figure for the district rather than proved item by item, and a tribunal that demanded receipts for food from an eighty year old applicant would be converting a summary inquiry into the kind of proof contest the Act was designed to replace.

On the means side, the inquiry reaches the respondent's earning, the yield of the respondent's property, and the respondent's own obligations, including a spouse, minor children and, not infrequently, another parent. Salary and pension are the easiest means to establish, through the employer and the paying authority, both of which the tribunal's civil court powers for compelling production of documents can reach. Business and agricultural means are harder, and here the sub-divisional officer's local knowledge, the design feature on which the forum choice rests, does work that no paper procedure replicates: the presiding officer frequently knows, or can learn through the revenue machinery, what a holding yields and what a shop in the sub-division turns over. Apportionment among respondents follows from the structure of the application. Where several children are liable, the tribunal distributes the total it has found needed among them by reference to their respective means, and the respondent's right to implead other liable persons ensures that the child who happens to live nearest, or to be on worst terms with the applicant, does not carry the whole duty by accident of residence. For a childless senior citizen claiming against relatives, the apportionment rule is supplied by Section 4, sub-section (4) itself: maintenance is payable by the relatives in the proportion in which they would inherit the property, a rule that makes the succession computation part of the tribunal's order.

Two contested questions recur in the inquiry and should be flagged rather than resolved by assertion. The first is the married daughter. The definition of children includes a daughter without qualification, and the obligation of a daughter is not displaced by her marriage; tribunals have so applied the Act, consistently with the position under the criminal procedure route, where a married daughter with means is liable for a parent's maintenance. The second is the applicant's own past conduct, including the transfer of property to one child to the exclusion of others, or estrangement said to be of the applicant's making. The Act writes no disentitlement for conduct into Section 4, and the tribunal's textual questions remain inability, relationship and means; conduct may bear on credibility and on the Section 23 condition where that section is invoked, but a tribunal that refuses maintenance to an indigent parent as a judgment on the parent's life has left the statute it administers. The ceiling then does its work at the end of the inquiry rather than the beginning: the tribunal totals the needs, apportions the means, and only then discovers, in the high need cases, that the figure it has justified exceeds the figure it may order, at which point the order is written at the maximum and the shortfall is absorbed by the applicant. That sequence, repeated across the tribunal system, is the lived meaning of the next section's argument.

## The ceiling as design, and as failure

The ceiling deserves a fair hearing before it is condemned, because it was not a drafting oversight. Three reasons supported a low fixed maximum in 2007, and each can be stated in the form its proponents would recognise. The first is jurisdictional discipline: a summary tribunal presided over by a revenue officer, proceeding without lawyers, was thought a suitable forum for need-based support at a subsistence-plus level, while larger claims, involving disputed means, businesses, partitions and accounts, belonged in civil courts with pleadings, evidence law and appeals. The ceiling marked the boundary of the summary jurisdiction, much as pecuniary limits mark the boundary of any small forum. The second reason is fiscal and administrative uniformity: a prescribed state maximum within a national cap let states calibrate the order to local costs while preventing the tribunal system from becoming a lottery of districts. The third reason is relational: the order was conceived as a floor of decency extracted quickly, not as a settlement of the family's whole economy, and a modest certain sum paid monthly was valued over a large contested sum paid never.

Each reason has decayed. The jurisdictional argument is undermined by the Act itself, which gave the same tribunal, in Section 23, an uncapped power over property of any value, exercised in the same summary procedure and with the same bar on lawyers; a forum trusted to unwind a crore rupee gift deed cannot coherently be distrusted with a maintenance claim of fifteen thousand rupees a month. The uniformity argument survives only as a description of the prescription mechanism, not of the figure, since uniformity in an eroded figure is uniformity in inadequacy. The relational argument retains real force, because speed and certainty do matter to an applicant who needs money monthly, but it justifies a summary procedure and a prompt interim order rather than a permanent nominal cap; nothing in the case for speed requires the maximum to be the same number in the Act's twentieth year as in its first. The decisive decay is arithmetic. A ceiling fixed in rupees and never indexed falls in real value every year, and the Karnataka High Court, measuring the figure against the cost inflation index published by the Ministry of Finance, put the comparison in terms a legislature cannot easily unhear: what one hundred rupees procured in the year of enactment requires, on that index, very substantially more in the years since, while the ten thousand rupee cap has remained, in the court's word, petrified. The court's conclusion was institutional rather than merely sympathetic. It asked the Union government to revisit Section 9 and revise the ceiling in line with the cost of living, holding that so meagre a maintenance cannot achieve the objects of an Act whose own standard is a normal life.

The verdict this pillar records is the one the evidence supports. The ceiling was a defensible design choice in 2007 and is an indefensible one in its unrevised state, not because ten thousand rupees was the wrong number at enactment but because a nominal figure in a welfare statute is a promise with a half-life, and Parliament built no mechanism, neither indexation nor periodic revision nor a delegated power to raise the cap, by which the promise could keep its value. The 2019 Bill's solution, deletion of the ceiling and trust in the tribunal's assessment of means and needs, was the cleaner of the two available fixes, and its lapse is examined below. The alternative fix, an indexed or revisable cap, would have preserved the jurisdictional theory. Parliament has enacted neither, and the tribunal system therefore administers a standard of normal life through a maximum that the standard itself refutes.

## State variation: one Act, many operative versions

A reader who has understood the commencement structure will expect the operative Act to vary by state, and the expectation is correct in at least four dimensions, each of which a practitioner must check in the state rules before advising on any figure or form.

The first dimension is time. The Act came into force state by state on the dates state governments appointed by notification, tribunals were to be constituted within six months of the commencement applicable to the state, and the transitional provisions, including the right of a parent with a pending application under Chapter IX of the criminal procedure code to withdraw it and come to the tribunal, operated by reference to that state commencement. Two identical disputes arising in different states in the Act's early years could therefore meet different legal worlds, one with a functioning tribunal and one with none, without either state being in breach of anything but the six month direction.

The second dimension is money. The ten thousand rupee figure is the Act's outer wall, but the operative ceiling in a given state is the maximum that state has prescribed by rule under Section 9, sub-section (2) read with the rule making power in Section 32, and states prescribed different maxima, several of them well below the wall in the Act's early period. An order is lawful or excessive by reference to the state prescription, not by reference to the headline figure alone, and commentary that treats ten thousand rupees as the amount a tribunal may order in every state overstates the position wherever a state has prescribed less.

The third dimension is procedure and personnel. Section 8 subjects the tribunal's summary procedure to state rules. Section 32 empowers states to prescribe the manner of the inquiry, the tribunal's powers and procedure for other purposes, the management scheme for old age homes, the powers and duties of implementing authorities, and the action plan for protection of life and property. States have used this space differently: some prescribe application forms, conciliation panels and tribunal composition in detail, some fold the tribunal's work into the revenue administration's ordinary business, and the designation of Maintenance Officers and Conciliation Officers, on which the applicant side's experience of the forum largely depends, is a state act throughout. The result is that the Act's text is national and its texture is local, and comparative statements about how the Act works, unqualified by state, should be read with that reservation.

The fourth dimension is the welfare chapters. The old age home floor of one home in each district for a minimum of one hundred and fifty indigent senior citizens is expressed as the beginning of a phased programme, and the medical support duties of Section 20 are duties to ensure facilities in government and government funded hospitals, so the distance between the text and the institution is travelled, or not travelled, by state budgets and state health administrations. The Standing Committee's district coverage figures, recorded in the section on the 2019 Bill, are a national aggregate of many different state stories, and the Act's own review mechanism in Section 31, periodic central review and monitoring of implementation by state governments, has not produced the kind of published state by state account that would let a reader rank those stories on evidence. The honest comparative statement is therefore structural rather than numerical: the maintenance chapters vary by commencement, prescription and rules, and the welfare chapters vary by appropriation, and both kinds of variation are features of the design Parliament chose, not accidents that befell it.

## Two applications, two outcomes

The findable artifact of this pillar sets the Act's two great applications side by side, because separating them is the single most useful thing a reference table can do for this statute. The Act is named for the first column and used mainly for the second.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Who may apply | A senior citizen or parent unable to maintain himself or herself; an authorised person or registered voluntary organisation for an incapable applicant; the tribunal may act suo motu | A senior citizen who has transferred property after the commencement of the Act; an authorised organisation may act for a senior citizen incapable of enforcing the right |
| Against whom | Children, meaning son, daughter, grandson or granddaughter, not being a minor; for a childless senior citizen, a relative who holds or would inherit the property | The transferee who received the property subject to the condition of providing basic amenities and basic physical needs and who refuses or fails to provide them |

Read the two columns against each other and the statute's litigation history becomes predictable. The left column offers a small, capped, monthly sum, obtainable quickly, against respondents whose duty is plain. The right column offers the recovery of the family home, obtainable in the same forum, at the same speed, with the same absence of lawyers, against a transferee whose failure to maintain is frequently as plain. Families in conflict choose the remedy that decides the real dispute, and the real dispute, in a country where the house is the principal asset of most elderly households, is the house.

## Section 23: the transfer provision

Section 23, sub-section (1) must be read in its own words before it is read in anyone else's. Where any senior citizen who, after the commencement of this Act, has transferred by way of gift or otherwise his property, subject to the condition that the transferee shall provide the basic amenities and basic physical needs to the transferor, and such transferee refuses or fails to provide such amenities and physical needs, the transfer shall be deemed to have been made by fraud or coercion or under undue influence and shall, at the option of the transferor, be declared void by the tribunal. Every clause of that sentence is a working part. The transferor must be a senior citizen; a parent under sixty who transfers property cannot invoke the section, however meritorious the complaint. The transfer must be after the commencement of the Act in the relevant state, a temporal condition examined in the questions at the end of this article. The transfer may be by gift or otherwise, words the Supreme Court in Sudesh Chhikara confirmed cover all kinds of transfers, including a release deed. The transfer must be subject to the condition that the transferee provide the basic amenities and basic physical needs of the transferor. And the transferee must refuse or fail to provide them. On those elements being established, the statute performs its striking move: it does not say the transfer is voidable for breach of condition, a familiar property law result. It deems the transfer to have been made by fraud or coercion or undue influence, importing the vitiating factors of contract law by fiction, and makes the transfer void at the option of the transferor on the tribunal's declaration.

The fiction deserves attention because it explains the provision's reach and its limits. A gift under the general law of transfer, the law organised by the [Transfer of Property Act, 1882](/2014/01/01/transfer-of-property-act-india/), is a voluntary transfer without consideration, complete on acceptance, and irrevocable except on grounds the deed itself reserves or the general law of fraud, coercion and undue influence supplies, to be established in a civil suit. Section 23 relocates that inquiry. Instead of the transferor suing to prove that a deed executed years earlier was procured by undue influence, a fact intensive question on which elderly plaintiffs routinely failed, the transferor proves two present facts: a condition of care attached to the transfer, and a failure of care. The statute then supplies the vitiating factor by deeming. The condition is the hinge, which is why the case law is entirely about the condition: how it may be expressed, how it may be proved, and whether it can be implied from the circumstances of a gift made, as the Supreme Court put it in Sudesh Chhikara, out of love and affection. That case law is the next section.

Sub-section (2) is a quieter and older idea. Where a senior citizen has a right to receive maintenance out of an estate and the estate or part of it is transferred, the right to receive maintenance may be enforced against the transferee if the transferee has notice of the right or if the transfer is gratuitous, but not against a transferee for consideration and without notice of the right. This is the doctrine of the bona fide purchaser written into a maintenance statute: the maintenance right follows the estate into the hands of a donee and of a purchaser who knew, and stops at the door of the purchaser who paid value in ignorance. Sub-section (3) permits action on behalf of a senior citizen incapable of enforcing the rights under either sub-section to be taken by an organisation of the kind Section 5 contemplates. Together the three sub-sections make Chapter V a small property code for the elderly: undo the conditional transfer, follow the maintenance right into transferred hands, and let an organisation act for the citizen who cannot act alone.

The relationship between Section 23 and the general law of gifts deserves one more pass, because applicants arrive at tribunals carrying the general law's assumptions and must leave them at the door selectively. Under the general law organised by the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, a completed gift is a serious act: it requires a registered instrument for immovable property, attestation, and acceptance by the donee, and once complete it cannot be revoked merely because the donor regrets it or because the donee's conduct disappoints. The parties may agree that the gift shall be revocable on the happening of a specified event not dependent on the donor's will, and a gift procured by fraud, coercion or undue influence is voidable on proof of those facts in a civil suit. Section 23 keeps one element of that scheme and discards the rest. It keeps the idea that a care condition must be part of the bargain; Sudesh Chhikara is, at bottom, the general law's insistence that a condition be proved, carried into the tribunal. It discards the requirement to prove a vitiating factor, supplying fraud, coercion and undue influence by deeming once the condition and its breach are proved. It discards the civil suit, substituting the tribunal. And it discards the general law's indifference to the donor's welfare after the transfer, making the transferee's continuing provision of basic amenities and basic physical needs the price of keeping the property. An applicant who understands that exchange understands why the section is neither a general power to revoke gifts, which it is not, nor a mere restatement of revocation doctrine, which it is much more than.

Two boundary questions recur and should be answered in the pillar rather than left to the questions section. The first is the transfer that is not a gift: a settlement, a release, a relinquishment, a transfer to a family arrangement. The words by way of gift or otherwise, confirmed in Sudesh Chhikara to cover all kinds of transfers, bring them in, provided the senior citizen transferor, the condition and the failure are proved; the form of the instrument is not the boundary, the condition is. The second is the transfer to a stranger for value, where the transferee's failure to maintain is not the complaint because no care was ever promised. Section 23, sub-section (1) does not reach it, and sub-section (2) protects the maintenance right against such a transferee only where the transferee had notice or took gratuitously. Between them, the two sub-sections draw a coherent map: care based transfers can be undone, maintenance rights follow the estate against those who knew or paid nothing, and the market in property is otherwise left alone. A provision is best respected when its boundaries are stated as clearly as its powers, and Section 23's boundaries are textual, deliberate and, after the two Supreme Court decisions, well patrolled.

### What makes Section 23 different from an ordinary claim to set aside a gift?

Section 23 does not require the senior citizen to prove fraud, coercion or undue influence as facts. It requires proof of a conditional transfer and of a failure to provide basic amenities and basic physical needs, and then deems the vitiating factor into existence, in a summary tribunal rather than a civil suit.

The provision's practical supremacy within the Act follows from the economics set out in the table above. It is uncapped. It concerns the asset that matters most. It bypasses the civil court whose jurisdiction Section 27 bars for matters to which the Act applies, substituting a forum measured in weeks. And it reaches a result, the deed undone, that no maintenance order can approximate, because a parent restored to ownership of the house has recovered security, status and bargaining power together, of which the ten thousand rupee month was only ever the smallest part.

## The courts on Section 23: condition, fiction and possession

The jurisprudence on Section 23 answers three questions in sequence. What must be proved about the condition? How strictly is the provision to be read? And what follows from a declaration of voidness: can the tribunal put the senior citizen back into the house, or must the citizen take the declaration to another forum and sue for possession?

Sudesh Chhikara v. Ramti Devi, decided by the Supreme Court on 6 December 2022, answers the first question and frames the second. A mother had executed a release deed in favour of her daughters and then sought its cancellation under Section 23, and the tribunal and the High Court had obliged. The Supreme Court set the orders aside. For sub-section (1) of Section 23 to be attracted, the Court held, two conditions must be fulfilled: the transfer must have been made subject to the condition that the transferee shall provide the basic amenities and basic physical needs to the transferor, and the transferee must refuse or fail to provide such amenities and needs. If both are satisfied, the deeming fiction operates and the transfer becomes voidable at the transferor's instance. The Court's reasoning is as important as its test. When a senior citizen parts with property by gift or release in favour of near and dear ones, the Court observed, a condition of looking after the senior citizen is not necessarily attached to it; very often such transfers are made out of love and affection without expectation in return. Therefore, when a condition is alleged, its existence must be established before the tribunal. In the case at hand the petition did not even plead that the release deed was executed subject to such a condition, and neither the tribunal nor the High Court had recorded a finding on it. The lesson for practice is exact: plead the condition, prove the condition, and obtain a finding on the condition, or the most sympathetic failure of care will not engage the section. The lesson for doctrine is that Section 23, for all its protective purpose, is not a general power to revise family property arrangements that have turned unhappy. It is a conditional power, and the condition is its boundary.

Urmila Dixit v. Sunil Sharan Dixit, decided by the Supreme Court in January 2025, answers the second and third questions and recalibrates the first. A mother had executed a gift deed in favour of her son in 2019 in respect of property she had acquired decades earlier, and a contemporaneous promissory note, a vachan patra, recorded the son's undertaking to take care of her. Alleging that the undertaking stood grossly unfulfilled and that relations had broken down, she invoked Section 23. The tribunal set the gift aside and a single judge of the Madhya Pradesh High Court upheld it, but a Division Bench reversed, treating Section 23 as a standalone provision requiring the condition to appear in the deed itself and denying the tribunal any power to transfer possession. The Supreme Court restored the tribunal's order. The Act, the Court held, is a beneficial piece of legislation aimed at securing the rights of senior citizens in view of the challenges they face, and it must be interpreted in that backdrop, adopting a construction that advances its remedies. The two conditions from Sudesh Chhikara were restated, but they were to be appropriately interpreted to further the beneficial character of the legislation and not so strictly as to render the legislative intent futile. On the facts, the gift deed read with the promissory note established the condition, and its breach was made out. On possession, the Court was categorical: it cannot be said that tribunals exercising jurisdiction under Section 23 cannot order possession to be transferred, and tribunals may order eviction if it is necessary and expedient to ensure the protection of the senior citizen. A contrary view, the Court reasoned, would defeat the purpose of providing simple, speedy and inexpensive remedies to the elderly. The gift was quashed and possession directed to be restored to the mother.

Read together, the two decisions settle the section's working law with a clarity neither has alone. Sudesh Chhikara supplies the elements and forbids their casual assumption; Urmila Dixit forbids their pedantic application, locates the condition in the transaction documents read together rather than in a single deed's recitals alone, and confirms that the tribunal's declaration carries possession with it. Between them lies the tribunal's real task, which is evaluative rather than mechanical: to find, on the documents and the conduct, whether this transfer was made on the footing of care, and whether the care has failed. A tribunal that asks those two questions honestly will rarely be disturbed. A tribunal that skips the first, as in Sudesh Chhikara, or that reads the first so narrowly that no real family transaction could satisfy it, as in the Division Bench view reversed in Urmila Dixit, will be corrected.

### What did the Supreme Court decide in Urmila Dixit?

In Urmila Dixit, decided in January 2025, the Supreme Court held that the condition of care in Section 23 may be established from the transaction documents read together, that the Act being beneficial legislation the conditions must not be read so strictly as to defeat it, and that tribunals may order eviction and restore possession.

The possession holding closes a long contest. High Courts had divided on whether a tribunal that declared a transfer void could also direct the transferee or other occupants to vacate, or whether the senior citizen, declaration in hand, had to file a separate civil suit for possession, a sequence that would have reimposed exactly the delay and expense the Act exists to remove. The beneficial construction line, confirmed at the level of the Supreme Court, treats the power to declare void and the power to restore the transferor to the position the voidness implies as a single remedial power, with eviction available where necessary and expedient for the senior citizen's protection. The qualification matters. Eviction under this Act is not a decree that follows automatically from every declaration, and it is not available at all in a pure maintenance case as a free standing punishment for a defaulting child. It is a consequential power, exercised where restoring the property without restoring possession would leave the senior citizen with a paper victory in a house controlled by the person whose failure of care the tribunal has just found proved. The limiting principle that disciplines the power from the other side is the subject of the next section, and it comes from the same beneficial method: other protective statutes protect other vulnerable people in the same house, and their rights do not dissolve because a senior citizen's claim is just.

## The domestic violence balance: S. Vanitha

Smt. S. Vanitha v. The Deputy Commissioner, Bengaluru Urban District, decided by a three judge bench of the Supreme Court on 15 December 2020, is the most important limiting decision on this Act, and its facts are the hardest the statute generates. A senior citizen couple sought the eviction of their daughter in law and granddaughter from the residential house. The authorities under this Act ordered eviction, and the Karnataka High Court upheld the orders. The daughter in law's answer was the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005: the house, she said, was her shared household, in which Section 17 of that Act gives every woman in a domestic relationship a right to reside, whether or not she has any right, title or beneficial interest in it. Two welfare statutes, each armed with protective purpose, pointed at the same doorway in opposite directions.

The Supreme Court set the eviction order aside and laid down the method. The right of a woman to secure a residence order in respect of a shared household cannot be defeated by the simple expedient of securing an order of eviction by adopting the summary procedure under the Senior Citizens Act. The tribunal may have authority to order eviction where it is necessary and expedient to ensure the maintenance and protection of the senior citizen, but the remedy can be granted only after adverting to the competing claims of the parties; reliefs under the two statutes must be moulded so that neither obliterates the other. The Court's reasoning works through the architecture of both Acts. Section 3 of the Senior Citizens Act gives it overriding effect, but Section 36 of the Domestic Violence Act provides that its provisions are in addition to and not in derogation of any other law, and the enquiry where two special statutes with non-obstante features meet is into their dominant purposes, which are here harmonious in aim even where they collide in application: both protect the vulnerable within the home. The detailed working of the residence right, its orders and its remedies belongs to the pillar on the [Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005](/2015/12/01/domestic-violence-act-2005-explained/), which owns that side of the balance; this pillar's duty is to state what Vanitha requires of tribunals under this Act, and it requires three things. A tribunal facing an eviction request in an occupied family house must identify whether any occupant claims a right of residence under the domestic violence legislation. It must consider that claim on its terms, not treat the senior citizen's title or the summary character of its own procedure as an answer to it. And it must mould its relief, which may include maintenance, protection and, in a proper case, eviction, so that the senior citizen is protected without the summary procedure being used as an instrument to extinguish a competing statutory right by ambush. In Vanitha itself the Court protected the daughter in law's position for a period under its constitutional powers while leaving her to pursue her remedies, an order that captures the decision's spirit exactly: no one's protection is to be built by summary demolition of another's.

What moulding relief means in practice can be made concrete. A tribunal that finds a son has failed to maintain his parents, and that the parents' house is also the shared household of the son's wife, who asserts her residence right and against whom no failure is alleged in her own right, has a range of orders available that a binary grant or refusal of eviction conceals. It may order maintenance and secure it against the son. It may restrain interference with the parents' occupation of identified portions of the house and guarantee their access to its amenities, which is protection of the senior citizens in the sense the Act intends. It may, where the house and the relationships permit, direct arrangements that separate the households within the property. And it retains eviction for the case where the senior citizens cannot be protected in the house at all, exercised after the competing claim has been heard and for reasons recorded against it. The moulding discipline costs the tribunal time, and time is the currency the summary procedure was designed to save. Vanitha holds, in effect, that this is a price the Constitution's own commitments exact: two protective statutes enacted for two vulnerable classes cannot be administered as though only one class were in the room, and a tribunal that saves its ninety days by ignoring the second statute will spend the parties' years in the High Court instead.

The balance Vanitha strikes is sometimes misreported as a holding that senior citizens can never obtain eviction where a daughter in law is in occupation, and sometimes as a holding that the domestic violence right always prevails. It holds neither. It holds that the claims must be confronted together, by a tribunal that knows both statutes are in the room. Urmila Dixit's confirmation of the eviction power four years later does not disturb Vanitha; the two decisions govern different configurations. Where the occupant is the defaulting transferee and no competing residence right is asserted, possession follows voidness. Where an occupant asserts an independent statutory right to reside, the tribunal must advert to it and mould relief. The complete working rule is the conjunction of the two cases, and a tribunal order that recites only one of them is an order waiting to be set aside.

## Protection of life and property beyond Section 23

Chapter V is wider than its famous section. Section 21 obliges the State Government to take all measures to ensure that the provisions of the Act are given wide publicity through public media including television, radio and print at regular intervals, that central and state officers, including police officers and members of the judicial service, receive periodic sensitisation and awareness training on the issues relating to the Act, and that effective coordination is maintained between the services of the ministries and departments dealing with law, home affairs, health and welfare, with periodical review. Section 22 empowers the State Government to confer such powers and impose such duties on a District Magistrate as may be necessary to ensure the provisions of the Act are properly carried out, with the District Magistrate able to specify subordinate officers to exercise those powers and duties within local limits, and obliges the state to prescribe a complete action plan for providing protection of life and property of senior citizens. These sections are the Act's administrative conscience. A maintenance tribunal can decide the application before it; it cannot publicise its own existence to the elderly person who has never heard of it, train the police officer who receives the abandoned parent at the station, or coordinate the hospital, the revenue office and the social welfare department around a citizen whose problem spans all three. Sections 21 and 22 assign those tasks to the state acting through its district head, and the implementation verdict on this Act, in district after district, is substantially a verdict on whether those assignments were performed or merely recited.

Section 27 completes the protection scheme from the jurisdictional side: no civil court shall have jurisdiction in respect of any matter to which any provision of this Act applies, and no injunction shall be granted by any civil court in respect of anything done or intended to be done by or under this Act. The bar is the obverse of the tribunal's summary mandate, preventing the migration of tribunal matters into the slower forum and the use of civil injunctions to stall tribunal orders. It is not a bar on constitutional review, and the High Courts' writ jurisdiction over tribunal orders is the channel through which Sudesh Chhikara, Vanitha and Urmila Dixit all travelled before reaching the Supreme Court. The miscellaneous chapter also makes every officer appointed under the Act a public servant, protects action taken in good faith, gives the Central Government power to direct state governments on carrying the Act into execution and to review and monitor implementation periodically, and confers the rule making power on state governments, whose rules prescribe the ceiling within the statutory maximum, the inquiry procedure, the scheme for old age homes and the action plan. The Centre's review power in Section 31 is worth a sentence, because it is the Act's only built in mechanism for learning whether its own design is working, and a monitoring power that produces no published, comparable state by state account of disposals, ceilings and homes is a mechanism that exists in the text rather than in the administration.

## The offence of abandonment


Chapter VI creates the Act's single offence. Section 24 provides that whoever, having the care or protection of a senior citizen, leaves such senior citizen in any place with the intention of wholly abandoning that senior citizen, shall be punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three months, or with fine which may extend to five thousand rupees, or with both. Section 25 makes every offence under the Act cognizable and bailable, and requires it to be tried summarily by a Magistrate. The elements are narrow and deliberately so. The accused must have the care or protection of the senior citizen, a relational and factual condition that excludes the stranger and the merely unhelpful neighbour. The act is leaving the senior citizen in any place. The intention must be wholly to abandon, a mens rea that excludes the hospital admission, the respite placement and the temporary arrangement, and that prosecutors must prove like any other intention, from conduct. The penalty, three months at most, is the Act's smallest number and its most debated. Defenders of the scale treat the offence as a signalling provision, marking abandonment as public wrongdoing rather than private failure, and point to the maintenance and property remedies as the Act's real enforcement. Critics answer that a signal priced at five thousand rupees signals its own unseriousness, and the 2019 Bill, which proposed more stringent punishment for abuse and abandonment, accepted the criticism in principle. The offence is, on any account of the reported record, rarely the vehicle of a senior citizen's protection. Its function in the scheme is boundary marking: the point at which family failure becomes crime is named, even if the naming is nearly all the state does about it.

The rarity has structural causes that a fair assessment should record, because they qualify the easy conclusion that the penalty is merely too small. A prosecution under Section 24 must ordinarily be set in motion by complaint, and the complainant in the paradigm case is the abandoned citizen or the police who receive that citizen at a station or a hospital, at the moment of maximum dependency on the very family the prosecution would punish. The citizen who prosecutes a son for abandonment has burned the bridge back into the household, and many citizens, offered the choice, prefer the tribunal's maintenance order and the possibility of reconciliation to a conviction that feeds no one. The intention element compounds the reluctance, since a family that has left a parent at a hospital can characterise the act as an admission for treatment with a planned return, and the prosecution must prove the contrary purpose. The offence therefore operates in practice as a charge available in the extreme case, the parent found abandoned at a public place with the family's intention unmistakable, and as a background threat that gives police officers a statutory vocabulary for intervening in a household crisis. The 2019 Bill's proposal of more stringent punishment for abuse and abandonment addressed the scale of the penalty without addressing either of these structural causes, which is a reason to doubt that enactment of that clause alone would have changed the offence's place in the scheme. The deeper reform would have been the Bill's protective apparatus around the offence: nodal police officers in every station, a district special police unit and a helpline, the machinery by which an abandoned citizen is found, advised and routed to the tribunal. That machinery lapsed with the rest of the Bill.

## What the state owes: old age homes and medical care

Chapter III, Section 19, provides that the State Government may establish and maintain such number of old age homes at accessible places as it deems necessary, in a phased manner, beginning with at least one in each district to accommodate in such homes a minimum of one hundred and fifty senior citizens who are indigent, with indigent defined as a senior citizen not having sufficient means, as determined by the State Government from time to time, to maintain himself. The state may prescribe a scheme for the management of the homes, including standards and the types of services necessary for medical care and means of entertainment for the inhabitants. The drafting is a study in soft obligation. The section says may, and then builds a floor inside the discretion: phased establishment, beginning with at least one home in each district, of a stated minimum capacity, for the indigent. The Standing Committee that examined the 2019 Bill recorded that, of more than seven hundred districts, only a fraction in the region of four hundred and eighty two had care homes at all, a figure the Committee used to recommend that the Bill mandate at least one care home and one multi-service day care centre in each district. The figure is the Committee's, stated here as its record of the position it examined, and it captures the chapter's history: a floor expressed as a beginning, administered as an aspiration.

Chapter IV, Section 20, obliges the State Government to ensure that government hospitals and hospitals funded fully or partially by the government provide beds for all senior citizens as far as possible, that separate queues are arranged for senior citizens, that facilities for treatment of chronic, terminal and degenerative diseases are expanded, that research activities for chronic elderly diseases and ageing are expanded, and that earmarked facilities for geriatric patients exist in every district hospital, duly headed by a medical officer with experience in geriatric care. The verbs are stronger here, shall ensure, and the content is specific enough to be auditable hospital by hospital, which is precisely what makes the chapter's uneven performance legible: a separate queue is not a matter of resources on the scale of a tribunal system, and its absence in a district hospital is a small, verifiable failure of a clear duty. The 2019 Bill proposed to extend these facilities to all hospitals including private ones; the enacted text confines them to government and government funded hospitals, and the boundary is one more measure of the distance between the Act's welfare ambitions and its enacted reach.

The welfare chapters have one significant judicial chapter of their own. In Ashwani Kumar v. Union of India, a public interest petition before the Supreme Court concerning the rights of the elderly, the Court in 2018 issued directions directed at the implementation of this Act and the allied pension and welfare schemes, pressing governments on the establishment of old age homes, the provision of geriatric care and medical facilities, and the effective working of the statutory machinery, and it kept the matter within the Court's supervision rather than treating the petition as concluded by a single order. The significance of the intervention for this pillar is diagnostic rather than doctrinal. The maintenance and property provisions generated their jurisprudence through thousands of individual applications, each with an applicant who had something to gain by litigating. The welfare chapters generated theirs through a single public interest petitioner, because no individual senior citizen holds a personal claim under Section 19 or Section 20 that a tribunal can convert into an order against the state. Where a statute's duties run to a class and are administered by the duty holder itself, enforcement depends on supervision from outside the scheme, and Ashwani Kumar is the record of the Supreme Court supplying that supervision for chapters that the Act left without a forum. A complete account of the Act must therefore report an asymmetry that its own table of contents conceals: Chapters II and V are litigated by their beneficiaries, Chapters III and IV are litigated, when they are litigated at all, by strangers on the beneficiaries' behalf.

## The 2019 amendment Bill: the reform that lapsed

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens (Amendment) Bill, 2019 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 11 December 2019 by the Minister of Social Justice and Empowerment, Thawarchand Gehlot, and was referred to the Standing Committee on Social Justice and Empowerment, which submitted its report on 29 January 2021. The Bill was not passed, and it lapsed on the dissolution of the Lok Sabha that had received it. It is therefore not law, was never law, and no provision described in this section may be relied on as the position under the Act. Its importance is diagnostic: it is the government's own catalogue, twelve years into the Act's life, of what the Act gets wrong, and nearly every item in it corresponds to a criticism developed in this pillar from the enacted text.

The Bill's Statement of Objects and Reasons proposed to expand the scope of the Act by modifying the definitions of children, parents, maintenance, welfare and senior citizen, so that parents and senior citizens may lead a life of dignity. In detail, the definition of children was to include step children, adoptive children, children in law and the legal guardian of a minor child; the definition of parents was to extend to parents in law and grandparents; maintenance was to include healthcare, safety and security; and welfare was to be widened in step. The Bill proposed to enlarge the modes of submitting a maintenance application, to provide for expeditious disposal with special preference for applicants above eighty years of age, to extend the right of appeal to children and relatives who continue to pay the amount ordered while their appeal is pending, and to provide for registration of senior citizens care homes, multi-service day care centres and institutions providing home care services, with minimum standards. It proposed a special police unit for senior citizens in each district and nodal officers for senior citizens in every police station, a helpline for senior citizens, and more stringent punishment for those who abuse or abandon parents or senior citizens. And at the head of the list, in the clause order of the objects statement itself, it proposed to remove the upper limit of ten thousand rupees as the monthly maintenance amount that a tribunal may award.

The Standing Committee's report of January 2021 engaged the Bill seriously and, on the care home question, asked for more than the Bill offered, recommending a mandated care home and day care centre in every district against the background of the district coverage figures recorded earlier in this article, together with time bound designation of registration authorities and strengthened geriatric facilities. The report changed nothing in the law, because the Bill it examined was never taken up for passage, and on the dissolution of the seventeenth Lok Sabha the Bill lapsed with it, as the government itself confirmed in answer to a later parliamentary question. The sequence is the standard tragedy of Indian social legislation reform: the defect is conceded by the executive, the remedy is drafted by the executive, the committee improves the remedy, and the legislative calendar quietly buries both. Any future amendment will have to begin again from introduction, and the enacted Act in the meantime continues to cap maintenance at the 2007 figure, to confine the appeal to the applicant side, to leave children in law outside the maintenance definition, and to leave care homes unregistered and unregulated by any central standard. Accounts of this Act that describe the ceiling as removed or the definitions as widened are describing a Bill that died, and a reader who acts on such an account before a tribunal will be corrected by the tribunal's copy of the Act, which is the 2007 text.

## The honest assessment

A statute pillar ends in a verdict, and this Act requires the verdict to be delivered in parts, because its components have performed very differently.

The maintenance machinery works as a design and unevenly as an administration. Where a state has commenced the Act, constituted tribunals in each sub-division, designated maintenance officers and prescribed its ceiling, an elderly applicant has something that did not exist in 2006: a local forum, a ninety day clock, an interim power, a conciliation stage, free copies, portable enforcement and an appellate tier that must endeavour to decide in a month. Where any link of that chain is missing, the entitlement in Section 4 is a right to apply to a forum that is not there. The Act's federal skeleton, which this article identified at the outset, means that the statute's real commencement story is fifty state stories, and its real implementation record is the sum of decisions by state governments that the central text cannot compel beyond the six month direction in Section 7 and the review power in Section 31.

The ceiling fails, for the reasons already given, and it is the clearest case in the Act of a provision defeating its own statute's stated standard. A tribunal ordered to secure the needs of a normal life through a maximum fixed in 2007 rupees will, in a large class of cases, be unable to obey both instructions, and it will obey the number, because the number is the one that can be appealed against. The Karnataka High Court's intervention is best understood as the judiciary telling the legislature that the contradiction has become visible from the bench. Until Parliament deletes or indexes the figure, the maintenance chapter promises dignity and delivers a token whose real value falls every year, and the honest description of a ten thousand rupee maximum in a metropolitan housing market is that it prices the statute's central promise at the level of a gesture.

On the tribunal record itself, honesty requires a pattern statement rather than figures, because the published material does not support a national disposal statistic that this article is willing to assert. The pattern that emerges from the reported orders, the committee record and the High Court case law is consistent across states. Applications cluster overwhelmingly in two kinds: maintenance claims by parents against sons, and Section 23 claims about houses, with the second kind generating the appellate and writ litigation almost alone. Conciliation succeeds in a visible minority of maintenance cases and fails predictably where the real dispute is property wearing maintenance clothing. Interim orders are the strongest predictor of a maintenance order being complied with, because a respondent who has paid during the proceeding has priced the obligation into the household. Enforcement, by contrast, is the machinery's weakest stage: levy warrants require the applicant to return within three months, imprisonment is used sparingly against family respondents, and arrears accumulate in the cases where the relationship has broken down completely, which are the cases of greatest need. None of that is an indictment of the tribunal idea. It is the ordinary profile of a summary welfare forum, and it identifies where administrative attention buys the most relief: service of notice, early interim orders, and a maintenance officer who follows the order after it is made.

The bar on legal practitioners requires the two-sided treatment the brief directs, because it is the Act's most genuinely arguable design choice. The case for the bar, at its strongest, runs as follows. The proceeding is about a family's means and needs, not about rights of a technical character. Lawyers convert conversations into contests: they introduce adjournment as a tactic, cross-examination as an instrument of pressure, and cost as a weapon, and the party who suffers most from each of those conversions is the elderly applicant, whose need is monthly and whose stamina for litigation is the scarcest resource in the room. A forum in which a parent states a need and a child states a means, before an officer who knows the district and can test both statements against local knowledge, with a Maintenance Officer to speak for a parent who wants one, may reach the just figure faster and with less relational damage than any court. The bar is also symmetrical in form: it excludes the child's lawyer as completely as the parent's, and so prevents the arms race in which the wealthier side of a family dispute professionalises first.

The case against the bar, at its strongest, begins where the Act's own development has taken it. Whatever may be said of a maintenance conversation, a Section 23 proceeding is a property adjudication. It determines title to what is usually the family's principal asset, it turns on documents, conditions, execution and proof, and it ends, after Urmila Dixit, in eviction and restoration of possession. To conduct that adjudication while forbidding the respondent, who faces the loss of a home, any legal representation before an executive officer is to impose the procedure of a welfare inquiry on the substance of a title suit, and the fairness objection is not answered by pointing to the applicant's age, because the applicant too may be ill served by informality when the condition in a deed must be pleaded and proved to the standard Sudesh Chhikara demands. The courts have managed the tension rather than resolved it, by insisting on pleading, proof, findings and natural justice inside the informal forum, which is a judicial way of saying that the proceeding must supply by discipline what representation would have supplied by advocacy. The verdict here is that the bar is defensible for the maintenance inquiry for which it was designed and increasingly indefensible for the property jurisdiction the Act has grown, and that the cleanest reform is the one nobody has yet proposed in a Bill: keep the bar for maintenance applications, and lift it, or provide for legal aid representation as an entitlement, where a declaration under Section 23 and possession are in issue. The 2019 Bill, notably, did not touch Section 17 at all.

Section 23 itself is the Act's success, and its success should be stated without the embarrassment that sometimes attends praising a provision for being used. It identified a real and common harm, the house signed away in expectation of care and the care then withdrawn, and it built a remedy proportioned to the harm: undo the transfer, restore the citizen, do it quickly, and do it in the district. The Supreme Court's two decisions give the provision a workable law of conditions and consequences, and Vanitha gives it a conscience, requiring the tribunal to see the whole household before it orders anyone out of it. If the measure of a welfare provision is whether the people it was written for actually obtain its relief, Section 23 is among the most effective provisions in modern Indian social legislation, and it achieved that standing while sitting in a chapter most summaries of the Act treat as an afterthought.

The welfare chapters are the Act's weakest performance, and their weakness is of a familiar statutory kind: obligations distributed to states in the language of discretion, floors expressed as beginnings, and no consequence for non-performance that anyone can invoke. A senior citizen cannot sue for the district old age home Section 19 contemplates in the way the same citizen can apply for maintenance, and the difference between the Act's Chapters II and III is the difference between a right with a forum and a hope with a chapter heading. Teachers and students who organise this statute for the classroom will find that contrast the most instructive thing in it, and those who keep their teaching material as slide decks can open and read presentation files in the browser through a [PowerPoint viewer for statute teaching slides](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which is the natural companion step for the reader payload this pillar serves last: the teacher's.

## Verdict

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 is two statutes wearing one short title. The first is a maintenance code of genuine procedural imagination and a deliberately small purse, which replaced an expensive and slow remedy with a fast and cheap one and then froze the cheap one's value in the year of its birth. The second is a property protection code of one great section, which found the elder's real asset and real vulnerability and armed a local tribunal to defend both, subject to a condition the courts have taught tribunals to find honestly and to the competing rights Vanitha taught them to respect. The series thesis this pillar was asked to carry can be stated in its earned form. A statute's operative provision is often not the one it is named for, and here the ceiling on the headline remedy is precisely what pushed usage toward the other one: families litigate the house because the house is what the Act lets a tribunal actually decide. Parliament knows what repair requires, having drafted it in 2019 and let it lapse. Until a successor Bill is introduced and passed, the Act will continue to be, in the districts where its machinery exists, the fastest route to a small monthly sum and the only summary route to a recovered home, and a senior citizen or a family member who understands both halves of that sentence understands the statute.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who can claim maintenance under the Senior Citizens Act?

A parent, whether biological, adoptive or step, and whether or not the parent has reached sixty, may claim maintenance from one or more children who are not minors, where the parent is unable to maintain himself or herself from his or her own earning or property. A senior citizen, being an Indian citizen of sixty years or above who is unable to maintain himself or herself, may claim in the same way, and a childless senior citizen may claim against a relative who is a legal heir, is not a minor, and is in possession of the senior citizen's property or would inherit it. Grandparents claim as parents against their own children.

### Q: How much maintenance can a tribunal order?

A tribunal may order a monthly allowance at the rate it deems fit on the inquiry into needs and means, but Section 9, sub-section (2) caps the maximum: the allowance may not exceed the maximum prescribed by the State Government, and that prescribed maximum may not itself exceed ten thousand rupees per month. The figure is set by the Act of 2007 and has never been revised. In addition to the allowance, the tribunal may order interest at a rate it determines between five per cent and eighteen per cent, may order interim maintenance during the proceeding, and may order the allowance to run from the date of the application rather than the date of the order.

### Q: Can a lawyer represent a party before the maintenance tribunal?

No. Section 17 provides that, notwithstanding anything contained in any law, no party to a proceeding before a Maintenance Tribunal or an Appellate Tribunal shall be represented by a legal practitioner. The bar applies to both sides equally. The Act substitutes a different form of help for the applicant side: the Maintenance Officer, designated by the State Government from the district social welfare machinery, shall represent a parent if the parent so desires during the proceedings. A party may also be assisted in preparing an application outside the proceeding, and may appear through a duly authorised representative at the appellate hearing in the manner the appeal provision allows, but advocacy inside the tribunal by a legal practitioner is barred.

### Q: Can a gift of property to a child be cancelled?

A gift or other transfer by a senior citizen can be declared void by the tribunal under Section 23 where two conditions are established: the transfer was made subject to the condition that the transferee shall provide the basic amenities and basic physical needs to the transferor, and the transferee refuses or fails to provide them. If both are proved, the transfer is deemed to have been made by fraud, coercion or undue influence and is void at the option of the transferor. A gift made without such a condition, including a gift made purely out of love and affection with nothing said about care, is not cancelled under this section merely because relations later deteriorate, as the Supreme Court confirmed in Sudesh Chhikara.

### Q: Can a senior citizen get possession of a house back?

Yes, where Section 23 is engaged. The Supreme Court in Urmila Dixit held that tribunals under the Act may order the transfer of possession and may order eviction where it is necessary and expedient to ensure the protection of the senior citizen, since a declaration that a transfer is void would be an empty remedy if the senior citizen still had to sue separately for the house. The power is consequential on the transfer being declared void on the Section 23 conditions. It is qualified by S. Vanitha, under which an occupant's competing right of residence under the domestic violence legislation must be considered and relief moulded before an eviction is ordered.

### Q: What is the punishment for abandonment of a senior citizen?

Section 24 makes it an offence for a person who has the care or protection of a senior citizen to leave that senior citizen in any place with the intention of wholly abandoning him or her. The punishment is imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three months, or a fine which may extend to five thousand rupees, or both. Every offence under the Act is cognizable and bailable and is tried summarily by a Magistrate. The prosecution must prove both the relationship of care or protection and the intention wholly to abandon, so a hospital admission, a respite placement or a temporary arrangement made for the citizen's care is not the offence.

### Q: What happened to the 2019 senior citizens amendment bill?

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens (Amendment) Bill, 2019 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 11 December 2019 and referred to the Standing Committee on Social Justice and Empowerment, which reported on 29 January 2021. It proposed removing the ten thousand rupee ceiling, widening the definitions of children and parents, adding safety and security to maintenance, registering care homes and giving applicants above eighty preferential disposal. It was never passed by Parliament, and it lapsed on the dissolution of the Lok Sabha. None of its proposals is law, and the Act continues in its 2007 form on each of those points.

### Q: Does this Act override a daughter in law's right to residence?

No. In S. Vanitha the Supreme Court held that the Senior Citizens Act does not override the right of a woman to reside in a shared household under Section 17 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005. A tribunal may order eviction where necessary and expedient for the maintenance and protection of the senior citizen, but only after adverting to the competing claims, and relief under the two statutes must be moulded so that one does not obliterate the other. The summary procedure under this Act cannot be used as a simple expedient to defeat a residence claim, and an eviction order made without considering such a claim is liable to be set aside.

### Q: How long does a maintenance tribunal have to decide an application?

Section 5, sub-section (4) requires an application for the monthly allowance to be disposed of within ninety days from the date of service of notice of the application on the respondent. The tribunal may extend that period once, for a maximum of thirty days, in exceptional circumstances and for reasons recorded in writing. The clock runs from service rather than filing, so that a respondent is not bound by proceedings of which the respondent had no notice. Separately, the Appellate Tribunal, once an appeal is filed, must endeavour to pronounce its order in writing within one month of receiving the appeal, although the Act states that duty as an endeavour rather than a strict limit.

### Q: Can a tribunal order interim maintenance while an application is pending?

Yes. Section 5, sub-section (2) empowers the tribunal, during the pendency of a proceeding for a monthly allowance, to order the children or relative to make a monthly allowance for the interim maintenance of the senior citizen or parent and to pay it as the tribunal directs from time to time. The interim power exists because the Act's ninety day disposal period would otherwise be a period in which the applicant, whose case is precisely that he or she cannot maintain himself or herself, receives nothing. The amount ordered at the interim stage is provisional and is absorbed into the tribunal's final determination of the allowance on the full inquiry.

### Q: In which district can a maintenance application be filed?

Section 6 allows proceedings to be taken against a child or relative in any district where the applicant resides or last resided, or in any district where the child or relative resides. An elderly applicant is therefore not required to travel to a respondent who has moved to another district or another state, and the applicant's own district is an available forum in the ordinary case. Where the respondent is residing outside India, the summons is served through the authority specified by the Central Government by notification for that purpose, and the Act itself extends to citizens of India outside India, so emigration of a child does not by itself remove the claim from the Act's reach.

### Q: What happens if the maintenance ordered by a tribunal is not paid?

The respondent must deposit the amount ordered within thirty days of the announcement of the order, as Section 13 requires. If the respondent fails without sufficient cause to comply, the tribunal may, for every breach, issue a warrant for levying the amount due in the manner provided for levying fines, and may sentence the defaulter to imprisonment for a term which may extend to one month, or until payment if sooner made, for each month's allowance remaining unpaid after the warrant. The applicant must apply for the levy within three months of the amount falling due, because the Act bars a warrant on an application made later than that.

### Q: Can a maintenance order be altered after it has been made?

Yes. Section 10 empowers the tribunal, on proof of misrepresentation or mistake of fact, or of a change in the circumstances of the person receiving the allowance or of the person ordered to pay it, to make such alteration in the allowance as it thinks fit. The power works in both directions: an applicant whose medical needs have grown may seek an increase within the statutory ceiling, and a respondent whose means have genuinely collapsed may seek a reduction. Section 10 also requires the tribunal to cancel or vary its order where a decision of a competent civil court makes that course necessary, so that the tribunal's order does not stand in conflict with a civil adjudication on the same subject.

### Q: Can maintenance be claimed under both this Act and the Code of Criminal Procedure?

No. Section 12 provides that a senior citizen or parent who is entitled to maintenance under Chapter IX of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 and is also entitled under this Act may claim maintenance under either of those Acts, but not under both. The applicant must elect a forum. The choice is a real one, because the two routes differ in material ways: the criminal procedure route has no ten thousand rupee ceiling and is heard by a Magistrate with legal representation permitted, while this Act offers a summary tribunal, conciliation, suo motu cognizance and a Maintenance Officer. Section 14 also allows a parent with a pending criminal procedure application at the Act's commencement in the state to withdraw it and apply to the tribunal instead.

### Q: Who may appeal against a maintenance tribunal order?

As the Act is enacted, Section 16 gives the appeal to a senior citizen or a parent aggrieved by an order of the tribunal, who may appeal to the Appellate Tribunal for the district within sixty days from the date of the order. The Appellate Tribunal may entertain a late appeal if it is satisfied the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause. A child or relative against whom an order has been made is not given a corresponding appeal by the section, an asymmetry the 2019 Bill proposed to correct by extending the appeal to children and relatives who continue to pay the ordered amount, and which, while the enacted text stands, leaves the respondent to constitutional review by the High Court.

### Q: What does a Maintenance Officer do under the Act?

Section 18 requires the State Government to designate the District Social Welfare Officer, or an officer not below that rank, as Maintenance Officer. The Maintenance Officer's defining function is representation: the officer shall represent a parent, if the parent so desires, during the proceedings before the Maintenance Tribunal or the Appellate Tribunal. The office is the Act's answer to the bar on legal practitioners, supplying a public officer who knows the machinery and can present the parent's case without cost to the parent. Maintenance Officers also appear in the Act's conciliation machinery, since a Maintenance Officer is among the persons who may be referred an application as Conciliation Officer before the hearing.

### Q: Does Section 23 apply to a transfer made before the Act commenced?

No. Section 23, sub-section (1) opens with the words of a senior citizen who, after the commencement of this Act, has transferred by way of gift or otherwise his property. A transfer completed before the Act came into force in the relevant state falls outside the sub-section, however harsh the transferee's later conduct, and the transferor in such a case must rely on the general law, including a civil suit on fraud, coercion or undue influence or on a condition the deed itself contains, or on the maintenance provisions of this Act, which are not limited in the same way. The temporal condition also fixes attention on the state's commencement notification, since commencement under this Act is state by state.

### Q: Can a maintenance right be enforced against a person who has taken a transfer of the estate?

Section 23, sub-section (2) answers this directly. Where a senior citizen has a right to receive maintenance out of an estate and the estate, or part of it, is transferred, the right to receive maintenance may be enforced against the transferee if the transferee had notice of the right, or if the transfer was gratuitous, that is, made without consideration. It may not be enforced against a transferee for consideration who took without notice of the right. The provision follows the familiar bona fide purchaser principle: a donee and a knowing purchaser take the estate with the maintenance burden attached, while a purchaser who paid value in ignorance takes it free of the burden.

### Q: What must state governments provide by way of old age homes and medical care?

Section 19 provides for old age homes to be established and maintained by the State Government in a phased manner, beginning with at least one in each district to accommodate a minimum of one hundred and fifty indigent senior citizens, with a state scheme for their management, standards and services including medical care and means of entertainment. Section 20 requires the State Government to ensure that government hospitals and government funded hospitals provide beds for senior citizens as far as possible, arrange separate queues, expand facilities for chronic, terminal and degenerative diseases and ageing research, and maintain earmarked geriatric facilities in every district hospital headed by a medical officer with experience in geriatric care.

### Q: Why are the civil courts barred from deciding matters under this Act?

Section 27 provides that no civil court shall have jurisdiction in respect of any matter to which any provision of this Act applies, and that no injunction shall be granted by a civil court in respect of anything done or intended to be done by or under this Act. The bar serves the Act's founding purpose: the tribunal route was created because the ordinary courts were judged too slow and expensive for an elderly applicant, and the bar prevents tribunal matters from migrating back into that slower forum or being stalled by injunctions obtained there. The bar does not exclude the constitutional jurisdiction of the High Courts, and tribunal orders are routinely tested in writ proceedings.
