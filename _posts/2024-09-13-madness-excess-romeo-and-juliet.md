Madness and excess are the two charges Romeo and Juliet keeps bringing against its own lovers, and the play brings both charges in the lovers' hearing. A friar warns the bridegroom, minutes before the wedding, that violent delights have violent ends and that the sweetest honey sickens its own eater. A father, refused by his daughter, opens his longest speech with the oath that her refusal makes him mad. A bride, alone with a vial at midnight, rehearses the fear that she will wake among the bones of her ancestors and, distraught, dash out her own brains with a kinsman's bone. The vocabulary is not decoration laid over the story. It is the play's own system of measurement, the scale on which Verona weighs feeling and finds it, scene after scene, over the limit. This article reads that scale, follows its vocabulary from the first lovesick soliloquy to the frenzy in the tomb, and argues a double verdict, because the play's verdict is double. Romeo and Juliet condemns excess in its doctrine and celebrates it in its language, and the tragedy happens in the gap between those two judgments. Every authority in the play states the doctrine of measure. Nearly every speech that audiences carry out of the theatre violates the doctrine beautifully.

![Romeo and Juliet](/assets/images/blog/blog-31.webp)

Excess, as the word will be used here, is a quantity under judgment: feeling that has passed the amount its object, its occasion, or its city's rules can hold. Madness is the name the play gives that quantity when it seems to unseat reason altogether. The two terms belong to one economy. Again and again a character measures someone else's feeling, finds the measure exceeded, and reaches for the language of derangement to say so. The Friar measures Romeo's grief in the cell and calls its owner a madman who has no ears. Lady Capulet measures her husband's rage and asks whether he is mad. Juliet measures her own wooing and calls it too rash, too sudden, too unadvised. Romeo measures nothing at all, which is itself the point: he is the play's great unmeasuring consciousness, the one who experiences every feeling at full volume and trusts the volume as proof of the feeling's truth. Between the measurers and the unmeasured, the play builds its whole account of what love and grief do to a mind.

Three boundaries keep this article to its own ground. The play's disease imagery, its fevers, plagues, and humours, forms a system of the body that a companion article in this series treats on its own terms; the vocabulary of sickness matters here only where it becomes a sickness of the mind, a disorder of judgment. Haste, likewise, is one species of excess, the temporal form of it, and receives a pointer rather than a treatment. The heat of passion, its fires and powders, is an imagery system with its own article; what concerns this one is the quantity those images judge, the too-muchness that every voice in Verona names and no voice in Verona escapes. What remains, and it is a great deal, is the play's doctrine of measure and the record of its universal refusal.

## Madness in Romeo and Juliet: What the Word Meant Before It Meant a Diagnosis

The first discipline the subject demands is lexical. When a modern reader hears that a character is mad, the word suggests a clinical condition, a diagnosis that removes the sufferer from the community of the responsible. The word carried no such settled meaning for the play's first audiences. In the Verona of the text, mad moves across a wide field: enraged, beside oneself, reckless, foolish, transported, frenzied. Capulet's "God's bread! it makes me mad" names anger, not illness. Benvolio's warning that "the mad blood stirring" will breed a brawl names the blood's heat on a hot day, a temper of the streets. Juliet's fear that she will be "distraught" in the vault names a mind pulled apart by terror, and distraught, from the Latin for dragged in different directions, is the period's precise word for a reason unseated by force. The play distributes these senses with care. Nobody in Romeo and Juliet is mad in the modern psychiatric sense for longer than a scene, and that is exactly why the word does so much work: it is the community's all-purpose verdict on feeling that has exceeded the share of feeling the community allots.

Behind the word stood a medical literature that the play's first audiences could know at second hand if not at first. Timothy Bright's Treatise of Melancholie, published in 1586, within a decade of the play, described melancholy as a disorder in which the imagination misjudges its objects, magnifying sorrows and fixing the mind on a single conceit it cannot release. Bright's melancholic is not raving. He is worse off than the raving man in one respect: his judgment errs while seeming to reason, so that every argument he builds stands on a foundation his humour has already tilted. Romeo in the first act is a portrait from Bright's pages before the fact: shut in his chamber, making himself "an artificial night", sighing, weeping at dawn, and producing from his condition a poetry of perfect formal control, oxymoron balanced on oxymoron. The form is sane. The fixation is the disorder. A generation later, Robert Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy would give love-melancholy, the erotic species of the humour, its fullest taxonomy, treating the lover's fixation as a disease of the imagination with symptoms, causes, and cures. Burton codified what the play already stages: love experienced as a sickness whose seat is the mind's image-making power, and whose first symptom is that the sufferer calls the sickness by every name except its own.

The mechanism Bright describes is worth one more paragraph, because the play stages it with a fidelity that suggests design rather than coincidence. In the faculty psychology the period inherited, the imagination is the mind's storehouse of images, set between the outward senses and the judgment that should govern them. Health, in this scheme, is a chain of command: the senses report, the imagination presents, the judgment decides. Melancholy breaks the chain at its middle link. The imagination, heated or corrupted, begins presenting its images with a force the judgment cannot discount, magnifying the loved or dreaded object until it fills the whole field of the mind, and the sufferer then reasons, often brilliantly, from premises his fancy has rigged. This is why the play's lovers are never stupid and rarely wrong about particulars. Their logic is intact. Their premises are inflated. Romeo's syllogisms in the cell are formally flawless and founded on a world in which Verona's walls bound existence; Juliet's vault speech shows the same machinery run in reverse, a fancy deliberately fed with the worst images available until each has been looked at and priced. The period's medicine thus gives the article its central distinction in period dress: madness, for this play, is not the loss of reason but the loss of proportion inside reason, judgment outvoted by the images it was appointed to weigh. Every counsellor in Verona addresses the judgment. The tragedy is that the judgment was never the department in revolt.

The play itself states the connection in its second scene of the lovers' story, before the lovers have met. Romeo, asked to look at other beauties, is prescribed a cure by substitution: "Take thou some new infection to thy eye, / And the rank poison of the old will die." Benvolio's prescription assumes the whole doctrine. Love is an infection caught through the eye; it lodges; it can be displaced by a rival infection. What Benvolio does not see, and what the play will spend five acts proving, is that the prescription treats the object and ignores the appetite. Romeo's disorder is not Rosaline. It is a capacity for total fixation that finds objects the way a flame finds fuel, and the proof arrives within the day, when the same mind transfers its whole infection to Juliet at a glance and calls the transfer a conversion. The speed of the cure is the diagnosis restated. A man cured of lovesickness by one look at another woman was never sick for the first woman at all; he was sick with loving, and the play's measurers, from Benvolio onward, keep mistaking the object for the disease.

This is why the question the article keeps returning to is not whether the lovers are mad, in any sense a physician could certify, but who gets to apply the word, to whom, and at what moment. The word mad in this play is a social instrument before it is a medical one. It is spoken by the measured about the unmeasured, by parents about children, by a friar about a penitent, by a wife about a husband, by a bride about her own imagined future self. Its distribution maps the play's politics of feeling. The lovers are called mad, or call their own states by madness's names, at every crisis. The feud, whose hatred has filled the streets with blood for a generation before the action opens, is called mad by nobody. That asymmetry is one of the play's quietest and sharpest judgments, and the sections that follow track it through the ledger of the play's images, the Friar's doctrine, the cell, the household, and the tomb.

## A Graded Vocabulary: Mad, Wild, Desperate, Distraught

The play does not use its keywords carelessly, and a reader who sorts them discovers a scale. Mad stands at the social end of the scale: it is the verdict other people deliver, the word spoken about a sufferer by the community that has decided his feeling exceeds its share. Distraught stands at the inward end: it is Juliet's word for a condition she imagines from inside, a mind dragged apart by the terrors it is rehearsing, and it is spoken by no one else in the play. Between those poles the vocabulary arranges its middle registers, and the middle registers are where most of the play's feeling actually lives. Wild is the register of behaviour: the Friar reads Romeo's "wild acts" in the cell as the sign of a reason unseated, and Romeo, at the tomb, promotes the word into his own self-description, "savage-wild", as though the diagnosis had finally been internalised by its patient. Violent is the register of outcomes: it belongs to the doctrine, to delights and ends, and it measures not the feeling's temperature but its destination, what a quantity of joy this large must become when it finishes becoming. Desperate is the register of the will, and it is the play's most travelled word of all: the Friar checks Romeo's "desperate hand", Romeo in Mantua watches mischief enter "the thoughts of desperate men", Paris at the tomb begs not to be tempted as "a desperate man", and the poison is finally addressed as a "desperate pilot". Desperation, in this economy, is the point at which excess stops being a condition a man has and becomes a policy he follows. The mad are pitied or scolded. The desperate are obeyed by their own hands.

The scale matters because characters climb it. Romeo's career can be plotted as an ascent through the registers: reported as lovesick in the first act, a condition other people name on his behalf; wild in the cell, a behaviour his counsellor names to his face; desperate in Mantua, a policy he names himself; savage-wild at the tomb, the whole scale compressed into a single compound spoken by the patient as his own epitaph in advance. Juliet's career runs the scale in the opposite direction, which is the play's subtlest piece of characterization by vocabulary. She begins with the doctrine's words in her own mouth, too rash, too sudden, and she ends having passed through the imagined distraught of the vault speech into a final scene where her language is the plainest in the play, imperatives and identifications, a cup, a dagger, a refusal. The excess grows as the vocabulary of excess falls away. By the tomb she no longer needs the words for the condition, because she is no longer describing it. Romeo, still describing himself at the threshold of death, still narrating his own frenzy in the play's most gorgeous verse, remains to the last the poet of his condition rather than its master. The scale thus delivers a verdict of its own, independent of the Friar's: the play trusts the lover who stops talking about measure, and it buries the one who never does.

One more word belongs to the scale, and its placement is a deliberate provocation. Excess itself, the article's title word, is spoken in the play exactly where a reader of the doctrine would least expect it: not by the Friar, whose vocabulary runs to measure, moderation, and use, but by Juliet, at her wedding, as a boast. "My true love is grown to such excess / I cannot sum up sum of half my wealth." The doctrine's keeper never uses the term because his system has no need of it: inside the herb philosophy, there is no such thing as excess in the abstract, only goods strained from their uses, proportions failed at particular joints. Juliet's use of the word imports a foreign system, the counting-house's, in which quantities can simply be too large, and then glories in failing that system's test. The play's whole argument is latent in the distribution of this one word. The measurer speaks of uses and proportions. The measured speak of too-muchness and overflow. Between those two vocabularies there is no translation, and the tragedy is, among its other identities, the record of their failure to meet. A companion fact completes the distribution. The doctrine's own keyword, moderately, is spoken in the play exactly once, in the imperative, by the Friar, over a wedding that is already beginning: "Therefore love moderately." No character ever takes the word up. It is never repeated, never quoted back, never declined in argument, never even echoed by the bride who shares the counsellor's gift for self-audit and who might have been expected to test it against her bounty. The play's most important adverb has no descendants. Against that single occurrence, set the crowd of its rivals: too, in speech after speech; violent, desperate, wild, distraught, mad, each recurring, each finding new mouths. A vocabulary's frequency is a culture's confession. Verona can conjugate excess in a dozen registers and can say the doctrine's word only once, in passing, at a wedding, to a man who is not listening.

The ledger's lesson is structural. Excess in this play is not a trait of the young, though the young carry its most beautiful instances. It is the common medium of Verona, spoken as fluently by the doctrine's own spokesman as by the doctrine's chief offender, and the tragedy's engine is not the presence of excess but the failure of every measuring instrument the city owns. The Friar's scales are accurate and unused. The parents' scales are rigged by honour. Mercutio's scales are a jest that stops working at the moment of his death. Juliet alone keeps recalibrating her own instrument, which is why her excess, the largest in the play by her own account, is also the most governable, and why the catastrophe requires her to be asleep, drugged into a counterfeit of the death her measure might have prevented, before it can proceed. The ledger habit transfers beyond this play: readers who keep their own research in a structured personal archive, such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), can log each warning as it falls and test any reading of the play against the full count rather than against the two or three quotations that anthologies repeat. A ledger also records its silences, and this one's are eloquent. Rosaline, the cause of the play's first lovesickness, speaks no entry: her excess exists entirely in other men's reports, a quantity estimated by a committee. The feud, the city's largest and oldest excess, appears in no row as a named madness, for reasons the household section below will pursue. And the Prince, who sets the forfeit of the peace at men's lives, contributes no image of excess at all, though his sentences will prove as miscalibrated as any lover's transport. What a culture's ledger omits is the measure of what it cannot afford to count.

## Love as a Sickness of the Mind: Romeo Before Juliet

The play opens its account of excess before it opens its account of love, and it opens both in the same speech. Montague, reporting his son's behaviour to Benvolio in the first scene, describes a young man who has withdrawn from every measuring community the city offers: "But he, his own affections' counsellor, / Is to himself, I will not say how true, / But to himself so secret and so close, / So far from sounding and discovery." The report continues into the image of the bud "bit with an envious worm" before it can spread its leaves, and into the plain facts of the case: Romeo shuts his windows, locks fair daylight out, and makes himself "an artificial night". Every element of the period's lovesickness is present in the father's summary, and the father, significantly, can describe all of it without naming any of it. He has the symptoms. He lacks the word. The word will be supplied by the son, in the speech that remains the play's first great set piece of deranged reason.

### Where does the play first present love as a disorder of the mind rather than of the heart?

It happens in Romeo's oxymoron catalogue in Act 1 Scene 1, where love is named "sick health" and "still-waking sleep": the disorder is located not in feeling's strength but in judgment's machinery, a mind whose categories have collapsed into their opposites while its syntax stays perfect.

The catalogue deserves its reputation and rewards a slow reading: "O brawling love, O loving hate, / O anything of nothing first create! / O heavy lightness, serious vanity, / Misshapen chaos of well-seeming forms, / Feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health, / Still-waking sleep, that is not what it is!" Critics of the speech sometimes treat it as a young man's borrowed finery, Petrarchan counters passed from hand to hand until they ring false. The dramatic placement argues otherwise. Shakespeare gives the speech to a character the audience has just heard described as sealed, solitary, and night-making, and the speech's perfection of form is the symptom, not the refutation. A mind in Bright's melancholy does not lose its rhetoric. It loses its proportions. Each oxymoron is a small act of mismeasurement, a thing weighed against its opposite and declared equal: lead as light as feather, health that is sickness, sleep that wakes. Romeo's judgment has not stopped working. It has started returning the same answer for every quantity, which is the formal definition of the fixation the physicians described. "This love feel I, that feel no love in this," the speech concludes, and the line is the catalogue's own verdict: a feeling that reports itself as its own absence, experienced at full intensity, about a woman who will not have him.

Mercutio, arriving in the second act with the cure of ridicule, supplies the diagnosis the father could not name, in the idiom of the streets: "Alas, poor Romeo, he is already dead, stabbed with a white wench's black eye, run through the ear with a love-song, the very pin of his heart cleft with the blind bow-boy's butt-shaft." The joke is precise. Romeo's love is a wound inflicted by images, an eye and a song, organs of perception rather than persons; the sickness is in the receiving apparatus. When Romeo reappears, cured of Rosaline by the simple expedient of Juliet, Mercutio greets the recovery as a return of wit: "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in. Laura to his lady was a kitchen wench, marry, she had a better love to berhyme her." The welcome is warm, and it is also a misreading that the play will punish. Mercutio takes the wit's return as the madness's end. In fact the madness has changed its object and doubled its stake, and the wit is now in service to a secret marriage that will require Romeo, within hours, to refuse a duel he cannot explain refusing. The diagnostician's error is the city's error in miniature: Verona keeps reading Romeo's condition from his manner, and his manner keeps recovering a scene before his condition worsens.

Rosaline's place in the economy of excess is easily missed because she never appears. Romeo's madness over Rosaline is entirely reported: by Montague, by Benvolio, by Mercutio, by the Friar, and by Romeo himself in retrospect. The play stages the second love and narrates the first, and the difference is a judgment. Reported madness can be managed by other people's counsel; Benvolio prescribes, the Friar scolds (young men's love lies in their eyes, he rules, and not in their hearts), and the community treats the case as a phase of youth that a new object will cure. Staged madness is another matter. Once the fixation fixes on Juliet, the audience watches the measuring instruments fail in real time, one by one, and the reports stop because there is no longer an outside from which to report. The Friar's scolding in Act 2 Scene 3 is the hinge between the two regimes. He hears the confession of the transfer and names it exactly: "Is Rosaline, that thou didst love so dear, / So soon forsaken?" Young men's love, the Friar concludes, lies in their eyes rather than in their hearts. It is the play's most accurate diagnosis of Romeo ever delivered, and Romeo's answer converts the diagnostician into an accomplice inside four lines: "I prayed for her, at his hands, the doctrine of measure; the patient instead recruits the physician." By the scene's end the Friar has agreed to marry the couple, for reasons of statecraft he will state plainly, and the doctrine of measure has acquired its lifelong habit of arriving as analysis and leaving as assistance.

## The Friar's Doctrine of Measure: Grace and Rude Will in One Plant

Every doctrine needs a founding text, and the play gives its doctrine of measure one of the great founding speeches of the canon. Friar Laurence enters at dawn in Act 2 Scene 3 carrying a basket, gathering herbs, and thinking aloud about the doubleness of everything he gathers: "For naught so vile that on the earth doth live / But to the earth some special good doth give; / Nor aught so good but, strained from that fair use, / Revolts from true birth, stumbling on abuse." The key word is strained. A good does not become a vice by changing its nature. It becomes a vice by being strained from its fair use, pulled past the quantity and the purpose for which it was made. The speech drives the point home in its most quoted couplet: "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied, / And vice sometime by action dignified." Measure, in the Friar's philosophy, is not a property of things. It is a relation between a thing, its amount, and its use, and it must be recalculated at every application, which is why the doctrine's keeper is a working herbalist rather than a theologian: he deals in doses all day long. The speech then turns the doctrine inward, onto the human case: "In man as well as herbs, grace and rude will, / And where the worser is predominant, / Full soon the canker death eats up that plant." Man is a plant with two tenants, grace and rude will, and the plant dies not when the rude will appears but when it predominates, when the proportion fails. Excess, in the founding text, is predominance: the moment one tenant stops paying rent to the other.

### Which of the play's warnings against excess is spoken by someone already committing it?

The Friar's whole doctrine is the case. He states the law of measure over his herb basket in the same hour in which he agrees to a secret, unconsented, day-old marriage, the single most immoderate act of statecraft in the play, undertaken for a political hope he states and the play never grants.

The doctrine speech is usually read as wisdom the play endorses and the lovers ignore. The ledger suggests a harder reading. The Friar delivers the speech to an empty stage, and within minutes he is applying its categories to Romeo with complete accuracy and then abandoning its counsel entirely. His reason for the marriage is stated with a statesman's candour: the alliance may so happy prove, he hopes, as to turn the households' rancour to pure love. Weighed by his own doctrine, the hope is a good strained from its fair use. A sacrament is being applied as an instrument of civic policy, in secret, without the consent of either house, on the strength of a day's acquaintance, by a bridegroom whose previous fixation the Friar has just diagnosed as a disease of the eyes. Every element is a predominance of one consideration over all the others that should have checked it. The play does not underline the irony; it simply proceeds, and lets the doctrine's author become the doctrine's most consequential refuter. When the Friar warns in the wedding scene that violent delights have violent ends, the audience has already watched him light the fuse he is describing, and the warning's authority and its futility have the same source.

The doctrine's later career in its author's hands completes the portrait, and the play is merciless about the sequence. In Act 4 the Friar prescribes the potion, a dose, note, from his own pharmacopoeia, administered to a desperate girl in his cell with a knife at her own throat: the keeper of measure responds to an emergency of excess with the boldest single act of immoderation in the play, and justifies it by the desperation of the case, which is the eternal justification of every excess the play records. In Act 5, hearing that his letter has miscarried, he does not revise the plan's proportions; he hurries to the vault to be present at the waking, arriving in time to witness the catastrophe and then, at the sound of the watch, to flee it, abandoning a waking girl among the corpses with the confession that he dare not stay. The measurer's nerve, not his knowledge, is what fails at the last. Every instrument he owns has read the situation correctly. The herb speech read the lovers. The wedding warning read the delights. The cell rebuke read the grief. What no instrument could read was the operator, and the play's doctrine of measure finally stands revealed as a complete philosophy with one unexamined term: the man applying it, whose own predominant tenant, when the reckoning came, was fear.

None of this makes the doctrine false. The play is careful to separate the truth of the measure from the conduct of the measurer, because its subject is not hypocrisy but something more unsettling: a city in which the knowledge of measure is universally available and operationally useless. The Friar knows. Benvolio knows, in his smaller way, prescribing proportion as a change of object. Juliet knows best of all, as the next sections show. Knowledge is not the scarce resource in Verona. The scarce resource is a situation in which knowledge can act before feeling has finished acting, and the play's compression of its action into a handful of days is the formal expression of that scarcity. Haste, the temporal species of excess, has its own full treatment in the companion study of [time and haste in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/01/12/time-and-haste-romeo-and-juliet/); here it is enough to note that every doctrine in the play arrives after the decision it was meant to govern, and that the Friar's herb speech, delivered before any of the catastrophes, is the exception that proves how early even the earliest warning comes too late for the man who speaks it.

## These Violent Delights: The Warning Spoken Over the Wedding

Act 2 Scene 6 is the shortest major scene in the play and the one where its two systems, doctrine and desire, stand closest together. The Friar and Romeo wait in the cell for Juliet. Romeo, at the height of his transport, challenges the world: "But come what sorrow can, / It cannot countervail the exchange of joy / That one short minute gives me in her sight." The verb is a merchant's: countervail, to weigh against and equal. Romeo is doing arithmetic, and his arithmetic is the arithmetic of excess, in which one minute is declared to outweigh any quantity of sorrow whatsoever, including quantities not yet known. The Friar's answer is the doctrine's most famous application: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which as they kiss consume." The image of fire and powder has its own full analysis elsewhere in this series; what belongs to the economy of measure is the sentence that follows, in which the doctrine descends from explosion to digestion: "The sweetest honey / Is loathsome in his own deliciousness, / And in the taste confounds the appetite. / Therefore love moderately. Long love doth so. / Too swift arrives as tardy as too slow."

### What does the Friar mean by warning that the sweetest honey is loathsome in its own deliciousness?

He means that a pleasure can destroy the capacity to enjoy it by quantity alone: honey eaten past appetite turns against the eater, confounding the taste it was meant to please, so that the excess punishes itself from inside the pleasure, without any external judge.

The honey sentence is the doctrine's subtlest moment, because it relocates the punishment of excess inside the pleasure itself. Fire and powder threaten an accident, an external catastrophe that befalls the lovers from the logic of their materials. Honey threatens something quieter and more certain: surfeit, the palate's revolt, the moment at which the sweetest thing becomes loathsome not because it has changed but because the eater has had too much of it. Between the two images the Friar covers both of the play's endings. There will be an explosion, in the square, within hours. And there will be a surfeit, slower and sadder, in the discovery that a love fed on secrecy, urgency, and stolen minutes has consumed the conditions that might have let it live in daylight. "Therefore love moderately" is the plainest sentence the doctrine ever gets, four words of counsel with no image at all, and the scene's staging gives it its bleak punctuation: Juliet enters, the warning is never answered, and the wedding proceeds. Romeo does not rebut the doctrine. He does something the play will repeat at every level: he lets it stand, unargued with, while the action walks past it.

A detail of the scene's construction sharpens the doctrine's tragedy, and it is easy to miss because the scene is so short. The warning is delivered to Romeo alone. Juliet has not yet entered; the Friar speaks his fire and powder, his honey, his therefore love moderately, to the one auditor in the play least capable of acting on counsel, while the auditor most capable of it, the girl who audits herself as a habit, waits offstage for a ceremony at which the doctrine will not be repeated. Romeo's response to the counsel, as noted, is silence and a change of subject effected by an entrance. But consider what the play has arranged: the doctrine of measure, in its most complete practical form, is spoken once, in a cell, to a bridegroom vibrating at a pitch the speech itself diagnoses, minutes before the rite it was meant to govern. No sermon in the play is better aimed or worse delivered. The Friar will spend the remaining acts compensating for the delivery's failure with plans of increasing immoderation, as though the doctrine, having failed as speech, might succeed as engineering. It is the play's most modern lesson in the economy this article has tracked: counsel that arrives as information, to the wrong hearer, at the wrong pitch of feeling, does not enter the ledger as counsel at all. It enters as one more beautiful speech the action walks past.

Juliet's own contribution to the scene completes the pattern and supplies the article's epigraph in all but name. Asked, in effect, to declare her love's amount, she refuses the instrument: "They are but beggars that can count their worth. / But my true love is grown to such excess / I cannot sum up sum of half my wealth." The word excess is hers. Shakespeare puts the technical term of the whole inquiry into the mouth of the character least governed by it, and the speech that follows is a theory of why: counting is for beggars, for those whose wealth is finite and must be guarded. Her love, as she theorized it in the orchard, obeys a different arithmetic, in which giving increases the stock: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep. The more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite." The play does not mock the theory. It does something more dangerous: it lets the theory be beautiful, lets it be spoken by the play's most intelligent character, and then spends three acts discovering the one quantity her arithmetic omitted. Her bounty may be infinite. Her time, her information, her family's patience, and her margin for a single failed letter are not. Excess in this play is rarely an error of feeling. It is an error of accounting, committed by people whose ledgers leave out the finite columns.

## Juliet's Arithmetic: The One Lover Who Measures Herself

Juliet is the play's standing refutation of the idea that excess and intelligence cannot coexist. She falls as fast as Romeo, feels as much, and says the largest things in the play about the size of her love; she is also the only lover who audits herself while falling. The orchard scene gives the audit its classic form. Romeo, overheard, is pouring out vows; Juliet, discovered, does the opposite of pouring: "I should have been more strange, I must confess, / But that thou overheard'st, ere I was ware, / My true-love passion." Then, within a dozen lines, she delivers the play's most exact self-diagnosis: the contract is "too rash, too sudden, too unadvised, / Too like the lightning, which doth cease to be / Ere one can say 'It lightens'." Three adjectives of measure, each one accurate, each one spoken by the person committing the act measured. No counsellor in the play ever achieves this precision about anyone, and Juliet achieves it about herself, in the moment, and proceeds anyway, having first built the only safeguards the night allows: the oath refused ("O, swear not by the moon, th' inconstant moon"), the question asked ("What satisfaction canst thou have tonight?"), the procedure set (the messenger at nine), the condition named ("If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage"). Her excess travels with its own instruments. It is the tragedy in embryo that the instruments measure everything except the one variable that will kill them, which is the world outside the orchard wall.

The grief of Act 3 Scene 2 submits the same mind to a harder test and produces the article's central contrast. The Nurse's muddled report convinces Juliet, for a few minutes, that Romeo is dead; then that he has killed Tybalt and been banished. Juliet's first response is the play's most violent storm of oxymoron since Romeo's catalogue in the first act: "O serpent heart, hid with a flowering face! / Did ever dragon keep so fair a cave? / Beautiful tyrant, fiend angelical! / Dove-feathered raven, wolvish-ravening lamb! / Despised substance of divinest show!" The catalogue matters because of what follows it. Romeo's Act 1 catalogue had no sequel; it simply stopped when the scene did, the fixation intact. Juliet's catalogue corrects itself inside the speech. "Shall I speak ill of him that is my husband? / Ah, poor my lord, what tongue shall smooth thy name / When I, thy three-hours wife, have mangled it?" Then the reasoning begins, step by step, each step a recalibration: "But wherefore, villain, didst thou kill my cousin? / That villain cousin would have killed my husband." The conclusion re-weighs the whole event: "My husband lives, that Tybalt would have slain, / And Tybalt's dead, that would have slain my husband. / All this is comfort." A mind has talked itself from frenzy to proportion in a single speech, without a friar, without a friend, without new information, purely by submitting its own images to audit. Later in the same scene she performs the complementary feat, measuring the word banished against the deaths of thousands and finding the word heavier: the arithmetic is extravagant, and it is still arithmetic, quantities compared and a verdict drawn.

### How does Juliet correct her own grief in Act 3 Scene 2 without any counsellor's help?

She cross-examines it. The oxymoron storm gives way to a question addressed to herself, the question produces the fact that Tybalt would have killed Romeo, and the fact re-weighs the event from catastrophe to comfort, frenzy audited into proportion inside one speech.

Set this beside the cell scene, to which the article now turns, and the play's deepest asymmetry comes into focus. Juliet's grief is met by no one and masters itself. Romeo's grief is met by the play's professional of measure, in person, with every rhetorical resource the doctrine owns, and the scene still ends with a drawn dagger and a man on the floor. The difference is not strength of feeling. By Juliet's own accounting her feeling is the larger. The difference is the direction of the audit: hers runs inward, against her own images, while every audit offered to Romeo runs against his circumstances, his sentence, his fortune, anything except the machinery of the grief itself. The Friar will count Romeo's blessings. He will not, perhaps cannot, teach him Juliet's trick of counting his own speech.

Juliet's scene with Paris in the Friar's cell, Act 4 Scene 1, completes the portrait by exhibiting the public instrument that all this private auditing has built. Paris has come to arrange his wedding to a woman already married, and Juliet must receive him with courtesy, dismiss him without offence, and concede nothing true, all in the presence of the one man who knows her position. Her performance is a masterpiece of measure. Every answer is formally adequate and substantively empty; every compliment Paris pays is received at its exact ceremonial weight and returned with the change withheld. When he presses his claim to her face as his property, she returns the claim in a form that satisfies the register and evacuates the fact. Critics sometimes read the scene as mere equivocation, a desperate girl lying prettily. The economy of measure reads it as the audit's final product: this is what Juliet's self-government looks like when it is turned outward, proportion maintained under interrogation, the surface calm at exactly the temperature the room requires. The contrast with every other character's public conduct is the point. Her father, interrogated by no one, loses all proportion in his own hall. Her husband, counselled by a professional, loses his on a cell floor. Juliet, trapped in the play's most dangerous conversation, loses nothing at all, and walks out of it carrying the vial that her composure has purchased. Excess governed is still excess; the potion in her hand is the proof. But the play has shown, in this scene, the one pair of hands in Verona steady enough to carry it.

## The Cell: Romeo's Grief on Trial as Madness

Act 3 Scene 3, the cell scene, is the doctrine of measure's fullest trial and its most instructive failure. Romeo has killed Tybalt, the Prince has commuted the death sentence to banishment, and Romeo receives the mercy as a sentence worse than the one escaped: "There is no world without Verona walls / But purgatory, torture, hell itself. / Hence banished is banish'd from the world, / And world's exile is death." The full weight of that claim, and of the Mantua exile that follows it, is traced in the companion study of [Romeo's banishment and the exile he calls worse than death](/2022/06/17/romeo-banishment-mantua-exile/); what concerns the economy of measure is the form of the grief, which is inflation. Every quantity in Romeo's speech has been multiplied past its denomination. Banishment is not exile but the end of the world; Verona's walls are not a city's boundary but the edge of existence; a fly has more validity, more honourable state, more courtship than the heir of the Montagues, because carrion flies "may seize / On the white wonder of dear Juliet's hand / And steal immortal blessing from her lips". The Friar, who has heard the speech, answers with the single most important exchange in the article's whole subject: "O, then I see that madmen have no ears." Romeo, from the floor, returns the play's neatest definition of the measurer's despair: "How should they, when that wise men have no eyes?" Madness, in this exchange, is not a condition either man believes in as a clinical fact. It is the name each gives the other's failure to perceive: the Friar cannot see that the sentence is torture; Romeo cannot hear that it is mercy. The word has become a token in an argument about whose measurement of the same event shall count.

The scene then stages the doctrine's full remedial programme, and the programme deserves enumeration because the FAQs of the scene usually stop at its failure. The Friar first rebukes the body: Romeo has thrown himself on the floor, and the rebuke reads the posture as a category error: "Hold thy desperate hand! / Art thou a man? Thy form cries out thou art. / Thy tears are womanish; thy wild acts denote / The unreasonable fury of a beast. / Unseemly woman in a seeming man!" The taxonomy is brutal and period-true: grief in excess unmans, unmans so far that the griever drops a species. Then the Friar rebukes the accounting. Romeo has priced banishment above death; the Friar reprices it by inventory: "A pack of blessings lights upon thy back: / Happiness courts thee in her best array." Juliet lives; Tybalt, who would have killed Romeo, is dead; the law that threatened death has taken a gentle term. The rebuke even supplies its own summary of the patient's error in a single line of pure measure language: the sentence Romeo curses is "dear mercy" that he does not come to see, "a lover of his own misery" being the diagnosis underneath the courtesy. And then, the rebuke complete, the scene delivers its verdict on the whole method. Romeo has asked where in his body his name lodges, so that he may sack "the hateful mansion"; the dagger is out; the doctrine's programme of shame and enumeration has brought its patient not to proportion but to the edge of suicide in a holy man's cell. What stops the dagger is not an argument. It is a knock at the door.

One element of the Friar's programme deserves separate notice, because it is the doctrine's most ambitious offer and its most complete rejection. Before the shame and the inventory, the Friar offers Romeo a discipline: philosophy itself, figured first as armour and then, in the speech's strangest turn, as food. He will give Romeo armour to keep off the word banishment, he says, and the armour has a name: "Adversity's sweet milk, philosophy, / To comfort thee, though thou art banished." The image repays attention. Milk is the first measured food, the nourishment doled out to a creature that cannot yet take anything stronger, and to call philosophy adversity's milk is to prescribe it as a diet: small, regular, sustaining doses of reason, taken by a patient whose digestion of stronger fare has failed. Romeo's rejection of the prescription is the scene's most quoted refusal, and its terms are an accountant's. "Hang up philosophy! / Unless philosophy can make a Juliet, / Displant a town, reverse a prince's doom, / It helps not, it prevails not. Talk no more." Philosophy is being audited for output, and the audit demands miracles at production scale: a person created, a city moved, a sentence reversed. Anything less registers as zero. It is the arithmetic of excess applied to consolation itself, the same all-or-nothing ledger that priced one minute of joy against any sorrow whatsoever, and it explains why every remedy the scene offers fails in the same way. The remedies are all partial, and partial is the one denomination Romeo's grief cannot accept. The Friar, to his credit, reads the refusal correctly and changes instruments on the spot, abandoning philosophy for the inventory of blessings and the plan, counsel's descent from the diet of reason to the management of events. The play registers the descent without comment. It is the moment the doctrine stops trying to cure the patient and starts trying to schedule around him, and every later catastrophe is latent in the change of method.

This is the point at which the question whether [Romeo is impulsive or doomed, a creature of his own rash choices or of the machinery around him](/2022/06/10/is-romeo-impulsive-or-doomed/), stops being a parlour question and becomes the scene's own problem. The cell gives the impulsive reading its best evidence and then complicates it past usefulness: Romeo's impulses are real, violent, and repeated, and every one of them has been produced by a situation engineered by other people's excesses, the feud's, the Friar's, the Prince's merciful severity. The Nurse's entrance closes the scene's argument with a mirror. She reports Juliet in the identical posture, on the ground, "blubbering and weeping, weeping and blubbering", and Romeo's response to the report of a grief matching his own is to ask whether he is its cause and reach again for the dagger's logic. The measurers leave the scene with a plan, a ring, and a promise of a night visit: remedies of action, every one, for a disorder of proportion. The doctrine has won the argument and lost the patient, and the play lets both outcomes stand, because both are the truth about how counsel works on grief at this temperature.

## The Household's Madness: A Father's Rage and the Word Nobody Aims at the Feud

If the lovers' excess is the play's music, the household's excess is its weather: constant, unexamined, and treated by everyone indoors as the natural state of the air. Act 3 Scene 5 brings the weather into a single room. Juliet has refused Paris. Capulet enters in good humour and leaves it in a speech that opens with the article's most telling use of its keyword: "God's bread! it makes me mad. / Day, night, hour, tide, time, work, play, / Alone, in company, still my care hath been / To have her matched." The oath is worth pausing on. Capulet does not say the refusal grieves him, or shames him, or ruins his arrangements, though all three are true and all three appear in the speech. He says it makes him mad, reaching for the word of derangement to describe a father's thwarted will, and the scene around him treats the reach as unremarkable. His wife supplies the play's smallest act of measurement: "Fie, fie! What, are you mad?" It is the correct question, asked in the correct tone, by the person closest to the patient, and it accomplishes nothing at all. The rage proceeds through its inventory of insults, "green-sickness carrion", "baggage", "tallow-face", to the ultimatum of church on Thursday or exile from his face forever. The check has been applied and has failed, in four words, and the play moves on, because in this household the failure of checks is not an event. It is the routine the event interrupts.

The asymmetry the article flagged at the outset now stands in full view. Romeo's grief is diagnosed to his face as madness by a professional, in a cell, with a programme of cure attached. Juliet's terror is rehearsed by herself, in advance, with the word distraught ready for the condition she fears. Capulet's rage is named madness by its owner as a boast of injury and by his wife as a mild reproach, and no programme follows, no inventory of blessings, no threat of the beast taxonomy, though the rage will do more damage in one afternoon than Romeo's grief does in the whole play: it advances the wedding a day, which advances the potion a day, which breaks the letter's timetable, which empties the tomb's margin to nothing. The play distributes the diagnosis of madness by rank and by gender with a precision that cannot be accidental. The young are diagnosed so that they may be governed. The old diagnose themselves as a form of emphasis, and govern on.

Lady Capulet's excess in the same scene is the colder variety and the more instructive for it. She does not rage. She plans. Juliet's grief for Tybalt, which Lady Capulet has misread all scene, becomes in her hands a commission: she will send to Mantua, where "that same banish'd runagate doth live", a man who shall give Romeo "such an unaccustomed dram / That he shall soon keep Tybalt company". The poison plot is delivered in the tone of a household arrangement, between the wedding plans and the exit, and it reveals the household's true economy of feeling: grief, in the Capulet interior, is not experienced past measure; it is converted, promptly and efficiently, into vengeance, which is grief's excess wearing the mask of a task. Nobody calls this madness either. The word is reserved, all through the play, for feelings that threaten the household's arrangements, never for the feelings that constitute them, and the feud itself, the oldest and largest excess in Verona, a hatred that has outlived its cause and eaten three generations, passes its whole dramatic life without once receiving the diagnosis that falls so readily on a boy weeping on a cell floor.

The household's economy produces one more measurer before the act closes, and her instrument is the most quietly corrupt in the play. The Nurse, returning from her failed embassy of comfort, delivers her counsel to the weeping Juliet in the form of an actuarial judgment: Romeo is banished and the world to nothing, as she puts it, chances are he will never come again; Paris, by contrast, is present, prosperous, and a gentleman; the second marriage, in her summing, is the better bargain, and the first can be treated as though its quantity were zero. It is the doctrine of measure translated into the household's native arithmetic, in which feelings are weighed by the availability of their objects and a sacrament can be rounded down to nothing when its asset is out of town. Juliet's response is the economy's most frightening entry of all, because it is perfectly measured: she praises the counsel aloud, sends the Nurse away with a show of comfort taken, and begins, in the same breath, the soliloquy of a girl who has just learned that every instrument in her house is rigged. "I'll to the Friar to know his remedy. / If all else fail, myself have power to die." The power to die enters her ledger at the exact moment the last counsellor's scale is exposed as false. The play could not state its thesis more plainly if it used the doctrine's own words: where measure is corrupt, excess becomes the only honest arithmetic left.

## Mercutio: The First Diagnostician of Love's Unreason

Mercutio holds a position in the economy of measure that no other character occupies: he is its satirist, the one who treats the whole vocabulary of excess as a joke in poor taste, and the play's treatment of him is its most elegant piece of accounting. His mockery of Romeo's love is diagnostic in form. The conjuring scene in Act 2 Scene 1 reduces the beloved to a catalogue of body parts, love to an appetite with a mailing address; the bawdy register of that mockery, its puns and its conjurations, belongs to the companion study of [Mercutio's bawdy wit and wordplay](/2022/09/16/mercutio-bawdy-wit-wordplay/), and what belongs here is the mockery's premise: that Romeo's transports are a fit, a temporary derangement of a sound man, which ridicule can sweat out of him like a fever. The conjuring deserves a closer look, because its method is the mockery's whole theory in miniature. Romeo has hidden in the orchard darkness after the feast, and Mercutio undertakes to raise him the way a magician raises a spirit, by naming the beloved's parts in sequence: her eyes, her forehead, her lips, her foot, her leg, and downward. The joke's structure is an anatomy, and anatomies are measuring instruments; to itemize the beloved is to insist that the transport she causes can be decomposed into the sum of its triggers, each one nameable, each one, frankly, common. If the sum can be listed, the spell can be priced, and what can be priced can be laughed back into proportion. Benvolio's objection, that Romeo will be angry at being conjured in this likeness, is brushed aside, and the scene closes with Mercutio shouting his anatomy at an absent man and an empty orchard. The play lets the method have its full brilliance and its full futility in the same gesture. The patient is not there. The diagnosis is delivered to the trees, at the top of the diagnostician's voice, while the disease, fifty feet away, is climbing the wall into the one conversation that will render every item on the list irrelevant. Queen Mab is the premise at full stretch. The speech begins in fairyland, among walnut shells and grasshopper wings, and darkens by degrees into a catalogue of the dreams that appetite writes on sleeping bodies: lovers dreaming of love, courtiers of fees, lawyers of money, soldiers of cutting foreign throats, until the fairy herself is pressing maids into their first lessons in bearing. Romeo stops the speech, "Peace, peace, Mercutio, peace! / Thou talk'st of nothing", and Mercutio agrees with a lightness that fools nobody: "True, I talk of dreams, / Which are the children of an idle brain, / Begot of nothing but vain fantasy." The diagnostician has described, in his set piece, a world in which every mind in Verona is nightly unseated by its own desires, and has then exempted the description from seriousness by calling it nothing. It is the finest speech about madness in the play's first half, delivered as disposable entertainment by the man who will be dead of the city's real madness before the play's midpoint.

The accounting completes itself in Act 3 Scene 1, and Benvolio, of all people, supplies the vocabulary. Benvolio opens the scene with the doctrine in its street form: "The day is hot, the Capels are abroad, / And if we meet, we shall not scape a brawl, / For now, these hot days, is the mad blood stirring." Mad blood: the feud's excess, at last, gets the diagnosis, from the play's habitual peacemaker, as a remark about the weather. What follows is the diagnosis's demonstration. Mercutio, who has spent two acts treating other men's transports as fevers to be laughed off, meets Tybalt's formal challenge with a transport of his own so complete that he draws on Romeo's peacemaking interference and dies of the wound it lets through, cursing both houses with the play's most famous act of measurement: "A plague o' both your houses." The plague belongs to the disease system and its own article; the measure belongs here. The satirist of excess dies discovering that the exemption he claimed was the one delusion the play never grants anyone. His mockery had assumed a standpoint outside the economy, from which fits could be observed and priced. Verona has no outside. The mad blood was stirring in the diagnostician too, and the proof is that his death, the play's first, is the direct product of a feeling, contempt for Tybalt's formality and for Romeo's submission, that he would have diagnosed in any other man as a fit of the hot blood, and ridden out of the square laughing.

## Grief Overspent: The Laments, the Rehearsal, and the Mother Who Dies of It

Act 4 Scene 5, the discovery of Juliet's counterfeit corpse, is the play's set piece of grief performed past every limit, and it is constructed as a contest. The mourners enter in sequence and take their turns like singers: Paris, the Nurse, Lady Capulet, Capulet himself, each lament formally shaped, each striving to outbid the last in the rhetoric of loss. Capulet's contribution states the scene's method in a single image: "Death lies on her like an untimely frost / Upon the sweetest flower of all the field." The grief is real; the Capulets have lost, as they believe, their only child. And the grief is also, unmistakably, a performance with an audience, a household ritual in which sorrow must be produced at ceremonial volume to count as sorrow at all. The Friar, arriving with the funeral's arrangements, supplies the doctrine's verdict in the doctrine's plainest terms, rebuking the household for loving its child so well that heaven's share in her has been forgotten, and the rebuke slides off the scene like water. What actually punctures the lament is not doctrine but farce: the musicians, hired for a wedding and retained for a funeral, refuse to play, and Peter's wrangle with them over silver sounds and wooden wit drags the scene's register down from ceremonial grief to the economics of a cancelled booking. Shakespeare places the puncture deliberately. A grief that can be interrupted by a dispute about musicians' fees was a grief whose volume was partly theatrical, and the audience is trusted to hear the difference between the lament's excess and the thing itself. The play is measuring its mourners even as they perform, and finding the performance larger than the loss it can yet know, since the loss, at this point, is counterfeit.

Juliet's own rehearsal of grief and madness, one scene earlier, is the antitype of her parents' performance, and the pairing of the two scenes is the play's quietest piece of structural criticism. Act 4 Scene 3 gives her no audience at all. She dismisses the Nurse, locks the door of the scene against every witness, and then performs, alone, the most disciplined act of imagination in the play: a step-by-step rehearsal of everything the potion might mean. The fears arrive in ascending order, each one stated, examined, and answered before the next is admitted. The Friar might have given her poison in earnest, to escape the shame of the first marriage; the draught might simply fail; she might wake too early, and suffocate; she might wake on time and find the terror of the place, the bones of all her buried ancestors packed around her, bloody Tybalt festering in his shroud, the spirits resorting at their hours, too much for reason to survive. It is at the summit of this ascent that the play's key word makes its most frightening appearance, in her own mouth, about her own future self: if she wakes among the shrieks "like mandrakes torn out of the earth, / That living mortals, hearing them, run mad", then she too might be distraught, might "madly play with my forefathers' joints, / And pluck the mangled Tybalt from his shroud, / And, in this rage, with some great kinsman's bone, / As with a club, dash out my desperate brains". The horror of the passage is not the gore. It is the precision. Madness is imagined with a stage manager's completeness, props assigned, sequence blocked, and the imagining is then simply set aside, neither denied nor indulged, while she drinks: "Romeo, I come! This do I drink to thee." Set the two scenes side by side and the play's scale of grief inverts every expectation. The household with every counsellor, priest, and ritual at its disposal produces grief as noise. The girl alone in her chamber produces grief as method. Hers is the larger terror by an order of magnitude, and hers is the only one in Act 4 that finishes its business.

The economy has one more entry to make, and it is the ledger's most quietly devastating line, delivered by report in the final scene. Montague, arriving at the tomb too late for everything, explains his wife's absence: grief for her son's exile has stopped her breath; Lady Montague is dead of the banishment before the banishment's consequences have finished unfolding. The play gives this death no scene, no lament, no statue. It is mentioned inside another man's speech about a larger catastrophe, and its placement is the judgment. Here is grief's excess in its purest form, a feeling that exceeded the body that contained it and stopped its breath as a draught stops a pulse, and Verona's economy of measure has no ceremony for it at all, because the woman who died of it never held a rank the ceremonies serve. The lovers' excess will be gilded. The fathers' excess will be priced in gold against gold. Lady Montague's is entered in the record as a subordinate clause. When the article's final section speaks of a measure refused, this is the refusal at its starkest: the play can count every other grief in the city, and it counts this one in passing, in someone else's sentence, on the way to the bodies that matter more.

## Frenzy in the Tomb: Excess at the Point of No Return

The last movement of the play compresses the whole economy into two scenes, and the compression begins with a refusal of measure so pure that it can be timed. Balthasar reaches Mantua with his report. Romeo hears it, and the play gives him no interval of inquiry at all: no question about the funeral, the witnesses, the letter that should have come, the Friar's silence. The report is converted into a resolution inside a single speech: "Well, Juliet, I will lie with thee tonight. / Let's see for means. O mischief, thou art swift / To enter in the thoughts of desperate men!" The defiance that follows, "I defy you, stars", has been read as the play's most heroic line and as its most deranged, and the economy of measure explains why both readings persist: defiance of the stars is the one act in the play that excess and courage perform in identical language. What the scene actually shows, before any stars are defied, is a mind declining to gather the information that measure requires. Romeo in Mantua is the anti-Juliet. Given the worst news of his life, she would have cross-examined the messenger until the report confessed its gaps. He dismisses the messenger, pays him, and goes shopping.

### What does Romeo's purchase from the Apothecary reveal about excess meeting want?

It shows the economy's two poles in one transaction: a buyer with gold beyond his need and a will beyond the law, a seller whose poverty has starved his refusal away, so that the death passes between surplus and famine, each man's excess supplying the other's lack.

The Apothecary scene is the ledger's grimmest entry because it is the only transaction in the play conducted entirely without illusion. Romeo reads the man's body as an inventory of want: "Famine is in thy cheeks, / Need and oppression starveth in thine eyes, / Contempt and beggary hangs upon thy back." The Apothecary states the terms of his own measurement and its failure in the play's most quietly devastating line: "My poverty, but not my will, consents." Romeo answers with the doctrine inverted: "I pay thy poverty, and not thy will." Everything the Friar taught about use and abuse is present, reversed. A compound that Mantua's law forbids on pain of death changes hands for forty ducats, and Romeo's commentary on the gold outruns the poison in its bitterness: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell." The speech is lucid, moral, even wise. It is spoken by a man in the act of buying the means of his own death, and the lucidity changes nothing, which is the play's last word on the doctrine's practical power: a man may hold the entire philosophy of measure in his mouth and be carried past it by a grief that has stopped consulting him.

The tomb scene distributes the frenzy with a fairness the rest of the play has withheld. Romeo arrives naming his own condition with a precision his measurers might envy: "The time and my intents are savage-wild, / More fierce and more inexorable far / Than empty tigers or the roaring sea." Savage-wild: the taxonomy of the cell, the unreasonable fury of a beast, is now self-applied, and it does not slow him. Paris, keeping his vigil among the flowers, meets the frenzy with the period's correct protocol for a desperate man, which is a plea and a warning: "Good gentle youth, tempt not a desperate man." Paris dies of the encounter, the play's moderate suitor, whose courtship had observed every form and waited on every consent, killed by the excess he had spent the play declining to practise. Romeo's address to the corpse is the frenzy's strangest product, a courtesy so complete it reads as a second madness laid over the first: he begs pardon of Tybalt, he grants Paris's dying wish, he arranges the bodies like a sexton. Then the poison: "Come, bitter conduct, come, unsavoury guide! / Thou desperate pilot, now at once run on / The dashing rocks thy sea-sick weary bark! / Here's to my love! O true apothecary! / Thy drugs are quick. Thus with a kiss I die." Steering, the faculty whose failure the whole article has tracked, is formally handed to the drug. The pilot is desperate. The bark is sea-sick and weary. The passenger has stopped pretending to navigate.

The frenzy, inspected closely, has a second form, and the form is courtesy. Romeo arrives at the vault having made his arrangements with an administrator's completeness: a letter for his father explaining everything, instructions and a ring for his servant, a warning to that servant, enforced by a threat to tear him joint by joint, against following or interrupting. Nothing in the night's business has been left unplanned except the one thing planning cannot supply, which is a reason to stop. Inside the vault the courtesy continues, directed now at the dead. He apologizes to Tybalt's corpse, "Forgive me, cousin", and frames his own death as a favour performed for the man he killed, the debt of the square discharged by the hand that incurred it. He grants Paris's last request and lays him in the vault beside Juliet. He compliments the apothecary. He toasts his love. The sequence is horrifying precisely because it is so well conducted: this is not a mind dissolved into noise but a mind executing a programme, every social form observed at full polish while the programme's destination is a draught of poison in a tomb. The play's period psychology had a category for this, and the cell scene has already supplied it: reason intact, premises rigged, judgment outvoted by the image it was appointed to weigh. Romeo's last hour is Bright's melancholy at its most legible, the fixation conducting its own funeral with perfect manners. Set beside it the Friar's arrival, his stumbling run, his flight at the sound of the watch, and the tomb scene's distribution of madness completes itself: the patient dies composed, the physician flees distraught, and the only person in the vault who neither raves nor runs is the girl who has just woken from a counterfeit of death into the real one's antechamber.

Juliet wakes into the economy's final settlement, and her part in it is the article's last piece of evidence. She wakes with her instruments intact. She reads the scene correctly at a glance, identifies the cup, the poison, the sequence of events, cross-examines the Friar in three lines, and refuses his remedy, an offered hiding "among a sisterhood of holy nuns", with the play's flattest sentence of self-government: "Go, get thee hence, for I will not away." Her death follows, and its form is the form of her whole career in the play: where Romeo's excess is a flood, hers is a decision. "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath. There rust, and let me die." The Friar's terror in Act 4 Scene 3, her own rehearsed fear that she would wake distraught and play madly with her forefathers' joints, is answered by the event: she wakes, reasons, refuses rescue, and acts, in the vault, among the bones, exactly as she feared she could not. The play's largest excess is committed by its best measurer, in full possession of her measure. That fact, more than any speech, is what the critics of the next section are really arguing about.

## Condemn or Celebrate? The Critics and the Editor's Text

The double verdict this article has argued is not a modern compromise; it is the oldest quarrel in the play's criticism, and it can be stated as a disagreement between two named readers about what the play's forms do to its feelings. Harry Levin, in "Form and Formality in Romeo and Juliet", argued that the play's conspicuous formalities, its sonnets, set speeches, patterned debates, and rhetorical symmetries, are not ornaments on the passion but a counter-principle to it: the art of the play keeps imposing shape on material that strains toward shapelessness, and the tension between the formality and the feeling is the play's meaning. On Levin's account the doctrine of measure has an ally inside the poetry itself. The sonnet the lovers share at their first meeting is the doctrine enacted: two speakers, fourteen lines, desire arriving already rhymed, already counted, already shared out between two voices in equal portions. Catherine Belsey, in Desire: Love Stories in Western Culture, reads the same evidence from the other end. For Belsey, Romeo's desire is not a natural force that forms then discipline; it is produced by the Petrarchan discourse he has learned, a script of sighs, oxymorons, and unattainable ladies that precedes any particular woman and supplies the feelings it claims to express. On her account the madness of the first act is not Romeo's at all. It is the discourse's, a borrowed derangement worn like the borrowed finery of the oxymoron catalogue, and the play's interest lies in watching the script fail to contain what happens when the lady answers back.

The adjudication this article offers runs between them, with the text as referee. Belsey is right about Act 1, and the play says so: Romeo's Rosaline fixation is script-driven, object-indifferent, and cured by substitution, exactly as a discourse model predicts, and the Friar's diagnosis, eyes not hearts, is Belsey's point in the play's own words. Levin is right about the forms, and the play says that too: the shared sonnet, the patterned aubade, the balanced laments are measuring instruments, and the audience feels the feeling through them. Where both accounts fall short is Juliet's bounty speech and the tomb. No Petrarchan script taught Juliet to theorize an infinite that grows by giving; the discourse she inherited had no such theorem, and she states it as a correction of the counting world, not an inheritance from it. And no formality in the tomb contains what happens there: the speeches lengthen, the courtesies to corpses multiply, the rhymes thin, as if the play's instruments were being read past their scale. The verdict the text supports is that excess in Romeo and Juliet is real feeling that begins in borrowed language and ends by outrunning every form built to hold it, including the forms of the doctrine that condemns it. Levin supplies the scale. Belsey supplies the starting weight. The play supplies the overflow.

The aubade of Act 3 Scene 5 is the test case both critics would claim, and it settles the adjudication in the text's favour. The parting at dawn is staged as a formal debate, lark against nightingale, each lover arguing the bird that prolongs the night, in balanced exchanges whose patterning Levin's account predicts and honours. Form is doing its utmost: the most dangerous minutes of the lovers' lives are being conducted as a rhetorical exercise with rules, turns, and a conceded conclusion. And the form fails on schedule, not because the feeling is too large for it but because the world outside the form, Lady Capulet's knock, the Nurse's warning, the day's arrangements, simply overruns the exercise. The debate's last exchange abandons the contest in a single couplet of pure measure language inverted: more light, and more dark to their woes, the quantities rising together. Belsey's script explains how the lovers learned to speak. Levin's formality explains the instrument they speak through. Neither explains the knock at the door, which is the play's own contribution: excess is what the world does to forms while the forms are still being beautifully observed.

The editor's part in the quarrel is quieter and prior to both. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, follows the second quarto of 1599, the fuller early text, and the decision matters to this article's subject in a specific way: the doctrine of measure survives in its most quotable forms, the full herb speech, the complete wedding warning with its honey and its moderate counsel, because the longer text preserves speeches that the shorter first quarto compresses. The play that condemns excess in its doctrine does so at a length that is itself a kind of abundance, hundreds of lines of counsel that performance after performance has trimmed, as if the theatre kept re-enacting the play's own discovery: the doctrine is indispensable, and there is always too much of it to hold.

## The Measure Refused: Paris, the Friar's Plan, and the Golden Statues

It remains to ask whether anyone in Verona loves moderately, and the play's answer is a graveyard. Paris is the moderate suitor, and the play is scrupulous about his moderation. He sues through the father, accepts the father's delays, waits on the two more summers, mourns at the tomb with flowers and sweet water, and keeps every form the city owns. His reward is to be killed at the tomb door by the excess he declined to practise, while performing the one act his moderation still required of him, the arrest of a vandal. The Friar is the doctrine's keeper, and his plan to rescue Juliet is the doctrine's final refutation by its own author: a scheme of such immoderate daring, a counterfeit death, a sealed letter, a waking timed to the hour in a vault of corpses, that it collapses under a single quarantined messenger. The plan is the herb speech read backwards, a good strained so far from its fair use that it revolts into the catastrophe it was compounded to prevent. The city's official measurer fares no better than its lovers. Prince Escalus opens the play with a sentence of perfect disproportion, death as the forfeit for street brawling, a penalty so far beyond the offence that three brawls are required to bring it anywhere near execution, and when the moment arrives in Act 3 he commutes it on the spot, substituting the banishment whose immoderation the cell scene then anatomizes. His judgment after the duel states the failure in his own words before he can catch it: he will be deaf to pleading and excuses, he announces, in the same speech in which he proceeds to hear the pleading and halve the sentence. The Prince is not a hypocrite. He is something the play finds more interesting, a measuring instrument whose scale was set by the feud's temperature long before he read it, so that his severities and his mercies alike arrive miscalibrated, each one an excess wearing the robe of its opposite. Verona's disorder is not that it lacks a chief measurer. It is that the office itself has been infected by the quantity it exists to govern.

And the fathers, at the end, answer the whole economy in kind. Their grief, arriving too late for measure, expresses itself as competitive expenditure: a statue in pure gold, a counter-statue, sacrifice priced against sacrifice. Verona's final act of feeling is an auction. The play closes its account of excess not with repentance of immoderation but with its apotheosis in gold, two old men outbidding each other over the bodies of the children their own unmeasured hatred spent, while the Prince, the city's chief measurer, pronounces the only verdict the economy has earned: all are punished. The doctrine was right about everything. It governed nothing. That is the play's judgment, stated in doctrine, celebrated in language, and sealed in the tomb. One coda belongs to the audience rather than to Verona. The phrase the culture has carried away from the wedding scene is the warning itself, violent delights have violent ends, and it circulates in the world as a thrill, a caption for pleasures their owners intend to keep, the doctrine quoted in the spirit of the delights. The migration re-enacts the play with a fidelity no staging could match. Given the choice the play dramatizes at every level, between the Friar's sentence and the lovers' experience of the sentence's truth, readers have chosen as Romeo chose: to keep the words, admire them, repeat them, and walk past them into the next delight. Excess, the play finally proposes, is not a failure of information, in Verona or anywhere else. It is the normal relation between a creature that can measure everything and a heart that experiences measurement as an interruption. The Friar's scales are still accurate. They are still on the table. The tragedy is that the table is in the cell, and the cell is empty, and the play's last image of measure is two fathers bidding in gold over the proof.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch excess ledger, and what pattern does it expose?
The InsightCrunch excess ledger is the table in this article that gathers the play's principal images of madness and excess in performance order, setting each image beside the counsel of measure it answers or refuses. The pattern it exposes is distribution: the vocabulary of the too-much belongs to every rank and generation in Verona, lovers, friar, friend, father, and bride alike, while the counsel of measure, though present beside nearly every excess, is obeyed by almost none of them. The ledger turns a familiar impression, that the lovers feel too much, into a countable claim: excess is the city's common medium, and the tragedy is a failure of measuring instruments, not a shortage of warnings.

### Q: Why does the Friar state his doctrine of measure twice, and what does the second statement add that the first does not?
The Friar states the doctrine twice because the play needs it first as philosophy and then as counsel. The herb speech of Act 2 Scene 3 states it abstractly, over a basket: virtue itself turns vice when misapplied, and grace and rude will share one plant. The wedding speech of Act 2 Scene 6 applies it to the case in front of him, and the application adds the two things the abstraction lacked, a mechanism and an instruction. The mechanism is surfeit, honey confounding the appetite from inside the pleasure. The instruction is plain prose urgency: love moderately, because too swift arrives as tardy as too slow.

### Q: What did the word "mad" most often mean in the play's first performances, and how does that differ from a modern diagnosis?
In the play's first performances, mad most often meant beside oneself: enraged, reckless, transported, or unseated by a passion, a temporary condition of the blood and the spirits rather than a fixed state of the mind. Capulet uses it for his own anger, Benvolio for the brawling temper of a hot day, Juliet for the distraught self she fears in the vault. A modern diagnosis names a condition that removes its subject from responsibility and invites treatment. The period word names a quantity of feeling that invites judgment. That is why it can be spoken as an accusation, a boast, and a self-description within a single scene, and why the play can build a whole economy of measure on it.

### Q: Why is Romeo's madness over Rosaline reported by other characters while his madness over Juliet is staged for the audience?
The difference is the play's judgment on two kinds of knowledge. Rosaline's case is reported because reports are how Verona manages the young: Montague describes the symptoms, Benvolio prescribes the cure, Mercutio mocks the patient, the Friar scolds the fixation, and the community treats the whole affair as a phase to be supervised. Juliet's case is staged because staging is how the play shows supervision failing. Once the fixation fixes on a woman who answers, the reports stop, the audience is placed inside the orchard and the cell, and every measuring instrument is watched failing in real time rather than described after the fact. Reported madness is governable. Staged madness is the tragedy itself.

### Q: How does the small word "too" carry the play's judgments of excess in the lovers' own speeches?
The word too is the doctrine's smallest instrument, and the lovers wield it against themselves. Juliet's self-diagnosis in the orchard is a triad of it: the contract is "too rash, too sudden, too unadvised", each adjective a measurement, each measurement accurate. Romeo's catastrophe can be told as the absence of the word: no quantity in his speeches is ever too much, not one minute of joy weighed against any sorrow, not exile weighed against the world. The Friar's wedding counsel turns on the same hinge, "too swift arrives as tardy as too slow". Excess in this play is rarely announced by its large words, madness and frenzy. It is confessed, or refused, by the small one.

### Q: Why does the Friar end the cell scene by sending Romeo to Juliet's chamber rather than straight into exile?
The Friar ends the scene that way because his remedies are actions, and the night visit is the one action that converts Romeo's despair into obedience. The rebuke has failed as argument: shame and the inventory of blessings have brought Romeo to a dagger, not to proportion. The visit gives the grief a task, a direction, and a deadline, and it promises the one quantity Romeo's arithmetic accepts, Juliet herself, weighed against the sentence. The choice also exposes the doctrine's limit. The Friar can govern Romeo only by feeding the fixation he has been diagnosing, which is why the plan's comforts keep requiring larger plans, until the last of them fills a vault.

### Q: What does Capulet's rage in Act 3 Scene 5 share with the lovers' passion that his household refuses to see in it?
Capulet's rage shares the passion's whole structure: total fixation on one object, contempt for counsel, the conversion of a setback into a cosmic injury, and the ultimatum that accepts no proportion. His opening oath names the condition himself, "God's bread! it makes me mad", and the speech then performs what the lovers' speeches perform, a feeling multiplying every quantity it touches, care, time, and obligation all inflated past their denominations. The household refuses the resemblance because the diagnosis of madness is, in Verona, an instrument of government applied downward. A father's rage is authority's weather. A daughter's love is a fit to be cured, and the same household speaks both sentences in one scene.

### Q: Why is the feud's hatred never called madness by anyone in the play, though the lovers' love is called it again and again?
The feud escapes the word because the word is assigned by the order the feud sustains. Madness, in Verona's usage, is the label for feeling that breaks the arrangements: a son's fixation, a daughter's refusal, a penitent's grief. The feud breaks nothing the city recognizes as an arrangement; it is the arrangement, older than any living speaker, woven into honour, kinship, and the street's daily expectations. Benvolio comes closest, blaming a brawl on "the mad blood stirring", and even his phrase makes the madness a property of hot days rather than of the hatred itself. The play leaves the largest excess in Verona undiagnosed on stage, and the omission is its sharpest comment on who owns the diagnosis.

### Q: How does Mercutio's mockery of Romeo's love differ from the Friar's warning against it?
The two critiques differ in their theory of the disease. Mercutio treats Romeo's love as appetite in costume: a fit of the body, curable by ridicule, by company, by the restoration of wit, and his mockery is a form of treatment, laughter as physic. The Friar treats the same love as a quantity in the soul's economy: a good strained from its use, curable only by proportion, and his warning is a form of doctrine, measure as medicine. Mercutio's critique is the funnier and the Friar's the more accurate, and the play tests both to destruction: Romeo's wit returns under Mercutio's treatment while the disease worsens, and the Friar's doctrine describes the disease perfectly while its author becomes its accomplice.

### Q: What does Mercutio's welcome of Romeo in Act 2 Scene 4 admit that his mockery elsewhere works to deny?
Mercutio's welcome admits that the wit he prizes and the love he mocks live in the same man and rise together. Greeting the recovered Romeo, "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in", he celebrates a restoration that has in fact been caused by the new love, not by freedom from love: Romeo is witty again because Juliet has replaced Rosaline, and Mercutio's delight quietly concedes that the transports were never the opposite of the intelligence. His mockery elsewhere needs the opposite premise, that the lover is a patient and the wit the healthy self underneath. The welcome scene gives the game away in a single burst of pleasure, hours before the square scene proves which of the two premises Verona can afford.

### Q: How does Juliet's terror in Act 4 Scene 3 exceed every fear she has shown before it, and why does she master it alone?
Juliet's terror exceeds her earlier fears because its object is her own mind. The orchard's fear was discovery; the wedding-morning fear was separation; the vault speech rehearses something worse, the prospect of waking distraught among the bones and playing madly with her forefathers' joints, reason itself dissolving before Romeo can arrive. She masters it alone because the play has systematically removed every counsellor: the Nurse has defected, her parents are the threat, the Friar is the plan's author and cannot be asked to audit it. What remains is the method she has practised all along, fear cross-examined fear by fear, each named and weighed, until the vial can be drained to a toast. Solitude is the condition; self-audit is the means.

### Q: Why does Romeo ask Balthasar no questions about how Juliet died before resolving to join her?
Romeo asks no questions because inquiry belongs to the economy of measure, and his grief has left that economy. A question admits an interval, the possibility that the report is partial, mistaken, or survivable; his speech in Mantua is built to exclude intervals, moving from the news to the resolve, "Well, Juliet, I will lie with thee tonight", within a breath. The contrast with Juliet is structural: given a false report in Act 3 Scene 2, she interrogates the Nurse until the facts surrender their order. Romeo in Act 5 interrogates no one, pays the messenger, and dismisses him. The play marks the tragedy of the ending as a failure of curiosity as much as a failure of fortune: the one man who needed to ask questions had been trained by five acts of counsel to stop hearing answers.

### Q: What does Romeo mean by calling his intents savage-wild in the tomb scene?
Romeo means that the taxonomy of the cell scene has become his own. In Act 3 Scene 3 the Friar had read his wild acts as "the unreasonable fury of a beast", a man dropped a species by grief; at the tomb door Romeo applies the same zoology to himself, "The time and my intents are savage-wild, / More fierce and more inexorable far / Than empty tigers or the roaring sea". The self-diagnosis is exact, and it changes nothing: he proceeds past Paris's plea, past his own lucid commentary, to the poison. The line is the doctrine's final evidence. A man can name his excess with a professional's precision and be carried by it in the same sentence, because naming was never the faculty the excess had disabled.

### Q: Why does Juliet refuse the Friar's offer to place her among a sisterhood of holy nuns at the tomb?
Juliet refuses because the offer asks her to survive as the plan's remainder, and survival on those terms would convert her whole story into the Friar's evidence. The sisterhood is a hiding place, a life resumed under concealment after the catastrophe, and her refusal, "Go, get thee hence, for I will not away", is the last act of the self-government she has practised since the orchard. It is also the completion of her pattern of excess. Every prior excess of hers was a commitment enlarged past the world's offer: a marriage past a meeting, a bounty past counting, a potion past fear. The refusal belongs to the series. She will not be the measured survivor of her own immeasurable choice.

### Q: Why does the Prologue call the lovers' love death-marked rather than excessive, and what judgment does that choice withhold?
The Prologue calls the love death-marked because the frame's business is outcome, not proportion: "the fearful passage of their death-marked love" announces where the story ends, not how its feelings should be weighed. The choice withholds the doctrine. An audience told in advance that the love is excessive would watch the play as a sentencing, each transport another count in the indictment. An audience told only that the love is marked by death watches the same transports as splendours under a shadow, and must do its own measuring, scene by scene, against the Friar's standard and Juliet's arithmetic. The play's double verdict depends on that withholding: the condemnation is left inside the action, where it can be argued with, and the celebration is left in the poetry, where it cannot be argued away.

### Q: How can a production let an audience enjoy the excess that the dialogue keeps condemning?
A production does it by trusting the poetry's scale rather than illustrating the doctrine's warnings. The orchard scene played at full rhetorical stretch, the bounty speech given its whole swell, the tomb speeches allowed their courtesies and their length: these let the house feel the quantity the Friar is measuring, and the condemnation lands precisely because the enjoyment was real. The opposite choice, lovers played as foolish children under a knowing directorial frown, settles the play's argument in advance and forfeits its tragedy. The text's own balance is the guide. Its doctrine is spoken by characters the action keeps overruling, and its excess is written in the verse the audience remembers. A staging that honours both, doctrine audible and excess glorious, reproduces the double verdict instead of resolving it.

### Q: In what way is the Friar's potion plan itself an act of excess, and where does the play register the fact?
The plan is an excess of ingenuity: a counterfeit death, a forty-two hour sleep, a sealed letter to Mantua, a waking timed to the hour in a vault of corpses, each stage exact and the whole structure resting on a single messenger. Weighed by the Friar's own doctrine, a remedy has been strained so far from its fair use, a sleeping draught asked to do the work of a public reconciliation, that it revolts into the catastrophe it was compounded to prevent. The play registers the fact in the plan's own language: the Friar presents it to Juliet as a remedy for desperate execution, wracked with its risks even as he lists them, and the scene's haste, a bride at the point of a knife, leaves no room for the moderation he preached at her wedding.

### Q: Does any character in the play love moderately, and what becomes of the attempt?
Paris is the attempt, and the tomb is what becomes of it. His courtship observes every measure the city owns: suit made through the father, delay accepted, the two more summers granted without protest, grief at the vault expressed in flowers, sweet water, and a vigil kept in person. The play does not mock the moderation; it honours it with the suitor's sincerity and then demonstrates its helplessness. Moderate love in Verona has no instrument but the forms, and the forms cannot protect it: Paris dies at the tomb door performing the one duty his code still assigns him, the arrest of a supposed vandal, killed by the excess he spent the play declining to practise.

### Q: How does Juliet's grief in Act 3 Scene 2 differ in measure from Romeo's grief in the Friar's cell?
The two griefs differ in the direction of their audit. Juliet's grief is audited by its owner: the oxymoron storm breaks over the news of Tybalt's death, and within the same speech she cross-examines it, recovers the fact that Tybalt would have killed her husband, and re-weighs catastrophe as comfort. Romeo's grief is audited entirely from outside: the Friar shames the posture, inventories the blessings, and reprices the sentence, while Romeo's own speech keeps inflating every quantity it touches, banishment into the end of the world, a fly above a Montague heir. Hers contracts toward proportion under its own pressure. His expands under correction. The scenes are a matched pair, and the play's sympathy follows the method, not the volume: her grief is the larger, and it is the one that learns.

### Q: Why does the play close its account of excess with golden statues rather than with any spoken repentance of immoderation?
The statues close the account because the fathers' grief speaks the only language their household ever trusted, expenditure, and the play wants the economy exposed at full size. Montague vows a statue in pure gold; Capulet matches it; the reconciliation is conducted as competitive bidding over the children's bodies, grief measuring itself against grief and each bid declared a sacrifice. A spoken repentance would belong to the doctrine's world, the world of the Friar's cell, where feelings are examined and repriced. The ending belongs to the world that actually governed Verona, where feelings are spent. The statues are the final entry in the excess ledger, and the Prince's sentence over them, that all are punished, is the only audit the city ever completes.
