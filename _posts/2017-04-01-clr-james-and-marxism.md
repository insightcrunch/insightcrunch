C. L. R. James was a Caribbean Marxist who wrote history, political theory, literary criticism, and one of the finest books ever written about a sport, and the work on which his reputation rests is a history of the only slave revolution that ever succeeded. The Black Jacobins, published in 1938, reconstructs the uprising in the French sugar colony of San Domingo that destroyed slavery there, defeated three European powers in turn, and founded the state of Haiti. Its central argument is not the moral one a reader might expect. James held that the enslaved workers of the great sugar plantations, because of the way their labour was organised, stood closer to a modern proletariat than any body of workers then existing in Europe. That claim, and not the narrative of suffering and heroism that surrounds it, is what makes the book a work of Marxist analysis, and it is the thread this profile follows through the whole of his long career.

![C.L.R. James, The Black Jacobins and the four Jameses - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-15.webp)

The profile makes three organising distinctions. First, it treats the proletarianisation argument as an argument: a claim about the organisation of production, with premises that can be inspected and a conclusion that can be resisted, rather than as a rhetorical tribute paid by a historian to his subjects. Second, it keeps separate the several Jameses who are usually blended into a single picturesque figure, the Trinidadian journalist and cricketer, the historian of the Haitian revolution, the American revolutionary organiser, and the returned West Indian man of letters, because the arguments change as the phases change and a blended portrait conceals the changes. Third, it states at full strength the dispute about where James stands in relation to Marxism itself. A major scholarly reading, published in 1983, places him as a figure who passed through Marxism into an autonomous Black radical tradition. James never accepted that placement and described his work as Marxist to the end of his life. Both positions are given here in their strongest forms, with their authors named, and the article does not adjudicate between them, because the dispute is itself one of the most instructive things in the whole field of Marxism and race.

What a reader should carry away is precise. The enslaved of San Domingo, on James's account, were not a proletariat because they suffered most, or because they were propertyless in the way a wage worker is propertyless, or because history needed a revolutionary class in the Caribbean and supplied one by courtesy. They were a proletariat, if they were, because the sugar plantation had already done to them what the factory would later do to European workers: gathered them in hundreds, divided their labour into coordinated operations, disciplined their working day by a bell and a production process that could not pause, and made their cooperation a technical requirement of the product itself. Sugar cane, once cut, must be milled quickly or the juice spoils, so field and factory on a great estate formed a single integrated process, and the people caught inside that process acquired the habits of combination that James believed made collective political action possible. Whether the classification survives scrutiny is a genuine question, and the strongest objection to it is stated and answered in this profile. But the claim's character is not in doubt. It is a claim about how people worked, made by a Marxist who thought that how people worked was the deepest fact about them.

## The Black Jacobins and the proletarianisation argument

The book appeared in London in 1938, written by a Trinidadian then in his late thirties who had arrived in England six years earlier with a manuscript about West Indian self-government and a reputation earned in the island's journalism. It was not the work of an academic. James had no university post and no archive of his own; he worked from printed sources, from the French historiography of the revolution, and from a political education acquired inside the Trotskyist movement he had lately joined. Yet the history he produced has outlasted nearly all the professional scholarship of its decade, and it did so because its narrative is load-bearing: every episode is arranged so that the reader can watch a class discover its own capacity. The enslaved begin the book as objects of policy, traded, branded in the records of others, counted as cargo and as capital. They end it as the makers of a state, having defeated the armies of Spain, Britain, and France. The transformation between those two conditions is the book's real subject, and the proletarianisation argument is James's explanation of why the transformation was possible in San Domingo when slave revolts elsewhere, however brave, had been crushed or had dissolved.

To see the argument's distinctiveness, place it beside the two treatments of slavery available to a Marxist writer in the nineteen thirties. The first treated slavery as a pre-capitalist survival, a barbaric residue that capitalism would sweep away as it matured, so that the enslaved belonged to history's waiting room and their revolts were at best anticipations of a politics that only free wage labour could conduct. The second treated the enslaved as a peasantry in chains, whose natural aim was land and subsistence, and whose revolts therefore belonged to the long history of peasant insurgency. James rejected both framings, and he rejected them on evidence drawn from the plantation itself. The great estates of the North Plain of San Domingo were not survivals of an older world. They were among the most advanced productive enterprises of the eighteenth century, capital-intensive, export-oriented, worked by labour forces numbering in the hundreds, and integrated into Atlantic markets for sugar, credit, and insurance. The colony supplied an enormous share of the world's sugar and a large share of its coffee, and it did so through an organisation of production that had more in common with a factory than with a feudal demesne. A workforce assembled on that scale, in that discipline, for that market could not be analysed as a residue. It had to be analysed as a product of capital itself, and once that step is taken the classification of its workers becomes a serious analytical problem rather than a courtesy title.

### Why does James call the enslaved a proletariat?

Because of how they worked. On the large sugar plantations they laboured in disciplined groups under a coordinated process producing for export markets, which he argued made them closer to a modern proletariat than any European workers of the time. The claim concerns organisation, not status.

The reasoning runs through the labour process, and it deserves to be followed step by step. A sugar estate was a combined operation. Gangs cut cane in the fields; carts moved it to the mill; the mill ground it through rollers; the boiling house reduced the juice through a series of coppers, each stage timed against the next; coopers, masons, carpenters, and stockmen maintained the plant. Because cut cane deteriorates within a day or two, the harvest imposed a rhythm that no individual worker could set: the mill's appetite governed the field's pace, and the boiling house's sequence governed the mill. Work began before dawn and, in crop time, ran in shifts through the night. The enslaved therefore experienced something that European artisans of the same period almost never experienced, which is labour as a continuous collective process in which each person's task presupposed the simultaneous performance of many others. James's inference was that this experience, and not any legal attribute, is what forms a proletariat in the politically relevant sense: a body of people who already cooperate as a matter of daily routine, who know their own numbers, who share grievances that are structural rather than personal, and who can therefore act together when the moment arrives.

Contrast the European comparison class he had in mind. The workers of nineteen thirties mythology, the miners and mill hands of the industrial revolution, were still two generations in the future for most of Europe in the seventeen nineties. The typical European labourer of that decade was a peasant on a smallholding, a journeyman in a workshop of three or four, a domestic servant, or a handloom weaver working alone at home. Such workers were exploited, often brutally, but their exploitation was dispersed. It did not gather them, instruct them in combination, or give them a common daily experience of an integrated process. James's provocation was to invert the expected geography of modernity: the most modern labour process in the Atlantic world, judged by scale, coordination, and market integration, was to be found not in Manchester but on the slave plantations of the Caribbean, and the people inside it were correspondingly the workers most nearly formed for collective action. European Marxism, organised around the gradual maturation of an industrial working class at home, had simply not looked at the place where capital had built its largest workplaces first.

### What does the labour process mean in this argument?

The labour process is the concrete organisation of work: who labours, with what tools, in what groupings, under whose direction, and at what pace. James argues that this organisation, rather than legal status or wage form, is what forms a class's capacity to act together.

Unpacking that definition shows how much of the argument is carried by a single analytical choice. A class, in the Marxist vocabulary James inherited, can be defined by several different criteria, and the criteria do not always coincide. There is definition by legal status: slave, serf, free citizen. There is definition by relation to the means of production: owner, tenant, propertyless labourer. There is definition by income form: rent, profit, wage, subsistence. And there is definition by position in the labour process: the artisan who controls a whole task, the peasant household that organises its own work, the operative who performs one fragment of a coordinated whole. Orthodox usage treated the wage as the decisive marker of the proletarian, because the sale of labour power is the specific relation that defines capitalism in Marx's own construction. James did not deny that construction. He subordinated it, for the purposes of explaining a revolution, to the labour-process criterion, because the question he needed to answer was not how surplus was extracted from the enslaved, though he answered that too, but how a population held in the most extreme unfreedom imaginable acquired the discipline, the cohesion, and the strategic intelligence to defeat empires. His answer was that the plantation had trained them. The extraction relation explained their misery; the labour process explained their power.

It follows that the argument is empirical in a way its critics sometimes miss. It stands or falls on the description of the estates: their size, their integration, their gang organisation, their working rhythms. If the typical San Domingo plantation had been a smallholding worked by a dozen people under a resident owner, the argument would collapse, whatever one's sympathies. James built his opening chapters to establish the contrary description, dwelling on the scale of the great properties, the hundreds held on the largest of them, the absentee ownership that left management to salaried attorneys and overseers, and the refinements of the boiling house. Later economic historiography has complicated the picture in detail, emphasising variation between estates and regions, but the broad description of the sugar sector as large-scale, capitalised, and process-integrated has held. The argument's vulnerability lies elsewhere, in the classification step, and the profile returns to that vulnerability when it states the strongest objection. What the labour-process definition gives the reader, even if the classification is refused, is a way of seeing the plantation that moral history cannot supply: as a workplace, with a sociology, whose organisation had political consequences that its owners never intended.

### What does James not claim about the enslaved?

He does not claim they sold their labour power, owned nothing but their capacity to work, or were free to leave an employer. He does not claim their condition resembled the wage worker's in law or in daily security. The claim is narrower: organisation formed capacity.

The narrowing matters because much casual criticism of the book attacks claims it never made. James knew precisely what slavery was. No writer of his generation described the Middle Passage, the branding, the calculus of working people to death and replacing them from Africa because replacement was cheaper than maintenance, with colder fury than he did, and he never suggested that the enslaved occupied the juridical position of a Manchester operative. His claim was about what the organisation of production had made of them collectively, set against what the law said they were individually. Indeed the tension between those two registers is the book's engine. Legally they were things, items in an inventory, valued in account books between the livestock and the buildings. Functionally they were the coordinated workforce of the Atlantic world's most productive export sector. A revolution, on James's telling, is what happens when the functional reality breaks the legal fiction: when people treated as things act on the capacities their work has given them and the inventory stands up. Readers who miss the distinction between the two registers will find the proletariat label merely perverse. Readers who hold it can see the label as a deliberate analytical instrument, chosen to force attention from status to organisation.

There is a further non-claim worth registering, because the book is often praised in terms that would have embarrassed its author. James does not argue that the enslaved were a proletariat because their suffering was the greatest in the world, as though class position were awarded by a tribunal of comparative misery. He was contemptuous of that register of politics, in which oppression is catalogued and ranked and the ranking substitutes for analysis. Nor does he argue that race explains the revolution, or that some African essence survived the Middle Passage and expressed itself in 1791. The appendix he added to the later edition states his ordering with famous economy: "the race question is subsidiary to the class question in politics", and the burden of that appendix is that slavery in San Domingo was an economic institution whose racial features, however poisonous, were its costume rather than its cause. What formed the revolutionaries, for James, was not ancestry but work: the plantation made them, as it made the sugar, and the revolution was the moment the product turned on the process. That is a colder and harder claim than the sentimental alternatives, and it is the claim this profile calls the proletarianisation argument, because naming it precisely is the only way to argue with it honestly.

## The first James: Trinidad, London, and the making of a historian

Cyril Lionel Robert James was born in 1901 in Tunapuna, Trinidad, then a British sugar colony, and he grew up in a household that embodied the colonial order he would spend his life analysing. His father was a schoolmaster, his family literate, respectable, and Anglican in a society stratified by colour and crowned by a white plantocracy and a colonial administration sent out from London. The scholarship boy of the island's best school, Queen's Royal College in Port of Spain, he absorbed the English literary canon with an completeness that his later critics sometimes found disconcerting, and he carried two formations out of that colonial education which never left him: a mastery of the European classics, from Thucydides to Shakespeare, and an intimate knowledge of cricket, the game through which the colony's hierarchies were both enacted and, on the field, occasionally suspended. Both formations became instruments. The classics gave him the narrative architecture of The Black Jacobins, whose debts to Greek tragedy and to the French revolutionary historiography are worn openly. Cricket gave him his method of reading a society through its organised play, and eventually the subject of the book many regard as his masterpiece of a different kind.

The young James taught at his old school, wrote fiction, and moved in the circle of the island's literary magazine The Beacon, which in the early nineteen thirties was the centre of an emerging West Indian literature. His short stories and his novel Minty Alley, written in these years and published in London in 1936, rendered the life of the Port of Spain yard, the barrack ranges where the urban poor lived, with a fidelity that made it a founding text of Caribbean fiction. But the writing that pointed toward the historian was political. His biography of Captain Arthur Cipriani, the Trinidad labour leader and mayor of Port of Spain, published in 1932 as The Life of Captain Cipriani, coupled an account of British misgovernment in the islands with a demand that ran beyond its subject: The Case for West-Indian Self Government, issued by the Hogarth Press in 1933, argued that the Caribbean colonies should govern themselves. The pamphlet matters to this profile because its author was not yet a Marxist of any description. He was a colonial liberal nationalist with a historian's instincts, convinced that the islands' subordination was a political fact rather than a natural one, and already practised in the argumentative move that defines his mature work: taking an institution that presented itself as eternal and showing, from its own record, that it had been made and could be unmade.

### What did James write before the Haitian history?

Fiction about Port of Spain yard life, a biography of the labour leader Cipriani that indicted British administration in the islands, and a pamphlet demanding West Indian self-government. The history was his fourth kind of book, and the earlier writing supplied its narrative craft and its politics.

He sailed for England in 1932, carrying the Cipriani manuscript and an introduction to the cricketer Learie Constantine, in whose Lancashire mill town of Nelson he lodged while working as a cricket correspondent for the Manchester Guardian. The England he entered was the England of the slump, of hunger marches and a Labour movement in disarray, and London in the middle thirties was also the improbable capital of anti-colonial politics: the city where exiles from every empire met, published, and organised. James moved through both worlds. He joined the Trotskyist movement, entering the small Marxist Group that worked inside the Labour Party, and he threw himself into the pan-African agitation that gathered around George Padmore, the Trinidadian organiser who had broken with the Communist International and was building an independent network of African and Caribbean activists. With Padmore, Peter Abrahams, and Ras Makonnen, James helped found the International African Service Bureau in 1937, the organisational seedbed of much postwar African nationalism. His A History of Negro Revolt, published in 1938, surveyed slave revolts, Garveyism, and African resistance as a single tradition of insurgency. The political education was double: from Trotskyism, the analysis of the Soviet Union as a degenerated workers' state and the discipline of factional argument; from the pan-African circle, the insistence that the colonies were not an appendix to world politics but one of its engines. The Black Jacobins is what happened when those two educations were turned, together, on a single archive.

### How did colonial Trinidad shape the young James?

It gave him a stratified society in miniature, in which colour, property, and imperial authority sorted every person he knew, and it gave him the counter-experience of the cricket field and the classroom, where merit briefly rearranged the sorting. He spent his career generalising that miniature.

The point deserves one more degree of specificity, because it explains the unusual angle of his Marxism. A European socialist of his generation typically encountered class first and empire second, as an extension or an embarrassment. James encountered them fused. In Trinidad the planter was white, the merchant was often of mixed or South Asian descent, the labourer was African or Indian, the official was English, and the whole arrangement was underwritten by a metropole that also supplied the school texts assuring the island's children of their place in a benevolent imperial story. To analyse such a society at all was already to analyse race and class as a single fabric, and to ask, as James would ask of San Domingo, not whether the fabric was racial or economic but how its economic organisation distributed its racial meanings. When European Marxists later found his treatment of race in the Haitian history surprisingly supple, they were registering the advantage of a formation in which the two questions had never been separable. When he in turn found European Marxists obtuse about the colonies, he was registering the corresponding disadvantage of theirs. The dispute over his position, treated later in this profile, cannot be understood without that formative asymmetry: he came to Marxism from a place where its categories had to work on slavery's direct descendants or fail at once.

His historical study of the Communist International, World Revolution, published in 1937, completed the preparation. It was a Trotskyist history, written to explain how the Russian revolution had issued in Stalinism, and it taught its author the craft of narrating organisations: how a party's internal regime expresses its relation to the class it claims to lead, how bureaucracies consolidate, how defeats are rationalised into doctrines. Readers of The Black Jacobins who wonder at its political sophistication, its attention to the French revolutionary assemblies, to the interplay of Parisian factions and colonial events, to Toussaint's impossible position between the French Republic and the insurgent masses, are reading the fruit of that apprenticeship. The Haitian history is a book about a slave revolution written by a man who had just spent years anatomising a workers' revolution and its betrayal, and the parallels are deliberate: in both cases James asks what the makers of a revolution understood, what their leaders understood, and what happened in the gap between the two understandings. The gap, in his work, is where tragedy lives.

## The Haitian history: the revolution as the enslaved made it

San Domingo on the eve of revolution was the richest colony on earth, and its wealth was a function of its cruelty. The French western third of the island of Hispaniola produced roughly half the world's sugar and a comparable share of its coffee on plantations worked by some half a million enslaved Africans, a population that vastly outnumbered the white colonists and the free people of colour combined, and that was consumed at a rate requiring constant replenishment from the slave trade because the regime of work, disease, and punishment killed faster than it reproduced. James opens his history inside this economy, and the cold-start chapters are among the most disciplined in the book: an account of the colony's class structure, grands blancs and petits blancs, free coloureds pressing against the colour bar that excluded them from status while they accumulated property, and beneath all the enslaved, differentiated by origin and occupation yet bound in a common subjection. The pre-revolutionary chapters also make the first move of the reciprocity argument. The French Revolution, proclaiming the rights of man in Paris, immediately posed in the colonies a question its makers had not intended: whether those rights ran in San Domingo, and if so, for whom. Colonial assemblies, the lobbying of the planter interest in Paris, the agitation of the Société des Amis des Noirs, the revolts of free coloureds demanding citizenship, each of these transmitted the metropolitan crisis into the colony and returned it amplified.

The rising of August 1791, planned in the famous ceremony at Bois Caïman and executed across the North Plain, destroyed in a few weeks the plantation system of the richest agricultural region in the Atlantic world. Here the proletarianisation argument does its narrative work. James's enslaved do not rise as a crowd and subside; they organise. They form armies with officers drawn from the plantations' own hierarchy of drivers and skilled men, they negotiate, they learn the arts of war against Spanish, British, and French forces in succession, and they produce in Toussaint Louverture a leader of genuine strategic genius. When the French Republic, desperate to hold the colony against Britain and Spain, abolished slavery in 1793 and 1794, James treats the abolition as the revolution ratifying on paper what the enslaved had accomplished in fact. Toussaint, rallying to the Republic, drove out the Spanish and the British and by 1801 had made himself governor-general of a colony that was formally French and actually an autonomous Black state, its plantations restored to production under a militarised labour regime that Toussaint imposed to keep the export economy, and with it the revolution's material base, alive.

### How did the two revolutions shape each other?

Each forced the other's hand. The French Revolution's rights language and its colonial crisis armed the enslaved and divided their masters; the colonial revolution in turn compelled the French abolition decree, ruined Napoleon's American design, and pushed France to sell Louisiana.

The reciprocity is the book's second great argument, and it is conducted with a historian's patience for mechanism. Paris did not graciously extend liberty to San Domingo; the enslaved took liberty, and a hard-pressed Republic, fighting for the colony against rival empires, recognised the taking because it needed the takers as soldiers. Conversely, the revolution in the colony was no spontaneous combustion sealed off from Europe: its leaders read the metropolitan decrees, exploited the successive crises of monarchy, republic, and directory, timed their moves to European wars, and understood, as Toussaint understood pre-eminently, that San Domingo's fate would be settled in the space between French need and French principle. When Napoleon resolved to restore slavery and sent his brother-in-law Leclerc with the largest expedition ever to cross the Atlantic to that date, the war of extermination that followed, atrocities committed by French forces and reprisals by the insurgents, the death of Toussaint in a French fortress in 1803, and the final victory of his successors at Vertières, closed the exchange. France's American empire ended in the colony that had been its jewel; the Louisiana territory was sold to the United States the same year, an admission that the New World design had collapsed with San Domingo. James's claim, that the Haitian and French revolutions belong inside one another's histories rather than in separate national volumes, has since become close to the consensus of the scholarship, and it is worth remembering that in 1938 it was a colonial subject's insistence against the grain of both French and British historiography.

### Where does Toussaint's tragedy lie in the account?

In a failure of explanation. Having restored the plantations to save the revolution's economy, Toussaint would not tell the labourers plainly why their freedom required their return to the estates, and the silence cost him the confidence of the people who had made him.

James's treatment of Toussaint is the book's tragic centre, and it is built on a distinction that runs through all his later political writing: between what a leader sees and what a leader says. Toussaint saw clearly. He knew the new state would be strangled without exports, that the French would return, that the labourers' freedom had to be defended by an economy that only the plantations could sustain in the short term. His policy of binding the formerly enslaved to the estates as paid cultivators, enforced by military discipline, was in his own terms a tragic necessity. But he would not explain it. He issued proclamations in the language of French republican virtue, he shot his own nephew Moïse, the commander who had sided with the labourers against the regime, and he governed the black population as an enlightened administrator governs subjects rather than as a revolutionary speaks to the citizens who made the revolution. When the French expedition arrived, the labourers' suspicion that the old order was returning under new management paralysed the defence until Toussaint's arrest forced the issue and the war became total. James's verdict is that the masses were right in their instinct and Toussaint was right in his economics, and that the catastrophe flowed from the refusal to unite the two by candour. He generalises the lesson quietly, and his readers in the revolutionary movements of the nineteen forties were not slow to apply it nearer home.

The closing movement of the history follows Jean-Jacques Dessalines, who understood what Toussaint had not: that the war had become a war of peoples, and that independence, declared in 1804 after the French defeat, was the only settlement the labourers would defend. James does not flinch from the violence of the founding, on any side, and he records the massacres that accompanied the French campaign and the retaliatory killing of remaining French colonists after independence as facts of the record, to be stated without relish and without euphemism. His interest is analytical to the end: the new state's turn to a militarised landed order, the failure of the land question, the isolation imposed by slaveholding powers that refused recognition, all are traced as consequences of choices made inside a revolution that had achieved the impossible and then had to live in a world organised against it. The appendix added to the 1963 edition, From Toussaint L'Ouverture to Fidel Castro, drew the line forward to the Cuban revolution and to the Caribbean of James's own time, arguing that the region's history formed a single revolutionary tradition. The appendix is also where the ordering of race and class receives its most quoted statement, and where the reader can test this profile's claim that the proletarianisation argument, and not a politics of racial affirmation, is the book's analytical spine.

### How does James test the claim against the plantation record?

He points to the estates themselves: workforces in the hundreds, gang labour under drivers, mills that could not pause, a working day set by the process rather than by season or custom. Where the record shows small, loosely organised holdings, his claim does not reach.

That qualification, which the book's own evidence imposes, is the honest boundary of the argument, and James is more careful about it than some of his popularisers. The sugar sector of the North Plain is the argument's home ground: there the great estates stood, there the rising began, and there the armies of the revolution were first assembled from the plantation hierarchy. The coffee estates of the mountains, smaller and worked by fewer hands, and the urban enslaved of the ports, occupied different positions in his analysis, and the revolution's geography reflects the difference. A reader testing the proletarianisation argument should therefore test it where James staked it, on the plains estates, and should resist two opposite errors: dismissing the argument because it does not cover every enslaved person in the colony, and extending it, as a compliment, to populations whose labour was not organised in the way the argument requires. Precision about the claim's domain is part of the claim.

## The four Jameses

A life that runs from a Trinidad schoolroom to the Manchester Guardian's cricket reports, from a Trotskyist faction in New York to the editorship of a governing party's newspaper in Port of Spain, resists summary as a single career. The table below, the findable artifact of this profile, separates the four Jameses so that their writings, arguments, and alliances can be compared without being confused. The phases overlap in the man, who carried the historian into the faction fight and the cricketer into the political essay, but they do not argue the same theses, and much confusion in the secondary literature comes from quoting one phase against another.

### Why divide one life into four phases?

Because the arguments change with the situations, and a blended portrait quotes each phase against the others unfairly. The organiser of the American years held positions the London journalist had not reached, and the returned editor judged parties the young nationalist had served.

The table also performs a defensive function for the reader. James attracts two kinds of misreading that flourish on blended portraits. The first is the antiquarian misreading, in which the cricket writer and the literary man are detached from the revolutionary and exhibited as a charming colonial polymath, safe for commemoration. The second is the sectarian misreading, in which the faction fighter of the nineteen forties is detached from the historian and the whole output is graded by the standards of a tendency dispute. Holding the four Jameses apart blocks both. The cricket book, as this profile will show, is a political work about colonial society; the faction documents are applications of a historical method; the history is an organiser's book about how masses learn. The phases discipline one another, and the reader who keeps them distinct can see the continuities, which are real, without dissolving the differences, which are also real.

## The American James: Johnson-Forest and the state capitalism thesis

James arrived in the United States in 1938 on a lecture tour for the Socialist Workers Party, the American Trotskyist organisation, and stayed for fifteen years. The visit that was meant to last months became the central political episode of his life. He travelled to Mexico in 1939 to consult Trotsky, then in exile there, on the question of Black Americans' organisation, arguing for the legitimacy of an independent Black movement and for a sympathetic attitude toward Black nationalism that much of the movement's leadership resisted. The consultation mattered to both men, and the record of their discussions, on self-determination and on the independent organisation of the oppressed, shows James already pressing the theme that would define his American work: that the self-movement of oppressed people is not an auxiliary to the class struggle but one of its primary forms. Within a few years that conviction, generalised, would carry him out of orthodox Trotskyism altogether.

When the Socialist Workers Party split in 1940, James went with the minority that formed the Workers Party, and there, with the Russian-born theorist Raya Dunayevskaya and the Chinese-American philosopher Grace Lee, he built the tendency that took its name from their party pseudonyms: Johnson for James, Forest for Dunayevskaya. The Johnson-Forest Tendency began as a faction arguing two connected theses. The first concerned the Soviet Union. Against both the Stalinist account of a socialist state and Trotsky's own account of a degenerated workers' state resting on nationalised property, Johnson-Forest argued that the Soviet order was state capitalist: that the bureaucracy functioned as a collective capitalist, that the plan operated as capital's drive to accumulate by other means, and that the exploitation of the Russian worker differed from exploitation in Detroit in its political form rather than in its economic substance. The full analysis of the Soviet economy belongs to the treatment of Trotskyism and Stalinism elsewhere in this series, specifically [trotskyism vs stalinism](/2014/11/15/trotskyism-vs-stalinism/); here what matters is the thesis's origin inside James's reading of the modern workplace, because the second thesis is the one that bears his signature most clearly.

### What did the Johnson-Forest Tendency argue?

That the Soviet Union was state capitalist rather than socialist or a degenerated workers' state, and that the decisive political fact of the age was the self-activity of ordinary workers, which socialist organisation should record, clarify, and serve rather than direct from above.

The self-activity thesis was drawn from observation as much as from theory. The tendency studied the American working class at close quarters: the wildcat strikes that ran against union leadership as well as against employers, the shop-floor organisations that no party had built, the Black movement's autonomous energies, the revolts of women and young people against the disciplines of factory and household. Its newspaper and its internal documents treated these movements as the raw material of a new society announcing itself, and it developed a distinctive practice to match, publishing workers' own accounts of their working lives and holding that the task of Marxist theory was to give those accounts conceptual form. Dunayevskaya's philosophical work on Hegel, undertaken inside the tendency, grounded the practice in the dialectic: the self-movement of the oppressed was not a slogan but, on their reading, the content of Marx's method itself. James's Notes on Dialectics, written in 1948, is the tendency's most Jamesian document, an attempt to read world politics through the logic of organisations rising, consolidating, and turning into their opposites.

### Why did self-activity displace the party at the centre?

Because the tendency's American experience kept showing organisation arising where no party had planted it, in wildcat strikes and autonomous movements, while the parties that claimed to lead stood aside or suppressed. A politics honest about that record had to reverse the direction of instruction.

The organisational itinerary followed from the theory. The tendency returned to the Socialist Workers Party in 1947, judging the Workers Party too small a vessel, and left again in 1951, when the disagreement over the party's role had become unbridgeable, to form an independent organisation around the newspaper Correspondence. The movement it left behind, orthodox Trotskyism, receives its own account in this series under [fourth international legacy](/2014/12/01/fourth-international-legacy/), and the reader who wants the institutional story should go there; this profile's concern is what the itinerary did to James's thought. It completed the trajectory that The Black Jacobins had begun. The historian who had argued that the enslaved of San Domingo made their revolution themselves, and that their leader's fatal flaw was governing them without explanation, became the theorist for whom the autonomous action of the oppressed was the axiom of politics. The Toussaint lesson had been generalised, and it was now aimed at James's own camp: a revolutionary party that would not trust the masses with the truth of their situation would reproduce, in miniature, the tragedy of the governor-general. Whether this position can sustain an organisation is a fair question, and the tendency's later fragmentation, Dunayevskaya departing in the nineteen fifties to found her own current, is part of the record. But the position's genealogy is unambiguous, and it is why the American James cannot be treated as an interruption of the historian's career. He is the historian, drawing the political conclusion of 1938 in the conditions of 1948.

The American period ended in the machinery of the Cold War state. James, who had entered on a visitor's visa and remained, was arrested in 1952 under the McCarran Act, held on Ellis Island, and deported in 1953. In detention he wrote Mariners, Renegades and Castaways, a study of Herman Melville's Moby-Dick that read the Pequod as a floating factory and its crew as the modern world's dispossessed, and he sent copies, in a gesture entirely characteristic of his confidence in argument, to every member of the United States Congress. The Melville book is sometimes treated as a curiosity. It is better read as the American James's farewell application of his method: a canonical text of the very nation expelling him, reread from the forecastle, from the standpoint of the multinational crew whose labour process the novel records. Expelled from the United States, refused at first the ordinary securities of residence in Britain, he entered the last long phase of his life, in which the Caribbean called him home.

## The return: Trinidad, Williams, and the limits of nationalist parties

James came back to Trinidad in 1958, invited by Eric Williams, the historian who had become the island's chief minister and leader of the People's National Movement, the party carrying Trinidad toward independence. The reunion was freighted with an older relationship. Williams had been James's pupil in Port of Spain, and the younger historian's Capitalism and Slavery, published in 1944, had argued in systematic form two theses that James had been advancing in lectures and in print: that the profits of the slave trade and the slave plantations had supplied a decisive part of the capital that financed British industrialisation, and that British abolition, when it came, followed the declining profitability and strategic weight of the old West Indian sugar interest rather than the triumph of humanitarian sentiment. Together those theses form the Williams thesis, and its connection to James is examined in the questions below; here the point is biographical. The master had returned to serve the student's government, as editor of the party newspaper The Nation and for a period as party secretary, and the experiment ended as James's organisational experiments tended to end: in a break over the distance between a leadership and the people it claimed to embody. James opposed the settlement Williams reached over the American naval base at Chaguaramas, resisted the centralisation of the party, and by the early nineteen sixties was outside the movement, publishing Party Politics in the West Indies in 1962, the audit of a nationalist party by the standard of the self-activity doctrine he had formulated in America. He left Trinidad again, spent years in Britain and in travel, lectured in the United States during the Black Power years to audiences who received him as a patriarch of the Black radical tradition, and died in London in 1989.

### How did the Williams connection run in both directions?

Williams sat in James's classroom and built his account of slavery and British capitalism on ground James had opened; James then returned to edit the newspaper of Williams's party and broke with him over the party's distance from the people it governed.

The scholarly dispute over the debt deserves its paragraph, since the questions in this field are usually asked badly. That Williams learned from James is not seriously contested: the teacher-pupil relation is documented, the younger man's historical education in Trinidad was formed in James's ambience, and the affinity between the argument of The Black Jacobins, that slavery was an economic institution to be explained by its profits rather than its prejudices, and the argument of Capitalism and Slavery is plain on the page. What is contested is the thesis itself, in its two parts. The accumulation claim, that slave-derived profits financed industrialisation, has been fought over by economic historians for generations, with estimates and counter-estimates of the flows involved; this profile states no figure, because the figures are precisely what is in dispute and belong to the specialist literature. The decline claim, that the West Indies had ceased to matter economically before abolition, met its classic refutation in Seymour Drescher's Econocide, which argued that the British slave system was at its most profitable when it was abolished and that abolition was therefore an act of economic self-harm imposed by popular politics. A reader who wants the tradition within which both James and Williams worked, and the dispute their work opened, will find its fullest statement in this series at [black marxism racial capitalism](/2017/03/15/black-marxism-racial-capitalism/), where the relation between slavery and the origins of capitalism is treated as a question in its own right rather than as a biographical footnote.

The Caribbean after independence supplied James with his last large subject: the fate of anti-colonial movements in office. The movements he had helped to inspire, in the Caribbean and in Africa, built states, and the states built bureaucracies, and the pattern he had traced in the Russian case and in Toussaint's San Domingo repeated itself in the idiom of national liberation. His authority among the younger generation of Black radicals in the nineteen sixties and seventies rested partly on this consistency. He had not adjusted his doctrine to excuse governments that spoke his language. The anti-colonial movements in practice, their victories, their one-party settlements, and their economic ordeals, are treated at length in this series under [anti colonial marxism case studies](/2017/05/01/anti-colonial-marxism-case-studies/); the profile's narrower point is that James's late politics are unintelligible unless the reader sees that he had only one political standard and applied it without a colonial exception. The masses' self-activity was the test for a Trinidad ministry as it had been for a French governor-general and for an American Trotskyist faction. It is a demanding standard, arguably an impossible one for any government to meet continuously, and James's late work does not resolve the difficulty. But it is the standard that makes the four Jameses one thinker rather than four careers.

## Beyond a Boundary: why a Marxist wrote about cricket

Beyond a Boundary, published in 1963, is the book by which many readers who will never open The Black Jacobins know its author, and it is persistently misdescribed as a sporting memoir with political decorations. It is the reverse: a study of colonial society that uses cricket as its evidence, because cricket was the institution in which the colony's values were most completely codified and most dramatically contested. James grew up in a house from whose window he could watch the Tunapuna cricket ground; he played the game well, as a fast-medium bowler, and he played it inside a club system minutely stratified by colour and class, in which a Black man's choice of club was a choice of social world. The memoir traces his formation through that system, through the great Trinidadian players, and through the career of the Barbadian batsman George Headley and the Trinidadian all-rounder Learie Constantine, whose success in English league cricket made him a hero of the coming nationalism. Interleaved are essays on the game's history, on W. G. Grace as a figure of the Victorian social order, and on cricket as an art form with its own aesthetics of style, in which James, who wrote literary criticism all his life, was entirely serious.

### Why does a cricket book belong in a political profile?

Because the book argues that the game was a school of the colonial order and, at the same time, a stage on which the colonised displayed the mastery that denied the order's premises. Reading a society through its organised play is James's method, not his hobby.

The famous formulation stands at the head of the book's inquiry: "What do they know of cricket who only cricket know?" The question, adapted from Kipling's address to those who know only England, announces the method. Cricket in the West Indies could not be understood from inside the boundary rope, because the game carried the empire's code of fair play, hierarchy, and self-command into the colony, where it was taken up by the colonised and turned, in the mastery of Headley and Constantine and in the crowds' partisan intelligence, into a demonstration of equality that the code's authors had not intended to license. James's climactic case is the campaign of 1960, in which he used his editorship of The Nation to press for Frank Worrell to captain the West Indies, the first Black man appointed permanently to the role, because the captaincy had become the visible point at which the colonial order's racial ceiling contradicted the game's own meritocratic professions. The campaign succeeded, and James treats the appointment as a political event of the first order, which in the West Indies of 1960 it was. A reader who grasps why he thought so has grasped the argument of Beyond a Boundary: that in a colonial society, art and sport are not decorations on the political struggle but among the fields on which it is fought, because they are where a people rehearses its estimate of itself.

The cricket book also supplies the profile's best evidence for the continuity of James's concerns across the four phases. The child watching from the window, measuring the players and learning that the estate of a man could be read in his stroke play, became the historian who read a revolution off a labour process and the theorist who insisted that the meaning of a society is deposited in its ordinary practices rather than in its proclamations. There is even a formal continuity. Beyond a Boundary is built like The Black Jacobins: a personal frame, a historical excavation, a gallery of careers read as social documents, and a closing argument that the material belongs to the future. To ask why a Marxist wrote about cricket is therefore to ask the question the book itself answers. He wrote about cricket for the same reason he wrote about sugar. Both were organised activities in which a colonial people, working and playing under rules it had not made, revealed capacities that the rules' makers had declared impossible, and the revelation was the beginning of politics.

## The dispute over his position: inside Marxism, or beyond it

Every major figure in this series attracts a placement dispute, but James's is unusual because it concerns not which school of Marxism he belongs to but whether he belongs to Marxism at all. The reading that places him outside the tradition was given its canonical form by Cedric J. Robinson in Black Marxism: The Making of the Black Radical Tradition, published in 1983. Robinson's book is a large and serious work of scholarship, and its account of James must be stated as its author intended it, not in the flattened form in which it circulates. Robinson argued that the Black radical tradition is a tradition in its own right: a body of resistance, thought, and cultural memory carried by Africans and their descendants, with roots in African societies and in the experience of enslavement, which predates Marxism, developed independently of European radicalism, and cannot be treated as a local application of a theory made elsewhere. On his reading, Marxism itself, for all its power, remained entangled in the civilisation that produced it, its categories formed within European history and its revolutionary subject defined by European industry. James enters the argument as its most important modern exhibit: the thinker who began inside Marxism and whose greatest work, on Robinson's reading, broke its frame, recovering in The Black Jacobins an African and Black historical subject whose coherence came from the Black radical tradition rather than from the labour theory of value. The Haitian enslaved, for Robinson, act in James's pages not because a labour process classified them but because a people carried itself through catastrophe with its own resources of organisation and meaning, resources Marxism could register only by ceasing to be itself.

### What does the outside-Marxism reading claim?

In Robinson's 1983 study, that James's achievement belongs to a Black radical tradition older than and independent of Marxism, that his history recovered a Black historical subject on that tradition's terms, and that his work is best read as Marxism surpassed from within the Black experience.

Stated at that strength, the reading has genuine evidence to command, and an honest profile must list it. James's formation was in fact double, in the pan-African circle as much as in the Trotskyist faction, and his imagination was fed by Garvey's movement, by the literature of the Harlem Renaissance, and by the oral culture of the Caribbean in ways that no European Marxist's was. The Black Jacobins does in fact centre African people as historical subjects with a density of attention that European Marxism of the nineteen thirties nowhere matched, and it does treat their culture, their religions, and their memories as politically material. James did break, serially, with every Marxist organisation he joined, and his mature doctrine of self-activity sits awkwardly with the movement's inherited theory of the party. Robinson's reading, finally, performed an undeniable service: it forced the question of whether Marxism's categories are European in a way that limits them, and it built around James a scholarly field that has kept his work in print and in argument. The parallel career of Frantz Fanon, the subject of [frantz fanon and marxism](/2017/02/15/frantz-fanon-and-marxism/) in this series, attracted a structurally similar reading, and the resemblance is instructive: in both cases a colonial thinker's engagement with Marxism is redescribed, by later scholarship, as a passage beyond it, and in both cases the redescription tells us as much about the scholarship's moment as about the thinker's.

Against the reading stands the subject's own testimony, which steelmanning requires us to cite rather than to paraphrase into convenience. James described himself as a Marxist throughout his life, in print and in interview, to the end. The appendix he wrote for the 1963 edition of The Black Jacobins frames the Cuban revolution, and by implication his own history, inside the Marxist account of class and revolution, and states, in the ordering this profile has already noted, that questions of race rank below questions of class where politics is concerned. His theoretical writings of the American years, Notes on Dialectics and State Capitalism and World Revolution, are works of Marxist theory by any generic test: they argue about value, accumulation, the state, and the dialectic, with Marx's texts as their court of appeal. His political practice, the building of tendencies, the factional documents, the journalism, was the practice of a man who expected Marxism to be transformed by its encounter with the Black movement, not abandoned to it. He acknowledged the transformation gladly. In his judgment the movement of Black people, and the independent activity of oppressed populations generally, corrected a Marxism that had grown bureaucratic and Eurocentric in its organisational forms; the correction was Marxism's own method applied to Marxism's own institutions. Where Robinson saw a tradition surpassing a theory, James saw a theory being recalled to itself by the people it was for. Both descriptions can be quoted against the texts. The texts support each at different points, which is why the dispute has lasted forty years, and why this profile presents it as a dispute rather than resolving it into a verdict the evidence will not carry.

### What is the strongest objection to the proletarianisation argument?

That the classification cheats. A proletarian, in Marx's construction, is defined by selling labour power, and the enslaved sell nothing, being themselves sold. A category stretched to cover both, the objection runs, has stopped classifying and started complimenting.

The objection must be given its full weight before it is answered, because it is the point at which the whole argument can be lost. It is not a pedant's objection. The distinction between the worker who owns labour power and sells it by the week, and the enslaved person who is owned outright, is not a legal nicety within Marx's system; it is the hinge of the system, separating the specifically capitalist relation from every earlier form of exploitation and grounding the analysis of wages, of the working day, of the reserve army, and of the peculiar freedom, to sell or to starve, that defines the modern worker's unfreedom. A Marxism that dissolves that hinge, the objection continues, loses its ability to distinguish modes of production at exactly the point where distinction matters most, the point of transition between them. And the objection has a political edge as well as a theoretical one. If the enslaved were already a proletariat, then the abolition of slavery changed less than abolitionists and enslaved people themselves believed it changed; the category, applied as a compliment to the most oppressed, quietly demotes the specificity of their oppression and of the freedom they won. James, who wrote about the Middle Passage with undiminished horror, would have rejected that consequence with contempt. The objection therefore cannot be met by pointing to his intentions. It has to be met on the classification itself.

The answer begins by identifying precisely what James claims and what he does not. He does not claim that the enslaved stood in the wage relation, or that San Domingo's plantations were capitalist enterprises in the full sense, or that the distinction between slavery and wage labour is unreal. His own history depends on that distinction: the revolution he narrates is, among other things, the destruction of the slave relation and its replacement by new and bitterly contested forms of labour. What he claims is that the property which makes a proletariat politically formidable, the capacity for sustained collective action generated by cooperative labour on a large scale, was produced in San Domingo by the labour process in advance of the wage form, and produced there more completely than anywhere in the Europe of the same decade. On his account the classification is functional and comparative, not juridical: the enslaved stood nearer to a modern proletariat than any workforce in the Europe of that decade, in the one respect that his explanatory problem required, their formation by an integrated production process. The answer concedes what honesty requires. The category is being extended, and James nowhere fully resolves the tension between the extension and the wage-based definition he elsewhere accepts; readers can watch the tension work unresolved through the book's pages, and the strongest scholarship on James has treated it as a live problem rather than a settled one. But an extended category is not an empty one. The extension is disciplined by evidence about how the plantations actually worked, it generates the book's testable claims about where the revolution began and who made it, and it distinguishes James's position from the mere honorific use of proletariat for any suffering multitude, a use he despised. The objection stands as the correct statement of the cost of the argument. The argument survives as the correct statement of what the cost purchases.

## Writing The Black Jacobins: sources, method, and afterlife

The book was written fast, in the conditions of a political militant rather than a professor, and its method bears the marks of both. James worked in London, in the reading room of the British Museum and in the flats of comrades, from the printed record: the French revolutionary debates, the memoirs of participants, the nineteenth-century French historians of the colony, the correspondence and proclamations of the principals. He had no access to the colonial archives in Paris and no prospect of visiting Haiti. What he had instead was a question, and the question organised the sources more rigorously than archival completeness could have done. He wanted to know how a slave revolution had been possible, and every document was interrogated for what it revealed about the capacities of the enslaved and the calculations of their leaders. Documents that recorded the enslaved only as numbers, as property, or as a problem of police were read against the grain, the method that later generations of historians of slavery would formalise under other names. A planter's complaint that his people had grown insolent becomes, under that reading, evidence of organisation; a French officer's lament that the Blacks would no longer work becomes evidence that labour discipline, the plantation's own creation, had been withdrawn by the people who had been subjected to it.

### How did James write a history without an archive?

From printed sources read against the grain. Planter complaints, officers' reports, and assembly debates were made to testify about the enslaved they were written to control, and a single guiding question, how the revolution was possible, disciplined what he took from them.

The narrative form deserves the same scrutiny as the evidence, because the form is part of the argument. James modelled his history, consciously, on the classical and revolutionary historians he had absorbed as a schoolboy: the great set-piece chapters, the portraits of leaders rendered as character studies, the tragic architecture in which a revolution rises on the capacities of masses and founders on the failures of its leadership. Toussaint is built as a tragic protagonist in the full sense, a man whose virtues and whose ruin have the same source, and the French revolutionaries, from the Jacobins to Napoleon, are drawn as a parallel cast whose decisions in Paris carry consequences in the Caribbean that they neither foresee nor control. Critics have sometimes treated this literary architecture as a decoration laid over the analysis. It is better understood as the analysis in another register. A history that claims a class was formed by its labour process must show that class in motion, learning, dividing, choosing, and the narrative of San Domingo, in which the enslaved move from conspiracy to insurrection to statecraft within a decade, is the demonstration. The book's famous readability is therefore not an accident of style. It is the method made visible, and it explains why the work colonised the imagination of readers who had never heard of its author's politics.

The afterlife of the book is a study in the politics of reputation. Published in October 1938, it was respectfully reviewed and then overtaken by the approach of war; its author left for America, its subject fell outside every national historiography that might have kept it in print, and for two decades it survived in the keeping of the revolutionary left and of Caribbean readers who passed copies hand to hand. The American edition of 1963, issued with the new appendix on Castro, changed its fortunes. It arrived in the middle of the civil rights movement, into a country arguing about Black history and Black agency, and it was received as what it was: a demonstration, from the hardest case available, that enslaved people had been historical protagonists of the first rank. From that edition forward the book's readership widened steadily, into universities, into the syllabi of the new Black studies, into the hands of historians of the French Revolution who could no longer write the abolition of 1794 as a Parisian gift. By the time of its author's death it had become what publishers call a modern classic, a status that would have interested James chiefly as evidence for his doctrine that the judgement of ordinary readers, given time, corrects the judgements of institutions.

### Why was the book first neglected and then revived?

War buried the first edition, and its subject belonged to no national tradition that maintained a canon. The civil rights era revived it, because a movement arguing about Black agency found in it the strongest historical proof that the argument could have.

Later scholarship has refined James at many points, and the refinements should be recorded as plainly as the achievement. Historians with the archives he lacked have corrected his chronology in places, complicated his picture of the insurgent bands, recovered the African-born majority's religious and military cultures in ways that press against his emphasis on the plantation as the revolution's school, and questioned the degree to which his Toussaint is a figure of the documents rather than of the author's design. The Africanist correction is the most significant for this profile's argument, because it offers a rival account of the revolutionaries' capacities: that the enslaved brought organisational resources from Africa, Kongolese military experience among them, which the plantation did not create. James's text is not defenceless against that correction, since he registered African origins and the maroon communities in his narrative, but the emphasis is his critics' to press, and it marks the live frontier of the proletarianisation argument. What has not been displaced is the book's founding insistence. The Haitian revolution is now written, by scholars of every school, as an event made by the enslaved themselves and as an event inside the history of the French Revolution rather than at its margin. In 1938 both propositions were a colonial subject's provocations. Their migration into the consensus is the measure of the book.

## The Mexico consultation and the argument over Black organisation

The journey to Mexico in 1939 is the episode in which the young James's distinctiveness inside the revolutionary movement first becomes documentary. Trotsky, exiled at Coyoacán, was receiving the American party's leadership for discussions on the questions that divided them, and the position of Black Americans was on the agenda because James had forced it there. The American movement's inherited position treated Black Americans as workers whose emancipation would arrive as a by-product of the general class struggle, and it regarded separate Black organisation with suspicion, as a diversion of energy into a racial channel. James argued the contrary with a confidence his hosts found startling in a colonial visitor. The independent movement of Black people, he held, had its own legitimacy and its own revolutionary potential; it was not a distraction from the struggle but one of the struggle's principal forms in America; and a Marxist party that could not recognise the fact would understand neither the country's history nor its future. He supported, further, the right of Black Americans to determine their own aims up to and including territorial self-determination, should they ever demand it, a position whose logic placed the oppressed group's own judgement above the party's programme.

Trotsky, to the surprise of some of his followers, argued substantially on James's side of the room. The old exile had his own reasons: he had drawn from the Russian experience the lesson that a revolutionary party ignores the autonomous movements of the oppressed at its mortal peril, and he had watched the Stalinised International sneer at colonial revolt with consequences he regarded as catastrophic. The discussions produced agreement that the party should support and help build independent Black organisation rather than merely recruit from it, and they left James with a credential he valued for the rest of his life. But the episode's significance for this profile is not the credential. It is the pattern. At thirty-eight, in the presence of the most authoritative Marxist alive, James's contribution was to insist that the oppressed are the authors of their own politics and that theory's business is to learn from their movement. Every later position of his, the state capitalism thesis, the break with the vanguard orthodoxy, the doctrine of self-activity, the judgement on Toussaint, is that insistence at a higher level of generality. Readers who grasp the Mexico discussions hold the key to the whole shelf.

### What did James argue in Mexico about Black organisation?

That an independent Black movement was legitimate and revolutionary in its own right, not an auxiliary to be recruited and dissolved into the class struggle, and that Marxists should support its self-chosen aims, including self-determination if its members ever demanded it.

His wartime and postwar writings for the American movement developed the argument into a full analysis of the United States. In essays and internal documents, most fully in his 1948 statement on the revolutionary answer to the Negro problem, he traced the Black population's position from slavery through the betrayed promises of Reconstruction to the Great Migration that had made Black workers a strategic fraction of American industry. The analysis is worth characterising precisely, because it is the American counterpart of the proletarianisation argument and suffers the same misreading. James did not hold that Black Americans were revolutionary because they were the most oppressed population in the country, though he said their oppression was the republic's founding crime and its standing reproach. He held that their history, the passage from plantation labour through the betrayal of emancipation to concentration in the basic industries, had given their movement an independence and a continuity that the official labour movement lacked, so that the initiative in American politics could be expected, again and again, to come from them. The civil rights movement's emergence a decade later, from churches and campuses and bus boycotts rather than from any party's instruction, struck him not as a surprise but as a confirmation; and his late American lectures, delivered to the generation of Black Power, restated the analysis to audiences who recognised their own movement in it. The continuity between the Haiti book and the American documents is once again the labour process and its political pedagogy: people are formed for action by the organisation of their common life, and the analyst's task is to find where that formation is happening, not to issue it instructions.

The Mexico pattern also clarifies the dispute over his position treated in this profile. When Robinson's reading presents James as a thinker whom Marxism could not contain, it has the Mexico record to draw on: here is a Marxist pressing, against his movement's instincts, claims that look like the charter of an autonomous Black politics. When James's own testimony is set against the reading, it has the same record: here is a Marxist whose heterodoxy was argued in Marxism's own forums, defended from its founder's method as he understood it, and accepted in part by its greatest living exponent. The two readings are not symmetrically careless. They are two disciplined accounts of one genuinely double career, which is why the profile declines to close the question and why students should be warned against any summary, from either camp, that presents the matter as simple.

## Every cook can govern: the self-activity doctrine at full stretch

If one short text carries the whole of James's mature politics, it is the essay he published in the tendency's paper Correspondence in 1956 under the title Every Cook Can Govern, taking his text from Lenin's remark that under socialism every cook would learn to govern. The essay is a study of the Athenian democracy, and its argument is characteristic in method: reach for the hardest case. Athens, James argued, was not a polite forerunner of modern parliamentarism but a society in which the ordinary citizen, the craftsman and the small farmer, actually governed: sitting in the assembly that decided war and peace, serving by lot in the courts and on the boards that administered the city, holding generals and officials to account before audiences of thousands. The political capacity that modern societies reserve to a professional caste was there exercised as a routine by men whose education was the workshop and the marketplace. James's point was not antiquarian. It was that the Athenian arrangement exposed the premise on which modern politics, including the politics of the workers' movement, quietly rests: the premise that the mass of people are fit to labour and to vote but not to rule. Athens refuted the premise in practice for two centuries, and its decay, when it came, came from the slave economy beneath it and the empire it fed, not from any incapacity of its cooks.

### What does the cook essay claim about ordinary people's capacity?

That it is a fact demonstrated by the Athenian democracy, where craftsmen and farmers governed directly for generations, and that the belief in popular incapacity is an ideology of the castes, including party castes, whose position depends on being believed.

The essay's destination is the revolutionary movement itself. If every cook can govern, then the organisational form that treats the working class as material to be led, instructed, and represented by a stratum of professional revolutionaries is not a necessity imposed by the masses' limitations. It is a choice with a class meaning, the choice that reproduces inside the movement the division between rulers and ruled which the movement exists to abolish. James pressed the argument home against his own tradition with the memory of Toussaint behind him: the governor-general, too, had believed that the people who made the revolution could not be trusted with its reasons. The essay was written by a man in his fifties, deported from one country and marginal in another, whose own organisation numbered in the dozens, and it has the serenity of a position arrived at by elimination. He had tested the party form from inside, in three countries and four organisations, and he had concluded that its theory of leadership contradicted the single insight on which his whole work rested. The conclusion cost him any prospect of leading a movement of consequence, and he paid it without visible regret, holding that a truth about the capacities of ordinary people was worth more than an apparatus.

The doctrine has a corollary in his treatment of culture that the reader of Beyond a Boundary will recognise. If ordinary people can govern, they can also judge, and James's criticism consistently treats the judgements of mass audiences, the cricket crowd's estimate of a batsman, the cinema audience's grasp of a star, the reading public's sense of a novel, as data of the first importance rather than as symptoms to be corrected by the enlightened. His unfinished study of American civilisation, drafted in 1950 and published only after his death, extended the method to Hollywood, to the gangster film and the comic strip, finding in popular culture the record of a population's real desires and real frustrations, legible to any analyst willing to take the audience seriously. The book's incompleteness is a loss, but its premise is the cook essay's premise transferred to aesthetics: the tastes of the multitude are not a problem for politics. They are politics, in one of its earliest forms, and the Marxist who cannot read them has disabled himself from reading his own century.

## James and Africa: Padmore, Ghana, and the second apprenticeship

The pan-African thread of James's career runs longer than any other in his life, from the London of the nineteen thirties to the Accra of independence, and it completes the profile's account of his formation. George Padmore, his fellow Trinidadian, was the thread's other end. Padmore had run the Communist International's work among Black workers from Hamburg and Moscow, had broken with the International when its colonial priorities were sacrificed to Soviet diplomacy, and had rebuilt in London a network of African students, seamen, and exiles that became the effective foreign office of the coming African nationalism. James, who wrote the biography of Cipriani and pamphlets under Padmore's eye and argued politics in his rooms, absorbed from that circle a map of the world in which Accra, Lagos, and Nairobi were not the periphery of anything. The International African Service Bureau, their joint creation of 1937, agitated over the Italian invasion of Ethiopia, the event that radicalised a generation of Africans as the colour line's global hypocrisy stood exposed, and it published the journalism from which the postwar liberation movements drew their first cadres.

### What did the Padmore connection give James, and what did he give Ghana?

Padmore gave him the pan-African network and its world map, in which the liberation of Africa was a central event of the century. James gave the Ghanaian movement his pen, his prestige, and, in his book on Nkrumah, an audit that praised the revolution and indicted its bureaucracy together.

Kwame Nkrumah, who had been Padmore's protégé and became his successor as the network's political instrument, carried the line to its first victory in the Gold Coast, and James followed the Ghanaian experiment with the attention of a stakeholder. His Nkrumah and the Ghana Revolution, drafted in the late nineteen fifties and published in 1977, is the characteristic late work: a study of a liberation movement's rise written from inside its tradition and judged by the single standard. The mass party that broke the colonial order, the Convention People's Party, receives its due as one of the century's great organisations of the oppressed; the bureaucracy that congealed around independence receives the analysis James had practised on every apparatus since the Russian one, and the book's argument that the Ghanaian masses remained ahead of their government reads, after the coups that followed, as prophecy of a melancholy kind. The African writings matter to the placement dispute as well. Robinson's Black radical tradition draws heavily on this James, the mentor of nationalists, the theorist of a Black world in motion. James's own account of the same years is organised, as always, around class formation and the state: Ghana interested him as a society in revolution, its market women and its cocoa farmers and its striking railwaymen, and his categories travelled to Accra unchanged. The reader who holds both facts together, the pan-African substance and the Marxist grammar, holds the dispute in its proper form, which is not a contradiction to be dissolved but a tension that powered the work.

There is a smaller, human document of the African connection that deserves its sentence. In 1957, the year of Ghana's independence, James, who could not easily travel and was not rich, wrote for the celebrations and watched from London the arrival of the first African state of the new era, achieved by men he had known as penniless students in the rooms off Gray's Inn Road. His letters of those years, to correspondents across three continents, have the tone of a man settling accounts with his century: the colonial world whose permanence his schoolmasters had taught was dissolving in his lifetime, and dissolving, as he had always insisted it would, by the action of the colonised themselves. It is the tone as well of The Black Jacobins, written twenty years earlier, when such an outcome was a heresy. The Haitian history had argued that the enslaved of 1791 were contemporaries of the future rather than relics of the past. The Africa of 1957 was the argument's second demonstration, and James did not need to say so. His readers said it for him.

## The labour regime: Toussaint's hardest decision and the Moïse affair

The moral centre of The Black Jacobins is not a battle but an administrative decision, and any profile of its author must slow down over it, because James's treatment of the decision displays his method at its most exacting. By 1800 Toussaint governed a colony whose emancipation he had secured and whose economy lay in ruins. War had burned estates, dispersed labour, and collapsed the export trade on which food imports, arms, and credit depended. The formerly enslaved, understandably, were voting with their feet for the alternative the revolution seemed to promise: small plots, provision grounds, family labour, a peasant independence. Toussaint, looking at the French fleets that would surely come and at the empty treasury, concluded that the plantations had to be restarted, under new ownership in many cases, with the labourers bound to the estates as salaried cultivators under military discipline. He imposed the regime with characteristic combination of proclamation and force, leasing confiscated estates to his generals, fixing the cultivators' share of the produce, and punishing flight from the estates as desertion.

James's judgement of this regime is the book's most quoted complexity, and it repays exact statement. He does not condemn the policy's economics, which he treats as forced by the situation: a San Domingo of smallholders, in his analysis, would have been reconquered piecemeal, its people re-enslaved or massacred, its freedom a memory. Neither does he celebrate the policy's politics, which recreated under republican forms the essential coercion of the old order and taught the labourers to see their liberator as a new master. His criticism is aimed at the junction between the two: Toussaint never explained. He did not go to the people whose arms had made him and lay the terrible arithmetic before them, that their freedom's survival required a period of plantation labour under discipline, nor did he invite their assent to it as citizens of the republic they had created. He ruled them, benevolently and incorruptibly, as a French administrator rules a population he respects but does not consult. In James's vocabulary the failure is precise: it is the substitution of administration for politics, and it is the same failure he would diagnose, in later decades, in parties and governments that claimed to act for classes they would not argue with.

### Why did Toussaint bind the labourers to the estates?

Because the revolution's survival, as he calculated it, required export revenue, arms, and credit, and only the plantations could supply them quickly. The alternative of general smallholding promised freedom in the short term and reconquest, in his judgement, soon after.

The Moïse affair brought the contradiction to its crisis. Moïse, Toussaint's adopted nephew and the commander of the North, was the officer closest to the labourers of the plains estates, the revolution's original heartland. He opposed the labour regime, protected cultivators who resisted it, and spoke, in reports that reached Toussaint, the language of the labourers' grievance: that the whites and the new Black officer caste were rebuilding the old world on the old estates. In 1801, when a rising against the regime broke out in the North and was put down with severity, Toussaint had Moïse arrested, tried by a military commission on charges his contemporaries and most historians have found thin, and shot. James's pages on the execution are among the most controlled in the book, and their verdict is devastating in its quietness: the leader had destroyed the one lieutenant who spoke for the people in the idiom of the people, and in doing so had cut the channel through which the truth about his own policy could reach him. When the French expedition landed months later, the cultivators of the North hesitated, some of them reasoning that one French-speaking master was much like another, and the hesitation nearly lost the revolution before Dessalines and Christophe turned it into a war of national extermination and survival. The causal chain is the historian's master argument in miniature. A class formed by the plantation had made a revolution; its leader, formed by France, could not speak to it; the silence cost him everything, and the revolution was saved by being taken out of his hands.

For the profile's purposes, the episode also guards against a lazy reading of the proletarianisation argument. James's enslaved are not a collective hero with a single will. They are a population divided by origin, by skill, by proximity to the great estates, by the competing pulls of plantation discipline and peasant autonomy, and the revolution's politics is precisely the working out of those divisions under pressure. The labourers who resisted Toussaint's regime were not betraying the class the historian had constructed for them; they were pursuing, inside the revolution, a different and entirely intelligible answer to the question of what freedom should mean in the working week. A Marxism worth the name, on James's showing, does not override such answers with its schema. It reads them as the schema's verification and its rebuke at once, since a theory of the masses that cannot accommodate the masses' own choices has mistaken its subject. That sentence, generalised, is the whole of his later politics, and it was learned here, on the plains of the North, in the gap between a governor's proclamation and a cultivator's refusal.

## Two collaborators: Dunayevskaya, Lee, and the making of a tendency

The Johnson-Forest Tendency is conventionally described as James's faction, and the description, while natural given his fame, falsifies the intellectual division of labour that made the tendency distinctive. Raya Dunayevskaya, born in Ukraine and raised in the United States, had served as a secretary to Trotsky in Mexico, had broken with him over the Russian question before James knew her, and brought to the partnership a philosophical equipment he did not possess: a reading of Hegel's Logic, undertaken in the original and pursued through the nineteen forties, from which she extracted the tendency's grounding claim that Marx's method was a philosophy of liberation whose centre was the self-movement of the oppressed. Her translation and interpretation of Hegel's absolute idea chapters, and her insistence that the dialectic was not a decorative method but the content of Marxist theory, gave the group its intellectual spine and much of its later direction. Grace Lee, later Grace Lee Boggs, a Bryn Mawr-trained philosopher of Chinese-American background, brought a second equipment: a rigorous training in the Hegelian and Marxist texts, a gift for theoretical exposition that the tendency's documents everywhere display, and, in the decades after, a trajectory of her own into the Black movement and community politics of Detroit, where she became a figure of national standing in her own right.

### What did the collaborators contribute that was not James's?

Dunayevskaya supplied the philosophical foundation, the Hegel scholarship that grounded the doctrine of self-activity in Marx's method itself. Lee supplied theoretical exposition of the first rank and, across a long later career, carried the doctrine into American community politics.

Why does the division of labour matter to a profile of James? Because the tendency's characteristic positions were genuinely joint products, and attributing them to a single charismatic founder repeats, inside scholarship, the theory of leadership that the tendency existed to refute. The state capitalism thesis had antecedents in Dunayevskaya's earlier work and in the European debates; the Hegel renaissance inside American Marxism was her project before it was the group's; the newspaper Correspondence, with its unprecedented practice of building issues around firsthand reports by workers on their own jobs and shifts, was edited collectively and written substantially by its working-class correspondents, whose bylines the paper carried as a matter of doctrine. James was the tendency's most famous name, its most powerful prose stylist, and the author of its two most durable books of the period, the Melville study and the notes on dialectics. He was not its solitary brain, and he said so, in terms, whenever the subject arose. The point connects to the profile's larger argument about his distinctiveness. Thinkers are usually tested by the quality of the schools they found, and the schools usually fail the test, dwindling into the founder's echo. Johnson-Forest's members conspicuously did not echo: Dunayevskaya founded her own organisation and pursued her philosophy for forty years; Lee built an independent political life in Detroit; the younger members carried the ideas into the labour and Black movements of the nineteen sixties, where a Detroit formation descended from the tendency, and figures formed in its orbit, played their part in the era of the League of Revolutionary Black Workers. A doctrine of self-activity that produced echoes would have refuted itself. What it produced was practitioners.

The tendency's documentary legacy rewards the reader who approaches it as literature as well as doctrine. Its internal bulletins argue about the Russian economy, the nature of the party, and the meaning of Hegel with a ferocity that assumes the stakes are world-historical, and in the small hours of a defeated century there is something moving in the assumption. James's own contributions to that archive, above all the 1948 Notes on Dialectics, are written in a style unlike anything else in Marxist prose: exploratory, associative, testing each category against the day's headlines and the century's defeats, more like a mind thinking in public than a doctrine being delivered. Scholars of his prose have noticed that the historian of San Domingo and the dialectician of 1948 share a signature rhythm, the long sentence that holds a whole historical process in suspension before releasing its verdict. The signature is the man: for James, theory was never the contemplation of a finished world but the effort to catch a world in the act of changing its form, whether the scene was a sugar estate in 1791 or a Detroit auto plant in 1947. The tendency gave that effort an institutional home for a decade, and when the home broke up, as his homes tended to, the effort continued unchanged into the Caribbean and African work of his last decades.

## The literary James: Melville, Shakespeare, and the audience

A profile confined to James's politics would miss the instrument with which he did his thinking, which was literary criticism practised at the level of the professional. He read Shakespeare as other Marxists read the trade statistics, as a primary document of his society, and his essays on the plays, on Othello's outsider and Lear's divided kingdom, treat the drama as the record of a world being remade by new property and new men. His criticism of the novel, from the Port of Spain stories of his youth to the essays of his maturity, holds consistently to a single standard: a work is judged by the truth of its rendering of how people live and work together, and by nothing else, neither by its author's opinions nor by its conformity to any programme. The standard explains the apparent eccentricity of his canon. The great writer of the nineteenth century, for the James of the American years, was not a European at all but Herman Melville, and the great book was the one the American academy of his day had only lately rescued from oblivion.

Mariners, Renegades and Castaways, written during his detention on Ellis Island in 1952 and 1953, reads Moby-Dick as the epic of industrial labour. The Pequod is a factory ship, its crew a multinational proletariat assembled from every port of the Atlantic world, its captain a figure of the totalitarian manager, consumed by a monomania that converts the enterprise's whole apparatus to the service of his private wound. Ishmael, the narrator, survives because he alone attends to the crew as people: the mariners, renegades, and castaways of the title, whose skills, solidarities, and jokes constitute, for James, the human material of a possible future set against Ahab's organised madness. That the book was written by a deportee, in a detention barracks, about the national epic of the state expelling him, and was then mailed at his expense to every member of Congress, tells the reader most of what is needed about its author's temperament. He did not petition the republic. He instructed it in its own literature and left the instruction on the record.

### Why did the Melville study matter to his politics?

It applied his method to the culture of the country that was expelling him: the factory read into the whaling ship, the crew read as the world's dispossessed, and the national classic returned to its owners as a document about labour and tyranny rather than adventure.

The literary work is not a recreation alongside the politics, and the profile insists on the point because the placement dispute turns partly on it. The writers who place James in a Black radical tradition point, with justice, to the imaginative range that overflows any European Marxist's formation: the cricket, the Shakespeare, the Greek democracy, the attention to the spirituals and to the yard life of Port of Spain. The writers who keep him inside Marxism reply, also with justice, that the range was put to a single analytical use. In every field his question is the same: where are ordinary people exercising capacities that their rulers deny they have, and what forms of organisation honour or betray those capacities? The question works on a sugar estate, a cricket eleven, an Athenian assembly, and a Detroit assembly line with equal facility because it was never, at bottom, a question about any of those places. It is a question about the relation between human capacity and the institutions that organise it, and it is the closest thing James has to a first principle. A reader who extracts that principle can follow him anywhere, into archives and subjects where this profile cannot go, and can also see the genuine limit that the strongest objection presses: a principle so general risks classifying by sympathy rather than by structure, which is exactly the charge the critics of the proletarianisation argument bring against its founding case. The objection and the principle stand or fall together, and it is a merit of James's work that it forces the choice so cleanly.

## The later James: Black Power, the Caribbean lectures, and the long authority

The final three decades of James's life present a spectacle without parallel in the Marxist tradition: a revolutionary of the nineteen thirties, organisationally homeless, becoming in old age an authority courted by movements that had never heard of his factions. The Black Power generation of the nineteen sixties discovered him as a living ancestor. Stokely Carmichael, who took the name Kwame Ture, acknowledged the debt of his politics to James's teaching and example; audiences in London, where James lectured to the Black community in 1967, and across the United States and the Caribbean, received the elderly Trinidadian as a man who had been where they were going forty years before them. The lectures of those years, on Black Power, on the Caribbean, on the Russian revolution's lessons, have the quality his listeners remembered: the schoolmaster's clarity, the classical range worn lightly, and the unembarrassed insistence that the people in the room, and not any leadership including his own, were the subject of the history being discussed. He was, in those rooms, the doctrine of self-activity with a walking stick, and the effect on young activists, by their own later testimony, was formative.

His Caribbean lectures of the same period, delivered in Trinidad and published as The Making of the Caribbean People, performed the regional version of the same office. They taught a new generation of West Indians to read their own history as a single civilisation's making: the plantation as the region's founding institution, the enslaved and indentured as its founders, the calypso and the cricket field and the carnival as its cultural archive, and the scattering of its people across London, New York, and Toronto as an extension of the civilisation rather than its loss. Walter Rodney, the Guyanese historian whose work carried the method forward in African and Caribbean history, worked in the ambience James had created and acknowledged the example. The line of descent from Tunapuna through the British Museum reading room to the lecture halls of the nineteen sixties is not fanciful. It is documented in syllabi, in memoirs, and in the reading lists of movements, and it constitutes a form of influence that the history of Marxism, organised as it is around parties and their congresses, has no ready category for. James influenced the century the way a teacher influences it, by forming readers.

### How did the Black Power generation receive him?

As a living ancestor: a Marxist of the revolutionary decades who had centred Black agency before their movement existed, who lectured to them without condescension, and whose doctrine assigned the authorship of their struggle to themselves rather than to any party or elder.

The last years were spent mostly in London, in Brixton, in a flat that functioned as an open seminar. Visitors from every continent climbed the stairs: students writing theses on the Haitian history, activists from the Caribbean and Africa, scholars of cricket, film-makers, the curious and the converted. He received them with the courtesy of a man who regarded an audience of one as a political opportunity, and he talked, by every account, as he wrote, in long periodic sentences that held a whole argument in suspension and landed it entire. He died in Brixton in May 1989, in his eighty-ninth year, having outlived the empire into which he was born, the movement he joined, most of his comrades, and the state whose collapse his tendency had predicted on a schedule it got wrong. The obituaries in the British press, respectful and slightly puzzled, commemorated the cricket writer and the historian of Haiti. The fuller reckoning, the one undertaken by the scholarship of the decades since, has restored the unity of the career: a single method, applied to sugar, cricket, Athens, Detroit, and Accra, and a single wager, that the capacities of ordinary people are the motor of history and the only reliable foundation of politics. The wager's standing is the reader's to assess, with the texts open. This profile's work is done when the texts are open, the argument named, the objection stated, and the dispute over the man's placement left exactly where the evidence leaves it, which is open too.

## The silencing of the revolution: James among the historians

One debt of the modern scholarship deserves separate statement, because it converts the profile's argument into a testable claim about influence. The Haitian revolution, as the anthropologist Michel-Rolph Trouillot argued in his study of how the past is produced and muted, was silenced by Western historiography for two centuries: not denied outright, which would have required acknowledging it, but rendered unthinkable within the categories the West used for the age of revolutions, since a successful revolution by enslaved Africans contradicted the premises on which those categories rested. Trouillot's account, written from Haiti, anatomised the formulas by which the event was trivialised, blamed on outside agitators, dissolved into French politics, or omitted from the revolutionary canon altogether. Readers of this profile will recognise the situation those formulas maintained. It is the situation The Black Jacobins was written against, half a century earlier, with a fraction of the evidence and none of the institutional support. James did not have Trouillot's vocabulary of silencing, but he had the phenomenon exactly in view: his opening chapters remark, with controlled anger, on the mountains of print the French Revolution had attracted and the shelf of neglect assigned to the greater drama in San Domingo, and his book's whole construction, its scale, its classical apparatus, its refusal of apology, is a refusal to let the event be miniaturised.

### What did later historiography owe to the book?

A subject. The proposition that the Haitian revolution was made by the enslaved and belongs inside the history of the French Revolution passed, largely through James, from provocation to the working assumption of the scholarship that succeeded him.

The point can be sharpened into a criterion for the reader who wants to test James's standing independently. Take any serious modern history of the age of Atlantic revolutions and examine the place of Haiti in it: the space allotted, the agency assigned, the connections drawn to France, to Spain's American empire, to the Louisiana Purchase, to the fears of slaveholders from Virginia to Brazil. Then take a comparable history published before 1938 and perform the same examination. The difference between the two shelves is, in considerable part, the effect of one book by a Trinidadian journalist with no archive, no post, and no permission. Influence of that order, exercised across the boundary between activist literature and professional scholarship, is rare enough in any field; in the historiography of slavery it has few parallels, and the parallel that suggests itself, the work of Eric Williams with its own Jamesian lineage, doubles the point rather than dividing it. A tradition of Caribbean historical writing, formed in the colonial schoolroom and the reading room of an imperial museum, re-educated the metropole in the history of its own empires. The pupil of Queen's Royal College would have appreciated the formulation, and the historian of Toussaint would have insisted on its mechanism: it happened not because the metropole grew generous but because the work was done, the evidence arranged, and the argument made impossible, in the end, to answer by silence.

## The French Revolution in James's reading: the parallel history

The Black Jacobins is two histories at once, and the French half of the book is not background scenery. James had steeped himself in the historiography of 1789, above all in the great French tradition of Jaurès and Mathiez, and his San Domingo narrative is interleaved with a reading of the French Revolution that carries its own thesis: that the metropolitan revolution, like the colonial one, was made and unmade in the gap between its masses and its leaderships. The Parisian sections follow the colonial question through the Constituent Assembly's evasions, the Amis des Noirs' philanthropy and its limits, the planter lobby's purchase of deputies, the insurrection of the Paris sections that forced the abolition decree of 1794 onto a reluctant Convention, and the Directory's and then Napoleon's systematic reversal of the emancipation the Republic had proclaimed. At each stage the colonial events and the metropolitan ones exchange causal force. It was the rising in San Domingo that made abolition a fact the Convention had to ratify or lose the colony; it was the Thermidorian reaction in Paris that licensed the colonial reaction in the Caribbean; it was Napoleon's racial legislation and the Leclerc expedition that converted Toussaint's French republican state into Dessalines's independent empire. Neither revolution, in James's construction, can be periodised, explained, or morally weighed in isolation from the other.

### What does James's French Revolution explain about his method?

That he wrote history as a single interconnected field, in which the colony could act on the metropole as decisively as the metropole on the colony, and in which assemblies, like plantations, were workplaces of politics whose internal organisation determined their outcomes.

The comparison also does quieter work that readers should notice, because it distinguishes James from the national schools on both sides of the Atlantic. French historiography of the revolution, republican and Marxist alike, had treated the colonies as a theatre where metropolitan principles were applied or betrayed, a matter of the revolution's honour rather than of its substance. British historiography had treated San Domingo as a horror story of the French system, useful for congratulating the British on gradualism. American historiography had treated it, when at all, as a cautionary tale about emancipation itself. James's construction dissolves all three frames at once. The colony in his pages is neither an application nor a warning. It is a co-author of the age, whose enslaved population forced the universalist claims of 1789 to disclose whether they were universal, and whose victory established, in the face of every empire, the fact that the racial order of the Atlantic world was contingent and could be broken. That is why the book could be received, in different decades, as an offence by so many different establishments, and why its recovery in the nineteen sixties was experienced by its new readers as the recovery of a suppressed chapter of their own history rather than as an antiquarian curiosity. The title itself performs the argument. To call the enslaved insurgents Jacobins was to claim for them the most honourable and most feared name in the revolutionary vocabulary of Europe, and to claim it on the evidence of their organisation and their achievement rather than as a metaphor of praise.

His treatment of the French leadership class sharpens the point by symmetry. The Jacobins of Paris, in his account, rose on the energy of the sans-culottes and were destroyed when they substituted the committee's administration for the movement's politics, a trajectory he sets, without underlining, beside Toussaint's. Napoleon completes the parallel as the administrator become emperor, the man who resolved every contradiction by decree and restored slavery by the same instrument. James's Bonaparte is recognisably the ancestor of the bureaucrats of his later work: the state capitalist planners of the Russian analysis, the nationalist ministers of the Ghanaian audit, all are versions of the figure who answers a people's movement with an office. The consistency is not a quirk of temperament. It follows from his criterion for judging any leadership, which the Haitian history states in its treatment of Toussaint and never afterwards abandons: a leader is measured by whether the people who follow him understand more of their situation at the end of his leadership than at the beginning, and by whether his organisation leaves them more capable of acting without him. By that criterion most of the century's revolutionary reputations collapse, and the criterion's severity is the price of the doctrine of self-activity taken seriously as a standard rather than recited as a devotion.

## Federation and the West Indian endgame

The last political campaign of James's life concerned the political form of the Caribbean itself, and it ended in the defeat that, in a sense, completed his education. He had argued since the nineteen thirties that the British West Indian colonies, small island economies chained to sugar and to the preferences of distant markets, could achieve real independence only together, as a federation with a common economy and a common state. The pamphlet of 1933 already implies the scale argument; the returned editor of 1958 served a party committed to the West Indies Federation that Britain had inaugurated that year; and when the federation faltered, on the jealousies of the larger islands and the calculations of their leaders, James watched the project to which he had given his return home dissolve within four years. Jamaica's referendum withdrew the federation's largest partner; Trinidad followed; the federation was buried in 1962, and the islands proceeded to separate independences whose economic fragility he had spent thirty years predicting.

### Why did federation matter so much to James?

Because the islands separately were, in his analysis, too small to be sovereign in substance: their economies, their bargaining power, and their cultures required the regional scale, and independence without it would reproduce dependence under national flags and national bureaucracies.

The defeat is worth the profile's attention because James's response to it was characteristic and instructive. He did not conclude that the island peoples had failed some test of political maturity. He concluded that the federation had been designed as an administrative arrangement among governments and colonial offices, negotiated over the heads of the populations it was meant to unite, and had therefore never acquired the popular understanding that alone could have defended it when the politicians' calculations turned. It was the Toussaint failure in modern dress: a correct policy, arguably a necessary one, carried by proclamation and committee rather than by the explained conviction of the people whose policy it was. His writings of the federation's collapse, and his addresses to West Indian audiences in London and the Caribbean in the years after, return obsessively to the missing element, a Caribbean public educated in its own history and therefore capable of judging its leaders' regional arithmetic for itself. The lectures on the making of the Caribbean people belong to that campaign of explanation. They are a defeated politician's remedy applied to his own defeat: if the people had possessed their history, no referendum campaign of slogans could have dissolved their state, and so the history, at least, would be supplied, by him, in person, in the lecture halls, at an age when most men of his formation were writing memoirs.

There is a biographical completeness in the sequence that the four Jameses table cannot show and that this section exists to record. The schoolboy of Queen's Royal College had been formed by an empire that taught him his island was a fragment, valuable as an appendage and helpless as a whole. The historian answered by writing the fragment's region into the centre of the modern world's founding revolution. The organiser answered by building, in America, a politics on the capacities of the people the fragment's rulers had despised. The returned editor answered by trying to give the region a state, and failing, and diagnosing the failure as a failure of explanation, the very sin his Toussaint had committed. A thinker whose categories survive that many translations, from archive to faction to newspaper to lecture hall, without changing their essential content, has earned the seriousness with which this series treats its subjects at their strongest. The remaining question about him is not whether he was systematic. It is whether the system, self-activity all the way down, is true, and that question the profile hands to the reader with the evidence arranged.

## Hegel in Detroit: the Notes on Dialectics and the philosophy of the tendency

The strangest document of the American years, and the one its author regarded as his most important, is the set of notes on Hegel's Logic that James composed in 1948, circulated inside the tendency, and saw published only in 1980. Notes on Dialectics is not a commentary in the academic sense. It is an attempt, written at speed and in the idiom of factional urgency, to re-found Marxist politics on the categories of the Logic: quantity passing into quality, the negation of the negation, the leap. Organisations, in the notes' treatment, are themselves dialectical objects. A revolutionary party arises as the negation of the existing order, consolidates its own forms, and then, at a determinate stage, passes over into its opposite, becoming an obstacle to the movement it was built to serve, at which point the movement produces new forms that the old organisation experiences as chaos and indiscipline. The schema is applied, with examples drawn from the Russian party, the American unions, and the tendency's own small history, to a single practical conclusion: that the socialist's allegiance belongs to the movement of the class, not to any organisational vessel, however glorious its past. The party is a phase. The class is the process.

### What does the Hegel study contribute to his politics?

A logic for his central doctrine: organisations necessarily arise, consolidate, and turn into obstacles, so fidelity to the self-moving class must outrank fidelity to any party, including one's own. The philosophy certifies the politics he had learned from Toussaint.

The notes' afterlife belongs to the pattern of his reputation. For thirty years they existed as a samizdat text, copied and argued over inside the small world of Hegelian Marxism, where Dunayevskaya's parallel and more systematic work developed the tendency's philosophy into her own Marxist-Humanism. When the notes were finally published, the philosophical world that received them was the one created by the nineteen sixties: readers formed by the new social movements found in the 1948 text a Marxist theory that assigned those movements their proper dignity as the self-movement of the oppressed themselves, and found it written not in response to them but in anticipation, out of Hegel and the Haitian revolution, by a colonial intellectual in Detroit. The episode strengthens, in a way worth noticing, the weaker side of the placement dispute. Whatever else James was doing in 1948, he was doing Marxist philosophy of the most orthodox lineage available, grounding his politics in the same text of Hegel from which Marx himself had drawn, and insisting that the grounding was the politics. A thinker can of course be claimed for a tradition he would not have recognised. But when the claim requires us to set aside his philosophical masterwork as a disguise for something else, the claim has begun to cost more than it explains, and the reader is entitled to enter that cost in the ledger beside Robinson's genuine insights.

The Hegel study also illuminates the literary work, by a route the profile should mark. James's criticism, of Shakespeare, of Melville, of cricket, is dialectical in the strict sense his notes define: it reads a form, a play, a game, a batting style, as the deposit of a whole society's organisation, and then reads the society back out of the form, attending always to the moment where quantity of change passes into quality, where the colony's copy of the imperial game becomes, by accumulation of mastery, a different game. That is why his judgements in criticism have the unnerving quality of arriving from very far away. He is never reviewing the season's performance. He is locating the performance in the centuries-long movement of a civilisation, and grading it by what it reveals about the capacities being formed in the people who perform and watch. Whether one finds the method exhilarating or exasperating will depend on one's tolerance for scale. It is not the method of a man who dabbled in politics between books. It is the method of a man for whom the books and the politics were a single inquiry, pursued wherever the evidence of human capacity was richest.

## The newspaper as theory: Correspondence and the workers' own words

The most original practical experiment of the American years was a newspaper. Correspondence, the journal the tendency founded after its final break with the Trotskyist parties in 1951, inverted the editorial doctrine of every socialist paper of its era. Where the orthodox press instructed the working class from the height of the editorial office, Correspondence was built to record it. Its pages carried letters and articles by workers describing, in their own words and under their own names or shop names, the actual organisation of their labour: the speed of the line, the foreman's techniques, the informal resistances, the wildcat strikes that no union had called, the texture of solidarity and resentment on the floor. Around those reports the paper arranged a minimum of commentary, its theory being that the class, speaking about its own working life, was already producing the knowledge that socialist theory existed to clarify, and that an editor's first duty was to listen at a scale no individual could listen alone. A companion volume of the period, The American Worker, assembled a General Motors worker's account of his working life with philosophical commentary, and remains one of the most unusual documents in the literature of labour: a factory narrative treated, by its editors, as a primary text of social theory.

### How did Correspondence put self-activity into practice?

By reversing the direction of instruction in socialist journalism: workers wrote the paper's core reports about their own labour, and the editors' commentary served the reports, treating the class's own account of its working life as the primary theoretical document.

The experiment matters beyond its small circulation, because it operationalises the profile's central theme. The doctrine that the masses make history is easy to affirm in a history book about the safely distant dead of 1791. Correspondence affirmed it about the living, in the present tense, with names and workplaces attached, and accepted the editorial consequences, which included printing workers' judgements that the tendency's own members found uncongenial. The connection to The Black Jacobins runs through the treatment of sources. The historian who had read planter complaints against the grain to recover the enslaved as protagonists was practising, in the archive, the same discipline the newspaper practised on the shop floor: attend to what the record of ordinary labour reveals when the powerful are not allowed to summarise it. It also connects forward, to the method of the later lectures, where West Indian and Black American audiences were addressed as the authorities on their own history and invited to correct the lecturer. James's consistency across these settings is the strongest stylistic evidence in the placement dispute, and it cuts in his favour: the practice of a man who spent fifty years arranging platforms for the oppressed to describe their own condition is the practice of a theorist with a doctrine, not of a romantic celebrating a tradition. The doctrine can be named, as this profile has named it, and argued with, which is what he always asked.

The paper's fate was the tendency's fate: small numbers, large ambitions, fragmentation as the founding generation dispersed into the movements of the nineteen sixties. But the experiment left descendants. The practice of workers' inquiry, of building theory from workers' own accounts of the labour process, passed through the tendency's European contacts into the Italian workerist tradition and, by other channels, into the shop-floor sociology and the oral history movements of the following decades. Scholars tracing those genealogies find the Detroit newspaper at an improbable number of origins. James, who had made his reputation by arguing that the most advanced labour process of 1790 stood in the Caribbean rather than in Europe, would have enjoyed the symmetry of the most advanced editorial practice of 1952 standing in a Detroit mimeograph room rather than in the offices of the great parties. The margins, in his geography of the modern world, kept turning out to be the centre, because the margins were where the new forms of labour, and therefore of politics, appeared first and were described by the people living them before the centre's categories had names for them.

## The insurgent armies: what the revolution's conduct proves for the argument

The narrative core of The Black Jacobins, the chapters on the war, contains the evidence on which the proletarianisation argument ultimately stands or falls, because it is in the war that the capacities attributed to the labour process had to be exercised in public. The record James assembles is remarkable whichever theory explains it. A population the French administration had classified as incapable of anything but supervised toil produced, within two years of the rising, armies that manoeuvred against European regulars; produced a diplomatic service that negotiated with Spain, Britain, and France as a power; produced, in Toussaint, a strategist whom James ranks without embarrassment among the great captains; and sustained, across thirteen years of blockade, invasion, and civil war, a state apparatus that taxed, judged, coined policy, and held its territory. The apparatus collapsed, in the end, only into independence, which it then defended successfully against the expedition of a France at the height of its military power. James's explanatory wager is that such a performance has a material basis, and that the basis is the plantation: the gangs were the revolution's first regiments in embryo, the drivers its non-commissioned officers, the estates' internal communications its intelligence network, and the discipline of coordinated labour, inverted, the discipline of coordinated war.

### What in the war record supports the labour-process explanation?

The speed of the organisation: armies, officers drawn from the plantation hierarchy, logistics, and a functioning state arising within years from a population held in legal incapacity, exactly the collective capacity the integrated estates had daily required and daily trained.

The rival explanations deserve their statement here, because the profile has undertaken to present the live frontier of the argument and not merely its classic form. The Africanist scholarship of the last generation has assembled a powerful alternative account of the same record. The majority of San Domingo's enslaved in 1791 were African-born, and a significant fraction of the men had military experience in the wars of West Central Africa, in the Kongo and Angola regions from which the trade had drawn heavily in the preceding decades. On this account the insurgent armies' competence came not from the plantation's discipline but from Africa's: Kongolese veterans brought tactics, organisation, and a political culture of kingship and Catholic kingship at that, into the revolution, and the maroon communities that had survived in the mountains for generations supplied its first cadres and its model of armed autonomy. James, for his part, did not deny the African composition of the population, and his narrative honours the maroons and the African-born; but his analytical weight falls on the plantation, because his question was not where the soldiers' skills originated but what had made a dispersed collection of the enslaved, African and Creole, into a people capable of acting as one historical force. The two accounts are not wholly exclusive, since an army can carry African tactics in a body assembled by plantation labour, and the most careful recent scholarship tends to distribute the explanation. But they differ in emphasis in a way that matters for Marxism: the Africanist account locates the revolution's resources in a culture the enslaved brought with them, the Jamesian account in a structure the masters built around them, and the difference between those locations is the difference between the two readings of James that this profile has kept in suspension throughout. Robinson's Black radical tradition is, at bottom, the Africanist location generalised into a theory of history. The proletarianisation argument is the structural location at its most exposed. The war record feeds both, which is why the argument of 1938 is still, in the best sense, unsettled.

There is also the question of what the enslaved were fighting for, where James's account contains a subtlety often missed by both camps. The insurgents' demands evolved: the first negotiations sought limited concessions, an end to the worst punishments, days of freedom, within a system the negotiators did not yet propose to abolish; the demand for general liberty emerged from the war itself, from the experience of arms and the discovery of French divisions, and was then ratified, never to be surrendered, by the abolitions of 1793 and 1794. James narrates this evolution without embarrassment, though it complicates any picture of a class marching out of the labour process with its programme already formed. His point is finer. The labour process had formed the capacity; the programme was formed in struggle, by the people themselves, at a speed that astonished every observer; and the historian's task is to hold both truths, the prepared capacity and the improvised consciousness, in a single account. A Marxism that cannot do that, that requires either a class with its consciousness delivered in advance by theory or a spontaneity with no material preparation at all, will misread every revolution it meets. James's Haiti is the demonstration case for the double truth, and its lesson is aimed at his own movement as directly as any page of the American documents.

## The movement's verdicts: how Marxists received him

James's standing inside the Marxist movement during his lifetime was a study in contradiction, and the record of it belongs in a profile of this kind because it explains the bitterness that sometimes edges his late references to the organisations he had served. The Trotskyist movement, which formed him, treated his mature positions as desertion. The state capitalism thesis struck at the movement's founding distinction, between the degenerated workers' state it defended and the capitalism it opposed, and a movement that defined itself by that distinction could not absorb the thesis without ceasing to be itself. His American organisation excommunicated in the style of the tradition, by resolution and by the rewriting of history, and the orthodox accounts of the movement's past long treated Johnson-Forest as a curiosity of gifted eccentrics. The Stalinist tradition, for its part, had no use for a writer whose whole work indicted bureaucracy, and the Communist parties of the colonial world, whose cadres he might have influenced, were directed by their internationals to regard Trotskyists of any colour as beyond the pale. The result was a fifty-year paradox: the most widely read Marxist historian of slavery in the world was a member of no Marxist organisation that the world could name, his books circulating through movements that officially disowned his politics.

### How did the organised movement treat its most famous historian of slavery?

Coldly, and at arm's length. His state capitalism thesis dissolved the Trotskyist movement's founding distinction, his independence broke its discipline, and for five decades his books travelled through movements whose official histories barely mentioned their author.

The later movement's judgement has been kinder and more interesting. The revival of interest in the Johnson-Forest documents, in the Hegel notes, and in the theory of self-activity has proceeded, since the nineteen eighties, inside universities and inside the fragments of the revolutionary left simultaneously, and the two revivals have fed each other in the manner this profile has described for the Haiti scholarship. What the academic revival took from James was a Marxism centred on agency, usable in the history of slavery, of labour, and of culture; what the activist revival took was an ancestry, a proof that resistance to both capitalist management and party bureaucracy had a theoretical pedigree older than the movements of 1968. Both revivals have their ironies, which James, who distrusted commemoration, would have savoured. The university that houses his papers is the institution his generation of colonial intellectuals had to besiege for admission; the parties that rediscover his organisational writings are organised on lines his writings condemn. But the ironies are the ordinary fate of a thinker whose work outlives its occasions, and they settle nothing in the placement dispute, which the profile now leaves, as it promised, in the reader's hands, with both cases stated at the strength their authors gave them and the subject's own testimony entered in the record.

## The colony's orders: grands blancs, petits blancs, and the free coloureds

The pre-revolutionary chapters of The Black Jacobins contain a piece of social analysis that deserves extraction, because it shows the proletarianisation argument seated inside a complete anatomy of the colony rather than floating as a slogan. San Domingo's free society was divided, in James's rendering, into orders whose mutual hatreds prepared the revolution's opening as surely as the enslaved prepared its substance. The grands blancs, the great planters and merchants, despised the colonial administration that taxed them and the metropole that monopolised their trade, and dreamed, some of them, of an independence that would free their commerce while preserving their slaves. The petits blancs, the overseers, artisans, clerks, and failures of the colony, possessed nothing but their whiteness and defended it with a ferocity proportional to its being their sole capital, supplying the revolution's first racial terror from below. Between the whites and the enslaved stood the free people of colour, the affranchis, many of them the children of planters, some of them rich, slaveholders themselves, educated, propertied, and excluded by an elaborating code of legal humiliations from the status their wealth should have commanded. Their campaign for citizenship, opened in Paris by Julien Raimond and in the colony by Vincent Ogé, whose rising of 1790 was broken and whose executioners broke him on the wheel, was the revolution's first colonial act, and James treats it with a sympathy disciplined by analysis: the free coloureds wanted equality with the whites, not the abolition of the system that had enriched them, and their tragedy was that the logic of their own claim, pressed home, dissolved the colour line on which slavery itself depended.

### What do the colony's free orders explain about the revolution's outbreak?

Why it began as a civil war among the free before it became a war of liberation: planter separatism, poor white racial panic, and the free coloureds' citizenship campaign had shattered the colonial order's unity before the enslaved rose and settled the question.

The anatomy matters for the argument because it assigns causal weight with precision. In James's account the free orders' quarrels created the opening: the assemblies, the rival delegations to Paris, the armed camps of whites and coloureds, the collapse of the colonial state's monopoly of violence in 1790 and 1791. But an opening is not a revolution, and the book's originality is to insist that what flowed through the opening came from below, from the population that no order had consulted. The enslaved did not inherit a programme from the free coloureds' campaign or from the Parisian declarations; they entered a crisis made by others and converted it, by the rising of August 1791 and by everything that followed, into a revolution of their own whose aims outran every free faction's imagination. James's formulation of the relation between the two levels, the crisis above and the power below, became a template for the analysis of revolutions well beyond his case, and it is one of the channels through which his book entered the general historiography of the revolutionary age. The template also disciplines the reader's understanding of the proletarianisation claim. The claim never asserts that organisation in the labour process mechanically yields revolution. It asserts that when crisis opens a society, the populations whose daily work has formed them for collective action can seize the opening and remake the society, while populations formed otherwise cannot, whatever their grievances. San Domingo is the proof case because its opening was unusually wide and its plantation population unusually formed, and the seventeen nineties in Europe supply the control case in James's parallel narrative: peoples whose labour had not so formed them, and whose revolutions were therefore made, and confiscated, by their leaderships.

The fate of the free coloureds after independence completes the anatomy with a lesson James presses quietly. The new state's ruling stratum was drawn substantially from their ranks and from the Black officer corps, and the colour gradations of the old order survived inside the republic that had abolished them, structuring Haitian politics for generations. James neither celebrates nor deplores the survival in the manner of a moralist. He records it as the ordinary behaviour of a social order's categories under a revolution that had destroyed the order's foundation but not its sedimented habits, and he leaves the reader to draw the general moral about revolutions and their inheritances. It is the same moral his Russian history had drawn about the party and his Ghanaian study would draw about the nationalist bureaucracy: the forms men build to liberate themselves deposit habits that outlive the liberation, and vigilance about the deposits is the price of the achievement. A profile that presented James as a simple celebrant of revolution would falsify him on every page. He is the tradition's great anatomist of what revolutions cost the people who make them, and the anatomy begins here, in the colony's orders, before a single shot of 1791.

## James and Marx on slavery: the classical texts behind the argument

It strengthens the profile's central exhibit to set James's argument beside what Marx himself wrote about slavery, because the relation between the two is more interesting than either the orthodox defence or the Robinson reading allows. Marx's scattered judgements on slavery are famous and double-edged. He held that slavery in the Americas was an anomaly grafted onto the world market, that, in his phrase in The Poverty of Philosophy, direct slavery was the pivot of bourgeois industry, without which there would be no cotton and no modern industry on its actual scale; he followed the American Civil War as the decisive conflict of his age and identified the working class of England, which resisted intervention on the slaveholders' side, as its heroes; and he treated the abolition of slavery as a precondition of the free labour market whose contradictions his life's work analysed. Nothing in those judgements classifies the enslaved as proletarians. Marx's enslaved are the foundation of the proletariat's world, not members of the class: their labour creates the wealth and the market within which wage labour becomes general, while their own relation to production remains, in his construction, pre-capitalist in form though capitalist in function.

### Where does James stand against Marx's own treatment of slavery?

He accepts the construction's foundation, slavery as the pivot of the modern economy, and extends its class analysis further than Marx took it, arguing that the plantation's labour process had already produced the proletarian formation Marx expected only from the factory.

The extension is therefore internal to the tradition in a precise sense: it applies Marx's own criterion, that classes are made by their place in production, to a case Marx treated only in passing, and it arrives at a classification Marx did not reach because the case's evidence was not before him in James's detail. That is the strongest form of the answer to the placement dispute, and it is the form this profile records without endorsing beyond the evidence. James's defenders can say, truly, that the proletarianisation argument is what historical materialism looks like when it is worked on the Caribbean archive by a man who knew the plantation's descendants at first hand, and that a method is vindicated, not betrayed, when its application corrects its founder's asides. Robinson's readers can reply, also truly, that the correction was possible only because the man who made it stood inside African and Caribbean experience as no European Marxist did, so that the tradition he corrected him from was as much a source of the correction as the method he corrected with. The profile has presented both replies at strength because both are, on the record, sustainable, and because a reader who can hold them together understands the whole field, Marxism and race, better than a reader who has been spared the tension by an editor's verdict. James himself, characteristically, would have recognised the tension and reached for the dialectical consolation his notes supply: that a living theory is known by the corrections its ablest practitioners force upon it, and that the forcing is the tradition continuing, whether the continuers sign the register or not.

One more classical text deserves mention, since it governed the movement James joined and therefore the expectations he disappointed. Lenin's writings on imperialism, which this series treats in the imperialism cluster, assigned the colonies their place in the Marxist scheme as reservoirs of superprofit and as the scene of national liberation movements that the metropolitan proletariat should support as allies. The colonial peoples, in that scheme, act in history as nations, under bourgeois or petty-bourgeois leaderships, in a drama whose proletarian centre remains European. James's Haiti breaks the scheme's geography from inside the movement that held it. In his account the colonial enslaved are not an allied nation in someone else's revolution; they are the most advanced working class of their age in the revolution that founded the modern Atlantic world. The difference is not a detail of emphasis. It relocates the exemplary revolutionary subject of the seventeen nineties from Paris to the North Plain, and it does so with the movement's own tools. Readers who wish to pursue the imperialism framework against which James was silently arguing should consult this series' guide at [marxism and imperialism guide](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/), where the classical positions are laid out in full; the profile's point is confined to noting that James's divergence from them was a divergence of a practitioner working the same seam, not the indifference of an outsider to questions he had outgrown.

## The fiction of the yard: Minty Alley and the stories

The literary career that preceded the history has been mentioned in this profile as preparation, and it deserves a fuller account, because the fiction contains the germ of the method and because its neglect repeats, in miniature, the pattern of James's reputation. The Trinidad stories of the late nineteen twenties and the novel Minty Alley, written when their author was in his twenties and published in London in 1936, are set in the barrack yards of Port of Spain, the long single-storey ranges, divided into rooms let to poor families, that formed the characteristic housing of the colonial urban working class. The novel follows a middle-class clerk who, ruined by a speculation, takes a room in No. 2 Minty Alley and lives among the yard's people: the washerwomen, the porters, the kept women and their keepers, the whole economy of respectability and survival conducted in public around a shared standpipe. James renders the yard without condescension and without romance, as a complete social world with its own codes of honour, credit, gossip, and judgment, and the rendering made the book, on its British publication, the first novel by a Black West Indian writer to reach a metropolitan audience.

### What does the early fiction contribute to the historian?

A training in the social world of the labouring poor, rendered from inside its own codes. The yard of the novel is the plantation's urban descendant, and the eye that learned to read its hierarchies and solidarities later read the estates of San Domingo.

The contribution is more than biographical colour. Consider what the fiction taught its author that the archive could not. The yard's people are judged, in the novel, by the standards of their world, and the clerk who narrates learns, slowly and at cost, that his imported standards of respectability measure nothing that matters in it. That discipline, the refusal to grade a labouring community by the values of the class that exploits it, is the exact discipline the historian of San Domingo would need in order to see the enslaved as political actors rather than as victims awaiting rescue, and it is rarer in the Marxist writing of the nineteen thirties than it should be. The metropolitan left of that decade was full of tender concern for the oppressed and remarkably short of imaginative acquaintance with them. James had the acquaintance before he had the theory, in the yard and on the cricket field and in his father's schoolroom, and the order of acquisition marks all his later work: the theory was always answering to an experience that preceded it, which is why it bends, in his hands, toward the self-activity of real people and away from the abstractions that satisfy committees. Readers meeting the fiction after the history often report a shock of recognition, as of the same voice in a smaller room. The recognition is sound. The voice did not change when the room enlarged to hold a revolution; only the evidence did.

His literary journalism of the London years extends the picture. He reviewed and argued in the small magazines of the left and of the Black world, wrote on the West Indian novel as it emerged, and maintained, against the grain of the movement's philistinism, that the aesthetic judgement was a political faculty and its corruption a political fact. The essays of those years on poetry and the theatre have dated less than the factional documents among which they appeared, and they help explain a feature of the mature James that visitors to the Brixton flat remembered: the conversation moved without transition from the Russian economy to the batting of Vivian Richards to the merits of a production of Lear, not from dilettantism but because, in his scheme, these were all inquiries into the same subject, the capacities of a civilisation as deposited in its practices. A profile of the thinker that omitted the fiction and the criticism would therefore omit the evidence for the breadth on which both sides of the placement dispute rely, and would leave the reader unable to judge whether the breadth was a Marxist's range or a tradition's overflow. The texts are the court. Begin, as the profile's reading advice proposes, with the history; but let the record show that the history's author arrived at it with a novelist's equipment, and that the equipment is visible in every chapter.

## The Castro appendix and the Caribbean revolution renewed

The appendix James added to the 1963 edition of The Black Jacobins, From Toussaint L'Ouverture to Fidel Castro, is a short text with an outsized place in his reputation, and it deserves examination as a document in its own right rather than as the history's tailpiece. Written in the first excitement of the Cuban revolution, it argues that the Caribbean possesses a single revolutionary tradition, running from the Haitian insurgents through the Cuban independence wars to the Sierra Maestra, and that the region's small societies, formed by the plantation and by nothing else so deeply, are therefore not the backward margin of the Americas but one of its political laboratories. The appendix celebrates Castro with the warmth of a man watching his own history's argument vindicated in his lifetime: here again a Caribbean people, dismissed by the great powers as incapable of self-government, had made its revolution itself and was defending it against the empire of the age. And it contains, in its most analytical passage, the ordering of racial and class questions that this profile has already cited, class given the primacy in political analysis while the neglect of race is warned against in the same breath. The two clauses together are the mature James in a single breath, and both sides of the placement dispute quote the sentence, each camp stressing the clause it needs.

### What does the Castro appendix claim for the Caribbean?

That the region has one revolutionary tradition, from the Haitian insurgents to the Cuban Sierra, because the plantation formed all its societies alike; and that its small peoples, so far from lagging the hemisphere, have repeatedly anticipated its future.

The appendix also marks the limit of James's optimism, and the limit is diagnostic. He wrote it before the Cuban state's consolidation had displayed the features his own theory taught him to watch for: the single party, the administered economy, the substitution of the leadership's judgement for the movement's. His later references to Cuba are markedly cooler, and the trajectory, from the appendix's ardour to the old man's reserve, repeats the pattern of Ghana and of Trinidad with a fidelity that is almost methodological. Every revolution he ever supported was audited, in the end, by the one standard, and none was exempted, not even the one that had seemed to enact his own book. There is an integrity in the pattern that his critics, in the movement and out of it, have generally conceded even while disputing everything else about him. He was not a courtier of successful revolutions. He was their most demanding creditor, presenting for payment, at each capital in turn, the same bill: the explanation owed to the people who had made the thing, and the power owed to them with it. The bill was never paid in full anywhere. His work is the record of its presentation, and the record's consistency across sixty years, four continents, and every change of regime is the deepest fact a profile can report about C. L. R. James, deeper than any school to which he might be assigned, since it explains why the schools must fight over a man who belonged, in the end, to his standard and to nothing else.

## The Atlantic consequences: Louisiana, Bolívar, and the long fear

The revolution James chronicled did not end at Haiti's shores, and a section on its consequences belongs in his profile because his reciprocity argument, France and San Domingo remaking each other, generalises across the hemisphere. The first consequence was cartographic. Napoleon's American design required a continental base in Louisiana to supply the sugar islands; with San Domingo lost, the design was a map without an engine, and the territory was sold to the United States in 1803 for reasons the French foreign ministry stated with unusual candour in its private correspondence: the colony that would have consumed Louisiana's produce was gone. The doubling of the American republic, with everything that followed for the expansion of slavery into the Mississippi valley, is therefore among the Haitian revolution's effects, an irony James savoured in print: the enslaved of San Domingo, defeating France, delivered the American slaveholders their empire of cotton land, and thereby prepared the greater slave society whose destruction would require the hemisphere's largest war. History in his telling is full of such transferred consequences, and the habit of tracing them, of refusing to let any national story close its borders, is among the most exportable parts of his method.

### How did Haiti's victory reach into other countries' histories?

By liquidation and by example: it forced the sale of Louisiana, it armed and provisioned Bolívar's expeditions on condition of abolition, and it stood for two generations as the slaveholding world's proof that emancipation could arrive by the enslaved's own arms.

The second consequence was a gift. In 1816 and again in 1817, Alexandre Pétion, president of Haiti's southern republic, received the defeated Simón Bolívar and gave him ships, men, weapons, and money for the liberation of the Spanish mainland, exacting a single promise in return: that slavery would be abolished in the territories Bolívar freed. The promise was kept imperfectly and slowly, as promises extracted at sword-point by the weak from the strong usually are, but the episode fixed Haiti's place in the South American founding as the one state that had converted its own liberation into a foreign policy. The third consequence was a fear, and it governed the century. In every slave society of the Americas, Haiti became the name of the catastrophe the masters organised their lives to prevent: cited in the United States Congress to justify the gag on abolitionist petitions, invoked in Cuba and Brazil to defend the trade's prolongation, held over every emancipation debate as the alternative to gradualism. The fear was itself a historical force, delaying abolitions and shaping the racial ideologies of the hemisphere's ruling classes, and it rested on the fact James's book exists to establish: that the catastrophe, from the masters' side, had been real, complete, and accomplished by the enslaved themselves. A revolution that could frighten three empires and two republics for a hundred years had plainly not been a footnote to anyone's history, whatever the textbooks said, and the gap between the fear's memory and the textbooks' silence is the silencing phenomenon that the later scholarship, following Trouillot, would anatomise. James's history stands exactly in that gap, which is another way of stating its originality.

The recognition question closes the section with the pattern of his whole subject. Haiti was quarantined by the slaveholding and colonial powers: the United States withheld recognition until its own civil war made the withholding untenable in 1862; France, in 1825, sold recognition for an indemnity whose burden structured the Haitian state's finances for generations, a transaction the profile records without figures, since the figures and their present-day accounting belong to a live scholarly and political dispute rather than to a thinker's biography. The quarantine's meaning, for James, was evidentiary. Powers do not isolate what they judge insignificant. The cordon around Haiti was the international system's tribute to the revolution's importance, paid in the currency of fear, and it confirmed from the outside what the plantation record showed from within: that the people of San Domingo had made an event the modern world was organised to prevent and could neither undo nor forget. His book's mission, as he conceived it in 1938, was to make the forgetting as impossible in the library as it had proved in the chancelleries.

## The style of the game: James's cricket criticism as social theory

Beyond a Boundary has been treated in this profile as a political book, and it remains to do justice to its other identity, as the most considered work of sports writing in English of its century, because James's cricket criticism displays his method in its purest form and because the book's aesthetic argument is one his political readers too often skip. His central claim about cricket is that it is an art, comparable to the drama, in which a people's character is expressed and judged through form. The batsman's style, in his analysis, is never merely technique. It is a social document: the Barbadian George Headley's compact mastery read as the dignity of a people making excellence out of restriction; Constantine's brilliance as the exuberance of a Trinidadian temperament refusing the colonial script of deference; the English professional's correctness as the ethic of a class that had codified the game as it had codified an empire. When he writes of W. G. Grace, the Victorian colossus, he writes of him as he writes of Toussaint, as a figure in whom an age concentrated its virtues and its contradictions, and the essay on Grace is, in consequence, one of the finest pieces of historical portraiture in the whole James canon, to be set beside the studies of the revolution's leaders without apology.

### What makes the cricket writing theory rather than appreciation?

Its premise: that a game codifies a society's values in its rules, its roles, and its honours, so that mastery, selection, and captaincy become evidence about the society itself. The boundary rope encloses a laboratory in which the colonial order could be watched working.

The aesthetic claim has a hard edge that his admirers in the sporting press sometimes missed. If style is a social document, then the judgements passed on style are social judgements, and the colonial cricket establishment's rulings on West Indian players, too flashy, too undisciplined, brilliant but not captaincy material, were not errors of taste but the ideology of a ruling order defending its monopoly of command. James's campaign for Worrell's captaincy was fought on precisely that ground: not that a Black captain would symbolise progress, though he would, but that the arguments against him were the colonial order's arguments, dressed in the vocabulary of cricketing prudence, and that defeating them required defeating them as arguments, in the newspapers, on the evidence of the game itself. The method is the historian's method transposed. Documents, in this case scorecards and selection decisions and press judgements, are read against the grain until they testify about the order that produced them, and the reader who has followed the profile this far will recognise the operation, since it is the operation performed on the planter correspondence of San Domingo. There is a unity of practice here that the table of the four Jameses, for all its uses, cannot display, because it runs not between the phases but through them: a way of reading the world, acquired in a schoolroom and a yard in Trinidad, that found its objects wherever organised human activity left records, and found them everywhere it looked.

## The scholarship of James: biographies, papers, and the making of a classic

The posthumous James has become an industry, and a profile written for a series of this kind should close its analytical work by accounting for the industry, since the reader's James will be met through it. The documentary foundation is the body of papers and the editions prepared under the care of his literary executor, the historian Robert A. Hill, whose labours made the unpublished manuscripts, the American Civilization study and the dialectics notes among them, available to scholarship. The biographical foundation was laid by Paul Buhle, whose edited volume on James's life and work appeared in 1986, in the subject's lifetime and with his cooperation, and whose biography, presenting him as the artist as revolutionary, followed in 1988; a second full political biography, by Kent Worcester, appeared in 1996, and the two together, with the memoirs of his collaborators and the growing shelf of studies of single works, allow the career to be followed in a detail that would have astonished the neglected author of 1938. An annotated reader of the essential writings, conferences on three continents, and the slow canonisation of The Black Jacobins in school and university syllabi complete the picture of a reputation still rising at the century's end.

### What has the scholarship settled, and what has it left open?

It has settled the career's outline and the documentary record beyond serious dispute. It has left open, because the evidence genuinely divides, the placement question this profile has staged: whether the Marxism was the substance and the rest the idiom, or the reverse.

The industry's growth carries a risk that James's own doctrine predicts. A thinker of self-activity, monumentalised, becomes an authority to be cited rather than an argument to be continued, and the citation industry has at times preferred the monumental James, the seer of Black agency and prophet of postcolonial studies, to the argumentative James who demanded to be answered. The antidote is in his texts and in the discipline this profile has attempted to practise: name the claim, test it against its hardest evidence, state the objection at the strength its authors gave it, and let the reader leave with a position rather than with a poster. The scholarship at its best does exactly that. The studies of the Haitian history's sources, of the tendency's documents, of the cricket book's place in the literature of sport, have sharpened every question they have touched, including the questions on which their subject was wrong, and a reputation that can sustain that treatment, and grow under it, is the kind that lasts. James asked, in the preface to his first history of a revolution, for readers who would argue. He has them.

## The state capitalism thesis in outline

The disambiguation proper to this series assigns the full analysis of the Soviet Union to the treatment of Trotskyism and Stalinism, and this profile has respected the assignment; but a reader cannot judge the American James, or the movement's quarrel with him, without the thesis's outline, which is therefore given here in brief. Johnson-Forest began from the fact that the Russian worker of the nineteen forties did not control the product of his labour, the pace of his work, or the disposition of the surplus his work created, and that these were controlled, against his resistance, by a managerial and party stratum whose collective position reproduced, in the tendency's analysis, the function of a capitalist class. The plan, on this account, did not abolish the law of value; it administered it. Production was driven by accumulation for accumulation's sake, disciplined by the world market and above all by military competition with the capitalist powers, so that the bureaucracy's drive to extract and invest the surplus differed from a board of directors' drive in its political costume rather than in its economic logic. Hence the thesis's name: capitalism in which the state, rather than competing private owners, is the form capital takes.

### What did state capitalism mean as a claim about the Soviet Union?

That the bureaucracy functioned collectively as capital: appropriating the surplus, driving accumulation under the discipline of world competition, and confronting the Russian worker as an alien power over his own labour, whatever the property forms said.

The thesis's consequences radiated through the tendency's whole politics, which is why the orthodox movement fought it as an existential threat. If Russia was capitalist, then the Trotskyist duty to defend it against imperialism lost its object, and the worldwide alignment of the movement, its parties oriented to the defence of a workers' state that did not exist, had to be rebuilt from the ground. If the bureaucracy was a class, then the bureaucratisation James had watched inside his own small organisations was not a moral failure of leaders but the embryonic form of a social order, and the struggle against it belonged inside every workers' organisation from the first day, not after some future victory. And if the Russian worker's condition was exploitation of the familiar kind, then the Russian working class was a revolutionary subject of the familiar kind too, whose self-activity, in strikes and resistances the tendency collected and publicised, was evidence of the same historical process the tendency tracked in Detroit. The symmetry was the doctrine's elegance and its scandal. It made the whole world legible to a single analysis at the moment when the century was insisting on two camps, and it asked a small group of exiles and autodidacts to hold that legibility against both. That they largely did, and that the analysis's descendants surface wherever later Marxists have theorised bureaucratic collectivism's alternatives or the autonomy of workers' struggle, is the measure of the tendency's weight relative to its numbers. James's own contributions to the thesis were characteristically literary-historical in form: the long documents read the Russian question through the logic of organisations he had first worked out on the French and Haitian revolutions, and the reader who comes to them from The Black Jacobins will find the same mind at work on a colder archive.

## Portraits of the led and the leaders: Dessalines, Christophe, and the ranks

A history's theory of leadership is best read in its minor portraits, and James's gallery of the revolution's secondary figures repays attention as a set of controlled experiments in his criterion. Jean-Jacques Dessalines, Toussaint's lieutenant and successor, is drawn as the leader the moment finally required: a former field slave of ferocious energy and limited education, without Toussaint's French formation or his scruples, who understood the one thing the crisis of 1802 demanded, that the war had become a war for existence between the peoples, and who prosecuted it without flinching and founded the independent state. James's admiration is real and unsentimental. He records the atrocities of the founding, the massacres that answered the French campaign of extermination, as the record requires, without euphemism and without relish, and he judges the founder by the criterion: Dessalines told his people the truth of their situation, that there would be no compromise and no return, and they followed him knowing what they followed. Henri Christophe, the northern king of the later years, receives the tragedy's other variant: the builder of the citadel and the palace, of schools and a court, whose authoritarian kingdom in the north displayed, in James's account, both the creative energy of the revolution's veterans and the speed with which a liberator's state hardens into a new machine of extraction, its labour codes binding the cultivators to the estates in a pattern whose familiarity the reader is left to register unaided.

### What do the secondary portraits test in James's history?

His criterion of leadership itself. Dessalines passes it by candour in extremity, Christophe fails it by rebuilding coercion in splendour, and Toussaint, the greatest of them, fails it at the height of his powers by silence.

Below the portraits runs the book's anonymous chorus, and no account of the history is complete that treats the chorus as scenery. James's rank and file are individuated wherever his sources permit: the coachman and the commandeur, the African-born veterans and the Creole artisans of the towns, the women who carried the revolution's logistics and its intelligence and who appear in the record, in his handling of it, as political actors rather than as camp followers. The famous set pieces, the burning of the North Plain, the defence of Crête-à-Pierrot, are written from within the ranks' experience of them, and the effect across thirteen years of narrative is cumulative: the reader finishes the book having spent it in the company of a people, which is precisely the experience the proletarianisation argument requires as its evidence. A class, in the only sense that ever mattered to James, is a company that has learned it is one. The history's form makes the reader undergo the learning, and that formal achievement, more than any single thesis, is why the book survives its corrections. Scholars may redistribute the credit between plantation discipline and African inheritance for as long as the debate lasts. The company's existence, as a subject of history, is no longer debatable, and it was James who seated it at the table.

## The canon reclaimed: Shakespeare, the Greeks, and the colonial schoolroom

There is a strand of James's work that the political summaries habitually omit and the literary ones habitually sentimentalise: his lifelong contention that the classical canon of Europe belonged, by right of use, to the colonised who had been educated in it. The colonial schoolroom had presented Shakespeare, Milton, and the Greek historians as the property of the rulers, evidence of the civilisation the ruled were invited to admire and forbidden to wield. James's whole practice was a refusal of the invitation's terms. He wielded them. His Thucydides is read as the analyst of a democracy's self-destruction whose categories serve Port of Spain as readily as Athens; his Shakespeare essays treat the plays as the common inheritance of anyone who can read them hard enough, and treat the colonial reader's reading as potentially the best reading, since the colonial reader knows from inside what the plays know about power, outsiders, and the price of a crown. When, in the autobiographical chapters of Beyond a Boundary, he describes the young colonial intellectual's formation, the Puritan conscience of his household, the cricket, the classics, the code of the English public school absorbed at a distance of four thousand miles, the account is neither gratitude nor resentment. It is an inventory of captured weapons.

### Why did the classical formation matter to the Marxist?

It supplied the instruments, narrative architecture, tragic form, and the analysis of democracy, with which the Haitian history and the cook essay were built; and his use of them enacted his claim that the canon was the common property of whoever could work it.

The claim has worn well. The postcolonial literature that followed James made the reclamation of the canon one of its signature operations: the Caliban figure, Prospero's slave taught language and returning it as curse, became in the hands of Caribbean and African writers the emblem of the colonial intellectual's situation, and George Lamming's meditation on Caliban, in his essays on exile, works the same seam James had worked when he made the forecastle of the Pequod the standpoint from which to judge the ship. What distinguishes James within that company is the absence of grievance in the operation. He does not read Shakespeare against the empire in a spirit of repossession, because he never conceded the empire's ownership in the first place. The plays were his, as the game was his, by the only title he recognised anywhere: the title of use, of work performed upon the material until it yielded its truth. It is the proletarianisation argument applied to culture. As the enslaved were made modern by the labour process imposed on them, so the colonial reader was made the canon's heir by the labour of reading imposed on him, and in both cases James's move is identical: take the master's own account of what the process produces, and present the bill.

The Greeks complete the inventory and explain the cook essay's audacity. A Trinidadian schoolmaster's son who grounds his theory of modern politics in the Athenian assembly is making a claim about the geography of political reason that parallels his claim about the geography of the proletariat: that the exemplary instances stand where the official map does not put them, in a small slaveholding city of the ancient world as in a slave colony of the modern one, and that the centre's monopoly of the future is a cartographer's fiction. Athens and San Domingo are, in his work, the same argument twice, the two cases in which ordinary people, inside societies built on slavery, exercised or seized a power their rulers' theories declared impossible for them. The pairing is the key to his optimism, such as it was. He did not believe progress was guaranteed; his histories are too full of confiscated revolutions for that. He believed capacity was perennial, that it re-formed wherever people laboured and played and governed together, and that the task of the intellectual was to be ready, with the record in hand, when it next announced itself. The schoolroom classics were his training in recognising the announcement. The colonies, in his judgement, had produced the modern world's finest readers of them, because the colonies had the most at stake in the reading.

## Among his peers: the West Indian writers' generation

James was the elder of the generation that created West Indian literature, and his relations with its younger masters form a coda to the literary sections of this profile, because their judgements of him are among the most penetrating on record. George Lamming, the Barbadian novelist whose work defined the region's fiction in the nineteen fifties, acknowledged the example in the strongest terms available to him: James had shown that a West Indian could write the region's experience at the level of the world's classics without ceasing to be of the region, and Lamming's own essays on Haiti, on the ceremony of the dead and the peasantry's persistence, carry the Haitian history's influence in their bones. The Guyanese writer Wilson Harris, whose fiction pursued the region's history into myth, represents the counter-current: a Caribbean imagination for which James's materialism was one necessary voice among others, to be answered by the spiritual and Amerindian depths his own work sounded. The exchanges were conducted mostly at a distance, in essays and in the common rooms of exile London, and they were not always warm; the generation that James had made possible repaid him, as generations do, by arguing with the making.

### How did the younger West Indian writers place him?

As the founder who had to be answered: Lamming by extending his Haiti into the region's imaginative charter, others by resisting his materialism, all by accepting the scale he had set, in which West Indian experience was a subject for the world's literature.

The prickliest of the reckonings came from V. S. Naipaul, the Trinidadian novelist of the next generation, whose work carried a famous contempt for the postures of the colonial intelligentsia among whom he numbered, on his bad days, nearly everyone but himself. Naipaul's Trinidad is not James's: where James found in the island the materials of a civilisation in the making, Naipaul found mimicry and the void, and his portraits of the region's political intellectuals are drawn with a cruelty that his readers have sometimes applied, by association, to the editor of The Nation. The contrast is instructive for the profile's purposes precisely because it is not a dispute about facts. It is a dispute about what a colonial society is: a prison of imitation from which the gifted escape into exile and honesty, or a workshop in which a people, under every disadvantage, is manufacturing itself. James's entire output is the second proposition worked out at book length, and Naipaul's is the first, and the West Indian reader's education consists, in no small part, in learning to hold the two Trinidads side by side and to see that the quarrel between them is the region's central intellectual event. For the placement dispute this profile has staged, the literary quarrel supplies a useful control. Nobody has ever proposed that James the novelist's elder belonged to any tradition but the one he was founding, West Indian literature itself; the proposal arises only for the Marxist, where the stakes are allegiances rather than shelves. The difference in treatment says something about the politics of classification that James, who classified for a living, would have relished pointing out.

## The limits of the record: women in the revolution and in his pages

An honest profile must register the most substantial silence in The Black Jacobins, because later scholarship has made it impossible to pass over and because the silence bears on the profile's central argument. The revolution James narrates is, in his pages, overwhelmingly a revolution of men. Women appear at its margins: as sufferers in the atrocity record, as figures in the domestic tragedies of the leaders, as the unnamed carriers of provisions and intelligence in the war chapters. Yet the historical record, as the scholarship of the last generation has established in detail, shows women everywhere in the revolution's substance: working the estates whose labour process the argument celebrates, in gangs whose composition was heavily female; sustaining the provision grounds and the markets through which the insurgent armies ate; serving as couriers and spies in the intelligence war; fighting, in documented instances, in the ranks; and enforcing, in the camps and towns, the discipline of the boycott and the refusal on which the war economy depended. A history of the revolution that centred the labour process might have been expected to find them, since the process ran on their labour as much as on men's. James's sources, the military and administrative record of a slaveholding century, are partly to blame: they record women least where women mattered most. But his questions are partly to blame as well. The historian who taught the world to read planter documents against the grain for the agency of the enslaved did not press the same documents, with the same suspicion, for the agency of the enslaved's women.

### What does the silence about women cost the argument?

It leaves the labour process half-described, since the gangs and the provision economy ran substantially on women's work, and it leaves the revolution's social depth understated: the scholarship that restored them has strengthened, not weakened, his central claim about collective capacity.

The cost accounting has a surprising direction, and the profile should follow it honestly. The feminist scholarship that corrected James, recovering the market women, the provision-ground cultivators, the camp organisers, and the combatants, did not thereby refute the proletarianisation argument. It extended the argument's evidence into the half of the workforce its author had under-read, finding exactly the collective capacities, the discipline, the organisation, the political intelligence, that his method predicts, in the population his narrative had slighted. The correction thus stands as the method's vindication against its author's application, a pattern the profile has met before, in the movement's correction of Marx and in James's correction of his own tradition, and the recurrence of the pattern is itself the deepest evidence for the placement this profile's dispute circles: whatever tradition James is assigned to, his work behaves the way he claimed a living theory behaves, surviving by being corrected in its own terms. Series readers who have followed the feminism cluster immediately preceding this one will recognise the structure of the case. A framework's treatment by its ablest critics, from within its own questions, is the test of its vitality, and by that test the Haitian history, corrected on its women as on its Africans and its chronology, remains alive in a way that flawless minor works do not.

His later work partially repairs the record, and the repair should be noted for fairness. The American documents and the tendency's practice gave women's self-activity a place the movement of the time rarely gave it; the lectures of the last decades address the Caribbean's women as the civilisation's carriers with a directness the history lacked; and the Brixton conversations, by the testimony of the younger scholars who climbed those stairs, many of them women historians of the Caribbean, were seminars in which the old man's questions had visibly changed. None of that rewrites 1938. The Black Jacobins stands with its silence in it, and the profile's duty is discharged by naming the silence plainly, measuring its cost to the argument, and recording that the argument, on the evidence of its correctors, was larger than its author's sight of it. There are worse epitaphs for a work of Marxist history, and James, who built his reputation on the doctrine that the makers exceed the leaders, would not have refused this one.

## Defeat and the long view: the optimism examined

James lived long enough to watch nearly every political hope of his lifetime defeated in its first form: the Russian revolution bureaucratised, the colonial revolutions administered, the West Indian federation dissolved, the American movement fragmented, the Ghanaian dawn couped away. A profile that ended without examining his response to that record would miss the quality his contemporaries most remarked in him, which was not brilliance but an unshakeable, argued serenity. The serenity was not temperament dressed as doctrine, though his temperament helped. It followed from the theory of history the notes on dialectics had formalised. If organisations necessarily consolidate and betray, then defeat is not an anomaly requiring explanation by treachery or by the people's failure; it is the normal fate of forms, and the political question is never whether a form will fail but what the people who built it have learned in the building and will carry into the next form. On that accounting his century was not a record of defeats at all. It was the century in which the colonised world rose, in which the working populations of every continent acquired, through their defeats as much as through their victories, the experience that his schoolroom classics had taught him to regard as the substance of political education. The Haitians of 1804, the Ghanaians of 1957, the crowds of his last lectures, were in his ledger a single accumulating asset, and he spoke of the future, to the end, as a creditor speaks of a debt certain to be collected by somebody, at some date, in some form that would surprise its collectors.

### How did James sustain a politics across a century of defeats?

By his dialectical accounting: forms fail necessarily, capacities accumulate permanently, so each defeat is tuition paid by a class rather than a verdict on it, and the historian's long view is the only honest unit of political measurement.

The reader is not required to buy the accounting, and the profile, true to its method, should state the price at which it is sold. A doctrine that converts every defeat into tuition risks consoling where it should accuse, and James's serenity was purchased, at times, by a refusal to dwell on the costs his own criterion imposed: the Ghanaian and Trinidadian disappointments are audited in his work with a brevity that his harsher critics have read as evasion, and the Brixton sage's pronouncements on the movements of the nineteen eighties sometimes mistake the audience's reverence for the movement's health. He was, in the strict sense, a nineteenth-century figure in his confidence that history has a direction and that the direction can be known, and the century he outlived had made that confidence expensive for everyone else. Yet the alternative ledger, the one kept by his generation's apostates and by the Naipaul of the counter-portrait, in which the colonial world's efforts issue only in mimicry and void, has its own unfalsifiable comfort, the comfort of never being disappointed again. Between the two comforts the reader must choose with the texts open, and the profile's final service is to note that James foresaw the choice and built his work to survive it. The histories, the essays, the cricket book, and the record of the life are all arranged so that the evidence precedes the consolation. Judge the evidence, he says on every page, by the hardest cases available. The consolation, if it comes, will have been earned, and if it does not come, the evidence remains, and evidence, unlike consolation, can be built on by the next reader who picks it up. It is as good a statement as any of why a series like this one exists, and it is the note on which this profile properly hands its subject back to his readers.

## The trade that built the ports: his account of the slave trade's economics

The Black Jacobins does not begin on the plantations. It begins with the trade that stocked them and with the French ports that the trade enriched, and the sequence is a methodological declaration. Before the reader meets a single enslaved person, the book establishes the system that consumed them as an economic order with addresses: the shipowners of Nantes and Bordeaux, the refiners who processed the sugar the ships returned with, the insurers and creditors and textile manufacturers woven into the triangular circuit, the respectable bourgeois fortunes founded, as James puts it with deliberate flatness, on the misery of Africans who would die in the holds and the cane fields. The effect is to deny the reader, from the first pages, the consolation of treating slavery as a crime of individual sadists or a stain on an otherwise innocent commerce. The trade was the commerce, conducted by the most advanced commercial classes of the most advanced commercial nation in Europe, defended in its parliaments, blessed in its churches, and woven so deeply into the prosperity of the Atlantic world that its abolition, when it came, required revolutions to accomplish.

### Why does the history open in Nantes and Bordeaux rather than on the estates?

To establish the system's address before its victims: the trade as the normal business of Europe's most advanced commercial classes, so that the revolution, when it comes, is legible as the answer of the consumed to the consumers' whole civilisation.

This opening also grounds the Williams thesis before Williams published it, and the profile notes the priority because the scholarly record benefits from precision. When Capitalism and Slavery appeared in 1944 with its argument that the slave system's profits had financed British industrialisation, its author was systematising, for Britain, an analysis whose Caribbean and French version had been in print since 1938 in the work of his old teacher, and extending it with an archival rigour the essayist had not attempted. The relation between the two books is the relation this profile described in the Williams section: the teacher opened the ground, the pupil built on it, and the scholarly quarrels that followed, over magnitudes and over the decline thesis, were quarrels inside a research programme that the pair of them, between Port of Spain and Oxford, had founded. James's own contribution to the quarrel was characteristic: he largely declined to fight over the numbers. His interest was in the structure, in the demonstration that the modern world's wealth and the modern world's crime were a single fact viewed from two sides, and that demonstration does not depend on any contested percentage. It depends on the addresses, on Nantes and Liverpool and the insurance market, and the addresses are not in dispute.

His treatment of the trade's human mechanics observes the offensive-source discipline this series applies to such material: the conditions of the Middle Passage are characterised accurately, in their calculated brutality, without the reproduction of the trade's own dehumanising vocabulary and without lingering description. The restraint is worth a sentence of analysis, because it is sometimes mistaken for squeamishness. It is the opposite. James's argument requires the enslaved to enter his book as a population whose capacities are the subject, and the literature of atrocity, however well intentioned, fixes its subjects as victims at exactly the moment his analysis needs them as agents. So the horror is registered, economically and in passing, and the narrative moves to the estates, where the survivors' work and organisation wait. Moral histories of slavery begin with the suffering and end with the rescue. His begins with the system and ends with the system's overthrow by the people it consumed, and the difference between the two arcs is the difference between the book he wrote and the books that were already on the shelf in 1938.

## Violence and its measure: his treatment of revolutionary violence

The question of violence runs through the Haitian revolution's history as it runs through every revolution's, and James's handling of it is among the most distinctive features of his writing, worth isolating because it clarifies his difference from both the apologists and the denouncers who divide the field. He neither averts the record nor feasts on it. The French expeditionary campaign's methods, the drownings, the burnings, the use of dogs against prisoners, the mass executions, are stated as facts of the war the French command chose to wage; the insurgent reprisals, culminating in the killings of the remaining French population after independence, are stated with the same flatness; and no casualty figures are offered for either, the estimates being, as this profile's protocol notes, the property of a contested specialist literature. What James supplies instead of enumeration is a criterion, applied impartially to every party: violence is judged by the political work it does, by whether it defends a people's existence or administers a people's subjection, and by whether the leaders who order it have explained it to the people in whose name it is done. By that criterion the French terror condemns itself, as the instrument of a re-enslavement its authors would not name honestly; the insurgent violence of the war's last phase is judged, more sorrowfully, as the answer of a people who had been offered extermination as the alternative to surrender; and the post-independence massacres are recorded as the revolution's wound, the act of a founding that had learned its enemy's grammar too well.

### By what standard does James judge the violence of the revolution?

By its function and its candour: whether it defended the existence of a people against re-enslavement or administered their subjection, and whether those who ordered it had explained it honestly to those in whose name it was done.

The criterion's interest is that it refuses the two standard positions between which writing on revolutionary violence usually oscillates. Against the liberal position, which condemns the violence of the oppressed and the oppressor in a single gesture of distaste, James insists that the gesture is a luxury of the safe, and that a people offered the choice between violence and re-enslavement does not face a moral dilemma of the kind seminar rooms imagine. Against the romantic position, which celebrates the violence of the oppressed as purifying in itself, he insists that violence is an instrument with costs that compound, that it deforms the states it founds, and that the Haitian state's subsequent century of militarism is part of the Leclerc expedition's harvest as surely as independence is. Readers of this series will hear, in the second insistence, the ground note that distinguishes James from the subject of the preceding profile in this cluster, Frantz Fanon, whose theory of violence assigns it a psychological and constitutive role that James, the older man and the historian of an actual war, never assigns it. For James violence founded nothing. Organisation founded; candour founded; violence, at best, cleared the ground on which they could work, and at worst salted it. It is a colder doctrine than Fanon's and a less quotable one, and its coldness is the temperature of a man who had counted, in his own history's pages, what the clearing of ground costs the clearers.

## The Dar es Salaam lineage: Rodney and the argument's second life in Africa

The clearest measure of a thinker's reach is the work his readers go on to do, and by that measure the Jamesian line in African historiography deserves its own section of this profile. Walter Rodney, the Guyanese historian who taught at the University of Dar es Salaam in Tanzania in the years around 1970, wrote the book that carried the tradition's central argument to a new continent: How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, published in 1972. Rodney's thesis, that Africa's poverty was not an original condition but a product, manufactured by the slave trade's demographic haemorrhage and by colonialism's deliberate arrest of the continent's development for the benefit of European accumulation, is the Williams argument extended from the Caribbean to the continent that supplied the Caribbean's people. The lineage is documented in Rodney's own acknowledgements of his intellectual formation, in the Dar es Salaam school of historians who gathered around the same questions, and in the structure of the book itself, which refuses, exactly as James refused, to write its subject population as the passive scene of other people's enterprise. Africa in Rodney is acted upon, massively and criminally, but it is also acting: its peoples' resistances, adaptations, and statecrafts fill the record, and its underdevelopment is an achievement of policy, not a fate of geography or culture.

### What did Rodney take from the James and Williams line?

The founding move: that the wealth of the modern West and the poverty of the colonised world are one process with two faces, to be explained structurally rather than pitied separately, and that the colonised are the process's protagonists and not its scenery.

Rodney's fate gives the section its gravity and its connection back to the profile's themes. Expelled from Jamaica in 1968 by a government that feared his organising among the Kingston poor as much as his scholarship, he built his career between Dar es Salaam and Georgetown, and he was assassinated in Guyana in 1980, at thirty-eight, by a bomb placed by agents of the government his party opposed. James, then seventy-nine, outlived his most brilliant inheritor, and the fact belongs in the record of the tradition the two men shared: it was a tradition whose practitioners paid for their conclusions in the currencies of exile and, in Rodney's case, of life itself. The point is not decorative. It bears on how the tradition's claims should be read by the comfortable. The argument that slavery and colonialism made the modern world, and that the modern world's official history is arranged to hide the fact, was not, for the men who developed it, a seminar position. It was a finding they reached from inside the societies the process had made, and defended in public, in the lecture halls and the streets of Kingston, Dar es Salaam, Georgetown, and Port of Spain, at prices the metropolitan academy rarely charges. A reader assessing the placement dispute staged in this profile might weigh that too: whatever else divided Robinson's James from the Marxists' James, the tradition the man actually founded was a practice before it was a classification, conducted by historians who organised, and its documents read differently when their authors' itineraries are kept in view.

The Dar es Salaam moment also completes the profile's account of the proletarianisation argument's progeny. Rodney's Africans are not classified as a proletariat, and the extension James performed on Haiti is not repeated for the continent; what travels instead is the method of which the classification was an instance. Begin from the organisation of production. Refuse the moralised geography that assigns agency to one continent and suffering to another. Treat the official record as the testimony of interested parties, to be read against its grain. Expect the decisive initiatives to come from below. Those four rules, abstracted from the Haitian case, proved portable to Africa in a way the specific classification could not be, and their portability is the strongest available evidence about what in James's work was essential and what was local. The classification was the local brilliance, fitted to the sugar estates like a key to a lock. The method was the durable instrument, and it is the method, rather than the label, that the historians of three continents have carried away from his work. Profiles of thinkers usually end by weighing the system against the influence. In this case the weighing answers itself: the influence is the system, minus its most famous word, still at work wherever a historian opens a plantation ledger, a factory inspector's report, or a colonial file and asks the Jamesian question, what did the people inside this process make of themselves?

## Haiti in the American argument over slavery

One theatre of the revolution's afterlife deserves a paragraph of its own, because James returned to it throughout his American years and used it to school his audiences. In the United States, Haiti functioned for seventy years as the slaveholders' strongest rhetorical weapon and the abolitionists' most dangerous exhibit. The weapon's use was brazen: every proposal for emancipation, however gradual, was answered with the island's name, with the massacres of the founding retailed as the necessary consequence of freeing enslaved people anywhere, and with the implication that the abolitionist, whatever his intentions, was an agent of San Domingo. The exhibit's danger was the mirror image. For the enslaved and for free Black Americans, Haiti was the standing proof that the thing declared impossible had been done, by people like themselves, against the three greatest military powers of the age, and Black newspapers, conventions, and emigration schemes kept the proof in circulation across the whole period of slavery's defence. James, lecturing in Detroit and New York, drew the moral his history had prepared: that the American argument over slavery was never an internal American argument, and that the enslaved of the United States had possessed, in the Caribbean, a university of revolution whose existence their masters' historiography laboured to conceal. The concealment's failure, in the long run, is his subject's vindication; the revolution that could not be written out of the hemisphere's politics by quarantine could not, in the end, be written out of its history either.

The traffic ran in persons as well as in arguments. In the eighteen twenties, Haiti's President Boyer invited free Black Americans to emigrate, offered land and passage money, and several thousand came, settling in communities whose descendants remained on the island for generations; in the years before the American Civil War the emigration scheme was revived, with the Episcopal priest James Theodore Holly leading a party of settlers and staying to build a church and a life in the Black republic. The schemes failed as mass movements, on the hardships of the settlers and on the pull of the American struggle itself, but their meaning for James's theme is plain: Haiti was not an abstraction in Black American politics but a place, with ships sailing to it, whose existence organised hope and argument alike. At the century's end the connection received its most distinguished embodiment when Frederick Douglass, the great abolitionist, served as the United States minister to Haiti and defended the republic, in his lectures after his return, against the American press's campaign of contempt, arguing that Haiti's treatment by the powers was the measure of the world's unfinished reckoning with slavery. James knew this whole record and taught from it. It allowed him to present the Haitian revolution not as the Caribbean's private glory but as an event inside the history of the United States, owed a place in that country's account of itself, and it let him close his lectures with the question his book had opened in 1938: if the enslaved of San Domingo could do what they did, what exactly was the foundation of the doctrine that peoples of African descent required the tutelage of others? The question has never received an answer from the doctrine's holders. It has only received, from James's readers, the book that asks it with the evidence attached.

## The counter-example: what James's case proves and what it does not

The series pattern this profile instantiates is the counter-example: a single figure whose existence constrains a general claim. James is routinely deployed against the claim that Marxism cannot grasp race, and the deployment is usually too loose to survive inspection. Precision about which claim he refutes is therefore the profile's last analytical task. He refutes, decisively, the claim in its biographical form: that a thinker formed inside Marxism, working with its categories and remaining answerable to them, cannot produce a first-rank analysis in which enslaved Africans are the central historical subjects and their revolution is explained rather than merely celebrated. The Black Jacobins exists; its method is the labour-process analysis described in this profile; its author's self-description is on record. Against the biographical claim, one book is a sufficient refutation, and it is the claim most often actually made in polemics about Marxism and race. He does not refute the claim in its structural form: that Marxism's classical categories, unmodified, are adequate to the analysis of racial formation. His own practice is the evidence against that adequacy, since the adequacy was achieved, in his hands, by extending the category of the proletariat past its classical boundary and by insisting, against his movement, on the autonomous organisation of the racially oppressed. A tradition that requires its most gifted practitioner to bend its central category and fight its central organisation has not been shown to contain the analysis ready-made. James's case shows that the analysis is achievable within Marxism by work, which is a different and more interesting conclusion than either of the polemical ones on offer.

### Which version of the claim about Marxism and race does James refute?

The biographical version: that no Marxist, working as a Marxist, can centre enslaved Africans and explain their revolution. The structural version, that the classical categories need no extension to do it, his own practice contradicts rather than confirms.

One further precision belongs here, because the profile has undertaken not to use James's stature to settle disputes that belong elsewhere. The debate over racial capitalism, over whether race is external to capital's logic or constitutive of it, is conducted in this series in the companion study of Black Marxism and racial capitalism, where the positions associated with Robinson and with his critics are set out in their own terms. James bears on that debate as a difficulty for one of its versions, the version that treats Marxist analysis and the analysis of racial formation as strangers to one another by necessity. He is not a verdict in it. Similarly, the category question that his argument raises, what a class is and how the labour-process and wage-relation criteria are to be weighted, belongs to the foundations laid out in [marxist theory of class guide](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/), and the reader who wants to pursue the strongest objection past the answer given here should start from that guide's treatment of class as a relation rather than a rank. What this profile claims for James is the thing that can be claimed exactly: a Trinidadian schoolmaster's son, formed by cricket, the classics, and a colonial education, joined the revolutionary movement of his century and used its method to write the history that the movement's European centre had not imagined, and the argument at the centre of that history, that sugar made a proletariat before the factory did, remains the sharpest single challenge to any account of Marxism that treats its geography as settled.

Where should a new reader begin with him? With The Black Jacobins itself, in the later edition with the Castro appendix, read as an argument and not as a pageant: the pre-revolutionary chapters slowly, the Toussaint chapters with attention to the distinction between the leader's sight and the leader's speech. Then Beyond a Boundary, which is shorter than its reputation and funnier, and which will recalibrate any reader who has filed its author as a solemn theorist. The American writings reward a third stage: the Melville study for the method applied to literature, and the state capitalism documents for the theory, read with the series' treatment of the Trotskyist disputes at hand. The fiction, Minty Alley and the stories, belongs to the reader who wants the Trinidad that formed him. What no reader should do is begin with the summaries, his own included, because James's power is in the conduct of an argument over hundreds of pages, in watching a mind refuse every easy resting place, and the summaries, however accurate, report conclusions whose force exists only in their earning.

The secondary literature can then be approached in the order of the questions it answers. For the life, the two biographies, Buhle's and Worcester's, read together correct each other's emphases. For the placement dispute, Robinson's study itself is indispensable and should be read whole rather than in extracts, since its James is built from a long argument about the Black radical tradition that summaries flatten. For the Haitian scholarship's movement since James, Trouillot on the revolution's silencing and the Africanist literature on the insurgent armies mark the two directions in which the field has travelled. And for the tendency, the published documents, the Hegel notes and the state capitalism writings, repay reading in their own abrasive voice, which no commentary reproduces. A reader who finishes that shelf will possess something rarer than an opinion about C. L. R. James: the means to test other people's opinions about him, including the opinions staged, at full strength and without a verdict, in this profile.

## For students and exam candidates

James rewards the student who treats The Black Jacobins as an argument rather than a monument. The examinable core of this profile is the proletarianisation argument: be able to state it in two sentences, name the evidence it rests on in the organisation of the sugar estates, and state the strongest objection to it, the wage-relation objection, together with James's answer and the concession the answer requires. That triad, claim, objection, concession, is the shape of every high-scoring answer on this material. The placement dispute is the second examinable asset: Robinson's 1983 reading and James's own testimony should be learned as a pair, with the work identified and the man's self-description cited, since questions on Marxism and race are set precisely to test whether a candidate can hold a thinker's self-understanding and his critics' redescription in view at once. Students preparing for the imperialism papers will find the companion treatment in [marxism imperialism exam answers](/2017/06/15/marxism-imperialism-exam-answers/), and the four phases of the life can be fixed for revision as a study card in the [VaultBook Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), where the argument of this profile is set out in memorisable form.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The proletarianisation argument is an unusually effective instrument for teaching what a relational definition of class actually does, and this profile recommends it for that purpose above its considerable interest as history. Set students the passage work in sequence: first the description of the estates, and have the class extract the criteria of class membership the description implies; then the orthodox definition, with the wage relation at its centre, and have them locate the exact point of divergence; then the strongest objection, and let the seminar divide on whether James's answer, that the labour process rather than the wage form produced the politically decisive capacity, repairs the divergence or merely renames it. The exercise teaches, in one text, the difference between a category used as a label and a category used as a tool, and it has the further virtue that the historical stakes are unmistakable: students who might drift through an abstract seminar on class formation attend to one in which the category decides how a revolution is explained. The Moïse affair supplies the companion seminar on leadership, and the two together fill a week that students remember. The four Jameses table at the head of the American sections can be issued as the week's map, with each seminar group assigned one phase to defend as the real James against the others.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on James divides into three live fields, and this profile has tried to mark each one's state of play. The Haitian field turns on the relative weight of plantation discipline and African military and cultural inheritance in forming the insurgent armies, a question on which the archival scholarship continues to move and on which James's text, read closely, is more ecumenical than his reputation suggests. The intellectual field turns on the placement dispute: the continuing publication of the American documents and the Hegel materials has strengthened the archival basis for reading James inside the Marxist tradition, while the growth of Black studies as an institution has entrenched the reading that claims him for an autonomous tradition, and researchers should expect the dispute to be settled, if ever, by accumulation rather than by verdict. The third field is the Williams thesis connection, where the documentary record of the teacher-pupil relation and the two books' arguments rewards the kind of precise intellectual history that the broader dispute tends to crowd out. Journalists approaching anniversaries of the Haitian revolution will find the essential correctives in this profile: the enslaved were the revolution's authors, its violence had authors on every side, and its historian was a Trinidadian Marxist whose placement is contested by design of the evidence. The companion study of the tradition and its disputes, the article on Black Marxism and racial capitalism elsewhere in this series, is where researchers will find the racial capitalism debate in its own terms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was C.L.R. James?

Cyril Lionel Robert James, born in Trinidad in 1901 and died in London in 1989, was a Marxist historian, theorist, novelist, and cricket writer. A scholarship boy at Queen's Royal College in Port of Spain, he wrote on West Indian self-government, joined the Trotskyist movement in London, and published in 1938 the history of the Haitian revolution on which his reputation rests. He spent fifteen years in the United States leading a revolutionary tendency, was deported in 1953, returned to Trinidad to edit a party newspaper, and wrote Beyond a Boundary, the celebrated study of cricket and colonial society, in 1963. Across every phase he described himself as a Marxist and centred his work on the self-activity of ordinary people.

### Q: What is The Black Jacobins about?

It is an account of the uprising in France's richest sugar colony, San Domingo, where the enslaved rose in 1791, defeated Spanish, British, and French armies in succession, and founded the independent state of Haiti in 1804. Its narrative follows Toussaint Louverture's rise, his government of the colony, his arrest and death in France, and the final war of independence under his successors. Its argument is that the revolution was made by the enslaved themselves rather than granted by France, that the French and Haitian revolutions shaped each other reciprocally, and that the enslaved plantation workers, by the organisation of their labour, stood closer to a modern proletariat than any European workers of the period.

### Q: Why does James call the enslaved a proletariat?

His reason is organisational. The great sugar estates gathered the enslaved in hundreds, set them to interlocking tasks in field, mill, and boiling house, and imposed a working rhythm dictated by a process that could not pause. James argued that such labour forms the habits that make collective action possible: combination, discipline, a shared structural grievance, and knowledge of one's own numbers. European workers of the same decade, mostly peasants and artisans in small workshops, lacked that formation. The label therefore records a comparison about how people worked and what their work had made of them, not a claim about their legal condition, which he never blurred.

### Q: Was the Haitian Revolution part of the French Revolution?

James's answer is that each belongs inside the other's history. The French Revolution supplied the rights language, the colonial crisis, and the divisions among the masters that opened the way; the colonial revolution then forced the French Republic's hand, producing the abolition decree of 1794, ruining Napoleon's American design, and driving the sale of Louisiana. Neither sequence can be explained in isolation: Parisian factions rose and fell on colonial events, and the enslaved timed their moves to European wars and decrees. The claim, provocative in 1938, has since become close to the working assumption of the scholarship on the revolutionary Atlantic.

### Q: Did James break with Trotskyism?

He broke with its organisations and with one of its defining doctrines, while continuing to work with its method and to call himself a Marxist. With the Johnson-Forest Tendency he argued that Russia was a state capitalist order rather than a degenerated workers' state, which dissolved the distinction on which orthodox Trotskyism rested, and he replaced the vanguard party's tutelage with the self-activity of workers as the centre of politics. The tendency left the Trotskyist parties definitively in 1951. Whether that itinerary counts as a break with Trotskyism or as its development depends on the definition in use, which is one reason the dispute over his placement has proved so durable.

### Q: What was the Johnson-Forest Tendency?

It was the revolutionary group James built in the United States with Raya Dunayevskaya and Grace Lee, named from the pseudonyms Johnson and Forest. Formed in the early nineteen forties inside the American Trotskyist movement, it developed two connected positions: that the Soviet Union was a state capitalist order whose bureaucracy functioned as a collective capitalist, and that the self-activity of ordinary workers, visible in wildcat strikes and autonomous movements, was the substance of socialism rather than material for party direction. After leaving the Trotskyist parties in 1951 it published the newspaper Correspondence, built around reports written by workers about the labour they themselves performed, before fragmenting in the later nineteen fifties.

### Q: Why did James write about cricket?

Because cricket, in the colonial West Indies, was a political institution: the game codified the empire's values, sorted its players by colour and class, and staged, in mastery and in the captaincy question, the colony's argument about itself. Beyond a Boundary, published in 1963, uses his own formation as a player and journalist to read that institution, treating batsmen's styles as social documents and the campaign for a Black captain as a constitutional event. The book asks what a society's organised play reveals about its distribution of honour and capacity, and its famous opening question proposes that cricket cannot be understood by those who confine themselves to cricket.

### Q: Did James move beyond Marxism?

That is the disputed question at the centre of his reputation. Cedric J. Robinson's Black Marxism of 1983 reads James as the exemplary figure of a Black radical tradition older than and independent of Marxism, whose greatest work surpassed the Marxist frame from within the Black experience. James never accepted the placement: he described himself as a Marxist to the end of his life, his theoretical writings argue within Marx's categories, and his appendix of 1963 states flatly that race ranks as the subordinate question in politics, class as the primary one. This profile presents both positions at full strength and does not adjudicate, since the evidence, his double formation and his lifelong self-description, genuinely supports elements of each.

### Q: What is the Williams thesis and how is James connected?

The Williams thesis, from Eric Williams's Capitalism and Slavery of 1944, holds that the profits of the slave trade and the slave plantations supplied a decisive part of the capital for British industrialisation, and that British abolition followed the declining economic weight of the old West Indian interest rather than humanitarian triumph. Williams had been James's pupil in Trinidad, and the affinity is visible: James's history had argued in 1938 that slavery was an economic institution whose profits built the French Atlantic ports, and he later served, and broke with, Williams's government in Trinidad. The thesis's two parts remain contested by economic historians, and no figures are stated here, since the figures are the dispute.

### Q: Where should you start with James?

Start with The Black Jacobins in the later edition, the one carrying the appendix that runs from Toussaint to Castro, and read it as an argument: the early chapters on the colony's economy slowly, the Toussaint chapters for the distinction between a leader's sight and a leader's speech. Follow it with Beyond a Boundary, shorter and funnier than its reputation, which recalibrates any picture of its author as a solemn theorist. The American writings come third: the Melville study for his critical method, the state capitalism documents for the theory. The early fiction, Minty Alley and the stories, rewards the reader who wants the Trinidad that formed him. Avoid beginning with summaries, including his own retrospective ones.

### Q: How does the proletarianisation argument grade the plantation evidence?

It grades it unevenly, by design. The claim is staked on the great sugar estates of the North Plain, where workforces numbered in the hundreds, gang labour was coordinated with mill and boiling house, and the process set the pace; there the evidence is at its strongest, and there the revolution began. On the smaller coffee estates and among the urban enslaved, whose labour was less integrated, the claim reaches weakly or not at all, and James's own narrative reflects the difference. The honest assessment is that the argument is a claim about a sector, whose political weight came from that sector's concentration at the revolution's heartland, and it should be tested there rather than averaged across the colony.

### Q: What should a student separate between James the historian and James the organiser?

The evidence each supplies the other. The historian's central finding, that the enslaved made their revolution and were failed by a leader who would not explain himself, became the organiser's doctrine that what ordinary people do for themselves outranks every party's instruction. Separating the two protects the student from two errors: grading the history as propaganda for the doctrine, when its research preceded and disciplined the politics, and grading the politics as a footnote to the history, when the American tendency's positions were argued in their own arena against their own opponents. The sequence matters. The book of 1938 taught the man of 1948 what he knew, and his organisations were the tuition he paid for applying it.

### Q: Why did a history of a slave revolution argue with European Marxism?

Because European Marxism's map of the modern world placed the revolutionary future in the industrial centres and assigned the colonies the roles of victim, reservoir, and auxiliary. James's evidence contradicted the map: the largest, most integrated labour process of the seventeen nineties stood on Caribbean plantations, and the people inside it made the age's most complete revolution while Europe's workers were still peasants and artisans. The argument was therefore conducted in Marxism's own terms, class and labour process, against Marxism's own geography. It claimed that the method, honestly applied to the colonial evidence its holders had not examined, relocated the centre of the story, which is a more damaging kind of argument than any external denunciation.

### Q: How does the four Jameses table order the life?

It separates the career into the situations that changed the arguments. The Trinidad and London phase supplies the formation: fiction, the self-government pamphlets, the pan-African circle, and the study of the Russian revolution's bureaucratisation. The Haitian history is the second phase and the enduring achievement, where the method is proved. The American tendency generalises the history's lesson into the state capitalism thesis and the doctrine of self-activity. The return phase tests the doctrine against nationalist government in Trinidad and produces the cricket book and the audit of the party he served. Read down the table's rows, the life appears as one question asked in four situations; read across, each phase shows what he wrote, argued, and worked with, and how it feeds the next.

### Q: What separates the enslaved workers' organisation from their legal status in his argument?

Everything the argument is about. Legally the enslaved were property: saleable, inheritable, entered in inventories between livestock and buildings, and forbidden every attribute of the free labourer, including ownership of their own capacity to work. Organisationally they staffed, in coordinated gangs, the most productive export economy of the Atlantic world, a process that required their cooperation and taught it daily. James's claim is that the second description, not the first, explains the revolution: when crisis came, the legal fiction of thinghood was confronted by the functional reality of a formed collective labourer, and the reality prevailed. The separation is the argument's core, and collapsing it in either direction destroys the claim.
