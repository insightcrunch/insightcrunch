The Reserve Bank of India Act 1934 is the statute that creates India's central bank, and everything about the institution that public argument treats as a matter of status or personality is, in the text, a matter of legal drafting. The Bank is not a constitutional body. No article of the Constitution establishes it, guarantees its tenure, or places its functions beyond the reach of an ordinary Act of Parliament. It exists because a statute of 1934 says it exists, it holds the powers that statute confers, and it operates inside boundaries that the same statute draws, including one boundary that surprises almost every reader who encounters it for the first time: a provision under which the Central Government may direct the Bank, after consulting its Governor, on anything the government considers necessary in the public interest.

![The Reserve Bank of India Act 1934 and the statutory boundary between the central bank and the government - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-83.webp)

That provision, Section 7, is the pressure point around which every serious argument about the Bank turns, and it cannot be understood in isolation. It has to be read alongside the appointment provisions, which give the government the choice of the Governor and Deputy Governors; alongside the composition of the Central Board, which the government substantially nominates; alongside the surplus provision, which sends the Bank's profits to the government each year; and alongside the monetary policy framework inserted in 2016, which gave the Bank a statutory objective for the first time in its eighty-two years of existence, assigned the setting of that objective to the government, and left the choice of instruments to a committee on which the government appoints half the voting members. The argument of this article, stated in advance so that the reader can test it against the text, is that the Reserve Bank's independence is operational rather than structural. The statute gives the elected government the objective-setting power, the direction power, and the larger share of the appointments. What protects the Bank in practice is convention, transparency, and the political cost of using powers that are plainly there. That is a defensible constitutional design, and several respected central banks work under versions of it, but it is a design in which the line between the bank and the government sits where politics places it, not where the law fixes it.

The article proceeds through the statute in the order a lawyer would read it: the problem the Act was built to solve, the institution it created, the functions it conferred, the two provisions that define the government relationship, the long silence on objectives and the amendment that ended it, and the courts' treatment of the Bank's powers. Where the public record is unreliable, and on Section 7 it frequently is, the article says what is documented and stops there.

## Before the Bank: the problem the Act was built to solve

A central bank is an answer to a specific set of failures, and the failures in British India were concrete. Currency was issued by the Government of India itself, through a Paper Currency Department, under a system in which the volume of notes was tied to reserves of silver, gold and securities held against them. Credit was supplied by a patchwork of presidency banks, merged in 1921 into the Imperial Bank of India, which acted as banker to the government, held government balances, managed the public debt, and at the same time competed for ordinary commercial business against the very banks it was supposed to stand behind. The exchange standard was managed by the Secretary of State in London through council bills, a mechanism that subordinated Indian monetary conditions to the convenience of the India Office. No institution combined the note issue, the custody of the banking system's reserves, the lender-of-last-resort function and the management of government debt, and the absence of that combination was felt in every banking crisis of the period, when small banks failed in runs that a reserve-holding institution might have contained.

Two features of that inheritance shaped everything the Act later did. The first was the dual character of the Imperial Bank. It was at once the government's banker, the holder of the government's balances and the manager of its debt, and a commercial competitor of the joint stock banks, opening branches, taking deposits and lending on the same terms as the institutions it was meant to stand behind. A lender of last resort cannot also be a rival for the same business, and the Imperial Bank's commercial appetite repeatedly compromised its public role, most visibly when banks in distress found that the institution holding the system's confidence was also competing for their customers. The second feature was the subordination of currency policy to the exchange question. The great monetary controversy of the 1920s in India was not the design of a central bank but the ratio at which the rupee should be stabilised against sterling, and the Hilton Young Commission was appointed as much to settle the dispute between the one shilling four pence and one shilling six pence standards as to design an institution. The Commission recommended the higher ratio and, alongside it, the Reserve Bank, and the pairing mattered: a central bank was wanted partly because a fixed exchange commitment required an institution that could defend it with reserves, discount policy and control of credit, functions that a government currency department could not perform. The Bank was therefore conceived from the beginning as an instrument of monetary management and not merely as a note-issuing office, even though the Act that created it spoke mainly of notes and reserves.

The intellectual groundwork was laid early. John Maynard Keynes, who had served on the staff of the India Office, argued in his 1913 book on Indian currency and finance for a state bank for India that would hold the reserves and manage the currency. The Chamberlain Commission of 1913 engaged with the proposal without delivering an institution. The decisive intervention came from the Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance, appointed in 1925 and chaired by Edward Hilton Young, which reported in 1926 and recommended the creation of a central bank, to be called the Reserve Bank of India, constituted as a shareholders' institution and deliberately insulated from party politics, which would take over the note issue from the government and the government business from the Imperial Bank. The Commission's reasoning set the template that the eventual Act followed: currency and credit should be managed by one authority, that authority should hold the reserves of the banking system, and its constitution should place it at arm's length from the legislature of the day.

Translating the recommendation into law took eight years and two failed attempts, and the delay is itself instructive about the politics that surrounded the Bank from birth. A Reserve Bank Bill introduced in 1927 foundered on disagreement about the constitution of the proposed board and the degree of legislative control over it, with Indian members resisting a design they saw as entrenching official and European commercial influence, and the government unwilling to surrender control over appointments. A second attempt in 1928 also failed. The Act that finally passed in March 1934 was shaped by the constitutional negotiations of the early 1930s, in which the transfer of financial responsibility to Indian hands was conditioned on safeguards, and a statutorily protected central bank, removed from day-to-day political direction, was one of the safeguards demanded. The bill that succeeded was introduced in the Legislative Assembly in 1933 and was debated against the background of the ratio controversy and of Indian demands that the proposed bank be answerable in some measure to Indian opinion; the government held to a constitution in which the Governor and the executive direction were appointed from above, and the Assembly's amendments did not displace that design. The Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, Act 2 of 1934, received assent in March 1934, and the Bank commenced operations on 1 April 1935, taking over the note issue from the government's Paper Currency Department and the government account from the Imperial Bank, which was released to become an ordinary commercial bank. The Bank's head office began in Calcutta and moved to Bombay in 1937.

The original preamble of the Act deserves attention because of what it does not contain. It recited that it was expedient to constitute a Reserve Bank to regulate the issue of bank notes and the keeping of reserves with a view to securing monetary stability in India, and generally to operate the currency and credit system of the country to its advantage. That is a description of functions, not an objective. It named no target for prices, no duty toward employment or growth expressed as a goal to be attained, and no standard against which the Bank's performance could be measured and held to account. For eighty-two years the Bank worked under that preamble, inferring its mandate from the functions it performed, until the amendment of 2016 rewrote the position, a story taken up in detail later in this article.

## A shareholders' bank: establishment, private ownership and the transfer of 1949

The institution the Act created was, in form, a company. Section 3 of the Act established the Reserve Bank of India as a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal, capable of acquiring and holding property and of suing and being sued in its own name. Its capital was fixed at five crore rupees, divided into fully paid shares of one hundred rupees each, and those shares were subscribed by private shareholders, not by the government. The Governor-General in Council appointed the first Governor and Deputy Governors, and the Central Board combined directors elected by shareholders with directors nominated by the government. The Bank's first Governor was Sir Osborne Smith, an Australian banker who had advised on the institution's design, and his tenure supplied an early demonstration of how contested the Bank's autonomy would be: he resigned in 1937 after sustained disagreement with Sir James Grigg, the Finance Member of the Government of India, over interest rate policy and over the degree of independence the Bank was to enjoy in practice. The statute had created the institution; the working boundary between the institution and the government had to be fought for from the start, and the fight began within two years.

The Bank's first decade was dominated by war. From 1939 the exchange control that would define the Bank's external work for half a century was imposed under emergency defence powers, with the Bank administering the rationing of foreign exchange as the government's agent. War finance transformed the balance sheet: the Government of India spent heavily in India on behalf of the British war effort and was paid in sterling credited in London, so that India accumulated very large sterling balances while the domestic note issue expanded against them, and the Bank managed both the inflationary pressure of that expansion and the political argument about the balances, which Indian opinion regarded as a forced loan to Britain. The episode fixed two durable features of the institution. It made the Bank an economic ministry in miniature, concerned with prices, food supply finance and the allocation of credit in ways the 1934 text had not contemplated, and it left the Bank holding the external reserves whose custody has remained central to its identity. When independence came, the division of the sterling balances between India and Pakistan, and the payments arrangements between the two new states, were among the first tasks of the Bank in its national incarnation, and the Governorship of C. D. Deshmukh, the first Indian to hold the office after his appointment in 1943, spanned the transition from colonial institution to the central bank of a republic in waiting.

The shareholders' form lasted fourteen years. After independence, the new government took the view that the central bank of a sovereign state directing a planned economy should be publicly owned, and Parliament enacted the Reserve Bank (Transfer to Public Ownership) Act, 1948, under which all shares of the Bank were transferred to the Central Government, with compensation paid to the private shareholders, and the Bank became a publicly owned institution from 1 January 1949. The transfer matters for two reasons that go beyond antiquarian interest. The first is that it separates ownership from independence in the Indian design: the Bank functioned as the central bank, with the same statutory powers and the same exposure to Section 7, while its shares were privately held, and it continued to function with the same powers after the state acquired the shares. Ownership changed the identity of the shareholder; it did not change the legal powers of either side. Arguments that treat government ownership as the source of government influence over the Bank therefore miss the structure of the Act, in which influence runs through appointments, directions and the surplus, not through shareholding. The second reason is comparative: the public ownership of the Reserve Bank preceded and anticipated the larger programme of bringing the commanding financial institutions into state hands, a programme whose statutory history is traced in the account of [how Parliament nationalised the commercial banks](/2013/01/01/bank-nationalisation-acts-india/). The Reserve Bank's transfer was the quiet first step of that programme, completed before the Constitution itself came into force.

Nationalisation also settled, in the negative, a question that recurs in public debate: whether the Bank's independence was ever meant to rest on the identity of its owners. It was not. Under private ownership, the government nominated directors, appointed the Governor, and held the Section 7 power. Under public ownership, exactly the same is true. What changed in 1949 was the destination of the dividend on the Bank's shares and the formal accountability of the institution to the state that owned it; what did not change was a single operative provision.

## The architecture of the Reserve Bank of India Act: how the statute is organised

The Act is organised as a constitution, a business licence, a set of central banking powers, and three later accretions that record the expansion of the Bank's mandate across the twentieth century. Chapter I contains the preliminary provisions: the short title, the extent and commencement, and the definitions on which the rest of the Act depends, including the definitions of the scheduled bank and of the financial institutions the Act reaches. Chapter II establishes the Bank, fixes its capital and its offices, and contains the management provisions: the constitution of the Central Board, the local boards for the four regions, the appointment and tenure of the Governor and Deputy Governors, the disqualifications for directors, and the meetings and committees of the Board. Chapter III is the operational heart of the original Act, containing the business the Bank may transact, its obligations and rights as banker to governments, the sole right of note issue and the machinery of the Issue Department, the cash reserve requirement for scheduled banks, the collection of credit information, and the Bank's rate. Chapter IV collects general provisions, among them the allocation of surplus profits and the power to make regulations. Chapter V contains penalties. Onto that 1934 skeleton, Parliament later grafted Chapter IIIA, dealing with the collection and furnishing of credit information; Chapter IIIB, inserted in 1963, on provisions relating to certain operations of non-banking institutions and financial institutions, which grew in 1997 into the registration regime for non-banking financial companies; and Chapter IIIF, inserted in 2016, containing the entire monetary policy framework of target, committee and failure report.

The division of labour between this Act and its companion statute is the single most useful structural fact a reader can carry away. The Reserve Bank of India Act constitutes the Bank and confers its central banking powers: note issue, reserves, government business, credit control and the monetary policy framework. The detailed licensing and supervision of individual banking companies, their incorporation, their boards, their statutory liquidity and the power to wind them up, sits in the companion statute examined in [the pillar account of the Banking Regulation Act 1949](/2012/12/15/banking-regulation-act-1949-explained/). Confusion between the two Acts is endemic in public writing: the cash reserve ratio is an RBI Act obligation while the statutory liquidity ratio is a Banking Regulation Act obligation, and a direction to banks to initiate insolvency proceedings is authorised by the Banking Regulation Act, not by this Act, a distinction on which a major judgment turned, as the judicial section of this article records.

The Act's two schedules are working parts of the scheme rather than appendages. The First Schedule assigns the states and territories to the areas of the four local boards. The Second Schedule is the list of scheduled banks, and inclusion in it, governed by the criteria in Section 42, is the gateway through which a bank accepts the cash reserve obligation and gains access to the Bank's facilities; the Bank may include a bank that satisfies it of the prescribed capital and conduct conditions and may exclude one that ceases to satisfy them, so that the schedule functions as a rolling register of the institutions inside the central banking system proper. Co-operative banks occupy a divided position on this map, historically supervised jointly by the Bank and by the state registrars of co-operative societies, a duality whose weaknesses were repeatedly exposed by failures among urban co-operative banks and which later legislation addressed by strengthening the Bank's hand under the banking statute rather than under this Act.

Two features of the drafting style deserve notice because they explain how the Act has aged. The first is that the Act confers functions in broad language and leaves the instruments to be specified by the Bank's own directions, notifications and regulations, which is why so much of Indian monetary and regulatory law lives in subordinate instruments made under short enabling sections. The regulation-making power in Section 58, under which the Central Board, with the previous sanction of the Central Government, may make regulations consistent with the Act for giving effect to its provisions, is the hinge between the statute and the Bank's internal constitution, and it deserves notice alongside the chapters. Regulations under it govern the Board's own proceedings, the constitution of the Board for Financial Supervision described later in this article, the conditions of staff and the conduct of the Bank's business in matters the Act leaves open, and the requirement of the government's previous sanction is one more quiet entry in the ledger of the executive's formal holds over the institution. Chapter V's penalties, by contrast, are a modest code addressed mainly to failures to furnish information, obstruction and false statements, and the smallness of their fines records the era of their drafting; the real sanctions in the system are the regulatory ones, refusal and cancellation of licences and registrations, and they live in this Act's registration chapter and in the banking statute respectively.

The second is that the Act was repeatedly amended by insertion rather than rewritten: the NBFC chapter and the monetary policy chapter were bolted onto a statute drafted for a simpler financial system, and both grafts took. The result is a single statute in which a reader can move from the note issue provisions of 1934 to the inflation targeting provisions of 2016 within a few pages, and in which the distance between those two worlds measures how far the institution's mandate travelled without its constitution ever being replaced.

## The amendment arc: how a 1934 statute absorbed a modern central bank

The Act has never been replaced, and its history is a history of insertions, each of which records a change in what the Indian state asked its central bank to do. The first great change was the transfer to public ownership in 1949, effected by a separate short Act rather than by amendment of this one. The second was the recalibration of the note issue in 1956, when the proportional reserve system, under which a fixed share of the note issue had to be backed by gold and foreign assets, gave way to a minimum reserve system under which the Bank was required to hold a statutory minimum of gold and foreign securities and was otherwise free to back the currency with rupee securities; the change released the note issue from a mechanical constraint that an expanding economy had outgrown. The third was the insertion of Chapter IIIB in 1963, which carried the Bank into the regulation of non-banking institutions and deposit-taking outside the banking system. The fourth was the cluster of amendments of the 1990s: the 1997 amendment that made NBFC registration compulsory, and the financial reforms of that decade that dismantled the apparatus of automatic deficit monetisation and left the Bank in practical command of monetary operations. The fifth was the Reserve Bank of India (Amendment) Act, 2006, which removed the statutory floor and ceiling on the cash reserve ratio, conferred express powers over money market and derivatives instruments, and continued the long migration of the Act from fixed statutory parameters toward discretionary powers exercised by notification. The sixth was the Finance Act, 2016, which inserted Chapter IIIF and the monetary policy framework. The seventh was the Finance Act of 2019, which strengthened the Bank's hand over non-banking financial companies, including powers in relation to the boards and auditors of regulated NBFCs, in the aftermath of failures among large non-bank lenders.

Read as a sequence, the insertions show a consistent direction of travel. Each generation of amendment has moved a question out of the statute's fixed text and into the Bank's discretionary instruments, or out of convention and into the statute's text. The cash reserve ratio travelled the first route, from numbers written in the Act to a power to specify. Monetary policy travelled the second, from an unspoken practice of the Governor to a framework of target, committee and report written into Chapter IIIF. The direction of travel is the surest guide to how the Act will change again: the questions that remain governed by pure convention, the tenure of the Governor, the quantum of the surplus, the publication of the failure report, are the standing candidates for the next insertion, and the assessment at the end of this article returns to them.

## Who governs the Bank: the Central Board and the appointment provisions

Section 7 of the Act vests the general superintendence and direction of the affairs and business of the Bank in a Central Board of Directors, and Section 8 constitutes that Board. Its composition, as the Act provides it, is the Governor and not more than four Deputy Governors, appointed by the Central Government; four directors nominated by the Central Government from the local boards, one for each region; ten directors nominated by the Central Government; and one government official nominated by the Central Government. Alongside the Central Board, Section 9 establishes local boards for the western, eastern, northern and southern areas, whose functions are largely advisory and representational, and whose members feed the regional seats on the Central Board. Every voting member of the institution's governing body therefore arrives there by appointment or nomination of the Central Government. Under the original shareholders' constitution there was an elected element on the Board; the amendments that followed the transfer to public ownership removed it, leaving a Board wholly constituted by the executive.

The tenure provisions are as significant as the composition. The Governor and a Deputy Governor hold office for a term fixed by the Central Government at the time of appointment, not exceeding five years, and are eligible for reappointment. The Act prescribes no minimum term, no security against premature removal, and no process by which a Governor may be removed or protected. A Governor's tenure is therefore exactly as long as the appointing government chooses to make it within the five-year ceiling, and the absence of a statutory removal procedure, which in a differently drafted statute might have been supplied by a clause requiring stated grounds and a parliamentary process, is one of the clearest instances of the Indian design leaving the boundary to convention. Successive governments have in practice appointed for terms of three to five years and have generally allowed Governors to serve them out, and departures before the end of a term have been rare enough to be individually memorable; but each of those facts is a fact about practice, not about law. The disqualification and vacation provisions of Sections 10 and 11 police the ordinary integrity of directorships, barring salaried government officials other than those the Act itself places on the Board, bank directors and persons of unsound mind or undischarged insolvency, without touching the tenure question.

The Board also houses the Bank's supervisory constitution. In 1994 the Board constituted the Board for Financial Supervision, under regulations made under the Act's regulation-making power in Section 58, as a committee of the Central Board chaired by the Governor and charged with the integrated supervision of banks, financial institutions and non-banking financial companies. The detail is worth a reader's attention because it shows the Act generating a second tier of governance by internal regulation: the body that in practice directs the Bank's supervision of the financial system is not described in the Act's text at all, and exists because the Board used its power to regulate its own proceedings and committees to create it. A weekly committee of the Board handles routine business between Board meetings, and the four local boards continue to meet and to feed regional intelligence upward, so that the formal picture of a single Central Board deciding everything coexists with a working structure of committees through which nearly everything is in fact decided.

The relationship between the Board and the Governor also matters, because Section 7(3) gives the Governor, and in his absence a Deputy Governor nominated by him, the powers of general superintendence and direction of the affairs and business of the Bank that are not required by the Act to be exercised by the Board, subject to regulations the Board may make. The institution is thus governed on two levels: a Board that meets periodically, includes a government official, and takes the formal decisions reserved to it, among them the acceptance of the annual accounts and the surplus transfer; and a Governor who conducts the Bank's affairs continuously and who, for most of the institution's history, personally embodied its monetary policy. The 2016 framework changed the second of those levels for interest rate decisions alone, transferring them to a statutory committee, while leaving the Governor's general superintendence intact over everything else.

Practice under these spare provisions has produced patterns that a reader should know, because they are the real constitution of the office. Terms fixed at appointment have varied between three and five years. Raghuram Rajan was appointed in 2013 for three years and served them; Urjit Patel was appointed in 2016 for three years and resigned a year early; Y. V. Reddy and Duvvuri Subbarao each served five-year terms. Extensions have been granted and withheld without any stated criteria, and the decision is taken by the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet on the advice of the Finance Ministry, which also conducts the search where one is held. The Deputy Governors present the same pattern in miniature. Up to four may hold office, their portfolios are assigned by the Governor, and by settled practice the posts are divided between officers promoted from within the Bank's own service and outside appointees drawn from the civil service, the commercial banks and academic economics; the Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, who sits on the Monetary Policy Committee ex officio, has in the framework era been an economist appointed from outside the Bank's career stream, a convention that imports a second professional centre of gravity into the committee's Bank-side membership. None of these allocations appears in the Act, which fixes only the ceiling of four and the government's power to appoint.

The career backgrounds of Governors have ranged across the civil service, academia, the Bank itself and the International Monetary Fund, and the office has more than once been a stage in a longer public career: Manmohan Singh moved from the Governorship to the Finance Ministry and eventually to the Prime Ministership, and Bimal Jalan from the Governorship to a nominated seat in the Rajya Sabha. None of this mobility is regulated by the Act, which neither bars a Governor from subsequent government office nor imposes a cooling-off period, an omission that commentators on central bank independence regularly identify and that Parliament has regularly left alone.

The appointment provisions also reach the one committee that decides the policy rate. As the monetary policy section of this article sets out, three of the six members of the Monetary Policy Committee are appointed by the Central Government from outside the Bank, on the recommendation of a selection committee, for four-year terms without eligibility for reappointment. Counted across the institution's governing bodies, the government's appointment reach is comprehensive: it selects the Governor, the Deputy Governors, the whole of the Central Board, and half of the committee that sets interest rates. No serious account of the Bank's independence can start anywhere else, and the honest conclusion from the text is that if the Bank is independent in its daily operations, it is so because the government of the day accepts restraint in the use of appointment powers it fully possesses, a point developed in the assessment that closes this article.

## The note issue: the oldest function and its statutory machinery

Section 22 of the Act gives the Bank the sole right to issue bank notes in India, and this monopoly, inherited from the government's Paper Currency Department in 1935, is the least contested and most visible of the Bank's functions. Every note in circulation above the one-rupee denomination is a liability of the Bank, bears the Governor's signature, and represents a claim structured by the Act's machinery. Section 23 separates the note issue from the Bank's other business by requiring it to be conducted through an Issue Department kept distinct from the Banking Department, and the assets held against the note issue, historically gold coin and bullion, foreign securities and rupee securities held to prescribed minima, stand in that department. Section 24 fixes the denominations of bank notes, which may be of two rupees, five rupees, ten rupees, twenty rupees, fifty rupees, one hundred rupees, five hundred rupees, one thousand rupees, five thousand rupees and ten thousand rupees, or such other denominations not exceeding ten thousand rupees as the Central Government may specify on the Board's recommendation. Section 26 declares bank notes to be legal tender throughout India for the sum expressed on them, and Section 28 completes the monopoly by barring any person other than the Bank or the Central Government from making or issuing a promissory note payable to the bearer on demand, the instrument that would otherwise allow private note issue to reappear in another form.

The promise printed on every note, by which the Governor promises to pay the bearer the sum named, is the visible residue of the note's legal character. A bank note under the Act is the Bank's promissory note payable to bearer, which Section 28 forbids anyone else to issue precisely so that the form cannot circulate as private money, and its exchangeability into other denominations at the Bank's offices is guaranteed by the Issue Department's obligation to exchange notes without charge. In a fiat system the promise is redeemed in other notes rather than in gold, a transformation the Act accomplished quietly when the minimum reserve system replaced convertibility discipline with a statutory floor of a wholly different kind, but the form of the promise survives on the currency as a reminder that the note began life as the Bank's debt instrument and remains its liability in the accounts.

Two qualifications to the monopoly repay attention. The first is the one-rupee exception: the one-rupee note and all coinage are issued by the Government of India, not by the Bank, which is why the one-rupee note carries the signature of the Finance Secretary rather than the Governor. The Bank distributes coins and one-rupee notes as the government's agent, but the liability is the government's, a surviving trace of the older arrangements under which currency was an obligation of the state and only the higher denominations passed to the Bank. The second qualification is the withdrawal power. Section 26(2) provides that on the recommendation of the Central Board, the Central Government may declare that any series of bank notes of any denomination shall cease to be legal tender, subject to the conditions the section prescribes. That is the provision under which, on 8 November 2016, the government withdrew the legal tender character of the existing five hundred and one thousand rupee notes, acting on the Board's recommendation. The episode is instructive for the purposes of this article precisely because of its legality: the decision was taken through a provision of this Act, by the government, on the recommendation of a Board the government had appointed, and the Bank's role was to implement a currency decision that the statute allocates, at the decisive step, to the executive. Critics of the demonetisation disputed its wisdom and its preparation; its statutory basis in Section 26(2) was the ground on which the Supreme Court later upheld it against challenge, treating the question as one of economic policy within the competence the Act assigns.

The backing regime behind the notes changed character once, and the change is a useful measure of the Act's flexibility. As enacted, the Act imposed a proportional reserve system: a prescribed share of the Issue Department's liabilities, set at two fifths, had to be held in gold coin and bullion and foreign securities, with the remainder in rupee securities. The system tied the permissible note issue to the Bank's holdings of external assets, so that the currency could expand only as reserves allowed. In 1956 Parliament replaced it with a minimum reserve system, under which the Bank must hold gold and foreign securities of an aggregate value not less than two hundred crore rupees, with gold constituting not less than one hundred and fifteen crore rupees of that total, and may back the balance of the issue with rupee securities. The figures have not been revised since, and their survival in the text, long after they ceased to bear any proportion to a note issue measured in tens of lakh crore rupees, is a small monument to the Act's drafting method: the number that mattered was quietly abandoned as a constraint while remaining on the page as a formality. The liabilities of the Issue Department and the assets held against them are set out in the Act's provisions on the department's accounts, and the weekly statement of the Bank's position, published under the Act, remains the document through which the note issue and the reserve position can be followed by any reader who cares to look.

The physical production of the currency sits downstream of the statute. Notes are printed at the Bank's presses, including those of its wholly owned printing subsidiary, and the designs, dimensions and distinguishing marks of each denomination are prescribed by the Central Government after considering the Board's recommendations, a further instance of the Act's habit of dividing a single function between the two institutions. The Bank's obligation runs the other way on quality: it must supply clean notes and withdraw soiled ones, and the elaborate machinery of currency chests across the country, through which notes move between the Issue Department and the banking system, is administrative practice built on the Section 22 monopoly.

The 2016 withdrawal was the third use of the state's power over the legal tender character of notes, and the comparison between the three episodes shows the Act's provision displacing a heavier legislative route. In 1946, high denomination notes were demonetised by ordinance in an exercise aimed at unaccounted wartime wealth. In 1978, the government of Morarji Desai demonetised notes of one thousand rupees and above by ordinance, replaced within the year by the High Denomination Bank Notes (Demonetisation) Act, 1978, a full statute that dealt with the cessation of liability, the exchange window and the penalties for holding the withdrawn notes. In 2016, no new statute was thought necessary: the government acted under Section 26(2) of this Act on the Central Board's recommendation, and the consequences for the Bank's liability were then regularised by the Specified Bank Notes (Cessation of Liabilities) Act, 2017, which extinguished the Bank's liability for the withdrawn notes after the exchange windows closed. The legal significance of the sequence is that a power drafted in 1934 as a housekeeping provision on the currency had, by 2016, become sufficient authority for the largest monetary intervention in the Republic's history, and the Supreme Court's majority in upholding the 2016 action treated that breadth as a reason for deference to the economic judgment involved rather than as a defect in the delegation. Justice Nagarathna's dissent took the opposite lesson from the same history, holding that the 1978 route of primary legislation was the constitutionally appropriate one for a measure of that consequence. The disagreement is the cleanest modern illustration of the question this article keeps returning to: how much of the monetary constitution Parliament has in fact handed to the executive and the Bank between them.

The note issue also explains the Bank's balance sheet, on which the surplus argument later in this article depends. Notes in circulation are liabilities matched by the Issue Department's assets, chiefly securities and foreign assets; the Banking Department holds the deposits of the government and of scheduled banks, including the cash reserves described in the next sections, and conducts the Bank's market operations. The seigniorage earned on the currency, the difference between the face value of the note issue and the cost of producing and backing it, is a principal source of the income that Section 47 then distributes.

## Banker to the government and banker to banks

The second great block of the Act's business provisions makes the Bank the government's bank. Section 17 sets out the business the Bank may transact, a long enabling list that includes accepting money on account of the Central and State Governments without interest, purchasing and selling securities of those governments, dealing in bills of exchange and treasury bills, granting advances and loans, holding custody of valuables, and generally conducting the business of a bank of issue and a bankers' bank. Section 20 then converts permission into obligation for the Union: the Bank is obliged to transact the general banking business of the Central Government, to make payments and remittances on its behalf, to manage the public debt, and to issue new loans when directed. Section 21 gives the Bank the corresponding right to act as banker to the Central Government across India, and Section 21A extends the model to the states, under which the Bank transacts the general banking business of state governments by agreement, arrangements under which every state government in the federation banks with the Reserve Bank. The public debt function, conducted under these provisions together with the Public Debt Act framework, makes the Bank the government's debt manager, issuing its securities, servicing its loans and managing its cash balances, a role that places the Bank at the centre of the government securities market whose prices its own monetary policy most directly moves.

The mechanics of the government's account repay attention, because they contain the one place where the Act's machinery was used, for decades, to finance the state automatically. Section 17 permits the Bank to make advances to the Central and State Governments repayable within three months, and under arrangements that grew up after independence the Union's cash shortfalls were met by the automatic creation of ad hoc treasury bills, instruments the Bank was obliged to take up, so that any gap between the government's receipts and its expenditure was monetised by definition and the money supply expanded with the fiscal deficit. The system was ended by agreement rather than by amendment: agreements between the Government of India and the Bank in 1994 and 1997 phased out ad hoc treasury bills and replaced them with a scheme of ways and means advances, under which both the Union and the states borrow from the Bank within limits fixed in advance, at interest linked to the policy rate, with defined consequences for overdrawing beyond the limit. The reform is generally counted among the most consequential monetary changes of the 1990s, and it was achieved entirely within the Act's existing text, by the two parties to Section 17 agreeing to use the advance power on market terms instead of as an entitlement. No episode better illustrates the article's thesis: the distance between a statute that permits fiscal dominance and a practice that refuses it was travelled by convention and agreement, and could in principle be travelled back by the same road.

The dual position deserves a pause, because it contains a structural tension that the Act simply accepts. As debt manager, the Bank serves the government as its agent and has an interest in the government's borrowing being cheap and orderly. As monetary authority, the Bank sets the price of money for the whole economy, including the price at which the government borrows. The Act does not resolve that tension by separating the functions, as some jurisdictions have done by moving debt management to an independent office; proposals to establish a separate public debt management agency have been advanced and debated in India without being carried through, and the management of the Union's debt remains with the Bank under Section 20. Readers who want to know where the pressure shows should look at the open market operations desk, which buys and sells the very securities the Bank has issued for its client.

The state government business conducted under Section 21A has a discipline of its own that the text leaves to agreement and the Bank has built into a code of practice. States may overdraw their accounts with the Bank only within limits and for a limited number of days, and persistent overdrafts oblige the Bank to stop payments on behalf of the defaulting state, a sanction that has been threatened and occasionally approached but whose full application against a state of the Union would be a constitutional event in itself. The Bank also manages the market borrowing of the states, issuing their development loans on a calendar coordinated with the Union's, so that the debt management function described for the Centre extends across the federation and the Bank sits on both sides of the federal fiscal relationship: it is the states' banker and debt manager while its monetary policy sets the yield curve on which every state borrows. State governments have at times pressed for the borrowing function to be separated or shared, and the debate is the federal echo of the Union-level argument about the Bank's dual position.

As banker to banks, the Act's central mechanism is the reserve requirement. Section 42 obliges every scheduled bank to maintain with the Bank an average daily balance, the cash reserve ratio, computed as a percentage of its demand and time liabilities that the Bank specifies by notification. The original Act bounded the Bank's discretion with a statutory floor and ceiling; an amendment in 2006 removed both, leaving the ratio wholly to the Bank's specification, which is a neat illustration of the statute's drafting philosophy of broad delegations. The reserve balances earn no interest, which makes the ratio a tax on intermediation as well as a monetary instrument, and the obligation to hold them is also the gateway to the Bank's facilities: a scheduled bank in the Second Schedule to the Act gains access to the Bank's lending and rediscount facilities and accepts in return the discipline of the reserve requirement and the Bank's directions. Around that core, the Act adds the machinery of a bankers' bank: the power under Section 45L and related provisions to call for information and give directions to financial institutions, the collection of credit information under Chapter IIIA, and the lender-of-last-resort lending embedded in the Section 17 business list, under which the Bank advances against securities, bills and other eligible paper to banks in need of liquidity. The statutory liquidity obligations that complete the reserve system sit, as noted earlier, in the Banking Regulation Act rather than in this Act, and the two should never be conflated.

## Credit control in practice: the instruments the Act supplies

Between the reserve requirement and the policy rate stands the apparatus by which the Bank moves money market conditions from week to week, and most of it is built from the general business powers of Section 17 rather than from any section labelled as an instrument. The Bank Rate, published under Section 49 of the Act, is the rate at which the Bank is prepared to buy or rediscount bills of exchange and other commercial paper; for most of the institution's history it was the headline signal of policy, the rate whose movement announced tightenings and easings, until the liquidity adjustment facility built around the repo rate displaced it as the operative signal and the Bank Rate settled into its residual role as the rate to which the marginal standing facility is aligned. Open market operations, the outright purchase and sale of government securities, rest on the Section 17 power to deal in securities of the Central and State Governments, and they serve two masters that the Act does not distinguish: the management of durable liquidity in the banking system and the orderly conduct of the government's borrowing programme, which the Bank conducts as debt manager under Section 20. The repo and reverse repo operations through which the Bank lends against collateral and absorbs funds overnight are likewise exercises of the Section 17 business powers, organised by the Bank's own frameworks and calendars rather than by any statutory scheme, which is why the corridor between the standing deposit facility rate and the marginal standing facility rate can be redesigned by the Bank without recourse to Parliament.

The corridor itself is a recent construction assembled from the same materials. The liquidity adjustment facility, introduced in 2000 on the recommendations of the Narasimham Committee on banking sector reform, organised the Bank's collateralised lending and absorption into a daily auction at announced rates, and the repo rate emerged from it as the single policy signal. The marginal standing facility followed in 2011, allowing banks to borrow overnight against their statutory liquidity holdings at a penal rate and thereby fixing a ceiling above the repo. The standing deposit facility, introduced in 2022 after the Finance Act of that year amended Section 17 to empower the Bank to accept deposits without the obligation to provide collateral in return, completed the floor: the Bank could at last absorb liquidity without parting with government securities, a technical constraint that had limited the old fixed reverse repo. Each element was a redesign of the Section 17 powers rather than a new statutory scheme, and the corridor's width, an instrument of policy in itself, has been set and reset by announcement. A reader tracing the authority for any of these facilities in the bare Act will find only the general business list and the Bank Rate, which is the clearest demonstration available of how completely the operating technology of Indian monetary policy lives below the level of the statute.

Alongside the general instruments stand the selective ones. The Act and the Banking Regulation Act together equip the Bank to vary margins on advances against particular securities and commodities, to ration credit for particular purposes, and to direct banks on the purposes for which advances may or may not be made, powers used extensively in the era of directed credit to restrain lending against sensitive commodities such as foodgrains and shares. Moral suasion, the Bank's practice of persuading banks by letter, meeting and public statement rather than by direction, has no statutory basis at all and is included here because an account of the Act's instruments that omitted it would misdescribe how Indian monetary control actually worked for long periods: the Governor's letters to bank chairmen were, in the decades of administered credit, among the most effective instruments in the system, and they derived their force entirely from the statutory powers standing behind them. The pattern across the whole apparatus repeats the Act's central lesson. Parliament supplied short enabling powers and a monopoly of the note issue and the reserves; the Bank built the operating system; and the boundary of the permissible has been fixed less by the text than by the courts' willingness, examined later in this article, to check an instrument against the section invoked for it.

## The forgotten chapter: non-banking financial companies

Chapter IIIB of the Act, inserted in 1963, is the part of the statute that public commentary most often forgets and depositors have most reason to remember. It began as a response to the growth of non-banking institutions accepting deposits outside the banking system and outside any regulator's reach, and it empowered the Bank to collect information from non-banking financial institutions, to regulate or prohibit the issue of prospectuses and advertisements soliciting deposits, and to give directions on the conduct of their business. The chapter's transformation came with the Reserve Bank of India (Amendment) Act, 1997, passed after a series of failures among deposit-taking non-banking companies and residuary non-banking companies had burned small depositors across the country. The 1997 amendment inserted Section 45-IA, which made registration compulsory: no non-banking financial company may commence or carry on business without obtaining a certificate of registration from the Bank and without maintaining the prescribed net owned fund. Around that gate, the amendment built a full supervisory perimeter, including the Bank's powers to determine policy and issue directions to NBFCs, to call for information and inspect, to regulate deposit acceptance including reserve requirements on deposits, and to cancel registration where a company conducts its business in a manner detrimental to depositors or fails to comply with the Act's conditions.

The perimeter has edges, and the edges are statutorily significant. Companies whose principal business is agricultural or industrial activity or the sale and purchase of immovable property fall outside the definition of a non-banking financial institution for the chapter's purposes, and whole classes of entity are regulated elsewhere: housing finance companies passed to the National Housing Bank and later back into the Bank's fold, insurance companies answer to the insurance regulator, and entities raising money through the securities markets answer to the market regulator, whose own statute is examined in [the pillar account of the SEBI Act 1992](/2013/04/01/sebi-act-1992-explained/). The recurring regulatory failures in this sector, from the residuary companies of the 1990s to later defaults by large non-banking lenders, have almost always occurred at those edges or in the gap between the Bank's registration power and its supervisory capacity over thousands of small companies. The failures of a very large infrastructure lender in 2018 and of a major housing finance company in 2019, both registered under the chapter, exposed the further weakness that the Act's scheme was built for a sector of small deposit-takers and not for non-banks of systemic size whose distress could transmit through the banking system that funded them. The responses came, characteristically, by layering rather than by replacement: the Finance Act of 2019 strengthened the Bank's powers over the governance of non-banking financial companies, including the power to remove directors and supersede boards in defined circumstances; the government notified large non-banking companies as financial service providers capable of being taken through insolvency under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, a route first used for the failed housing finance company; and the Bank moved the sector to a scale-based regulatory framework, organising companies into layers by size and activity and reserving the most intensive supervision for the upper layers. Each response used the chapter Parliament had built in 1963 and 1997, and the pattern confirms the chapter's place in the Act: it is the statutory foundation on which every subsequent architecture for non-bank regulation has had to be erected, because no other provision gives the Bank a registration gate over companies that take financial business without taking a banking licence. The courts' treatment of Chapter IIIB has consistently protected the Bank's authority: in the litigation over the Bank's directions governing residuary non-banking companies' deposit schemes, culminating in the Supreme Court's decision in the Peerless General Finance case of 1992, the Court upheld the Bank's power to issue binding directions in the interest of depositors and refused to reduce the chapter to a merely advisory scheme. The proposition that emerges, and that the judicial section of this article returns to, is that where the Bank acts to protect a class of depositors under an express statutory power, review is deferential; the discipline the courts impose is procedural and textual rather than a second-guessing of regulatory judgment.

## Exchange control and the Bank's external functions

The Act also carries the Bank's oldest external functions. The Issue Department's holdings of foreign securities were, under the original scheme, part of the backing of the note issue, and the Bank's custody of the country's external reserves grew from that function into the management of a very large portfolio of foreign currency assets, conducted under the Act's business provisions and the Bank's role as the government's agent in external transactions. Section 40 of the Act addresses transactions in foreign exchange, including the Bank's power to buy and sell foreign currency and to deal with the International Monetary Fund. The reserves the Bank manages under this constitution have their own political history that the Act's text conceals. For the first four decades the Bank operated in a world of scarce reserves, in which the defence of an overvalued exchange rate consumed the stock and the crises of 1957, 1966, 1981 and 1991 each found the cupboard nearly bare. After 1991 the doctrine inverted: the Bank accumulated reserves deliberately, buying the inflows of a liberalising economy as insurance against a repetition of the 1991 humiliation, until the stock of reserves became one of the largest in the world and itself a subject of the surplus argument, since a substantial part of the Bank's income and of its revaluation reserves is the yield and the valuation movement on those foreign assets. The intervention doctrine that accompanies the accumulation is convention from beginning to end: the Act nowhere prescribes an exchange rate objective, and the Bank's stated position, that it intervenes to curb undue volatility rather than to defend a level, rests on no statutory text. A reader who asks where in the Act the exchange rate regime of the Republic is to be found will not find it, and that absence, in the statute of a bank created in part to hold an exchange standard, is among the most eloquent silences in the book.

The regulatory control of foreign exchange, however, was built outside this Act in a succession of wartime and post-war statutes administered by the Bank, first as a temporary defence measure and then as a permanent control regime, until the philosophy of control gave way to the philosophy of management; that transition is the subject of [the account of the passage from FERA to FEMA](/2013/02/01/fera-to-fema-transition/), and under the successor statute the Bank remains the administering regulator for most external transactions even though the governing text is no longer its own Act. The division of labour under the 1999 Act is itself a small essay in the government and Bank relationship that this article traces. Current account transactions are regulated by rules the Central Government makes and directions the Bank issues, while capital account transactions, the ones that change the country's external balance sheet, are governed by regulations the Bank makes in consultation with the Central Government, with the statute expressly barring the Bank from imposing restrictions on current account payments except as the scheme provides. The pattern is the inverse of the monetary policy settlement: on the external account the government reserved the framework closer to itself and left administration to the Bank, and the steady liberalisation of the capital account has proceeded through the Bank's regulations and master directions rather than through parliamentary amendment, at a pace the Bank substantially controls. The distinction matters for readers tracing the Bank's powers: the exchange management function is exercised under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, while the reserves themselves are held and managed under the constitution this Act provides.

## The developmental Bank: institution building under a spare text

Nothing in the 1934 Act announces a developmental mandate, yet for four decades after independence the Bank's most consequential work included the deliberate creation of institutions the financial system lacked. The Agricultural Refinance and Development Corporation, the Industrial Development Bank of India, the Unit Trust of India and the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development were each promoted or incubated by the Bank, several of them initially as its own departments or subsidiaries, before being placed under separate statutes and, in most cases, separate ownership. The Deposit Insurance Corporation, established in 1961 under its own Act and later merged with the credit guarantee scheme, answered the deposit losses of the bank failures of the period and made the Bank the guarantor standing behind small deposits, a function discharged through a subsidiary corporation rather than under this Act. Training colleges, research departments and the Bank's statistical publications belong to the same era of institution building. The relevance of this history to a reading of the Act is that it demonstrates the elasticity of the Section 17 business powers and the preamble's general language about operating the currency and credit system to the country's advantage: a text drafted for a note-issuing bank was made to carry a development bank, a refinancing agency and a deposit insurance scheme without structural amendment, because the functions were conferred in terms of the business the Bank might transact and the institutions it might promote rather than by exhaustive enumeration.

The developmental era also explains a feature of the modern perimeter that otherwise looks accidental. The Bank's regulatory reach over non-banking finance, co-operative credit and specialised lenders grew by accretion as each new class of institution appeared, and several of the bodies the Bank created later became its regulatory counterparts or its wards, so that the map of Indian financial regulation is in large part a map of the Bank's own institutional children. The divestment of the Bank's shareholdings in institutions such as the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development and the National Housing Bank, completed in the last decade and a half on the principle that the regulator should not own the regulated, closed that chapter by ordinary executive and statutory action, without any amendment to the constitution of the Bank itself. The Bank's knowledge apparatus belongs to the same story and survives it intact: the statistical returns the Act empowers it to collect from banks and financial institutions feed the weekly statement, the monthly Bulletin the Bank has published since 1947, and the annual statistical handbook on which most empirical writing about the Indian financial system silently depends, while the Bank's own commissioned histories of itself, published volume by volume, remain the primary archive for the institution's first seven decades. A central bank that is also the financial system's statistician and historian holds an informational advantage in every argument this article has described, and that advantage, like the others, is an incident of the functions the Act conferred rather than of any privilege the Act states.

## Section 7: the direction power and the documented record

Section 7 is short enough to be stated almost completely, and its brevity is part of its force. Sub-section (1) provides that the Central Government may from time to time give such directions to the Bank as it may, after consultation with the Governor of the Bank, consider necessary in the public interest. Sub-section (2) provides that, subject to any directions given under sub-section (1), the general superintendence and direction of the affairs and business of the Bank shall be entrusted to the Central Board. Sub-section (3) vests the Governor with the powers of general superintendence and direction except in matters required to be done by the Board. Three features of the text should be fixed precisely, because public discussion garbles all three. The power is a power to direct the Bank as an institution, on any matter within its affairs and business, not a power confined to a list of subjects. The only procedural condition is consultation with the Governor; the section does not require the Governor's concurrence, does not require the direction to be in writing in terms the Act specifies, and does not require the direction or the consultation to be published. And the standard the government must satisfy is its own consideration of public interest, a formulation that leaves the judgment to the government and would make a judicial challenge to a direction an uphill undertaking on the face of the text.

The documented record of the provision is a record of proximity rather than use. In the autumn of 2018, when disagreement between the Ministry of Finance and the Bank over liquidity for non-banking lenders, the prompt corrective action framework for weak public sector banks, and the size of the Bank's transferable reserves had become public, it was widely and credibly reported that the government had written to the Bank initiating consultations by reference to Section 7, the first step the section requires before any direction could follow. The reporting was not officially denied in substance, and the Finance Ministry's own public statements of that period acknowledged that consultations with the Bank took place within the framework of the Act while insisting that the government's respect for the Bank's autonomy was firm. What the documented record does not contain, for 2018 or for any other year, is a formal direction issued under Section 7(1) and placed in the public domain. Successive official statements, including statements by former Finance Minister P. Chidambaram about the governments he served and reporting by officials familiar with the 2018 correspondence, converge on the position that no government has issued a binding direction under the section. The careful statement, and the one this article adopts, is therefore double-sided: the section has been formally approached, in that consultations under it were initiated in 2018 on the record; and it has not, on any documented evidence, been carried through to an issued direction. Accounts that say the section was invoked in 2018 overstate the record, because invocation in the section's own terms means a direction after consultation; accounts that say it has never been touched understate it, because the consultation step was taken and was understood on both sides as the opening of the one legal route to compulsion the statute provides.

The provision was not an accident of drafting, and its origins explain its breadth. The architects of the 1934 settlement worked in the shadow of the constitutional negotiations for Indian self-government, in which financial safeguards for creditors and for the sterling obligations of the Government of India were demanded as the price of transferring fiscal responsibility. A reserve power in the hands of the Central Government, exercisable after consultation with the Governor, was the mechanism by which ultimate political responsibility for the currency was retained while daily management was surrendered, and it was carried into the Act of a sovereign republic without reconsideration of its imperial purpose. Successive generations of officials then built the convention of non-use on top of it, a convention strengthened by every crisis in which it was not used: not during the balance of payments emergency of 1991, when the government of the day accepted an International Monetary Fund programme and the Bank executed the monetary side of it; not during the currency defence of 2013, when the Bank raised rates sharply and restricted imports of gold in measures the government publicly supported; and not during the inflation that followed the pandemic, when disagreement about the pace of tightening was conducted through speeches and minutes rather than through the section. The Finance Ministry's own public statement of 31 October 2018, issued at the height of the consultations controversy, is the clearest official exposition of the settlement: it affirmed that the autonomy of the central bank, within the framework of the Act, is an essential and accepted governance requirement, that governments in India have nurtured and respected it, and that the government holds extensive consultations with the Bank on many issues, a formulation that claimed the consultation habit while disavowing any intention to direct.

The surrounding events of 2018 supply the context in which the provision's meaning was tested without being used. On 26 October 2018, the Deputy Governor Viral Acharya delivered a speech on the independence of the central bank, arguing that governments which do not respect that independence would incur the wrath of financial markets and a crisis of confidence, a speech he stated was made with the Governor's knowledge. The Central Board met on 19 November 2018 in a sitting that ran for several hours and was followed by a joint statement recording discussion of the Basel capital framework, a restructuring scheme for stressed small enterprises, the prompt corrective action framework and the Bank's economic capital framework, on the last of which an expert committee was to be constituted. On 10 December 2018, Governor Urjit Patel resigned, citing personal reasons, before the end of his term. None of those events involved a direction under Section 7. Together they demonstrate the mechanism by which the section actually operates in the Indian system: its existence changes behaviour on both sides without being exercised, because a government that must consider whether to be the first to use it faces a reputational price, and a Bank that knows the power exists has reason to accommodate short of the point of compulsion. Two closing observations on the section belong in any complete account. The first concerns its interaction with the 2016 framework. Chapter IIIF vests the determination of the policy rate in the Monetary Policy Committee and makes its decisions binding on the Bank; a direction under Section 7 purporting to fix the rate, or to require a particular rate decision, would therefore collide with the later and more specific scheme, and commentators have argued both that the general direction power survives over the whole of the Bank's business including the committee's field, and that the specific chapter, enacted with knowledge of Section 7 and left unreconciled with it, must be taken to govern its own territory. Parliament has never resolved the question, and the prudent statement is that the interaction is untested and untestable in advance, since the political conditions that would produce such a direction would also determine how it was received. The second observation is testimonial. Duvvuri Subbarao, who held the Governorship through the financial crisis years, recorded in his published memoir of the tenure the sustained pressure brought to bear by the Finance Ministry for lower interest rates and cheaper liquidity, his resistance to it, and his view that the Bank's accountability for inflation gave it both the duty and the standing to resist; the memoir is the fullest first-person account of how the boundary behaves under load, and its evidence is that the load is carried by persons and conventions, exactly as the statute's silence predicts.

A comparative note confirms that the reserve power is a recognised, if extreme, feature of central bank statutes rather than an Indian aberration. The Bank of England Act 1998, the statute that conferred operational independence on that institution, itself preserves a Treasury power to give directions to the Bank of England, after consultation with the Governor, where the Treasury is satisfied that the directions are required in the public interest and by extreme economic circumstances. The English power is in one respect narrower, being tied to extreme economic circumstances, and in another respect more frankly stated, and it has likewise never been used. The parallel is instructive about what such clauses are for: they exist so that democratic responsibility for the currency is never wholly alienated, and their credibility as a last resort depends on their remaining visibly unused in all lesser circumstances. India's Section 7 lacks the English qualification of extreme circumstances and adds no publication duty, which makes it on paper the broader power of the two, and makes the Indian convention of restraint correspondingly the more remarkable.

The section is best understood not as a dead letter but as a reserve power whose shadow disciplines the relationship it governs.

### What makes the Reserve Bank's independence operational rather than structural?

It is operational because nothing in the Act entrenches it: the government appoints the Governor, the Deputy Governors, the whole Central Board and half the Monetary Policy Committee, sets the inflation target, and holds an unused power to direct the Bank, so autonomy survives on convention, transparency and political cost rather than on legal protection.

## The money question: Section 47 and the politics of the surplus

Section 47, headed as the allocation of surplus profits, provides that after making provision for bad and doubtful debts, depreciation in assets, contributions to staff and superannuation funds and all other matters for which provision is to be made by or under the Act or which are usually provided for by bankers, the balance of the profits of the Bank shall be paid to the Central Government. The provision makes the government the residual claimant on the Bank's income, and it leaves the decisive variable, the size of the provisions to be made before the balance is struck, to be determined within the Bank, principally by its Central Board. Every serious quarrel between the government and the Bank about money is therefore a quarrel about provisioning: how much capital and reserve a central bank needs against the risks on its balance sheet, and how much of what it has accumulated is genuinely surplus to that need. The statute does not answer that question. It names the heads of provision, gives the balance to the government, and leaves the quantum to judgment, which is why the quarrel recurs whenever the government's fiscal position is tight and the Bank's balance sheet is large.

To follow the quarrel a reader needs the rudiments of how a central bank earns, because the surplus is not a tax the Bank collects but the residue of its operations. The Bank's income arises principally from interest on the government securities it holds, which it acquires in its open market operations and as the counterpart of its lending; from interest and gains on its foreign currency assets, the reserves accumulated through its intervention in the exchange market; and from its lending to banks at the policy-linked rates of its facilities, offset by the interest it pays on the deposits it absorbs and by the cost of its operations. Its balance sheet carries, on the liabilities side, the notes in circulation and the deposits of governments and banks, and within its capital and reserves, two kinds of cushion that the surplus argument habitually confuses. The first is realised equity, built from retained earnings against credit, market and operational risks, of which the contingency fund is the core. The second is revaluation balances, the unrealised appreciation of the Bank's gold and foreign assets recorded in accounts such as the currency and gold revaluation account, which swell when the rupee depreciates or gold rises and shrink when they reverse, and which no one can distribute in cash without selling the underlying assets. Section 47's direction to make provision before striking the balance is the statutory doorway through which both cushions are defended, and the economic capital framework is in substance a published rule for how wide that doorway should be. The 2019 argument, stripped of its politics, was an argument about whether the contingency buffer needed to stand where the Bank had put it, and the Jalan Committee's range for that buffer is the nearest thing Indian law possesses to an answer.

The quarrel reached its most formal resolution in the economic capital framework process. In November 2018, in the settlement that followed the November Board meeting, the Reserve Bank, in consultation with the Government of India, constituted an expert committee under the chairmanship of former Governor Bimal Jalan to review the Bank's economic capital framework: how much realised equity and revaluation reserve the Bank should hold, and on what principles the distributable surplus should be computed. The committee recommended, among other things, that the Bank's contingency risk buffer be maintained within a range of 5.5 to 6.5 per cent of its balance sheet, that the surplus distribution policy be made formula-based, and that excess revaluation balances identified under the revised framework be transferred. The Central Board accepted the recommendations on 26 August 2019 and decided to transfer to the Government of India a sum of Rs 1,76,051 crore, comprising the surplus of Rs 1,23,414 crore for the Bank's accounting year 2018-19 and Rs 52,637 crore of excess provisions identified under the revised framework. The figures matter less than the method: the transfer was the largest in the Bank's history to that date, and it was reached not by direction, not by amendment, and not by capitulation recorded in any document, but by a committee process whose output both sides could present as technocratic. The episode is the surplus analogue of the Section 7 story, a dispute about the boundary between the Bank and the government resolved inside institutions the statute barely describes, with the Act supplying only the destination of the money and nothing about its amount.

The 2019 settlement was the second attempt to discipline the same question. In 2013, a committee under Y. H. Malegam, a member of the Central Board, had examined the Bank's capital and recommended that, after providing for a contingency reserve at a level the committee specified, the whole of the surplus be transferred, and the Bank adopted a policy on those lines for a period. The Jalan framework superseded that arrangement by expressing the buffer as a range and the distribution as a formula, and by drawing a principled line between realised equity, which can be distributed when it exceeds the buffer, and revaluation reserves, which represent unrealised gains on gold and foreign assets and cannot be paid out in cash without selling the assets themselves. The accounting setting also shifted beneath the argument: the Bank's accounting year, which for most of its history ran from July to June, was aligned with the government's financial year from 2020-21, a nine-month transition year intervening, so that the surplus for a year is computed on the same calendar as the budget that receives it. Interim dividends, paid to the government within the year in anticipation of the final surplus, complete the machinery, and their occasional use in years of fiscal stress is a further data point in the same negotiation, since an interim payment is in substance an advance against a provisioning judgment that has not yet been finally taken.

The case against drawing down the reserves was put in its fullest published form by Viral Acharya, the Deputy Governor whose 2018 speech on autonomy is described in this article, who argued in his subsequent account of the period that the Bank's capital is the economy's insurance against crisis and that treating it as a fiscal resource mistakes the purpose for which it was accumulated. The case the other way had been made from within the government itself before the committee sat: the Economic Survey of 2016-17, prepared under Chief Economic Adviser Arvind Subramanian, argued that the Bank was substantially overcapitalised by international standards and that the excess represented resources the economy could use. The Jalan Committee's own reasoning stood between the two positions, accepting that resilience must be maintained above the level of peer central banks while holding that capital beyond the needs of that resilience should not accumulate without purpose, and that a transparent formula is a better governor of transfers than annual negotiation. Both arguments are available on the statute, which is precisely the point: Section 47 creates a permanent negotiation and supplies no rule for settling it, so the settlement has to be manufactured each time by convention, committee or conflict.

## Eighty-two years without an objective: the road to the 2016 framework

The most consequential silence in the original Act was the absence of a statutory objective for monetary policy. The preamble spoke of monetary stability and of operating the currency and credit system to the country's advantage, language capacious enough to authorise almost any policy and to hold the Bank accountable for almost none. Through the decades of administered interest rates, directed credit and automatic monetisation of the government's deficit, the absence of an objective barely mattered, because the Bank's operations were substantially an extension of fiscal policy. After the balance of payments crisis of 1991 and the reforms that followed, including the agreements of 1994 and 1997 that ended automatic monetisation through ad hoc treasury bills, the Bank acquired the practical conduct of monetary policy without acquiring a statutory statement of what that policy was for. The decade itself deserves a sentence of record, because it was the Bank's operational liberation and it was accomplished almost entirely beneath the statute. The two-step devaluation of the rupee in July 1991 was executed through the Bank; the dual exchange rate of the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System in 1992 and the unified market-determined rate that replaced it in 1993 moved the exchange regime from administrative allocation to market pricing administered by the Bank; government securities began to be auctioned rather than placed at administered rates, creating the yield curve on which a monetary policy could act; and the interest rate structure was progressively deregulated until the Bank's rate decisions could transmit through the system instead of being announced to it. None of these changes amended the preamble. All of them presupposed a central bank conducting a recognisable monetary policy, which the law on paper still described only as the keeper of reserves with a view to monetary stability. It operated a multiple-indicator approach, watching growth, credit, the exchange rate and prices together, announced its stance in policy statements of increasing sophistication, and answered for its choices through convention and criticism rather than against a legislated standard.

### Why did the Reserve Bank operate for eight decades without a statutory objective?

Because the 1934 preamble described functions rather than goals, reciting the regulation of the note issue and the keeping of reserves with a view to monetary stability, without naming any measurable objective, and no later amendment supplied one until the framework inserted by the Finance Act of 2016.

The intervening decades were not empty of doctrine; they were full of doctrines that never reached the statute book. On the recommendation of the committee chaired by Sukhamoy Chakravarty, which reported in 1985, the Bank adopted monetary targeting with feedback, treating the growth of money supply as its intermediate objective and adjusting it as output and price data arrived; the regime assumed a stable relationship between money and prices that financial liberalisation progressively dissolved. From the late 1990s the Bank shifted to the multiple indicator approach described above, and the Patel Committee's later criticism of that approach, that it left the Bank's objective unknowable to the markets it was trying to guide, was the intellectual hinge of the 2014 reform. The sequence matters for the statutory story: for thirty years the Bank's operative mandate lived in committee reports, policy statements and practice, and at any point in those decades a reader of the Act would have found exactly the same preamble that had served the age of the sterling exchange standard. Indian monetary policy was, in the strictest sense, unlegislated, and the surprise is not that Parliament eventually legislated it but that the system functioned for so long on authority that the founding statute never conferred in terms.

The move to a statutory target came through a committee and an agreement before it came through legislation. The Expert Committee to Revise and Strengthen the Monetary Policy Framework, chaired by Deputy Governor Urjit Patel, reported in January 2014 and recommended that inflation be adopted as the nominal anchor for monetary policy, measured by the consumer price index, with a target of four per cent and a tolerance band of two percentage points on either side, and that decisions be taken by a monetary policy committee. In February 2015 the Government of India and the Reserve Bank signed a Monetary Policy Framework Agreement committing the Bank to bring consumer price inflation below six per cent by January 2016 and to the four per cent target thereafter, an executive agreement that reproduced the committee's design in advance of statutory authority. The Agreement deserves a closer statement, because it operated as law in substance for over a year before Parliament acted, and its terms supplied the template the statute copied. Signed on 20 February 2015 by the Finance Secretary and the Governor, Raghuram Rajan, it committed the Bank to adopt consumer price inflation as its nominal anchor, to aim to bring inflation below six per cent by January 2016, and to set the policy rate by reference to achieving four per cent inflation in the following years, with the two percentage point tolerance band on either side. It also contained, in embryo, the failure mechanism: if inflation stayed outside the band for three consecutive quarters, the Bank would have to explain itself. The sequence is constitutionally noteworthy. A reform that redefined the objective of monetary policy, and with it the practical accountability of the central bank, was made first by executive agreement between the two parties it bound, and was put into statute a year later substantially unaltered, so that Chapter IIIF is less a legislative design than the ratification of a bargain. Readers who want to understand why the 2016 framework divides powers as it does should therefore look for its logic in the politics of 2014 and 2015, in a government elected on a mandate against high inflation and a Governor whose committee had supplied the blueprint, rather than in any abstract theory of central banking.

The Finance Act, 2016 then amended this Act to give the framework a statutory home, inserting Chapter IIIF, comprising Sections 45ZA to 45ZO, and amending the preamble to declare that the primary objective of monetary policy is to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth. The amendment took effect in 2016, eighty-two years after the Bank's foundation statute had described everything the Bank was to do except the one thing it was to achieve.

## The 2016 framework: target, committee and failure report

Chapter IIIF divides the conduct of monetary policy between the government and the Bank along a line that this article has argued is the honest key to the whole statute. Section 45ZA assigns the objective: the Central Government shall, in consultation with the Bank, determine the inflation target in terms of the consumer price index once in every five years, and publish it in the Official Gazette. The first determination, notified in August 2016, fixed the target at four per cent consumer price inflation with an upper tolerance level of six per cent and a lower tolerance level of two per cent for the period ending 31 March 2021, and the same target was retained for the succeeding five-year period. Section 45ZB constitutes the Monetary Policy Committee and assigns it the instruments: the committee determines the policy rate required to achieve the target the government has set. The separation is exact and deliberate. The elected government chooses the destination and answers for it politically; the committee chooses the rate; neither can lawfully do the other's part, since the Bank cannot reset its own target and the government cannot set the rate except by the untested route of a Section 7 direction. The consultation Section 45ZA requires is not a formality, and its conduct at the first renewal illustrates the intended distribution of initiative: the target for the five years from April 2021 was retained unchanged after the consultation the section prescribes, so that the framework entered its second period with the destination confirmed by the government and the conduct of policy uninterrupted by any renegotiation of the objective in mid-flight. The durability of the number through a change in economic conditions, including a period of inflation above the band, is the strongest practical evidence that the five-year cycle performs its design function of taking the objective out of annual politics.

The committee's composition, the detail on which examinations and public argument most often err, is fixed by Section 45ZB. The committee has six members: the Governor of the Bank, who is its chairperson ex officio; the Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, ex officio; one officer of the Bank nominated by the Central Board; and three persons appointed by the Central Government. The government-appointed members are selected on the recommendation of a search-cum-selection committee, hold office for four years, and are not eligible for reappointment, a design that trades continuity for insulation, since a member who cannot be renewed has less reason to please the appointing authority. Section 45ZC and the surrounding provisions prescribe qualifications and disqualifications, barring among others members of Parliament and of state legislatures. Decisions are taken by majority vote, each member having one vote, and where the votes are equal the Governor has a second or casting vote, a rule that means the Bank's three members, voting together, cannot be defeated: the external members can prevail only if at least one member from the Bank's side agrees with them. Section 45ZO makes the committee's decisions binding on the Bank. Section 45ZI requires the committee to meet at least four times in a year, with a quorum of four, and in practice it meets on a published calendar of fuller frequency. Section 45ZL builds the transparency that the design relies on, requiring publication of the committee's resolution and, on the fourteenth day after each meeting, of the minutes, which record the vote of each member and the statement of each member on the resolution. Section 45ZM requires the Bank to publish, once in every six months, a Monetary Policy Report explaining the sources of inflation and forecasting inflation for a period of six to eighteen months. Readers revising the framework for the civil services or the Bank's own examinations, who need the composition, the target mechanism and the failure rule laid out as a single study artefact, can [work through the monetary policy framework as slides on the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), where the committee's structure is easier to retain than in the run of any single article.

### How often must the Monetary Policy Committee meet, and what must be published after it meets?

Section 45ZI requires at least four meetings a year with a quorum of four members. The committee's resolution is published, and Section 45ZL requires the minutes, recording each member's vote and individual statement, to be published on the fourteenth day after the meeting.

The framework's working life has tested its design in two instructive ways. The first is the conduct of the committee itself. The published minutes have recorded unanimity, majorities and dissents in both directions, with external members voting against rate decisions they considered too tight and members of the Bank's side voting against decisions they considered too loose, and the attribution of every vote by name has made the committee's internal disagreements part of the public record in a way that the pre-2016 system, in which the Governor decided after taking the advice of an advisory committee whose views were not published, never permitted. The casting vote, much discussed in examination answers, has mattered less in practice than the arithmetic it creates: because a tie is resolved for the Governor's side, an external majority against the Bank's three members requires at least one defection from within the Bank, and no resolution opposed by all three of the Bank's members can pass. The second test is the treatment of supply shocks. The framework's critics within the committee's own published record have argued that price rises driven by food and fuel supply cannot be corrected by demand instruments without sacrificing growth the preamble instructs the Bank to keep in mind, while the defence of the framework is that a target that bends for every shock ceases to anchor expectations, which is the function for which the target exists. The statute does not resolve that argument; it stages it, in public, six or more times a year, which may be its most valuable contribution.

The committee was first constituted by notification in the autumn of 2016 and held its first meeting in October of that year, inheriting a policy rate set by the Governor it replaced and a target already notified. The institutional machinery behind it sits inside the Bank: a monetary policy department serves the committee, prepares its forecasts and drafts its publications, and the Bank bears the framework's administrative cost, a reminder that the committee is a statutory body nested within the institution rather than a free-standing authority. The six-monthly Monetary Policy Report required by Section 45ZM is the Bank's document, not the committee's, and it is through that report, with its inflation forecasts and its account of the sources of price pressure, that the Bank as distinct from the committee explains the economy to which the committee's rates respond.

The accountability limb of the framework is Section 45ZN, and it is the provision that converts the target from an aspiration into an obligation. Where the Bank fails to meet the inflation target, it must send a report to the Central Government setting out the reasons for its failure to achieve the target, the remedial actions proposed to be taken, and an estimate of the time within which the target will be achieved through those actions. Failure is defined by regulation in terms of the average inflation rate remaining above the upper tolerance level, or below the lower tolerance level, for three consecutive quarters. The trigger was first pulled in 2022, when average consumer price inflation remained above the upper tolerance level for the three quarters from January to September of that year; the Bank convened an additional meeting of the Monetary Policy Committee in November 2022 and sent the required report to the government. The report itself was not published, a gap between the framework's transparency for decisions and its opacity for failure that commentators across the spectrum have criticised: the statute requires the explanation to be given to the government, and neither the Act nor the regulations require it to be laid before the public whose prices are its subject. Even with that gap, the mechanism changed the politics of a target miss. Before 2016 a period of high inflation produced commentary; after 2016 it produces a statutory event, a defined failure, a mandatory committee meeting and a written explanation owed by the Bank to the government of the day.

## The courts and the Bank: deference with a textual edge

The jurisprudence on the Bank's powers follows a consistent pattern: broad deference to regulatory and monetary judgment, combined with an insistence that the Bank act within the four corners of the power it invokes and with procedural fairness toward institutions specifically affected. The deference limb is the older and larger one. In the Peerless General Finance litigation, decided by the Supreme Court in 1992, the Bank's directions under Chapter IIIB regulating the deposit schemes of residuary non-banking companies were upheld against challenges that characterised them as unreasonable and beyond the statute; the Court treated the protection of depositors as a purpose squarely within the chapter and declined to substitute its view of sound regulation for the Bank's. The same posture appears in the demonetisation litigation, where a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court, by majority, upheld the 2016 withdrawal of the specified bank notes under Section 26(2), holding the measure to fall within the competence the Act assigns and refusing to weigh its economic wisdom; the dissent of Justice B. V. Nagarathna, who would have required the change to be made by legislation rather than executive notification, is valuable precisely because it identifies the point of principle, namely how much economic power the Act delegates and to whom.

### How have the courts treated the Reserve Bank's directions to banks?

With general deference on matters of economic and regulatory policy, refusing to second-guess the Bank's judgment, while requiring that each direction rest on the statutory power actually invoked and that institutions specifically affected be treated fairly and in accordance with the text.

The textual edge showed most sharply in the stressed assets litigation. By a circular of 12 February 2018, the Bank withdrew the older restructuring schemes and directed banks to resolve large stressed accounts within a fixed period, failing which the accounts were to be referred into [the insolvency process under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code](/2012/04/01/insolvency-bankruptcy-code-2016-guide/). In Dharani Sugars and Chemicals Ltd v Union of India, decided on 2 April 2019, the Supreme Court struck the circular down as ultra vires. The provision under which the Bank had purported to direct banks to initiate insolvency proceedings, Section 35AA of the Banking Regulation Act, authorised such directions only where the Central Government had authorised the Bank to issue them and only in respect of specific defaults; the circular was general in character and issued without that authorisation, and so fell, together with the proceedings founded on it. The judgment is frequently misread as a defeat for bank resolution; it is better read as a lesson in statutory geography, holding that the Bank's considerable powers are compartmentalised by the sections that grant them, and that a direction valid under one head cannot be borrowed from another. The Bank's response to the judgment is part of the record and part of the lesson. In June 2019 it issued a revised prudential framework for the resolution of stressed assets, recasting the regime as directions to lenders on recognition, provisioning and time-bound resolution that rested on powers the judgment had not disturbed, and abandoning the automatic reference to insolvency that had carried the earlier circular beyond its authority. The sequence, direction, annulment, recast direction, is the healthiest available evidence that the textual discipline the Court imposed operates as the Act intends: the Bank lost nothing it lawfully held, and the system gained a framework whose statutory basis its targets could no longer impeach.

Two years later the proportionality limb of review did the work in the virtual currencies case, Internet and Mobile Association of India v Reserve Bank of India, decided in March 2020, where the Court set aside the Bank's 2018 circular that had cut regulated entities off from persons dealing in virtual currencies, holding the measure disproportionate in the absence of any demonstrated damage suffered by the regulated entities, notwithstanding the breadth of the powers under which it was issued. Read together, the three lines of authority give the working rule: the courts will not run monetary or regulatory policy, but they will check the instrument against the section, and they will require a measure that extinguishes a line of business to justify itself by evidence.

One further strand of the case law deserves recording because it qualifies the deference described above. Where the Bank's action is directed at an identified institution rather than at the system, the cancellation of a licence or a registration, a direction that visits civil consequences on a named bank or company, the courts test the action against the requirements of natural justice and against the conditions the governing section prescribes, and the Bank's institutional-specific decisions have been set aside where the statutory preconditions or a fair hearing were absent. The distinction is coherent: policy is the Bank's, but a finding against a person is the law's, and the practitioner advising an institution affected by a direction will find the purchase for a challenge in the section's conditions and the record of the hearing rather than in the merits of the Bank's economics.

## What the Act does not do: the negative space

A pillar account owes the reader the boundaries as plainly as the contents, and the Act's silences are as instructive as its provisions. It does not license banks: the licence without which no banking company may do business in India is issued under the Banking Regulation Act, and the Reserve Bank's constitutive statute is not the source of its most familiar regulatory act. It does not insure deposits: deposit insurance arrived in 1961 under a separate statute and lives in a subsidiary corporation. It does not contain a lender-of-last-resort clause: the Bank's duty to stand behind a solvent but illiquid bank is inferred from its business powers and its custody of the reserves, and no section obliges it to lend to any institution, a silence that gives the Bank discretion in a crisis at the price of leaving the distressed institution without a legal claim. It does not enact a financial stability mandate: stability is pursued through the Bank's supervision and its convening power, and the Financial Stability and Development Council through which stability is coordinated across regulators is an executive creation, not a creature of this Act. It does not protect the consumer of financial services in terms: the charters of customer rights and the ombudsman machinery through which most citizens encounter the Bank's regulatory power are schemes made under its general powers and under the banking statute, not entitlements conferred by this Act. And it does not, as this article has had repeated occasion to observe, protect its own Governor. The pattern in the silences is consistent. Parliament legislated the Bank's core monetary constitution in this Act and has preferred to build everything else, stability, insurance, consumer protection and crisis management, in adjacent statutes and executive schemes where amendment is easier and the Bank's discretion is framed by others. The Act is therefore best read as the centre of a constellation rather than as a complete code of central banking, and the reader who treats it as the whole law of the subject will miss most of the law that governs the Bank's daily regulatory life.

The pandemic measures of 2020 supplied a late test of the judicial posture in a setting where deference carried immediate consequences for millions of borrowers. The Bank's circulars permitting lenders to grant a moratorium on loan repayments were challenged in Gajendra Sharma v Union of India, where borrowers sought a complete waiver of interest for the moratorium period. The Supreme Court declined to interfere with the economic policy embodied in the circulars, holding that the framing of relief in a crisis of that character was for the government and the Bank, while accepting a narrower relief in respect of compound interest for smaller loans that the government had separately announced. The case completes the trilogy this section has described: where the Bank's instrument is general and economic, review stops at the boundary of the power and the fairness of its exercise; the courtroom is not a second monetary policy committee, and no litigant has yet persuaded a court to sit as one over a decision of the committee itself, whose resolutions the Act makes binding and whose accountability the Act channels into reports and minutes rather than into appeals.

## Accountability the other way: the Bank's answerability to Parliament

A statute that gives a public institution this much discretion owes the reader an account of the answerability that runs in the opposite direction, and the Act supplies more of it than is generally noticed. The Bank's accounts are audited by auditors appointed by the Central Government under the Act's audit provisions, and the Bank is required to prepare and transmit to the government an annual report on its working, together with its annual accounts, which are laid before Parliament. The weekly statement of the Issue and Banking Departments, published under the Act, gives Parliament and the public a continuous view of the note issue, the reserves and the Bank's holdings. Beyond the documentary duties stands a convention with no statutory basis at all: the Governor appears before the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance after policy announcements and at the committee's invitation, and answers questions on the Bank's conduct of policy and supervision, a practice that has become one of the principal sites at which the Bank's independence is publicly justified and publicly challenged. The accountability design has an obvious gap that the 2016 framework widened rather than closed. The Bank reports to the government when it fails the target, and the government is the target's author; Parliament, which enacted Chapter IIIF, receives no statutory copy of the failure report and no statutory occasion to examine it. Whether that gap should be filled by requiring the report to be laid before Parliament is a live reform proposal with support across the usual divides, and its enactment would require only a short amendment to Section 45ZN, which is perhaps why it is the most frequently repeated specific recommendation in the literature on the framework.

## Assessment: two framings tested, and a verdict

The first framing holds that the Reserve Bank is a fiercely independent institution whose autonomy governments violate at their peril. Stated at its strongest, it points to real evidence: a statutory committee that votes on the policy rate in public, with named dissents; a failure-report mechanism that obliged the Bank to explain itself in writing when the target was missed; a run of Governors who defended the institution's positions in open disagreement with the government of the day; and a direction power that no government has dared to carry through. The second framing holds that the Bank is in substance a department of the Ministry of Finance with better branding. Stated at its strongest, it points to the same statute read from the other end: a Governor appointed and removable in practice at the executive's pleasure, a Board wholly nominated by the government, a target set by the government, profits swept to the government, a demonetisation decided by the government and implemented by the Bank, and a direction power whose existence the Bank must price into every disagreement.

Comparison with the other settlements a reader is likely to know sharpens the Indian position. The Federal Reserve in the United States holds a mandate written into its statute by Congress, price stability and maximum employment together, and its governors hold long staggered terms with removal protection expressed in the statute, so that its independence is structural in precisely the respects in which the Reserve Bank's is not. The Bank of England settlement of 1997 and 1998, which the Indian framework most resembles, gave the target to the Chancellor and the instruments to a committee, but expressed the Governor's and the external members' tenure in a statute and required the Bank's failure letter to the Chancellor to be published, the feature whose absence from Section 45ZN this article has already noted. New Zealand's arrangement, the ancestor of all target-setting by agreement, began as a published contract between the minister and the governor. India's framework sits inside this family but at its least entrenched end: the target is set by the government as in London, the committee votes in public as in London, and yet the Governor's tenure, the Board's composition and the unpublished failure report leave more of the settlement to goodwill than any of its cousins. The comparison is not an argument for copying any of them; it is a measurement of how much of the Indian arrangement depends on the restraint of the parties rather than on the text they work under.

Neither framing survives the text. The department framing ignores the genuine costs the statute imposes on interference: the transparency machinery of Chapter IIIF means a government that leans on the committee leans in public, the published minutes attribute every vote, and the 2018 sequence showed that even approaching Section 7 imposed a political penalty measurable in market commentary and a Governor's resignation. The fierce-independence framing ignores that every safeguard it cites is a safeguard of practice rather than of law, and that a determined government with a parliamentary majority could compress the Bank's autonomy within a single session without amending a word of the Constitution, because the autonomy was never placed in the Constitution to begin with. The verdict the evidence supports is the one this article stated at the outset. India has built a central bank whose objective is set democratically and whose instruments are exercised independently and visibly, and whose independence is therefore real in operation and contingent in structure. That is a coherent model, close to the settlement adopted for the Bank of England after 1997, in which the Chancellor sets the target and the Bank's committee sets the rate. Its weakness is the same in both countries and is constitutional in character: the settlement holds for exactly as long as the government of the day values the credibility the restraint purchases more than the policy it could command by abandoning restraint. What this general account cannot do is advise on any individual matter governed by the Act; the positions described here are the statute's general scheme, and anyone acting on a specific question under it needs advice on their own facts.

The standing reform agenda follows directly from the gaps this article has catalogued, and its principal items have named sponsors. The Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission, chaired by Justice B. N. Srikrishna and reporting in 2013, proposed in its draft Indian Financial Code a statutory monetary policy committee with published votes and a written framework of accountability, several years before Chapter IIIF enacted a version of the design, and the draft code remains the fullest statement of the road not fully taken. Proposals to give the Governor a fixed statutory term and a stated removal process recur in committee reports and in the writings of former Governors. The recommendation that the failure report under Section 45ZN be laid before Parliament and published has been made so consistently by commentators of every persuasion that its continued non-enactment is itself a datum about the preferences of the two parties the report runs between. And the separation of public debt management from the Bank, recommended by committees over two decades and announced in a Union Budget without being carried through, remains the unfinished item of the 1990s settlement. A reader who understands why each of these proposals exists understands the Act's silences as a reformer sees them: not as oversights but as decisions, renewed annually by inaction, to keep the boundary where convention puts it.

A practical word on reading the statute itself will serve the researchers and aspirants among this article's readers. The bare Act is a short document with long consequences, and it should be read in three layers. The first layer is the text as amended, in which the reader should mark the difference between the 1934 provisions, which speak in the language of functions and business, and the inserted chapters, which speak in the language of regimes. The second layer is the subordinate legislation the Act authorises: the regulations made by the Board under Section 58, the notifications by which targets, ratios and perimeters are fixed, and the master directions through which the Bank consolidates its instructions to regulated entities; most questions that present themselves as questions about the Act are in truth questions about this layer, and the answer will carry a date. The third layer is the record of practice: the Board's published statements, the committee's minutes, the Bank's annual report and its histories of itself, which are the sources from which the conventions described in this article are known. The reader who keeps the three layers separate will not make the characteristic errors of the public debate, which are to attribute to the Act what belongs to a notification, and to attribute to convention what the Act in fact commands.

The Reserve Bank of India Act 1934 therefore rewards being read as what it is: not a charter of independence and not an instrument of subordination, but a distribution of powers in which the government holds the appointments, the objective and the reserve power of direction, and the Bank holds the instruments, the information and the public record. The gap between what the statute permits the government to do and what governments have done under it is the space in which Indian central banking actually happens, and a reader who understands that gap understands more about the institution than any account that resolves it in either direction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Reserve Bank of India Act 1934 establish?

It establishes the Reserve Bank of India as a body corporate under Section 3, constitutes its Central Board and local boards, and confers the core central banking functions: the sole right to issue bank notes, the custody of scheduled banks' cash reserves, the business of banker to the Central and State Governments, the regulation of credit, and, through chapters added later, the regulation of non-banking financial companies and the statutory monetary policy framework of 2016. It is a statute, not a constitutional provision, and Parliament can and repeatedly has amended it by ordinary legislation.

### Q: Was the Reserve Bank always owned by the government?

No. The Bank was established in 1935 as a shareholders' institution, with its five crore rupees of capital held by private shareholders who elected part of its board. It remained privately owned through independence until the Reserve Bank (Transfer to Public Ownership) Act, 1948 transferred all shares to the Central Government, and the Bank entered public ownership on 1 January 1949. Its statutory powers, including the government's direction power under Section 7, were the same under both ownership forms, which is why independence in the Indian design has never rested on who holds the Bank's shares.

### Q: What is Section 7 of the RBI Act and has it ever been used?

Section 7(1) empowers the Central Government to give the Bank such directions as it may, after consultation with the Governor, consider necessary in the public interest. Consultations by reference to the section were initiated and publicly reported in 2018, during disagreements over liquidity, bank supervision frameworks and the Bank's reserves. No formal direction issued under the section has been documented for any year, and the careful position is that the power has been approached but never carried through to an issued direction.

### Q: What is the Monetary Policy Committee and who sits on it?

The Monetary Policy Committee is the statutory committee constituted under Section 45ZB that determines the policy rate required to achieve the inflation target. It has six members: the Governor of the Bank as chairperson ex officio, the Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, one officer of the Bank nominated by the Central Board, and three persons appointed by the Central Government for four-year terms without eligibility for reappointment. Decisions are by majority, and the Governor holds a casting vote on an equality of votes.

### Q: What is India's inflation target and who sets it?

The target is set by the Central Government in consultation with the Bank under Section 45ZA, determined once in every five years and published in the Official Gazette. The determination first notified in 2016 fixed consumer price inflation at four per cent, with an upper tolerance level of six per cent and a lower tolerance level of two per cent, and the same target was retained for the succeeding five-year period. The Bank's committee must set the policy rate to achieve the target the government has fixed.

### Q: What happens if the Reserve Bank misses the inflation target?

Failure is defined as average inflation remaining above the upper tolerance level, or below the lower tolerance level, for three consecutive quarters. Section 45ZN then requires the Bank to send the Central Government a report setting out the reasons for the failure, the remedial actions it proposes, and an estimate of the time within which the target will be achieved. The trigger first operated in 2022, when inflation stayed above the upper level for three consecutive quarters, and the Bank convened an additional committee meeting and sent the report.

### Q: Does the RBI Act regulate non-banking finance companies?

Yes. Chapter IIIB of the Act, inserted in 1963 and transformed by the amendment of 1997, brings non-banking financial companies within the Bank's regulatory perimeter. Section 45-IA makes registration with the Bank compulsory and prescribes a minimum net owned fund, and the chapter gives the Bank powers to issue directions, call for information, regulate deposit acceptance and cancel registration. Entities regulated under other statutes, such as insurance and securities market intermediaries, sit with their own regulators at the perimeter's edges.

### Q: Who has the sole right to issue currency notes in India?

The Reserve Bank has the sole right to issue bank notes in India under Section 22 of the Act, exercised through a separate Issue Department, and notes issued by the Bank are legal tender under Section 26. The single exception is the one-rupee denomination: one-rupee notes and all coins are issued by the Government of India rather than the Bank, which is why the one-rupee note is signed by the Finance Secretary and not by the Governor, and why every higher denomination carries the Governor's signature and promise.

### Q: Is the Reserve Bank of India a constitutional body?

No. Unlike the Election Commission or the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Reserve Bank is not created by the Constitution and enjoys no constitutional protection of tenure, salary or function. It is a statutory body, established by the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, and Parliament may amend its constitution, expand or curtail its functions, or restructure its governance by ordinary legislation. Every feature of the Bank's independence therefore rests on statute and convention rather than on constitutional entrenchment, and the Governor has neither the fixed tenure nor the removal protection that the Constitution gives to the holders of those other offices.

### Q: How is the Governor of the Reserve Bank appointed, and for how long?

The Governor is appointed by the Central Government under the management provisions in Chapter II of the Act. The term is fixed by the government at the time of appointment and may not exceed five years; the Governor is eligible for reappointment. The Act prescribes no minimum term and no procedure or grounds for removal, which is why the security of a Governor's tenure rests on convention and on the political cost of an early departure rather than on any legal protection the statute supplies.

### Q: Who serves on the Central Board of the Reserve Bank?

Section 8 constitutes the Central Board with the Governor, not more than four Deputy Governors, four directors nominated by the Central Government from the local boards for the western, eastern, northern and southern areas, ten directors nominated by the Central Government, and one government official nominated by the Central Government. The general superintendence of the Bank's affairs is vested in this Board by Section 7, subject to any direction the government may issue under that section after consulting the Governor.

### Q: What is the cash reserve ratio under Section 42 of the RBI Act?

Section 42 requires every scheduled bank to maintain with the Reserve Bank an average daily balance, the cash reserve ratio, computed as a percentage of its demand and time liabilities that the Bank specifies by notification. The balances earn no interest. The original Act bounded the Bank's discretion with a statutory floor and ceiling, which an amendment in 2006 removed, leaving the ratio entirely to the Bank's specification. The distinct statutory liquidity ratio is imposed by the Banking Regulation Act, not by this Act.

### Q: What business may the Reserve Bank transact under Section 17?

Section 17 is the Act's business licence. It permits the Bank, among other things, to accept money on account of the Central and State Governments without interest, to purchase, sell and rediscount bills of exchange and promissory notes, to deal in securities of governments and local authorities, to grant loans and advances against specified security, to buy and sell foreign exchange and gold, and to open accounts with other central banks. Business outside the section's list, such as lending directly to individuals or trading in immovable property, is closed to the Bank.

### Q: How does the RBI Act make the Reserve Bank the banker to governments?

Section 20 obliges the Bank to transact the general banking business of the Central Government, including making payments on its behalf, managing the public debt and issuing new loans when directed. Section 21 gives the Bank the right to act as banker to the Central Government throughout India, and Section 21A extends the arrangement to state governments by agreement, under which the states bank with the Reserve Bank. The Bank thus combines the roles of the government's banker, its debt manager and the monetary authority that sets the price of its borrowing.

### Q: Can notes issued by the Reserve Bank cease to be legal tender?

Yes. Section 26 makes bank notes legal tender for the sum expressed on them, and Section 26(2) provides that, on the recommendation of the Central Board, the Central Government may declare that any series of bank notes of any denomination shall cease to be legal tender, subject to the conditions the section prescribes. That provision was the statutory basis of the withdrawal of the existing five hundred and one thousand rupee notes in November 2016, which the Supreme Court later upheld against constitutional challenge.

### Q: What happens to the Reserve Bank's surplus profits under Section 47?

Section 47 requires the Bank first to make provision for bad and doubtful debts, depreciation in assets, contributions to staff and superannuation funds and other matters usually provided for by bankers, and then to pay the balance of its profits to the Central Government. The statute fixes the destination of the surplus but not its amount, which depends on the provisioning judgment. An expert committee under Bimal Jalan converted that judgment into a formula-based economic capital framework, on which the record transfer of 2019 was made.

### Q: Are the decisions of the Monetary Policy Committee binding on the Reserve Bank?

Yes. Section 45ZO provides that the decision of the Monetary Policy Committee shall be binding on the Bank. The committee determines the policy rate by majority vote, each member having one vote and the Governor holding a second or casting vote where votes are equal. The Bank then implements the rate the committee has chosen; it cannot substitute its own view of the appropriate rate, which is the feature that distinguishes the committee system from the earlier arrangement in which the Governor decided after receiving advice.

### Q: What did the Finance Act 2016 change in the Reserve Bank of India Act?

It inserted Chapter IIIF, Sections 45ZA to 45ZO, giving monetary policy a statutory framework for the first time: a government-determined inflation target fixed for five-year periods, a six-member Monetary Policy Committee to set the policy rate, published resolutions, minutes and votes, a half-yearly Monetary Policy Report, and a duty to report to the government if the target is missed for three consecutive quarters. It also amended the preamble to declare that the primary objective of monetary policy is price stability, kept in view with the objective of growth.

### Q: What role did the Hilton Young Commission play in the creation of the Reserve Bank?

The Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance, chaired by Edward Hilton Young and reporting in 1926, recommended the creation of a central bank for India, to be called the Reserve Bank, constituted as a shareholders' institution, which would take the note issue from the government and the government business from the Imperial Bank. Its report supplied the design the legislature eventually enacted, although bills introduced in 1927 and 1928 failed, and the Reserve Bank of India Act was passed only in March 1934.

### Q: Why is the one-rupee note signed by the Finance Secretary rather than the Governor?

Because the one-rupee note is not issued by the Reserve Bank. The Bank's sole right under Section 22 covers bank notes of two rupees and above, while one-rupee notes and all coinage remain an issue of the Government of India, distributed by the Bank as the government's agent. The signature on a note identifies the issuer liable for it, so Bank notes carry the Governor's signature and the one-rupee note, being a government liability, carries the signature of the Finance Secretary.
