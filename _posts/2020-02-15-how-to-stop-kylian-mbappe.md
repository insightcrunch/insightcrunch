Any plan for how to stop Kylian Mbappe that starts by naming a defender has already failed. The instruction usually given, which is to put the quickest full-back available on him and tell that full-back not to dive in, is not wrong so much as radically insufficient, because it answers a structural question with a personnel answer. The duel is decided before the duel begins. By the time the ball arrives at his feet, three things have already been settled: how much grass sits between him and your last line, whether his shoulders are open to your goal or closed toward the touchline, and whether a second defender can reach the contest before he reaches full speed. A plan that manages those three variables works with an ordinary full-back. A plan that ignores them fails with a fast one.

![How to stop Kylian Mbappe, a space-management defensive counter-plan - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-107.webp)

This blueprint therefore commits to something specific and pays for it openly. It keeps the runway short, forces the reception to happen with his body turned rather than open, and guarantees that a second defender arrives inside the first three strides. In exchange it concedes possession in front of the block, territory in the middle third, crosses from deeper and wider positions than the attacking side would like, and shots from outside the penalty area. Those are real concessions and they will produce real chances against you. They are the price of removing the one chance that beats you almost every time it occurs, which is a forward with an open body, thirty metres of grass, and a single defender to beat.

## The plan in one sentence, and the price on the ticket

Defend the space he wants rather than the man who wants it, hold your last line at a depth that keeps the runway under roughly fifteen metres, make your first defender an angle rather than a tackler, and put your nearest midfielder on the inside diagonal so every wide duel is arithmetically a two on one. That is the plan. Everything after this paragraph is detail, sequencing, and the honest accounting of what it costs.

The accounting matters because most published answers to this problem quietly refuse to pay. They describe a defensive scheme that stops the depth run, stops the cut inside, stops the transition, keeps the ball, and presses high, and a scheme that does all of those things does not exist. Every state that removes his runway hands something back. Sitting deeper removes the space behind and simultaneously invites the wide reception and the low cutback that his teams are built around. Pressing high shortens the pitch in front of the ball and lengthens it behind. Doubling the wide duel guarantees numerical safety on one flank and guarantees a free player on the other. A blueprint is only useful once it says which of these payments it is making, and then designs the rest of the team around covering that specific bill.

The concession this plan makes is deliberate and it should be stated to players in exactly these terms before kick-off: we will let them have the ball in front of us, we will let them cross from thirty-five metres, and we will not chase the game in their half until the scoreline demands it. What we will not do, at any point in ninety minutes, is allow a situation in which he receives facing our goal with one defender to beat and grass behind that defender. Every rule that follows exists to make that sentence true.

## Why stopping Kylian Mbappe is a space question rather than a marking question

Marking is a relationship between a defender and a body. It assumes the dangerous moment is contact, and that if you are close enough at the moment of contact, you have done your job. Against a large number of forwards this assumption holds well enough to plan around. Against this one it collapses, because the dangerous moment happens before contact, and closeness at the moment of contact is frequently the thing that creates the danger rather than the thing that prevents it.

Consider what a tight marking assignment actually produces. A full-back told to stay within two metres of his man for ninety minutes will spend the match with his eyes on that man and his body oriented toward him. When the pass is played into feet, the marker is close, which sounds like success, but the reception happens with the marker's momentum already committed toward the ball and with no cover behind. One touch away from the pressure, a body feint, and the marker is beaten by a metre with nobody behind him. Tight marking against an acceleration forward does not reduce the number of one on one situations. It manufactures them in the most dangerous possible location, which is the moment the defender's weight is on his front foot.

The alternative frame is to treat him as a set of conditions rather than a person. He is dangerous when he receives with space ahead; he is manageable when he receives with a defender goal-side and a second defender inside; he is close to neutral when he receives moving away from goal with his back to the pitch. Those are three different opponents wearing the same shirt, and which one you face is very largely under your control. That is the argument for planning conditions rather than assignments, and it is the reason this blueprint spends more paragraphs on the position of your holding midfielder than on the identity of your right-back.

The same logic sits under the general treatment of forward types in the guide to [how defensive plans are structured against elite strikers as a category](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/), which sorts forwards by the mechanism of their threat rather than by reputation. The classification that matters here is depth runner with a wide starting position, and the wide starting position is what makes this plan different from the plan against a purely central runner.

### What actually makes Kylian Mbappe dangerous?

Space ahead of him at the moment he receives, and an open body when he receives it. His acceleration turns a small advantage into a decisive one within three strides, so a defender level at the reception is already losing. Remove the grass or close the body angle and he becomes an ordinary wide problem.

That answer needs unpacking, because it contains the whole plan in compressed form and because the two conditions are not equally easy to control. Grass is a collective variable. It is set by where your defensive line stands, which is a decision your centre-backs make together and which your coaching staff can rehearse to within a couple of metres. Body angle is an individual variable, set by where the nearest defender's shoulders point in the second before the pass arrives. Both are coachable. Neither requires a sprinter.

## The mechanism: three variables that settle the duel before it starts

A counter-plan is only as good as its model of the threat, so the model has to be built explicitly. His threat is a function of three variables, and each of the three is owned by a different part of your team. Understanding which part owns which variable is what turns a general instruction into a set of assignments a coach can actually deliver on a Friday.

### Variable one: the runway

The runway is the distance between the point where he receives the ball and the point where your last defender is standing. It is the single most predictive number in the entire contest, and it is almost never discussed, because television coverage frames these moments as duels between two men rather than as a race against a distance.

The reason the runway dominates is straightforward physics applied to football. A forward who wins the first three strides gains perhaps a metre. One metre of separation is not enough to produce a shooting chance if the space runs out in ten metres, because the covering defender's angle closes the gap before the finish. The same one metre, given thirty metres of grass, becomes four or five metres, because the advantage compounds across the length of the run while the defender is still turning and accelerating from a standing or backpedalling start. This is why the same duel, with the same two players, produces a routine defensive win in one part of the pitch and a goal in another. The players did not change. The runway did.

The runway is owned by the defensive line, which means it is owned by your centre-backs and, in practice, by whichever of them organizes the line. It is not owned by the full-back marking him, and this is the first place most plans go wrong. A full-back cannot shorten the runway by himself; he can only decide where inside that runway the contest begins.

### Variable two: the body angle at reception

The second variable is whether he receives with his chest and hips open toward your goal, or closed toward the touchline, or square with the pass arriving into the back foot. An open reception means the first touch can go forward into space and the acceleration phase begins immediately. A closed reception means the first touch has to travel across the body before it can go forward, which costs a fraction of a second and, more importantly, tells the defender exactly which side the ball is going.

Body angle is not decided by him alone. It is heavily influenced by where the nearest defender is standing at the moment the passer commits. A defender positioned directly behind him invites an open reception because there is no reason to close the body. A defender positioned on the inside shoulder, half a body outside, gives the receiver a choice between a closed reception on the touchline side or a first touch back into a defender's body. Most forwards, correctly, take the touchline option, which is the option the plan wants because the touchline is a second defender that never gets tired.

The subtlety here is that the useful position is not the one that looks tightest on a replay. It is the one that pre-decides the direction of the first touch. A defender two and a half metres away on the correct angle achieves more than a defender one metre away on the wrong one, and the reason coaches keep reaching for the tighter option is that the looser one looks passive from the touchline and looks catastrophic on the one occasion it is beaten.

### Variable three: time to the second defender

The third variable is how long it takes for a second defender to arrive at the contest, measured from the moment of reception. If the answer is under about two seconds, the wide duel is a two on one and the attacking side needs a combination or a mistake to break it. If the answer is over three seconds, it is a genuine one on one in open space, and one on one in open space against this profile of forward is a losing proposition often enough that no plan should be built on it.

This variable is owned by the midfield, and specifically by the nearest central midfielder to the ball. It is not owned by the covering centre-back, whose arrival is usually too late and comes from the wrong angle, and it is not owned by the winger tracking back, whose recovery run typically arrives from behind and therefore adds a body without adding a solution. Adding a body from behind changes nothing, because a defender arriving from behind cannot tackle without fouling and cannot cut the inside lane he has already run past.

Put those three variables together and the shape of the plan is fully determined. The line height sets the runway. The first defender's angle sets the body. The midfield's lateral position sets the arrival time. The identity of the full-back appears nowhere in that list, which is exactly the point.

## The fifteen-metre rule

Here is the claim this article advances, and the one worth taking away if nothing else survives: **the fifteen-metre rule** states that against an acceleration forward, the plan lives or dies on the length of the runway between the reception point and your last line, and every decision in the plan should be evaluated by whether it shortens that runway. Not whether it looks aggressive. Not whether it wins the ball back higher. Not whether it puts your best defender on their best attacker. Only whether it shortens the runway.

Fifteen metres is not a magic number and it should not be sold as one. It is the approximate distance over which a first-step advantage stays a first-step advantage rather than compounding into a decisive one. Inside that distance, a covering defender's angle usually gets there. Beyond it, the geometry starts to favour the runner, and by twenty-five or thirty metres the contest is effectively over at the moment of reception. Different squads will find their own working figure depending on how fast their centre-backs recover and how well their goalkeeper covers ground behind the line. The number is a coaching reference, not a law. What is not negotiable is the principle that the runway is the variable you are managing.

The rule has three practical consequences, and the third one is where most teams get it wrong.

The first consequence is that line height becomes an active, continuous decision rather than a pre-match setting. Your last line should be moving several metres forward and back throughout every possession the opponent has, tied to whether the player on the ball can look up and play forward. When the carrier's head is down or he is under pressure, the line steps up and the runway shrinks. When he is free, the line drops and the runway lengthens, but it lengthens deliberately, from a position where recovery is possible, rather than accidentally.

The second consequence is that every pressing decision has to be evaluated for what it does behind the press rather than in front of it. Sending your full-back forward to press their centre-back may win the ball high, and it also opens a channel that is precisely the shape of the run he wants. The press is not banned by this plan, but every press has to come with a named player whose job is to hold the runway short while the press happens.

The third consequence is the one that catches good coaches: shortening the runway by dropping very deep does not satisfy the rule, it evades it. A team that sits with its last line ten metres from its own goal has a runway of ten metres and has also handed over the entire middle third, which means the reception now happens in a wide area with the whole attacking side arriving into the box behind it. You have solved the depth run by converting the contest into a cross and cutback contest, which against a well-built attacking side is not a solution but a change of subject. The runway must be shortened by holding a controlled medium line and by pressure on the ball, not by retreat. That distinction is the difference between a plan and a hope, and the section on line height below sets out where the line actually goes.

## The shape and the line height that frame the plan

Shape is downstream of the three variables, not upstream of them. Any of the common defensive structures can satisfy the fifteen-metre rule and any of them can violate it, so the choice is made on which structure makes the required behaviours easiest for the players you actually have.

The default recommendation is a 4-4-2 mid block that becomes a 4-1-4-1 when the ball settles on the flank he occupies. The reason is not fashion; it is that this shape produces the two features the plan needs most, which are a genuine screening presence in front of the back four and a compact chain of four defenders whose spacing can be rehearsed. A back four holds the offside line more cleanly than a back five because the four are used to stepping together, and it keeps a midfielder available for the inside diagonal that turns the wide duel into a two on one. A 4-2-3-1 works equally well as long as one of the two holding players is explicitly assigned to the ball side rather than both floating.

The back five deserves separate treatment because it is the reflex answer and it is not automatically wrong. A 5-4-1 or a 5-3-2 gives you a wing-back who can engage and a centre-back who can slide across as immediate cover, and it therefore solves the second-defender problem structurally rather than by instruction. It also does something valuable against an attacking side that overloads one flank: it lets the far-side centre-back stay home rather than being dragged. What it costs is compactness in central midfield, where three players now have to cover the width that four used to cover, and it costs the offside line, because a back five with wing-backs is habitually deeper and therefore lengthens the runway. Use it when your midfield can hold a narrow shape without help, or when your full-backs are not capable of the retreating footwork this plan asks for. Do not use it as a comfort blanket, which is what it usually is.

What the plan will not accept is a high-line pressing structure without a rest-defense rule attached. A high line is compatible with the fifteen-metre rule in the ideal case, because if the press succeeds the reception never happens and if the line is high the runway is short. The problem is what happens on the two or three occasions per half when the press is played through cleanly. In that state you have a very long runway, a goalkeeper as the only cover, and a forward receiving in stride. A plan that produces a defensive win eighty-five times and a clean chance three times has not defended well; it has bought a very expensive lottery ticket.

### How high should a defensive line sit against Kylian Mbappe?

Roughly ten to fifteen metres inside your own half, and dynamically rather than at a fixed mark. Set depth by pressure on the ball: step up when the carrier cannot look up, drop five to eight metres the moment he can. That keeps the runway short without surrendering the middle third.

The dynamic part is what separates teams who defend this well from teams who defend it badly, and it is the single most trainable element in the whole blueprint. A static line is readable. If your last line stands on the same reference for ninety minutes, an intelligent attacking side simply times the run to that reference, and the timing of the pass does the rest. A line that moves with the pressure on the ball is not readable in the same way, because the reference keeps moving and the runner has to solve a new geometry each time.

The mechanics are simple enough to teach in one session. The deepest centre-back calls the line. When the ball is being passed sideways or backwards, or when the carrier is being pressed and cannot lift his head, the line steps forward in unison, five metres at a walk or a jog, and the midfield line steps with it to keep the block compact. When the carrier turns and faces forward with time, the line drops, but it drops facing the ball with the body opened toward the touchline so that the drop can convert into a recovery sprint without a change of foot. The error to eliminate is the drop that becomes a backpedal with square hips, because a defender with square hips has to take an extra step to turn, and that extra step is exactly the advantage the plan is trying to deny.

The second mechanic is the rule for what happens when a ball is played over the top into the space. The line does not chase the ball; the line drops to the near post line and the goalkeeper takes anything that crosses into the area he can reach. That requires a goalkeeper who defends space rather than a goalkeeper who defends a goal line, and if you do not have one, the line has to be five metres deeper than it otherwise would be, which is a real cost and should be recognized as one.

## The first defender: what the full-back is actually being asked to do

The individual assignment is where most of the coaching time goes and where the least useful advice circulates. The instruction "do not dive in" is correct and useless, because it tells the defender what not to do without giving him the thing to do instead. The job has four components, and each of them is measurable in training.

The first component is the starting angle. The defender's inside shoulder should be roughly in line with the receiver's inside shoulder, with the defender's body positioned a half-body width to the inside of the direct line between the receiver and the goal. Put plainly, he stands slightly inside, not level and not outside. This is the position that pre-decides the direction of the first touch. From here, the touchline side is open and the inside is closed by the defender's own body, which means the reception happens facing the touchline in the large majority of cases. That single positional habit removes more danger than any amount of speed, because it converts an open reception into a closed one, and a closed reception is a completely different problem.

The second component is the arrival distance. The defender should be arriving at roughly two and a half to three metres, not at one metre and not at five. At one metre the defender is inside the range where a body feint works and where his own momentum is a liability. At five metres the receiver has time to take a touch forward into stride, at which point the contest is over. Two and a half to three metres is the band where the defender can still adjust his feet to whichever way the touch goes, and where the receiver has to beat him with a genuine action rather than with a change of pace into space that already existed.

The third component is the footwork. The defender retreats with the body opened at roughly forty-five degrees, leading with the inside foot, taking short steps and never crossing his feet. The purpose of the retreat is not to avoid the duel; it is to keep the duel happening at a constant distance while the situation improves. Every metre the carrier travels toward your goal is a metre closer to your cover, a metre closer to the touchline, and a metre closer to the moment the second defender arrives. Delay is not a passive outcome in this plan. Delay is the product the full-back is manufacturing.

The fourth component is the trigger to actually engage. There are exactly two moments worth committing to a tackle or a shoulder contact against a carrier of this type. The first is the moment the touch is too heavy and the ball leaves the carrier's feet by more than a stride, which is a window of perhaps a third of a second and which requires the defender to be on balance to exploit. The second is the moment the carrier is forced onto the touchline with the second defender arriving on the inside, at which point the defender can step in confident that the only escape route is backwards. Outside those two moments, the defender's job is to hold his angle and his distance. Committing at any other time is not aggression; it is a gift.

### Why is the geometry not the same on both flanks?

Because footedness makes the two routes unequal. A right-footed forward starting from the left shoots after cutting inside, so the inside route is the dangerous one and the touchline route is the cheap one. From the right, the same cut inside produces passes rather than shots, which reverses the priority.

That conditional is the whole answer and it is worth spelling out, because the general debate about showing inside or outside is usually conducted as if it were a matter of coaching philosophy rather than a matter of what is behind the defender. From a left-sided starting position, the inside route leads toward the top of the penalty area on his stronger foot, and it leads through the exact zone your holding midfielder should be occupying. The outside route leads toward the byline, where the end product is a cross or a cutback rather than a shot, and where a second defender can arrive with the touchline doing half the work.

So the default is to show outside. The condition attached is that the inside lane must be closed by a body, not by hope. If your midfield has been pulled across and the lane is open, showing outside is an invitation, and the correct adjustment is for the full-back to square up and hold a neutral position that defends both routes badly rather than one route excellently, buying the half-second the midfielder needs to recover. That is an ugly compromise and it should be recognized as a failure state rather than a plan, which is why the midfield positioning rules in the next section carry more weight than they appear to.

The picture inverts when he starts from the right or comes inside from a central position. From the right, cutting inside puts the ball on his weaker side for the shot but opens the through-ball and the pull-back, and the defender is better served showing him toward the byline on his right and accepting the near-post cross. When he starts centrally, the concepts of inside and outside stop applying and the question becomes which centre-back steps, which is treated separately below. A fuller treatment of the flank-by-flank geometry, including the asymmetry between a left-footed opponent on the right and a right-footed opponent on the left, sits in the broader guide to [defending elite wide forwards as a category](/2021/04/01/stopping-elite-wingers-complete-guide/).

## The second defender: turning a one on one into a two on one

This is the part of the plan that most teams believe they are doing and are not. Almost everyone agrees that you should not leave a defender isolated against a forward of this profile. Very few teams specify who the second defender is, where he comes from, and when he commits, and without those three specifications the second defender arrives late, from behind, and to no effect.

The second defender is the nearest central midfielder. Not the covering centre-back, and not the winger recovering from in front. The reason is angle. The centre-back arriving from inside has to leave the penalty area and the last line simultaneously, which either strands your remaining centre-back one against one or drags the whole line across and opens the far post. The recovering winger arrives from behind the play, where he can neither tackle without fouling nor cut off a lane that the carrier has already passed. The central midfielder arriving from an inside starting position is the only body that can occupy the inside lane before the carrier gets there, which is the whole objective.

The angle of arrival is inside and slightly ahead, never square and never from behind. The midfielder's job is not to tackle. His job is to stand in the lane between the carrier and the goal at a distance of three or four metres, so that the carrier's inside route is visibly blocked and the only remaining option is the touchline route into the full-back who is already angled to defend it. Two defenders, both defending the same route, is a wasted body. Two defenders defending the two different routes is the entire point.

The timing is defined by the ball rather than by the man. The midfielder commits to the inside lane when the carrier takes his second touch, which is the moment the direction of the attack is legible. Committing on the first touch is too early, because the first touch can still be a lay-off or a change of direction and the midfielder gets eliminated by a pass. Committing on the third touch is too late, because by then the carrier is at speed and a midfielder arriving at speed from an angle cannot change direction to match him.

The centre-back's step-or-drop decision belongs in the same conversation. The near-side centre-back has a live choice as soon as the ball goes wide: step out to become the cover for the full-back, or hold the line and stay compact with his partner. The rule this plan uses is that the centre-back steps out only when the ball is in front of the full-back and travelling toward the touchline, and holds in every other state. The reason is that a centre-back who steps early converts a wide problem into a central one by opening the space he vacated, and that space is precisely where the second forward is arriving. When the near-side centre-back does step, the far-side centre-back and the far-side full-back slide across and the far-side full-back becomes the third centre-back for the duration, which is a rehearsed movement and not an improvisation.

The last element is the cover shadow, which is a term used loosely enough that it needs a precise definition here. The cover shadow is the passing lane a defender closes with his body position while he is engaged in something else. For the holding midfielder in this plan, the shadow that matters is the lane from the wide carrier to the top of the penalty area, because that is the lane the cut-inside pass and the pull-back both travel through. He is not marking a player in that zone. He is standing in the lane, which is a different instruction and produces a different body position.

## The plan phase by phase: the space-denial ladder

The blueprint is easier to deliver as a set of match states than as a set of general principles, because players do not experience a match as principles. They experience it as a sequence of situations, each with a trigger that moves them into the next one. What follows is the sequenced version, and it is the findable artifact of this article: **the space-denial ladder against a running forward**, a five-state model with the required behaviour in each state and the trigger that moves the team between states.

| State | Runway to last line | First defender's angle | Cover defender's position | Trigger to change state |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Ball far, opponent building in their own third | 25 to 30 metres, line on the halfway reference | Full-back tucked inside, 8 to 10 metres off the touchline, watching ball not man | Holding midfielder central, screening the space between the lines | Ball travels into the middle third on the far side, or a forward pass is played toward his channel |
| 2. Ball in the middle third, far side | 18 to 22 metres, line stepped up with the block | Full-back at the edge of the box width, body half open to the pitch | Holding midfielder shifted one lane toward the ball, still inside the far centre-back's shadow | Ball switched to his side, or a diagonal pass is loaded |
| 3. Ball near, his side, feet reception | 12 to 15 metres, line dropped and tied to ball pressure | Inside shoulder aligned, half a body inside, arriving at 2.5 to 3 metres | Holding midfielder in the inside lane, 3 to 4 metres inside the duel, ahead of the ball | Carrier turns toward the byline, or the pass goes backwards |
| 4. Transition, possession lost in the opposition half | Whatever exists, minimize by delay rather than by depth | Nearest player presses the passer immediately, not the runner | Rest-defense pair holds the central lane and the offside line, full-backs recover inside the posts | Ball is recovered, the pass is forced backwards, or the block is reset |
| 5. Settled low block, ball in the final third | 6 to 10 metres, line on the edge of the penalty area | Full-back defends the cross line, not the touchline; second defender doubles | Centre-backs occupy the near post and the penalty spot, midfield screens the cutback lane | Ball cleared beyond the middle third, or possession regained |

The ladder is read downward when you are losing control of the match and upward when you are regaining it, and the states are not moral. State five is not a failure and state one is not a success. The failure mode is being in the wrong state for the ball's location, and the most common version of that failure is a team sitting in state five while the ball is still in state two, which surrenders forty metres for no defensive gain whatsoever.

Each state deserves its own paragraph of coaching detail, because the transitions between them are where matches are lost.

In state one, with the opponent building from their own third, the temptation is to do nothing and the correct behaviour is to do something specific: the ball-side full-back tucks inside rather than standing on the touchline. The reason is that the first pass into his channel is almost always a diagonal from a centre-back or a deep midfielder, and a full-back who starts eight to ten metres inside can defend the diagonal by intercepting or by arriving on the correct angle, whereas a full-back who starts on the touchline can only defend it by racing the receiver to a ball that was played to arrive in front of him. Starting wide feels responsible and defends the wrong pass.

In state two, with the ball in the middle third on the far side, the whole block steps up. This is the state in which the runway is most easily shortened at no cost, because the ball is far from your goal and the danger of a ball over the top is at its lowest. Teams routinely waste this state by sitting deep out of habit, which means that when the ball arrives on his side they are already at the bottom of the ladder with nowhere left to go.

State three is the contest the article exists to solve, and it has been covered in the two preceding sections. The one addition is the ball-far centre-back's job during it: he does not ball-watch, he holds the far post line and counts the far-side runner, because the pull-back to the back post is the pattern that punishes a team whose entire defensive attention has gone to one flank.

State four is a separate discipline and gets its own section below.

State five, the settled low block with the ball in the final third, changes the full-back's job completely. He is no longer defending the touchline route, because there is nothing behind him worth protecting. He is defending the cross, which means closing the space in front of the carrier's kicking foot rather than the space behind, and doing so with the second defender arriving to prevent the cut inside. The mistake in this state is the full-back who keeps retreating out of habit and ends up defending a cross from four metres away with his weight on his heels.

## Rest defense: the phase where the plan is actually won

The most important defensive work against a forward of this profile happens while your team has the ball. This is not a paradox and it is not a slogan. It follows directly from the fifteen-metre rule. The longest runways in any match occur immediately after a turnover in the opposition half, because at that moment your defensive line is high, your midfield is ahead of the ball, and the space between your last defender and your goalkeeper is at its maximum. If you never manage that phase, everything else in this blueprint is decoration.

Rest defense is the shape your team holds while attacking, designed for the moment the ball is lost. Against this threat it has three non-negotiable rules.

The first is the plus-one rule. Whatever number of forwards the opponent leaves upfield during your possession, you keep that number plus one behind the ball at all times, and the plus one is a genuine defender rather than a midfielder who happens to be deep. If they leave him high on his own, you keep two. If they leave him plus a supporting runner, you keep three. This sounds obvious and is routinely broken in the final ten minutes of a drawn match, which is precisely when the runway is longest and the consequences are largest.

The second is the no-simultaneous-full-backs rule. Both full-backs do not advance past the halfway line at the same time, ever, and the one who stays is the one on the side he occupies. This costs you a body in attack and it removes the single most common origin of a long-runway transition, which is a switch of play into the channel a full-back has just vacated with nobody covering it.

The third is the counter-press window. When the ball is lost in the opposition half, the two or three nearest players press the ball immediately for a strictly limited window of about two seconds, and if the ball is not recovered or the pass is not forced backwards within that window, they stop pressing and run back. The rule exists because a counter-press that continues past its useful life is worse than no counter-press at all: it commits bodies to a lost cause and leaves the runway open behind them. Give it two seconds and a hard stop.

Within that window, the target of the press is the passer, not the runner. This is the single most useful transition instruction in the plan and it is counterintuitive enough that it needs to be drilled rather than explained. When possession changes, the natural instinct of every player is to look at the dangerous forward and start running toward him. That instinct produces a converging group of defenders arriving at a player who has not received the ball yet, and it leaves the man about to play the pass entirely free to choose the moment and the weight of it. Pressing the passer removes the pass, which removes the run. Chasing the runner concedes the pass and then loses the race.

The complementary instruction is for the deepest defenders, whose job in the first two seconds is not to sprint toward the ball but to hold the offside line and to stay level with one another. A transition that begins with a defensive line breaking apart at different speeds creates the seam that a well-timed run attacks. Holding the line for two seconds, then dropping together, is slower in appearance and faster in effect.

## The six seconds after you lose it

Because state four is where the biggest chances originate, it is worth breaking down as a sequence rather than as a principle.

In the first second, the nearest player to the ball closes the passer's face and takes away the forward pass with his body rather than lunging for the ball. The second nearest player takes the nearest supporting option. Nobody else moves toward the ball. Simultaneously, the two centre-backs check their relationship to each other and to the halfway line, and the ball-side full-back begins recovering on a diagonal toward his own penalty spot rather than toward the touchline. Recovering toward the penalty spot is a rule worth stating explicitly, because full-backs instinctively recover along the touchline, which keeps them in their zone and out of the play.

In seconds two and three, the outcome of the press determines the branch. If the pass forward has been prevented, the block resets and the team climbs back up the ladder to state two. If the pass has been made, the entire team stops pressing and runs, and the deepest defenders now have one job, which is to delay the carrier and steer him away from the middle rather than to win the ball. The delay is worth more than the tackle, because delay allows the eight players who are behind the ball to get in front of it.

In seconds four to six, the recovering players arrive, and the priority order for who fills which space matters. The first recovering midfielder takes the central lane in front of the box, not the ball. The second takes the far-side runner. Only the third goes to help the ball. Filling the central lane first is what turns a three on two into a three on three, and a three on three against a set defensive shape is a manageable situation rather than an emergency.

Two further points about this phase deserve honesty rather than tidiness. The first is that the tactical foul is a real tool in this state, it is used by every serious team in the world, and it carries a cost in cards and in free-kick positions that has to be weighed rather than pretended away. If it is going to be used, it should be assigned in advance to a specific player in a specific zone, so that it happens early, high, and cleanly rather than late, deep, and dangerously. Assigning it to the wrong player in the wrong zone converts a yellow card into a red one and a free kick into a penalty. The second point is that the plan must include the possibility that the transition simply beats you. It will happen once or twice per match against a good attacking side. The measure of the plan is not whether it produces zero transitions, which is unattainable, but whether the transitions that do occur begin in front of your rest-defense shape rather than behind it.

## The goalkeeper's contribution to the blueprint

A goalkeeper who defends space is worth several metres of line height, which makes him a direct contributor to the fifteen-metre rule rather than a last resort behind it. Three specific behaviours matter.

The first is starting position. In states one and two, the goalkeeper's default position is on the edge of his penalty area or a couple of metres outside it, angled so that the first step toward any ball played over the top is a forward step rather than a turn. That position is what allows the back line to hold five metres higher than it otherwise could, and five metres of line height is exactly the difference between a runway that can be defended and one that cannot.

The second is the decision rule for balls played into the space behind. The rule is simple and should be absolute: the goalkeeper takes everything he can reach with a straight-line run and nothing he cannot. Hesitation is the failure mode, not aggression, because a goalkeeper who starts and stops has removed himself from the situation while also removing the defender's certainty. The call has to be loud and early, and the defenders have to be trained to peel away rather than to compete for the ball once the call comes.

The third is the one on one itself, which is the situation the whole plan is designed to prevent and which will nonetheless occur. Against a forward who accelerates onto the ball, the goalkeeper's best approach is to close ground quickly while the carrier's head is down on his touch, then set his feet and stay tall as the carrier lifts his head. Staying tall matters because a forward at speed with a defender recovering behind him is under time pressure and will often take the first available option; a tall, set goalkeeper makes the near-post and the lifted finish both harder, and the shot that remains is across the body into the far corner, which is the lowest-percentage finish of the three. Going to ground early converts a difficult finish into an easy one by removing the goalkeeper's ability to react.

Angle work matters more against a right-footed forward cutting in from the left, because the geometry is asymmetric. Coming in from that side onto his right foot, the shooting angle opens toward the far post as he travels, which means the goalkeeper's near post has to be covered early and then his weight has to stay balanced for the far-side shot. Over-committing to the near post at the moment the carrier enters the box is the most common goalkeeping error against this pattern, because it turns a covered near post into a wide-open far one at the exact moment the shot is taken.

## Set pieces and the two dead-ball dimensions

Set pieces enter this blueprint from two directions, and the second one is more important than the first.

Defending the opponent's attacking set pieces is the smaller issue, because aerial work in the box is not the primary weapon of the forward this plan is built around. The relevant point is not how to mark him at a corner but where he goes when the corner is cleared. Against a side with a fast forward, the defensive corner is a transition risk dressed as a set piece, and the answer is to keep a genuine outlet high and to instruct the deepest defender not to push out into a line until the ball has cleared the penalty area. Teams that push out reflexively at a corner concede the exact geometry the whole plan exists to prevent, with the additional handicap that half the team is running the wrong way.

Your own attacking set pieces are the larger issue. This is the phase in which teams with the ball voluntarily create their own longest runways, by sending six players into the opposition box and leaving two defenders against one forward with sixty metres of open pitch. The rule this plan uses is that at any attacking corner or wide free kick, you keep three players behind the ball when they leave one forward high: two defenders occupying the central lane and a third player, usually the holding midfielder, positioned wide of the box on the side the clearance is most likely to travel toward. The third player is not there to defend; he is there to make the first clearance a contest rather than a free launch into space.

The trade is a real one. Three players held back is one fewer attacking body in the box and it costs you goals over a season. It is still the correct trade against this specific opponent, because the alternative is not a lost duel in your own box but a clean run at your goal from your own attacking third, which converts at a far higher rate. Say the trade out loud to the players so that the attacking group understands why they are one short.

The same accounting applies to your own throw-ins and goal kicks in the opposition half, both of which are commonly treated as safe moments and are not. A short goal kick played under pressure is the highest-runway turnover available in football, and against a side built to counter it should be attempted only when the receiving player has a genuine escape option facing forward.

## Fallbacks: what to do when the first plan fails

Every counter-plan needs an explicit failure ladder, because the version that fails is not the version that was drilled but the version that survived contact with an opponent who is also good at this. There are four fallbacks, in the order they should be reached for.

The first fallback, when he is receiving too often with an open body, is to change the midfield reference rather than the defensive one. Move the ball-side central midfielder five metres wider and instruct him to sit on the inside lane permanently, accepting that the opponent's deep midfielder is now free. You have traded a free player in a harmless zone for a closed lane in a dangerous one, and that is a good trade in almost every match state. If the free deep midfielder starts hurting you, the trade needs revisiting, but that is a different problem with a longer fuse.

The second fallback, when the wide duel is being lost repeatedly with the runway short, is to double the flank structurally rather than by instruction. This usually means moving to a back five with the near-side wing-back engaging and the wide centre-back covering, which guarantees the second defender without relying on a midfielder arriving in time. The cost is central compactness and it should be paid only when the wide duel is genuinely the source of the danger, which is a question the touchline should be able to answer by half time.

The third fallback, when the problem is the supply rather than the duel, is to stop defending the receiver and start defending the passer. Assign a man-oriented job to whichever player is repeatedly finding him: usually a deep-lying playmaker or a full-back with time to lift his head. The player doing the marking does not follow him everywhere; he simply denies the forward pass in the specific zone from which the ball is being launched. Cutting the supply is often easier than cutting the run, and it is the adjustment that most reliably changes a match at half time.

The fourth fallback is the state-five retreat, and it should be treated as a last resort rather than a plan. Dropping the whole block to the edge of the penalty area does shorten the runway to almost nothing, and it does convert the contest into a crossing and cutback contest that a well-drilled defensive side can survive for a period. It is a legitimate way to hold a lead for fifteen minutes. It is not a way to play a match, because it hands over the entire pitch and because the resulting sustained pressure produces set pieces, second balls, and eventually a chance. Use it as an ending, not as an approach.

## The defender profiles that fit this plan

The blueprint is deliberately built so that it does not require a specific athlete, but selection still matters, and the profile that fits is not always the one a coach reaches for. Four distinct full-back types can execute this job, and each has a different failure mode.

The recovery type is the fast full-back with average positional judgment. He is the popular choice and he is a reasonable one, provided the plan around him is intact. His value is that he raises the tolerance for error: when the line is caught fifteen metres too high or the midfielder is late, he can still make the situation survivable. His failure mode is that his own pace tempts him into positions the plan forbids, because a defender who knows he can recover will engage earlier, stand tighter, and gamble on interceptions. Coach him toward patience explicitly, and be aware that the moment his legs go, late in a match or late in a season, the plan around him has to tighten to compensate.

The positional type is the full-back with modest pace and excellent starting positions. He is usually the better fit, and coaches habitually underrate him for this specific assignment because the highlight of a fast defender catching a fast forward is memorable and the highlight of a slow defender never being in a race does not exist. His value is that he consistently produces the closed reception, which removes the race before it starts. His failure mode is the single moment of misjudgment, which for him is unrecoverable, so he needs the covering centre-back and the screening midfielder to be genuinely disciplined rather than nominally present.

The physical type is the strong, aggressive full-back who wins contact duels. He is the least well suited of the four to the wide isolation but he is valuable in the state-five low block, where the contest becomes a series of close-range battles on the edge of the box and where strength in the first contact is worth more than pace. Consider him as a second-half option once the match has settled into a defensive phase rather than as a starter against an opponent who will attack space early.

The converted type is a centre-back playing at full-back, which is a genuine option against a forward who starts wide and cuts inside, and one that serious teams use more often than the general discussion acknowledges. His value is that he defends the inside route naturally and does not panic in a one on one because he has spent his career in them. His failure mode is the width he cannot cover when the ball is switched away from him and the attacking side's wide player stays high. Use him when the opponent attacks predominantly through one flank and when you are content to defend the far side with a compact block rather than with a full-back covering ground.

The centre-back question is simpler and comes down to a single trait, which is the willingness and ability to defend the space behind rather than the man in front. A centre-back who is comfortable defending a large area behind him, who turns cleanly, and who reads the pass early is worth more in this plan than a dominant aerial defender who prefers to feel a body. If you have one of each, the front-foot reader plays on the side he occupies and the aerial defender plays on the other, where the crosses will be arriving.

The holding midfielder is the least discussed and most important selection in the whole plan. The requirement is not ball-winning and not passing range; it is lateral discipline, which means the willingness to move five metres sideways and stand in an empty lane while nothing appears to be happening. Players who need to be involved to feel useful do this badly. Players who accept a positional job and hold it for ninety minutes do it well, and the difference between the two is worth more to this blueprint than the difference between the best and worst full-back in your squad.

## Game state, fatigue, and the substitutions to plan for

A defensive plan that ignores the scoreboard is not a plan, because the pressure that breaks this blueprint is almost never tactical. It is emotional, and it arrives in three predictable forms.

When you are leading, the danger is the drift downward. A team that is a goal ahead retreats gradually, five metres at a time, without any decision being taken. The block ends up in state five with the ball in state two, the runway is short but the entire pitch is conceded, and the sustained pressure that follows produces the equalizer. The counter to it is a named reference and a loud voice: the deepest defender calls the line back up every time the ball goes backwards, and the coaching staff intervene from the touchline the moment the block sags. If you intend to defend deep to protect a lead, decide it and announce it rather than letting it happen by accident, and pair it with a genuine outlet so that clearances have a destination.

When you are level in the closing stages, the danger is the opposite. A team pushing for a winner sends its full-backs forward together, empties the rest-defense shape, and hands over the exact conditions the plan exists to prevent. This is the single most common way that a good defensive performance turns into a defeat against a counter-attacking side. The no-simultaneous-full-backs rule holds until the eightieth minute in every match state; after that, if you break it, break it deliberately, with the holding midfielder dropping into the vacated position rather than joining the attack.

When you are behind, the plan has to be abandoned in a controlled way rather than dissolved. Pushing the line up and pressing higher is the correct response to a deficit, and it lengthens the runway by definition. The controlled version keeps two players in the rest-defense shape and accepts long transitions rather than pretending they will not happen; the uncontrolled version pushes everybody and concedes a second goal within ten minutes, which is how a one-goal defeat becomes a three-goal one.

Fatigue deserves separate attention because it degrades the plan unevenly. The dynamic line degrades first, because stepping up requires a jog forward and tired defenders stop making the effort. The midfielder's inside-lane discipline degrades second, because holding a position with no ball involvement is mentally expensive. The full-back's angle degrades last, because it is a habit rather than an effort. That ordering tells you what to substitute and when: the holding midfielder is the most valuable defensive change you can make around the seventieth minute, and replacing him with a fresh player who will hold a lane is usually worth more than replacing a tiring full-back.

Prepare for the opponent's changes too. Against a side built around a running forward, the two adjustments to expect are an additional wide player introduced to stretch your block laterally, and a change of the forward's starting position from wide to central to attack a tiring line directly. The first is answered by dropping your wide midfielder alongside your full-back and accepting a five-man defensive line for a period. The second is answered by making the near-side centre-back the primary defender, tightening the pair, and being far more careful about stepping. Both should be rehearsed rather than improvised, and both should be in the pre-match briefing as expected events rather than surprises.

## Two attacking structures, two versions of the same problem

The forward is only half of the problem. The other half is the structure around him, and the same individual threat arrives in two quite different packages depending on how his team is built. A plan that does not distinguish them will be correct in general and wrong on the day.

The first package is the possession structure. Here his team holds the ball, circulates it patiently across your block, and looks for the moment your defensive line has shifted far enough across that the far-side channel opens. The dangerous pass is the switch, or the diagonal from the opposite half-space, and the reception happens against a defence that is organized but stretched. The runway in this version is created laterally rather than vertically: the space exists because your block has moved sideways, not because your line is high.

Against the possession package, the priority is the far-side full-back's discipline. He does not follow the ball across; he holds a position roughly in line with the far post and defends the switch by being there rather than by arriving. That means your block accepts being narrow on the ball side and slightly overloaded there, trading a wide overload in a non-scoring area for the removal of the diagonal that actually hurts. The second priority is the timing of the block's lateral shifts, which should be one pass behind the ball rather than one pass ahead of it, because a block that anticipates the switch is a block that can be manipulated into shifting early.

The second package is the direct structure, where his team defends deeper, invites pressure, and looks to release him in transition with one or two passes. Here the runway is created vertically, by your own team's position, and the dangerous pass is the first one after the turnover. The reception happens against a defence that is not stretched but is high and facing the wrong way.

Against the direct package, everything that matters happens while you have the ball. The block's shape when defending is almost irrelevant, because you will rarely be in a settled defensive shape; what decides the match is your rest defense, your counter-press window, and your discipline about full-backs. Teams that lose to this package almost always lose it during their own best spell of possession, which is why the tape review afterwards is so confusing to the players: they remember dominating and they cannot explain the scoreline.

Identifying which package you face is a pre-match question with a simple test. Watch where the opponent's defensive line sits when they do not have the ball, and watch how many passes elapse between their recovery and their first forward pass. A high line and patient circulation means the possession package and a lateral problem. A deeper line and a forward pass within two touches means the direct package and a vertical problem. Most sides are a mixture, and the useful output is not a label but a proportion, because it tells you where to spend your preparation time.

There is a third case worth naming, which is the match where the opponent switches packages at half time. This happens most often when a possession side has been frustrated for forty-five minutes and decides to invite pressure instead. The signal is the opponent's defensive line dropping ten metres and their forward starting deeper and wider. If you see it, the response is not a tactical change but an instruction: your full-backs stop advancing and your rest-defense pair becomes a trio. Teams that miss this transition concede within fifteen minutes of the restart with a numbing regularity, and they concede it in a phase they thought they were controlling.

## The dedicated man-marking option, and when it is worth the cost

Somewhere in the preparation of any match like this, someone suggests putting one player on him for ninety minutes and forgetting about everything else. The suggestion deserves a real answer rather than a reflexive dismissal, because it has worked historically and because the reasons it usually fails are specific enough to be instructive.

A dedicated man-marking assignment does one thing extremely well: it removes the free reception. If a defender is responsible for a single opponent everywhere on the pitch, that opponent never receives unattended, and the body-angle variable is solved permanently rather than situationally. That is a genuine gain and it is why the approach persists.

It also does three things badly. It hands the opponent control of your shape, because wherever the marked player walks, your defender follows, and a forward who drifts into midfield or across to the far touchline can drag a defender out of the structure without touching the ball. It creates a permanent one on one, which against this profile of forward is the exact contest the rest of this blueprint is designed to avoid. And it removes the marker from every other defensive job, so a back four with one man-marker is functionally a back three with a passenger.

The version that works is a compromise: zonal responsibility with a man-oriented reference, in which the near-side full-back treats him as his primary concern while the ball is on that side and hands him off the moment he crosses into a teammate's zone. The handoff is the part that needs rehearsing, because it fails silently. Two defenders each believing the other has him is worse than either arrangement done cleanly, and the fix is a verbal convention rather than a tactical one: the player taking over calls it, the player releasing acknowledges it, and the centre-back arbitrates when nobody speaks.

The full man-marking assignment becomes worth its cost in exactly two situations. The first is when your midfield cannot be trusted to hold the inside lane, in which case you are choosing between two flawed structures and the man-marker at least guarantees pressure at the reception. The second is late in a match you are leading, when a fresh defender introduced specifically to follow him for twenty minutes removes the free reception at a moment when your block is tired and your line is sagging. Outside those two cases, the zonal plan with a man-oriented reference produces better outcomes with fewer players committed, and it does not surrender the shape of your team to the movement of one opponent.

## What this plan concedes on purpose

A blueprint that does not name its concessions cannot be evaluated, and a coach who has not named them to his players will find those players improvising at the worst moment. This plan concedes four things, and it concedes them knowingly.

It concedes possession and territory in the middle third. The opponent will have the ball, will pass it sideways in front of your block, and will look comfortable doing so. Players and supporters both find this uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the reason teams abandon the plan around the half-hour mark. Comfort in the middle third is not a threat; it is the sound of the plan working.

It concedes crosses from deep and wide positions. Because the full-back is angled to send the carrier toward the touchline and the second defender is closing the inside lane, the ball will frequently end up being delivered from thirty to thirty-five metres. Those deliveries produce goals occasionally, and against a side with a strong aerial forward the calculation changes. Against a side whose primary threat is a running forward, a cross from that distance is the cheapest chance you will concede all day and you should be pleased every time you see one.

It concedes shots from outside the penalty area. A screened inside lane means the carrier's remaining option is often to shoot from twenty-two or twenty-five metres. This is a conversion rate you can live with, and the goalkeeper should be set for it rather than surprised by it.

It concedes the high press. You are not winning the ball in their third, which means you are not scoring the easy goals that come from high turnovers, which means your own attacking plan has to be built around longer transitions and set pieces. This is the concession that costs points rather than goals, and it is why this blueprint should be deployed against opponents whose transition threat genuinely warrants it rather than as a default.

What it does not concede, at any point, is a reception with an open body and a long runway. If that is happening more than once or twice per half, the plan is not being executed and the fallbacks above should be reached for immediately rather than at half time.

## When he plays through the middle rather than from the left

The blueprint above is written for the wide starting position, because that is the position from which the isolation problem arises. A central starting position produces a genuinely different contest and requires three adjustments, none of which are cosmetic.

The first adjustment is that the first defender changes from the full-back to a centre-back, which changes the nature of the individual duel completely. A centre-back cannot use the touchline as a second defender, because there is no touchline, and the compensating asset is that he has a partner beside him rather than a partner forty metres away. The angle rule survives but inverts: instead of steering the carrier outside, the near centre-back's job is to steer him toward his partner's side, and the two of them squeeze the channel between them rather than each defending a separate route.

The second adjustment is that the offside line becomes a live tool rather than a background condition. Against a wide start, offside is difficult to use because the run comes from behind the ball on a diagonal and the timing references are messy. Against a central start, the run behind the last line is the primary threat, the line is facing the passer, and a coordinated step from four defenders can eliminate a run entirely. This is the one context in which the offside trap is a worthwhile part of the plan rather than a gamble, and even here it should be a trigger rather than a habit: step only when the passer is under pressure or has his head down, never when he is free and looking up.

The third adjustment is that the screening job moves from the inside lane to the central lane in front of the back four, and it becomes a two-player job rather than a one-player job. Against a central runner supported by a playmaker in the pocket, one holding midfielder has to defend both the pass into feet and the pass over the top, which is impossible; two players sitting five metres apart can defend both, one high on the receiver and one dropping to shorten the runway.

The verdict on which version is harder is that they are hard in opposite conditions, which is why no single defensive setup handles both. The central version is more defensible with a good defensive line, because a well-drilled line can compress the space behind and can use offside. The wide version is more defensible with a deeper block, because depth removes the isolation and the touchline does the work. A team that tries to defend both with the same shape will find itself either too high against the wide threat or too deep against the central one, and the useful preparation question before a match is not how to stop him in general but which of the two versions the opponent's structure is likely to produce.

## The debates worth answering

Five positions circulate constantly in discussion of this problem. Each of them contains something real, and each of them fails in a specific way. Naming the deciding factor is more useful than dismissing them.

### Where does this plan usually break down?

At the second defender. Line height and full-back angles are easy to drill and easy to see. The midfielder's inside-lane discipline is invisible from the touchline, decays under fatigue, and disappears entirely when the team chases a goal. When the plan fails, the tape almost always shows a wide duel that became a genuine one on one.

The first position is that only an equally fast defender can cope, and everything else is theory. What is real in it is that pace buys forgiveness: a fast defender can be caught in a poor position and still recover, which raises the plan's tolerance for error. What is wrong with it is that it treats a one on one in open space as the unit of analysis, and a one on one in open space against this forward is a losing proposition even for a fast defender. He does not need to beat you by five metres; he needs to beat you by one, and one metre is available to him against almost anybody. The deciding factor is that pace is a useful margin, not a plan, and a slow full-back with a screened inside lane and a fifteen-metre runway will concede less than a fast full-back left alone with forty metres behind him.

The second position is that he is only dangerous in transition, so simply keeping the ball is enough. What is real in it is that transitions are indeed where the biggest chances originate, and a team that keeps possession well genuinely does reduce his output. What is wrong with it is the word simply. Keeping the ball is not a defensive plan; it is an ambition, and it collapses the moment you fall behind, the moment your best passer is pressed into a mistake, or the moment the opponent decides to defend deep and concede possession on purpose. A possession-based defensive plan is a plan that only works while you are winning, and a plan that only works while you are winning is not a plan. The deciding factor is what your team does in the six seconds after a turnover, which is why rest defense sits at the centre of this blueprint rather than at the edge of it.

The third position is that a deep block neutralizes him. What is real in it is the geometry: depth genuinely does shorten the runway, and a well-organized deep block genuinely does reduce the number of clean running situations. What is wrong with it is that depth does not remove the threat, it exchanges it. A team defending on the edge of its own box against a side with strong wide players and good crossers has swapped a running problem for a crossing and cutback problem, and the cutback from the byline to the top of the six-yard box is one of the highest-value patterns in football. Depth also concedes the second-ball zone and the corner count. The deciding factor is your box defending and your aerial ability: if both are strong, deep is a real option for a period, and if either is weak, deep is a slower way to lose.

The fourth position is that his defensive contribution is limited, so pressing his team is effectively free. This one requires care, because the difference between a tactical observation and a character verdict is the difference between analysis and noise, and only the first has any place in a preparation meeting. Framed properly, the observation is structural: a forward whose principal function is to threaten the space behind will start from a higher and wider position out of possession than a forward whose principal function is to lead a press, and the team behind him is built to absorb that. What follows tactically is that the build-up side of the pitch nearest him will often have a numerical advantage in the first phase, which is genuinely exploitable. What does not follow is that pressing is free, because the price of building through that zone is that your own players are committed forward in exactly the shape that his team wants to attack. Free possession in your own half against a side built to counter is not a gift; it is bait. The deciding factor is whether your build-up can progress the ball without leaving your rest-defense shape short, and the wider treatment of the out-of-possession question is owned by the companion piece on [the conditions that reduce his effectiveness and the criticisms that survive examination](/2020/03/01/kylian-mbappe-weaknesses-explained/).

The fifth position is that he is easier to defend centrally than from the left. This one is closer to right than the others, with an important qualification. Centrally, he faces two centre-backs rather than one full-back, the touchline is unavailable to him but so is the isolation he thrives on, and the offside line becomes usable. From the left, he gets a repeated one on one in a large space with the goal on his stronger side. So yes, the central version is easier for a well-organized defensive line. The qualification is that it is only easier if your line is genuinely coordinated, because a central runner against a line that steps at different speeds is the easiest goal in the sport. The deciding factor is the quality of your line's coordination: with it, defend him centrally; without it, you would rather he stayed wide where the touchline can help you.

## The three clips an analyst should pull first

Preparation time is finite, and the useful version of this analysis is not a compilation of goals. Goals show you the outcome and hide the mechanism, and a coach who shows his players a highlight reel of a great forward before a match has succeeded only in making his own team afraid. Three clip types carry almost all of the preparation value, and they can be gathered from two or three matches.

The first clip type is every reception in the attacking half where the space ahead was open. Count them, and note where the pass came from and which player played it. This tells you the supply route, which is the input to the third fallback above. If the majority arrive from one specific player or one specific zone, you have found the thing to defend, and defending a passer is easier than defending a runner.

The second clip type is every occasion where he received with a defender goal-side and a second defender inside. Watch what he does. This tells you the escape patterns to prepare for: whether he plays a one-two and runs behind, whether he goes outside and looks for a cutback, whether he stops the ball and waits for support, whether he goes back and switches. Every forward has a preferred escape and the preference is stable enough to plan around.

The third clip type is the origin of transitions. Do not start the clip at the moment he receives; start it eight seconds earlier, at the moment his team lost or won the ball. This is the clip that shows your players where the danger actually begins, and it is the one that makes rest-defense instructions land, because players who see the same turnover location three times stop treating the instruction as an abstraction.

Two further notes on method. Watch at least three full matches against different opponent structures, ideally one against a high-pressing side, one against a deep block, and one against a mid-block, because the version of the problem you face depends heavily on which of those your own team resembles. And build the dossier around the conditions rather than the player: your presentation should be three slides about where your line stands, where your midfielder stands, and what you are conceding, with clips as evidence, rather than twenty minutes of a great player being great. The broader analytical method for turning observation into a deliverable plan is treated separately in the series and should not be reconstructed here.

## How the plan degrades with a weaker squad

Almost every reader of this article is not preparing a professional team to face this particular forward. They are preparing a school team, a Sunday side, an academy age group, or a semi-professional squad, to face the quickest player in their division, and the question is which parts of this blueprint survive the drop in level.

Three things survive intact. The fifteen-metre rule survives, because it is geometry and geometry does not care about level. The full-back's angle survives, because standing half a body inside costs nothing and requires no athleticism. The rest-defense plus-one rule survives, and at amateur level it is worth more than at professional level, because amateur teams commit numbers forward far more carelessly and their recovery running is far worse.

Two things degrade badly. The dynamic line, stepping and dropping in unison with the pressure on the ball, requires a great deal of rehearsal and a defender who can organize it out loud, and a line that attempts it without both will break apart and create the exact seam it was meant to close. If you cannot rehearse it weekly, set a fixed reference instead, tell the line to hold the halfway line when the ball is in the opponent's half and the edge of the box when it is in yours, and accept a less efficient block in exchange for one that does not fall apart. The second casualty is the two-second counter-press window, which requires collective fitness and collective timing that most amateur sides do not have. Replace it with a simpler instruction: when we lose the ball, the nearest player pressures the passer and everyone else runs back. It is less sophisticated and it fails less often.

One thing gets easier, which is worth knowing. At amateur level the supply is far more fragile. A team whose fast forward is dangerous almost always depends on two or three players who can find him, and those players are far easier to press, screen, and disrupt than the forward himself. Cutting the supply is a lower-skill intervention than winning the duel, and at levels below professional it is usually the highest-return decision available.

## The verdict on how to stop Kylian Mbappe

The verdict is that this is a space-management problem with an individual duel attached, and that almost all of the available defensive value sits in the space management rather than in the duel. A team that gets the line height right, the full-back's angle right, and the midfielder's inside-lane discipline right will make the contest difficult with ordinary defenders. A team that gets those wrong will make it easy with excellent ones, and will then attribute the result to the forward's quality rather than to its own structure, which is how the same mistake survives from one match to the next.

The plan will not produce a clean sheet by itself, and no plan honestly described will. What it produces is a match in which the chances he gets are the ones you selected: shots from distance, deliveries from deep, receptions with his back turned, and perhaps one or two genuine openings from transitions that beat your rest defense. Against a forward of this quality, that is what a defensive win looks like. Chasing zero is how teams end up conceding four.

Three sentences are worth taking into the meeting. Shorten the runway with the line, not with retreat. Make the first defender an angle rather than a tackler. Guarantee the second defender from the inside, on the second touch, every time. Everything else in this blueprint is an elaboration of those three, and a team that does only those three will defend this problem better than most professional sides manage on a given weekend.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Kylian Mbappe?

By managing space rather than by assigning a marker. The plan has four moving parts: hold a controlled medium defensive line so the distance between his reception point and your last defender stays short, position the nearest defender half a body to the inside so the reception happens with his body closed toward the touchline, guarantee that the nearest central midfielder occupies the inside lane by the time of his second touch, and protect your rest-defense shape while you have the ball so that turnovers do not hand over forty metres of open pitch. The individual duel matters, but it is the last of the four in importance. Teams that execute the first three make the contest difficult with ordinary defenders, and teams that ignore them make it comfortable for him with excellent ones. The concession attached to the plan is possession and territory in front of your block, and that concession has to be accepted openly.

### Q: Should you drop deep against Mbappe?

Only as a temporary measure, not as an approach. Dropping deep does shorten the space he can run into, which is why the instinct exists, but it does not remove the threat so much as exchange it. A block sitting on the edge of its own penalty area has surrendered the entire middle third, which means his team arrives with numbers, and the contest becomes a crossing and cutback problem instead of a running problem. The pull-back from the byline is one of the highest-value patterns in the game, so this is not obviously a better trade. Depth is a reasonable way to protect a lead for the last fifteen minutes, particularly if your box defending and aerial ability are strong. As a plan for ninety minutes it produces sustained pressure, corners, second balls, and eventually a goal. Shorten the space with a controlled line and pressure on the ball instead of with retreat.

### Q: Can a full-back defend Mbappe one on one?

Sometimes, and that is exactly the problem. A one on one in open space is a probabilistic contest that a good full-back wins often enough to feel encouraged and loses often enough to lose matches, because the forward only needs a metre and a metre is available to almost anyone. Building a plan on a contest you win perhaps two times in three is building a plan that concedes a clean chance every twenty minutes. The correct framing is that the full-back's job is not to win the duel outright but to hold his angle and his distance until the situation improves: to steer the carrier toward the touchline, to delay him, and to survive until the covering midfielder arrives on the inside. Delay is the product he is manufacturing, not a consolation prize. Judge the full-back on whether the duel became a two on one, not on whether he made the tackle.

### Q: What line height works against Mbappe?

A controlled medium line, roughly ten to fifteen metres inside your own half, adjusted continuously rather than fixed. The depth should be tied to the pressure on the ball: step up several metres whenever the player in possession cannot lift his head, drop five to eight metres the moment he is free to play forward. A static reference is readable, and an intelligent attacking side will simply time its runs against it. The dynamic version is harder to attack because the reference keeps moving. Two mechanical details make or break it. The defenders must drop with their bodies opened toward the touchline so the retreat can become a sprint without an extra turning step, and the goalkeeper must be positioned high enough to take anything played into the space behind. If your goalkeeper does not defend space, the whole line has to sit around five metres deeper, which is a real cost.

### Q: How do you stop Mbappe in transition?

Press the passer, not the runner. When possession changes hands, the instinctive reaction of every defender is to look at the dangerous forward and start running toward him, which leaves the player about to deliver the pass completely free to choose its timing and weight. The nearest player closes the passer's face and takes away the forward ball with his body; the second nearest takes the closest supporting option; nobody else converges. Meanwhile the deepest defenders hold their relationship to each other and to the halfway line for two seconds before dropping together, because a line that breaks apart at different speeds creates the seam that a well-timed run attacks. The ball-side full-back recovers diagonally toward his own penalty spot rather than along the touchline. And the counter-press has a hard time limit of about two seconds: if the ball is not won or the pass forced backwards, everyone stops pressing and runs back.

### Q: Should you double up on Mbappe?

Yes, but structurally rather than by sending two players at the ball. Two defenders defending the same route is a wasted body; two defenders defending two different routes is the whole point. The first defender holds the outside route with his angle, and the second, who should be the nearest central midfielder rather than a covering centre-back or a recovering winger, stands in the inside lane three or four metres ahead of the duel. The centre-back arrives from the wrong angle and strands your remaining defender; the winger arrives from behind, where he can neither tackle cleanly nor close a lane he has already run past. Timing matters as much as identity: the midfielder commits on the carrier's second touch, because the first touch can still become a lay-off and the third arrives too late to change direction with him.

### Q: How do you deny Mbappe the space he wants to run into?

By controlling three things at once: the height of your defensive line, the position of your own full-backs while you attack, and the discipline of your team in the first seconds after a turnover. Space in behind is almost never created by a single defender losing a race. It is created collectively, usually by a defensive line that has drifted deeper than it intended, or by two full-backs advancing simultaneously and leaving a channel open, or by a counter-press that continued past its useful life and left five players ahead of the ball. Fix those three and the space largely stops appearing. The rule worth holding onto is that the space is shortened by pressure on the ball and a stepped-up line, not by retreating, because retreating shortens the running distance while handing over everything in front of it.

### Q: Which side should you force Mbappe onto?

Toward the touchline in most situations, but only when the inside lane is genuinely occupied by a body rather than closed in theory. As a right-footed forward whose most dangerous finishing comes from cutting in from the left, the inside route is the high-value one for him and the outside route leads to a delivery rather than a shot. So the default is to show outside. If your midfield has been dragged across and the inside lane is empty, showing outside becomes an invitation, and the defender is better squaring up to defend both routes imperfectly for the half-second it takes his cover to arrive. Recognize that as a failure state rather than a plan. When he starts from the right instead, the priority inverts: cutting inside there produces passes rather than shots, so steering him toward the byline on his stronger foot is the safer option.

### Q: What does a midfield have to do to protect against Mbappe?

Hold the inside lane, and keep holding it when nothing appears to be happening. The nearest central midfielder is the second defender in every wide duel, and his job is positional rather than combative: stand in the passing and carrying lane between the wide carrier and the top of the penalty area, three to four metres inside and slightly ahead of the ball, so the cut inside is visibly closed. He is not marking a player; he is occupying a lane. The requirement is lateral discipline rather than ball-winning, which is why the selection here matters more than most coaches assume: players who need involvement to feel useful drift centrally and out of the lane. This is also the first thing that fails under fatigue, which makes a fresh holding midfielder around the seventieth minute one of the most valuable defensive substitutions available.

### Q: How do you defend Mbappe when he plays centrally?

Three things change. The first defender becomes a centre-back rather than a full-back, which removes the touchline as a helper but adds a partner alongside; the two centre-backs squeeze the channel between them rather than each defending a separate route. The offside line becomes a genuine tool, because the run now comes at the line rather than across it, and a coordinated step can eliminate a run entirely, though it should be a trigger used when the passer is pressed rather than a habit used when he is free. And the screening job moves from the inside lane to the central lane in front of the back four, where it becomes a two-player task, one player pressing the pass into feet and one dropping to keep the space behind short. The central version is easier to defend if your line is genuinely coordinated and considerably harder if it is not.

### Q: Is pressing high the right answer against Mbappe?

Only with a named rest-defense structure attached, and rarely as the primary plan. A high press is compatible with keeping the space behind short in the ideal case, because if the press succeeds the reception never happens and if the line is high the running distance is small. The problem is the two or three occasions per half when the press is played through cleanly. In that state your line is high, your midfield is ahead of the ball, and the forward receives in stride with only the goalkeeper as cover. A defensive scheme that wins eighty-five duels and concedes three clean chances has not defended well. If you press, keep the plus-one rule intact behind it, never advance both full-backs at once, and accept that you are buying high turnovers with occasional clean chances against you.

### Q: How does a goalkeeper reduce Mbappe's finishing angles?

By starting high, closing ground early, and staying tall. A goalkeeper positioned on or just outside the edge of his penalty area allows the whole defensive line to hold five metres higher, which shortens the running space before any shot exists. On balls played into the space behind, the rule is absolute: take everything reachable with a straight-line run and nothing else, and call it loudly enough that the defenders peel away rather than compete. In the one on one, close ground while the carrier's head is down on his touch, then set the feet and stay upright as he lifts his head, which makes both the near-post finish and the lifted finish harder and leaves the lowest-percentage option across the body. Against a right-footed forward arriving from the left, the near post must be covered early without the weight committing, because that angle opens toward the far post as he travels.

### Q: What do you accept conceding in order to stop Mbappe?

Four things, and they should be said out loud to the players before kick-off. You concede possession and territory in the middle third, which will feel uncomfortable around the half-hour mark and is the sound of the plan working. You concede crosses from thirty to thirty-five metres, which is the cheapest chance available and should be welcomed rather than defended anxiously. You concede shots from outside the penalty area, because a screened inside lane pushes the carrier into them. And you concede the high press, which means your own attacking output has to come from longer transitions and set pieces, so this is the concession that costs points rather than goals. What you never concede, at any point in the match, is a reception with an open body and a long stretch of grass behind your last defender. If that is happening more than once or twice per half, reach for the fallbacks immediately.

### Q: How should an amateur side handle a forward as quick as Mbappe?

Keep three things and abandon two. Keep the principle that the running distance is what matters, keep the full-back's habit of standing half a body inside so the reception happens facing the touchline, and keep the rule that you always leave one more defender back than they leave forwards. Abandon the continuously adjusted defensive line, which needs weekly rehearsal and an organizer who talks, and replace it with a fixed reference: halfway line when the ball is in their half, edge of the box when it is in yours. Abandon the timed counter-press and replace it with a simpler instruction, which is that the nearest player pressures the passer and everyone else runs back. The good news at this level is that the supply is fragile: two or three players usually find the fast forward, and pressing them is far easier than winning the duel.

### Q: How do you defend the break when Mbappe starts wide?

Treat it as a race against a distance rather than against a player. The ball-side full-back does not sprint toward the ball; he recovers on a diagonal toward his own penalty spot, which puts him between the carrier and the goal instead of behind him. The first recovering midfielder takes the central lane in front of the box rather than chasing the ball, which is what converts a three on two into a three on three. The second takes the far-side runner, because the pull-back to the back post is what punishes a defence that has committed everything to one flank. Only the third recovering player goes to the ball. The near-side centre-back's task is delay and direction rather than the tackle: steer the carrier away from the middle and toward the touchline, and let the eight players behind the ball get in front of it.

### Q: Which defenders have coped with Mbappe and why?

The ones who cope share a profile rather than a physical gift, which is the useful finding. They tend to be defenders with excellent starting positions who consistently produce a closed reception, and who are supported by a screening midfielder holding the inside lane. Pace helps, because it buys forgiveness for a poor position, but the recurring pattern in matches where the threat is contained is structural rather than individual: a controlled line height, a covering body inside, and a team that does not empty its rest-defense shape while attacking. The corollary is that the same defenders look comprehensively beaten in matches where those conditions are absent, which is strong evidence that the conditions rather than the individuals are doing the work. Judge a defensive performance against this profile of forward by how many receptions were open and how many duels were genuinely one on one.
