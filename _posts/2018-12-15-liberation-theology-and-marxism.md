Liberation theology is a current within Latin American Christianity that emerged in the late nineteen sixties and did something no major theological movement had done before it: it took up the social analysis of Marxism as a working instrument while refusing, explicitly and on the record, the philosophy and the atheism historically attached to that analysis. Its practitioners were priests, religious sisters, bishops in a minority of dioceses, and lay organisers in very large numbers; they were not, with rare and famous exceptions, members of Marxist parties. Its signature commitment, the preferential option for the poor, held that Christian faith must be read and practised from the position of the impoverished majority of the continent, and that poverty itself was not a misfortune to be relieved by charity but a condition produced by identifiable structures and therefore open to structural remedy. Any account of the movement that misses either of those two facts, the deliberate and limited character of the Marxist borrowing, or the option for the poor as a structural rather than charitable commitment, will misdescribe everything that follows.

![A circle of readers studying scripture beside notes on the borrowing table, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-13.webp)

Two features of the movement are persistently lost in summary treatments, and recovering them is the purpose of the pages that follow. The first is precision about the borrowing. The theologians did not drift into Marxist vocabulary by osmosis, and they did not convert to Marxism while keeping a Christian vocabulary for decoration. They stated, in their own methodological writings, exactly what they were taking and exactly what they were refusing, and they gave the relationship a name: mediation. Marxist analysis, in their formulation, functioned as a socio-analytic mediation, a set of instruments for understanding why the poor were poor, interposed between the theologian's faith and the theologian's reading of society. Whether instruments of that kind can be detached from the philosophy that produced them is a genuine question, and it was pressed hard from two opposite directions, by the Vatican's doctrinal congregation and by Marxist critics who found the theology insufficiently materialist. The dispute is the intellectual centre of the subject, and it cannot even be stated correctly without the theologians' own distinction in hand.

The second feature is organisational. Liberation theology is usually shelved as a literature, a shelf of books by Gustavo Gutierrez, Leonardo Boff, Jon Sobrino and their contemporaries, and the books matter. But the movement's mass character rested on something the books served rather than created: a network of small local groups, the base ecclesial communities, in which working people and peasants read scripture together, discussed the conditions of their own neighbourhoods and villages, and organised practical responses. Without that network the movement would have been a current of publishing. With it, the movement became a social force that governments watched, that the Vatican addressed in two formal instructions, and that a generation of Latin Americans experienced as the place where their faith and their material lives were discussed in the same conversation. The regional setting of all of this, the tradition within which the theology sits as one current among several, is mapped in the series guide to [Marxism in Latin America](/2018/10/01/marxism-latin-america-guide/); the concern here is narrower and deeper, the theology itself and the terms of its encounter with Marxist analysis.

## Liberation theology defined: theology as critical reflection on practice

The canonical definition was given by the movement's founding text and refined across the nineteen seventies. Liberation theology is theology understood as critical reflection on historical praxis: reflection, that is, on action already undertaken in the world, carried out in the light of faith, and ordered back toward action. The definition reverses the order most readers expect. In the classical pattern, doctrine is settled first and application follows; the theologian's study precedes the believer's practice. In the liberation pattern, commitment comes first and theology second, because the reality that theology must interpret is a practice of liberation already under way among the poor themselves. Gutierrez gave the reversal its durable shorthand: theology is the second act, and the first act is involvement in the process by which an unjust order is transformed. The phrase is not a slogan about relevance. It is a claim about the place where theological knowledge is generated, namely in the encounter between faith and a concrete historical situation, rather than in the seminar room alone.

### What does it mean to call theology a second act?

It means that commitment precedes reflection in the order of knowing. The theologian first stands inside a practice, solidarity with the poor and work against the structures impoverishing them, and only then reflects on that practice in the light of scripture and tradition. Knowledge of God, on this account, is inseparable from the doing of justice.

The consequences of the definition ramify through everything the movement produced. Because theology reflects on praxis, its raw material includes the findings of the social sciences, which describe the praxis and the structures it confronts; this is the doorway through which Marxist analysis legitimately entered, as the most developed available account of how Latin American poverty was produced and reproduced. Because theology remains theology, its governing questions stay theological: what God wills for human history, what sin means when it is embedded in institutions, what salvation can mean for people whose suffering is social before it is private. And because the reflection is ordered back toward action, the movement's characteristic literary forms were not only treatises but pastoral letters, conference documents, catechetical materials and the discussion guides of the base communities, genres in which analysis and exhortation are deliberately fused.

The definition also fixes the movement's subject. In most theology the poor appear, when they appear, as objects: recipients of charity, occasions for the virtue of the giver, a problem for pastoral administration. In liberation theology the poor are the subject of the reflection, the place from which reality is viewed. Gutierrez called this the irruption of the poor into history and into the church: the arrival, as protagonists, of people who had been treated as absent from both. The epistemological claim attached to it was strong. Reality looks different from underneath; the mechanisms that produce poverty are more visible to those who bear them than to those who administer them; and a theology that begins from the beneficiary's chair will systematically mistake the nature of the problem. Critics inside and outside the church contested that claim of epistemic privilege, and the contest belongs to the objections examined later in this article. The claim itself, though, is indispensable to understanding the movement, because it explains why the theologians trusted an analysis of class and exploitation to tell them something about their own continent that the continent's official self-descriptions concealed.

### Why did the movement arise in Latin America rather than elsewhere?

Because the continent combined conditions found nowhere else together: a population overwhelmingly baptised into one church, poverty on a mass scale alongside visible wealth, a clergy close enough to popular neighbourhoods to register the contradiction daily, and social scientists arguing that underdevelopment was produced by the region's insertion into the world economy rather than by backwardness.

Latin America in the nineteen sixties was the one region where a single church held the allegiance of the great majority of the population while the population's material condition deteriorated in full view of that church's own personnel. Parish priests and religious sisters worked in the shantytowns growing around every major city and in villages where land concentration had been tightening for generations. The developmentalist promise of the postwar decades, that growth would dissolve poverty in time, was visibly failing in the places where church workers lived. At the same moment, the region's universities and its United Nations economic commission in Santiago were producing a body of analysis, dependency theory, which held that the poverty of the periphery was not an original condition but a product of the same world economy that enriched the centre. A theology looking for instruments to understand its situation found those instruments already forged, in Spanish and Portuguese, by the region's own intellectuals. The encounter between a pastorally exposed clergy and a structurally minded social science is the movement's birthplace, and it explains why the analogous movements elsewhere, in Korea, in India, in South Africa, arose where similar combinations occurred and took forms fitted to their own ground.

## The church before the turn: council, poverty and a continent examined

No theological movement begins with its founding text, and liberation theology's prehistory is written into the transformation of Latin American Catholicism across the nineteen fifties and sixties. The institutional church the theologians inherited was old, landed and accustomed to alliance with the continent's ruling orders; its clergy were thinly spread, heavily dependent on foreign missionaries, and concentrated in cities while the rural majority saw a priest rarely. Yet inside that institution, pressures for change had been accumulating for a generation. Catholic Action movements had trained lay people to read their social environment as a field of Christian responsibility. A French worker priest tradition, and the see judge act method associated with Joseph Cardijn and the Young Christian Workers, taught chaplains to begin from observed reality rather than from doctrine applied downward. Brazilian bishops in the poor northeast had begun, well before any theologian gave the posture a name, to describe their region's misery as a scandal that preaching alone could not answer.

The Second Vatican Council, which sat from 1962 to 1965, supplied the charter under which such local experiments could present themselves as obedience rather than rebellion. Its pastoral constitution on the church in the modern world directed Catholics to read the signs of the times and treated the joys and hopes, the griefs and anxieties of the people of the age, above all the poor, as the church's own. The council's decree on the ministry of bishops encouraged national and regional episcopal conferences, and its liturgical reforms put scripture into the hands and the language of ordinary congregations, a precondition for everything the base communities would later do with the text. Pope Paul VI's encyclical on the development of peoples, issued in 1967, carried the argument a step the theologians would not forget: it named the widening gap between rich and poor nations as a threat to peace, criticised an unbridled liberal capitalism, and conceded that in defined circumstances of manifest tyranny a revolutionary uprising could be legitimate, while warning of the new barbarism revolution could install. The encyclical's framing remained developmentalist, a matter of peoples advancing along a road the rich had already travelled, and the liberation theologians would break with precisely that framing. But its existence at the highest level of teaching meant that a Latin American priest who described poverty as structural was not inventing a grievance; he was radicalising a premise Rome had already conceded.

### What changed in the Latin American church before Medellin?

The council legitimised a method and the region's own institutions gave it a home. Episcopal conferences acquired real weight, clergy and religious moved into shantytowns and rural pastorals in growing numbers, lay movements trained members to analyse their surroundings, and the bishops of the poorest regions began speaking of misery as a collective condition requiring structural answers rather than alms.

Beneath the institutional changes ran a demographic and economic transformation that no pastoral method could ignore. The continent's population was moving to the cities at extraordinary speed, building the belts of improvised housing, the favelas, barriadas, poblaciones and villas miseria, that ringed every capital. Rural society was being squeezed between the latifundio, the great estate worked by tenants and day labourers, and the minifundio, the family plot too small to feed the family that held it. Military governments ruled much of the continent by the late nineteen sixties, several of them committed to a doctrine of national security that treated organised popular demand as subversion by definition. Church workers who accompanied communities through land disputes, union organising and the simple defence of a water supply found themselves harassed by the same authorities their bishops had once blessed. Experience of that kind is a theological fact before it is a political one: it poses, in unavoidable form, the question of what faithfulness requires when the order of society itself appears as the obstacle to the life the gospel announces. Liberation theology was the answer a particular generation constructed, with the instruments at hand, and the instruments at hand prominently included the social analysis descending from Marx.

### How did the region's poverty enter theological writing?

Through the pastoral encounter first and the library second. Priests and sisters in poor districts reported conditions that categories of devotion could not process: hunger beside export agriculture, disease beside clinics, evictions under colour of law. Theologians who received them began asking why the poverty persisted and whose interests its persistence served, and such questions required social science, not exhortation.

One further precondition deserves emphasis because it distinguishes the Latin American case from the European political theology developing at the same time. Thinkers such as Johann Baptist Metz in Germany were also arguing, in those years, that theology had privatised faith and must recover its public and critical force. The Latin Americans read and valued that work, but their situation differed in a decisive respect: they wrote from within societies where the poor were the majority and the church was the majority's church. European political theology addressed a secularised continent in which the church was losing the working class; Latin American liberation theology addressed a continent in which the church still held the poor and was being asked, by the poor's own organisers and by its own younger clergy, whether it would hold them as clients or accompany them as agents. The difference in setting produced a difference in genre. Where the European argument remained largely academic, the Latin American argument was drafted in conference documents, tested in pastoral practice, and argued over by bishops, because the institution that would have to live with its conclusions was present in the room.

## Medellin: the bishops give the movement its charter

In August and September of 1968 the Latin American Episcopal Council, CELAM, held its second general conference at Medellin in Colombia, with Pope Paul VI travelling to the continent to open it, the first such journey by a reigning pontiff. The conference's task was to apply the council's reforms to Latin American conditions, and its documents, on justice, on peace, on the poverty of the church and on joint pastoral planning, said things no comparable episcopal gathering had said before. The bishops described a continent held in a situation of injustice that could be called institutionalised violence, a phrase examined closely in a later section of this article. They committed the church to a preferential option for the poor, in the language that the movement would carry around the world. They endorsed the awakening of the popular classes to their own agency, spoke of conscientisation, the process by which the oppressed come to read their situation critically, as a legitimate pastoral aim, and encouraged the formation of small communities in which lay people would share responsibility for the church's life. A movement that might have remained a tendency among theologians received, at Medellin, an episcopal charter.

### What did the Medellin documents actually decide?

They decided that the continent's misery was a structural condition rather than a sum of misfortunes, that the church must stand preferentially with the poor, that lay people in small communities were agents of the church's mission, and that education should awaken awareness of social conditions. Each was framed as fidelity to the council and the gospel, not innovation.

The importance of Medellin is easy to understate from a distance, because the conference enacted no dogma and founded no organisation. Its work was to change the terms in which Latin American Catholicism described its own situation, and changes of description are what movements live on. Before Medellin, a bishop who denounced the land structure of his diocese spoke as an individual conscience; after Medellin, he could cite a continental episcopal consensus. Before Medellin, a priest who organised peasants to read the gospel against their conditions was exposed to the charge of importing politics into religion; after Medellin, he could reply that the bishops had named those conditions a form of violence and had asked the church to stand with the people enduring them. The documents also carried an ambiguity that the following decades would exploit in both directions. They condemned violence, including the violence of revolution, while naming the existing order itself violent; they affirmed the need for profound structural change while leaving the means underspecified. Conservatives would later read Medellin as a call to charity and gradual reform that unruly followers had radicalised. The liberation theologians read it as a mandate whose logic their work merely unfolded. The textual record supports elements of both readings, which is precisely why the conference remained a contested inheritance rather than a settled one.

The follow up conference at Puebla in Mexico in 1979 tested whether the Medellin settlement would survive a decade of military rule and Roman unease. Puebla's final document, drafted under heavier Vatican influence and after vigorous argument, nevertheless confirmed the option for the poor in explicit terms and retained the analysis of structural injustice, while adding warnings against ideological readings of the gospel. For the movement, Puebla functioned as proof that its central commitment had passed from insurgent slogan to received teaching, even as the institutional weather around it worsened. The later conferences of the episcopal council, at Santo Domingo in 1992 and at Aparecida in 2007, kept the option's language in place while shifting emphasis toward evangelisation and culture, a trajectory that captures the movement's institutional fate in miniature: its vocabulary absorbed, its sharper analytical claims retired, its organisational base left to contract. That pattern, absorption alongside contraction, is the key to the question of the movement's later condition, and it will recur when the Vatican instructions are examined below.

## The founding text: a Peruvian priest rewrites the theological agenda

The movement acquired its name and its programme in 1971, when the Peruvian priest Gustavo Gutierrez published Teologia de la liberacion: Perspectivas in Lima. Gutierrez had studied medicine before theology, had taken degrees in Europe, and had worked as a parish priest and chaplain to student and worker movements in the Peruvian capital; he had presented the outline of his argument at a meeting of priests and lay people at Chimbote in 1968, weeks before the bishops gathered at Medellin. The book's achievement was to convert a set of pastoral intuitions into a theological system. It defined theology as critical reflection on praxis in the light of the word of God. It treated the poor not as the church's clientele but as the privileged interlocutors of revelation. It drew a hard line between development and liberation as descriptions of what the continent needed. And it set out, with a candour its critics never let it retract, the terms on which Marxist social analysis would be used: as the indispensable instrument for understanding the causes of a poverty that charity had described for centuries without explaining.

The argument against developmentalism is the book's analytical hinge and the clearest early evidence of the Marxist borrowing at work. Developmentalism, the ideology of the postwar economic commissions and the Alliance for Progress, held that poor nations stood at an earlier stage of a path the rich nations had completed, and that capital transfer, technical assistance and patience would move them along it. Gutierrez, following the dependency analysts, rejected the picture outright. Underdevelopment, on the analysis he adopted, is not an original stage but a byproduct: the same historical process that produced developed economies produced underdeveloped ones, as their counterpart and condition. A continent whose poverty is manufactured by its mode of insertion into the world economy will not be cured by deeper insertion on the same terms. Hence the substitution of liberation for development as the governing word: what the poor require is not assistance within the structure but liberation from the structure, a break with the dependency that reproduces their condition. Whatever a reader concludes about the economics, the theological consequence is plain. Sin, in such a world, cannot be only a private transaction between the soul and God; it has a social and structural dimension that a theology of liberation is obliged to name.

### What are the three levels of liberation in the founding text?

The founding text distinguishes three mutually implicating levels: liberation from oppressive social, political and economic structures; liberation as the historical project of a new humanity, free persons assuming responsibility for their own destiny; and liberation from sin, the deepest level, understood as communion with God and with others. No level is reducible to another, and none is complete alone.

That threefold scheme is the founding text's own internal defence against the charge, later pressed from Rome, that liberation theology collapses salvation into politics. The third level is explicitly theological and explicitly irreducible: liberation from sin, communion with God, is named as the root and the fullness of the other two, and Gutierrez insists that the political process, however necessary, does not by itself save. At the same time the scheme refuses the opposite reduction, in which salvation is a purely interior affair and the structures of society are left to their own logic. The levels interpenetrate; growth in communion demands historical liberation, and historical liberation without the deepest level decays into a new domination. Whether the scheme succeeds in holding the levels together is one of the substantive questions in the assessment of the movement, and the Vatican's first instruction can be read as an extended argument that in practice the first level swallowed the other two. What cannot fairly be said is that the founding text itself performed the collapse. The architecture against it is present from the first edition.

### Why did Gutierrez reject the language of development?

Because the dependency analysis he adopted treated underdevelopment as a product of the world economy's structure rather than as an earlier stage of the rich countries' history. If poverty is manufactured by the relation between centre and periphery, assistance within that relation cannot end it, and the honest word for what the poor need is liberation from the relation itself.

The book's reception divided its audiences from the start. Within Latin America it became, quickly, the reference point for a generation of theologians, pastoral workers and religious educators, and it travelled through the base communities in simplified and excerpted forms. In Europe and North America it was read, often sympathetically and often selectively, as the theology of a continent in ferment. Among Marxist readers it posed an unsettling question, since here was a body of Christian thought that had absorbed the critique of political economy more thoroughly than most socialist parties of the region had done, while declining the materialism that was supposed to be inseparable from the critique. And in Rome it attracted the sustained attention that would eventually produce the two instructions of the nineteen eighties. A founding text that each of four audiences reads as a challenge is a text that has located a real fault line, and the fault line is the one this article is organised around: the claim that an analysis may be taken up while its philosophy is declined.

## The first generation: a school forms across a continent

A founding text becomes a school when other hands take up its method on other ground, and the nineteen seventies produced that uptake at remarkable speed. In Brazil, Leonardo Boff, a Franciscan formed in Germany, published his study of Jesus as liberator in 1972 and went on to write the movement's most searching ecclesiology, asking what the church itself must become if its message is liberation; his brother Clodovis Boff supplied the school's epistemology, a rigorous account of how theological knowledge is mediated, which the section on the mediation distinction examines in detail. In El Salvador, the Spanish born Jesuit Jon Sobrino rebuilt Christology from the standpoint of the crucified peoples of his adopted country, arguing that the historical Jesus and his fate supply the criterion by which all talk of God is to be judged. In Uruguay, Juan Luis Segundo developed the movement's hermeneutics, the theory of how scripture is to be read by communities already committed to change. In Brazil again, Hugo Assmann pressed the analysis hardest toward political economy and paid for his positions with exile. The Argentine philosopher Enrique Dussel, working from Mexico after his own exile, gave the school a history of the Latin American church and a philosophy of liberation that engaged Marx directly and at length.

### What divided Camilo Torres from the theologians who followed?

Torres, a Colombian priest, concluded in the nineteen sixties that revolutionary action was a Christian obligation, joined a guerrilla organisation and was killed in his first combat in 1966. The theologians who followed shared his diagnosis but declined his conclusion: they built analysis, communities and teaching rather than taking up arms, and their movement remained ecclesial rather than insurgent.

The Torres case matters to the school's self understanding because it marks the road not taken, and taken only by a handful. Camilo Torres Restrepo was a sociologist as well as a priest, a founder of the sociology faculty at the National University in Bogota, and his trajectory from university reform to a broad political front to the ranks of the National Liberation Army compressed into a few years the radicalisation that the continent's conditions pressed on many. He was killed in February 1966. The theologians who came after him treated his memory with respect and his decision as a boundary. Liberation theology, as a school, would be a movement of analysis, organisation and pastoral practice within the church, not a chaplaincy to armed struggle, and the distinction was maintained even in El Salvador and Nicaragua where the surrounding violence was extreme and the pressure to cross the line correspondingly strong. Individual priests did cross it, in several countries, and the Nicaraguan case, where priests accepted ministerial office in a revolutionary government after 1979, raised the boundary question in its sharpest institutional form, examined in a later section. The school's centre of gravity, though, stayed where Gutierrez had placed it: in reflection on the practice of communities, not in the strategy of armies.

What held the school together across its national variations was a shared set of convictions more than a shared system. Its members agreed that theology begins from the underside of history; that the social sciences, and preeminently the Marxist tradition within them, supply the indispensable analysis of how poverty is produced; that scripture read from the position of the poor yields meanings invisible from the position of the powerful; and that the church's own structures stand under the judgement of the liberation they preach. They disagreed, sometimes sharply, about the weight to give Marxist categories, about the church's institutional future, about political strategy and about the interpretation of their own continent's revolutions. Treating the school as a monolith is one of the standard errors of its critics and of its popularisers alike. It was a field of argument unified by a method and a commitment, and its internal arguments, between Boff's ecclesial radicalism and Segundo's hermeneutic caution, between Assmann's political economy and Sobrino's Christological concentration, are part of its substance rather than noise around it.

## The borrowing table

Everything argued in this article can be compressed into a single exhibit, and the exhibit is worth placing early and reading slowly. The borrowing table sets out, element by element, what liberation theology took from the Marxist tradition, what it declined, how its theologians justified each decision, and the objection that each decision attracted. The table is a finding device, not a summary of convenience: it exists so that the dispute about the movement can be conducted row by row, at the level where the actual disagreements live, instead of at the level of the slogan that the theologians were, or were not, Marxists.

| Element of the Marxist tradition | Decision of the theologians | Justification offered | Objection pressed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Class analysis | Adopted as an instrument | Society's conflicts are unintelligible without it; the poor's condition has class causes | Class becomes the exhaustive key to society and to persons |
| Exploitation | Adopted as an instrument | The wage relation and the land relation transfer value from the poor to the rich | A moral category is smuggled in as a scientific one |
| Ideology critique | Adopted as an instrument | Dominant ideas, including religious ones, can legitimate domination and must be tested | The critique is never turned with equal force on the theologians' own position |
| Dependency analysis | Adopted as an instrument | Underdevelopment is produced by insertion into the world economy, not by backwardness | The economics is contestable and dates the theology to one school of it |
| Historical materialism as philosophy | Refused | A total account of reality leaves no room for transcendence, freedom or grace | The analysis presupposes the philosophy; the refusal is therefore verbal |
| Atheism | Refused | The faith is the movement's ground, not its obstacle; God is not a hypothesis within the analysis | A materialism that exempts its user's beliefs is not materialism at all |

Read down the decision column and the pattern this article calls the mediation distinction is visible at a glance. The rows that name instruments of social analysis are adopted; the rows that name a philosophy of reality are refused. Read down the objection column and the pattern of the counter argument is equally visible: each objection, from whichever side it comes, denies that the line between the adopted rows and the refused rows can be held where the theologians drew it. Rome's objection concentrates on the upper rows, arguing that the instruments are not neutral and carry the philosophy with them. The Marxist objection concentrates on the lower rows, arguing that an analysis severed from its philosophy has been deprived of its explanatory engine and will decay into moralism. The theologians' replies, and the extent to which the replies succeed, occupy the centre of this article. But the table already performs the first service the subject requires: it shows that the borrowing was deliberate, differentiated and argued for, element by element, and that nobody involved, borrower, critic or defender, treated it as a casual matter of vocabulary.

A second service follows. The table disciplines the commonest journalistic error about the movement, which is to infer from any single row to the whole. A report that the theologians used class analysis establishes nothing about their view of God; a report that they rejected atheism is equally silent about their view of the land question. The rows are connected, and the connections are exactly what is disputed, but they are not identical, and an assessment that grades each row separately will reach conclusions that an assessment working from the label cannot. Graded row by row, the borrowing looks neither like conversion nor like theft. It looks like what its authors said it was: an attempt to use the strongest available analysis of a social order inside a faith that predated the analysis and expected to outlast it.

## The mediation distinction: the article's centre

The theologians had a technical term for the relationship the table displays, and recovering the term is recovering the argument. In the methodological writings of the school, above all in the work of Clodovis Boff, knowledge of society reaches theology through mediations: structured ways in which material from outside theology is taken up, tested and put to work inside it. The Marxist inheritance entered, on this account, as a socio-analytic mediation. It mediated between the theologian's faith commitment and the concrete society the theologian confronted, supplying the categories, class, exploitation, dependency, ideology, by which that society's production of poverty could be understood. What it did not do, on the theologians' account, was supply the theology's foundations, its account of God, of sin, of salvation, or of the human vocation. Those came from scripture and tradition, read through a second, hermeneutic mediation, and the whole was ordered toward a third, practical mediation, the pastoral and political practice in which reflection issues. The distinction between the mediations is the distinction between an instrument and a framework, and the school's entire defence of its procedure rests on keeping the two apart.

### How does liberation theology use Marxism?

as what its theologians called a socio-analytic mediation, meaning an instrument for analysing the causes of poverty, supplying categories of class, exploitation, and structural analysis, while rejecting philosophical materialism and atheism. Whether that separation is coherent is the central dispute about the movement.

The claim embedded in the distinction deserves to be stated with full clarity, because both sets of critics sometimes argue against a weaker version than the one the theologians held. The claim is not that Marxist analysis is a neutral technique, like statistics, indifferent to the purposes it serves. The theologians knew the analysis carried commitments: a suspicion of official self descriptions, an attention to material interests beneath professed ideals, a conviction that social orders are historical and therefore changeable. The claim is rather that the analysis's validity as an account of Latin American society can be assessed on evidence, case by case and category by category, without purchasing the metaphysics in which Marx and many of his followers embedded it. A theologian can find that the land structure of a diocese is unintelligible except in terms of class, and that the surplus extracted from its labourers is real, while denying that matter is the whole of reality or that religion is exhausted by its social function. The findings, on this view, are detachable because they are findings, answerable to the world they describe. The philosophy is detachable from them because it answers different questions, about the whole of reality, on which the theologian already holds a position derived from elsewhere.

### What are the three mediations in Clodovis Boff's account?

A socio-analytic mediation, which takes up the social sciences, Marxist analysis preeminently, to understand society; a hermeneutic mediation, which reads scripture and tradition in the light of that understanding; and a practical mediation, which orders the resulting theology toward pastoral and historical action. The scheme makes the borrowing from Marxism a single mediation among three.

Boff's scheme, set out in his work on theology and praxis at the end of the nineteen seventies, is the school's most rigorous methodological self description, and it cuts in two directions at once. Against romantic readings of the movement, it insists that the theology is disciplined: the social analysis must actually be learned and correctly used, the scriptural reading must actually answer to the text, and neither may borrow authority from the other. Against the charge of syncretism, it locates the Marxist material at a precise level, the first mediation, and denies it jurisdiction over the levels above and below it. The scheme also contains an admission its author made explicit: mediations are not watertight. What the analysis finds will shape what the reader notices in scripture; what the reader believes will shape which analysis seems plausible. The theologians' position, stated carefully, is therefore not that the levels are sealed from one another but that their traffic is governed: the faith judges the analysis's use, the analysis informs the faith's reading of society, and neither simply replaces the other. Whether governed traffic is a stable arrangement or a slow surrender by one side to the other is, once again, the question on which the whole assessment turns.

### What does the hermeneutic circle require a reader to do first?

Commit. In Juan Luis Segundo's account, the reader approaches scripture already engaged in changing a reality the reader has analysed, and that prior commitment changes the questions the text is asked and the answers it can give. The circle runs from commitment through analysis to interpretation and back to a deepened commitment, and it has no neutral entry point.

Segundo's circle is the point at which the school's method is most exposed to the objection that it reads its conclusions into the Bible rather than out of it, and Segundo, to his credit, stated the exposure himself. His defence was comparative: every reading of scripture proceeds from some prior understanding of the world, and the choice is not between a committed reading and an innocent one but between a reading whose commitments are acknowledged and examined and a reading whose commitments pass as common sense. The landowner's chaplain and the base community's catechist both bring a world to the text; the circle merely refuses to pretend otherwise. The defence has force, and it also has a limit that the school's critics in Rome identified correctly: a method that validates readings by their fruits in liberation needs a criterion for distinguishing liberation from its counterfeits, and the criterion cannot itself be supplied by the circle without circularity. The theologians located the criterion in the tradition, in the figure of Jesus and in the church's received teaching about human dignity. Whether that location is secure is a theological question, and this article, which adjudicates no theological questions, records it as the point where the school's method hands the argument back to theology proper.

## What was borrowed, piece by piece

Abstractions about borrowing become concrete when the borrowed items are examined one at a time, and the four adoptions in the table repay individual attention, because each did different work inside the theology and each attracted a different quality of objection.

Class analysis did the most immediate work. The Latin America the theologians confronted was conventionally described, in official and much academic discourse, in the vocabulary of national unity: one people, one culture, a shared destiny retarded by geography, tradition or the failings of the poor themselves. Class analysis cut through that vocabulary by asking who owned the land, who worked it, who set the prices, who commanded the army and who staffed the state, and by treating the answers as the explanation of the rest. Gutierrez's writings are saturated with the resulting picture: a continent divided between a minority integrated into the world economy's benefits and a majority whose labour sustained that integration at the price of its own impoverishment. The theological yield was direct. If the poor are poor because of where they stand in a structure of ownership and power, then poverty is not a natural condition to be consoled but an injustice to be dismantled, and the God who hears the cry of the oppressed in Exodus is not a metaphor for the process but a party to it. The objection, pressed by the Vatican's instruction and by sociologists of other schools, was that class in Latin America is cross cut by race, by region, by clientelism and by the informal economy in ways the borrowed categories flatten. The theologians' better work absorbed much of that objection as it matured; the categories were tools for seeing, and tools are judged by what they reveal and corrected by what they miss.

### How did class analysis change what counted as a theological fact?

It made structures visible to theology. Where older practice registered individual sins and individual needs, the borrowed analysis registered relations: landlord and tenant, exporter and labourer, creditor and debtor nation. Those relations, and the suffering they produced, became material theology was obliged to interpret, so that a land regime could be discussed as a theological fact and not the landowner's intentions alone.

Exploitation, the second adoption, supplied the mechanism inside the picture. The claim that the wealth of the few and the poverty of the many are not two facts but one relation, that value produced by the labour of the poor is appropriated by the owners of land, capital and credit, converted the moral rhetoric of concern for the poor into an account of why concern without structural change leaves the transfer running. In the theologians' hands the category also travelled upward from the workplace to the world economy: whole nations, they argued with the dependency school, are exploited through the terms on which they trade and borrow. The objection here came from economists as much as from churchmen, since the labour theory of value standing behind the classical category of exploitation is itself contested within economics, Marxist and otherwise. The theologians' response was pragmatic and remains available to their defenders: the pastoral and analytical work the category performed, directing attention to transfers of value and to the institutions that enforce them, does not stand or fall with every detail of the value theory in which Marx housed it. Critics reply that a category detached from its theory is a metaphor with footnotes. The exchange is a compact instance of the whole dispute, and it recurs at every row of the table.

Ideology critique, the third adoption, was the borrowing that made the movement dangerous to its own institution, because its first application was inward. If the dominant ideas of a society tend to legitimate the order that produces them, then the preaching of patience to the poor, the presentation of hierarchy as providence and the depiction of the existing distribution of land as the natural order all stand under suspicion as ideology, however sincere the preachers. The liberation theologians turned that suspicion on the church's own history in the continent: its alliance with conquest, its blessing of oligarchies, its counsel of resignation. They also turned it on the developmentalist common sense of the postwar decades and on the national security doctrine of the military regimes, which they analysed as the ideology of an order that required the poor to be policed. The objection from Rome was precise: ideology critique is a solvent that cannot be confined to approved targets, and a theology that teaches the faithful to unmask the interests behind doctrines will eventually be turned, by its own students, on the doctrines of the faith itself. The theologians answered that truth has nothing to fear from the unmasking of falsehood and that the gospel itself performs ideology critique on the religion of its time. The answer is stirring, and it does not fully meet the objection, which concerns not truth but authority: a church whose teaching office rests on trust in received doctrine is changed, perhaps irreversibly, when suspicion becomes a habit of its own schools. That change, for good and ill, is part of the movement's legacy.

### What did dependency analysis give the theologians that charity could not?

A location for the cause. Charity addresses the victim of poverty at the point of suffering; dependency analysis addressed the relation that produced it, the terms of trade, the structure of landholding, profit and interest flowing from periphery to centre. It converted benevolence into a question about systems and gave the option for the poor an economics to match its ethics.

Dependency deserves its sentence of separate emphasis because it is the borrowing that most distinguishes the Latin American theology from a merely moral protest, and because it was regional before it was Marxist. The analysis had been developed by the continent's own economists and sociologists, from the structuralism of the United Nations commission in Santiago through the sharper Marxist versions of the nineteen sixties, and the theologians took it up as the social science of their own place. The full account of that school, its variants and its fortunes, belongs to the companion study of [dependency theory in Latin America](/2019/01/15/dependency-theory-latin-america/), and is not repeated here. What matters for the theology is the use made of it: dependency supplied the causal story that connected the base community's discussion of a village's water supply to the structure of the world economy, and so made the movement's characteristic movement between the local and the structural something more than rhetoric. When the economics of dependency came under heavy academic fire in later decades, the theology built on its stronger claims was exposed to the same fire, a vulnerability the school's defenders answer by distinguishing the analysis's durable core, the asymmetry of centre and periphery, from the policy conclusions that were drawn from it in a particular decade.

## What was refused, and why the refusal was sincere

The lower rows of the borrowing table are as important as the upper ones, and they are the rows most often skipped by commentators who find the story of Christians borrowing from Marx dramatic enough without the complication that the borrowers refused the system's core. The refusal was neither decorative nor tactical, and understanding its grounds is necessary to understanding the movement.

Historical materialism as a philosophy, the claim that the whole of human reality is matter in motion and that consciousness, religion included, is the reflex of material production, was refused because the theologians' faith is a claim about that whole: that reality is created, that persons bear a dignity no production relation confers or removes, that history is open to a transcendence which judges every order including any order the liberation might build. To accept the philosophy would not have been to add an instrument to the theology; it would have been to replace the theology. Atheism was refused for the same reason in its direct form. The God of the movement's preaching is not a symbol for the class struggle or a name for human solidarity, and the theologians said so in terms their Marxist critics found frankly disappointing. Gutierrez's work is explicit that faith in God is the deepest root of the commitment to liberation, not its packaging, and that a liberation which ended in the denial of God would have lost, by its own account, the source of its hope and the ground of the poor's inviolable worth.

### Why was historical materialism refused as a philosophy while used as a method?

Because the two answer different questions. As a method, the materialist attention to production, ownership and interest explains how a society works and stands by its findings. As a philosophy, it pronounces on what reality is, and that pronouncement contradicts the faith at its foundation. The refusal marks the exact border the mediation distinction draws.

The sincerity of the refusal is best evidenced by the costs it imposed on the movement from the other side. A theology that had simply converted would have been welcomed, however warily, by the region's Marxist organisations; a theology that borrowed the analysis while refusing the philosophy was suspect to both camps and fully owned by neither. The theologians accepted that position, the position of a borrower who will not move into the lender's house, as the price of their project, and they theorised it as fidelity rather than compromise. There is also a theological argument inside the refusal that deserves recording, since it shows the refusal arising from the movement's own logic rather than from institutional caution. If the poor are subjects and not objects, if their faith is a reality and not a reflex, then a philosophy which explains their faith away as the product of their misery has misunderstood the very people the movement exists to accompany. The theologians had sat in too many base communities, listening to readings of Exodus by people whose belief was the strongest thing in the room, to accept an account on which that belief was merely the echo of an empty stomach. Their materialism, if the word is to be used at all, stopped at the door of the community's prayer, and the stopping was principled: the analysis serves the people, and where the analysis contradicts the people's own self understanding, the theologians chose the people.

### What happened to the doctrine of sin when structures were analysed?

It acquired a social dimension without surrendering its personal one. The theologians spoke of structural sin and of sinful structures: arrangements of ownership, law and force that produce death dealing outcomes whatever the intentions of those operating them. Personal conversion remained necessary, but no longer sufficient as an account of evil in a world of latifundios and death squads.

The doctrine of structural sin is the point where the borrowing most visibly transformed the theology's content rather than merely its vocabulary, and it is therefore the point where Rome's anxiety was most intelligible. Classical moral theology located sin in the will of the sinner; a structure cannot will, and so, the objection ran, structural sin is a metaphor that dissolves responsibility into systems and converts the church's moral teaching into sociology. The theologians' answer drew on resources inside the tradition itself: scripture speaks of powers and principalities, of a world lying in wickedness, of Pharaoh's Egypt as an order and not only a man; and the tradition had always recognised that unjust laws bind their makers and beneficiaries in a guilt not exhausted by any single act. The 1986 instruction, as will be seen, moved partway toward the theologians on this ground, accepting the language of structures of sin with careful qualifications about its analogical character. The exchange is one of the places where the dispute produced genuine theological development rather than mere victory and defeat, and it illustrates why the movement cannot be assessed as politics in religious dress: its central fights were about the content of doctrines, conducted in the vocabulary of the tradition, with consequences for how millions of believers understood their own moral world.

## The base communities: where the theology was actually practised

If the borrowing table is the movement's intellectual exhibit, the base ecclesial communities are its social exhibit, and any assessment that treats the theology as a literature will fail at exactly this point. A base ecclesial community, comunidad eclesial de base in Spanish, comunidade eclesial de base in Portuguese, was a small group of Christians, typically neighbours in a poor district or a village, who met regularly to read the Bible, to pray, to discuss the conditions of their common life and to organise responses to them: a cooperative, a campaign for a school or a clinic, a defence against eviction, a union, a mothers' club, a literacy circle. The groups were led by lay people, often women, visited intermittently by priests or religious who served many communities at once, and connected, in principle and often in fact, to the parish and the diocese above them. They spread from the late nineteen sixties, first and most densely in Brazil, and then across the continent, until historians of the movement describe the network as very extensive, a characterisation associated with the sociological literature on the movement, including Michael Löwy's work on religion and politics in the region. In accordance with the discipline this series applies to contested numbers, no count is asserted here; the scale that matters is qualitative, and it is not in dispute: for a generation, the communities were the largest school of popular organisation in Latin America that was not a party, a union or a state agency.

### What happened inside a base community meeting?

A passage of scripture was read aloud, from Exodus, the prophets or the gospels, and the group worked through a disciplined sequence: what the text said, what in their own situation it illuminated, and what action followed. The method joined interpretation to organisation, so that a reading of Exodus could end with a rota for digging a well.

The method had a pedigree and a name. Its skeleton was the see judge act discipline inherited from Catholic Action: observe the reality, judge it in the light of the gospel, act on the judgement. Its liberation form added the social analysis this article has been describing, so that the judging stage could draw on an account of why the reality was as it was, and it added a characteristic circularity: the action taken changed the reality, which was then observed again with the text in hand. Participants described the experience, in testimony collected across several countries, as learning to read two books at once, the book of the Bible and the book of their own lives, each interpreting the other. The description is homely and exact. A community that reads the story of the Exodus as the story of a people delivered from forced labour will not read its own landlord's ledger in the same way again, and a community that has organised a cooperative will bring questions to the parable of the talents that no commentary anticipated. The theology's celebrated hermeneutic circle, which in Segundo's writings can look like an academic construction, existed in the communities as a weekly practice before it existed as a theory.

### Why did scripture reading produce organisation rather than only devotion?

Because the readers were those whose conditions it concerned, in groups where every member could speak, and the method required the session to end in a decision. Devotion consoles the individual; this practice assigned tasks to the collective. That text, read in a parish hall, produced piety; read in a circle of neighbours with an agenda, it produced committees.

The communities' significance for the larger argument of this article is that they are where the mediation distinction was lived rather than merely stated. The analysis arrived in the community not as doctrine but as a set of questions and distinctions carried by a catechist, a pastoral agent or a visiting religious: who owns this, who profits from that, why does the price move, what does the law actually say. The faith arrived not as a system but as the community's own prayer, its saints, its dead, its reading of the text. The two met in the discussion, and the meeting produced the characteristic judgements of the movement at a scale no publishing programme could have achieved: that the landowner's power was a structure and not a fate, that the mayor's neglect was a policy and not a misfortune, that the community itself was a subject capable of acting on both. Marxist critics who doubted that the theology's separation of analysis from philosophy could hold in the seminar room were answered, in effect, by the parish hall: the separation held, for decades and at scale, in the practice of people who had never heard the word mediation. That is not a refutation of the critics, since practice can hold together what theory cannot justify, but it is evidence about the kind of thing the separation was: not a debating device but a description of how the movement actually worked.

### How did the communities relate to the parish and the bishop?

Unevenly, and the unevenness belongs to their history. Where bishops encouraged them, the communities were the parish's capillaries, supplied with materials, visited by clergy and represented in planning. Elsewhere they survived on toleration, in some places against suspicion that they were political cells in devotional dress. Their formal status, small groups within the church's ordinary structures, both protected and constrained them.

The communities also altered the position of women in the popular church in ways the movement's own literature was slow to theorise. The majority of community animators, the lay leaders who convened the meetings, kept the groups alive between clerical visits and carried their concerns into the federations the communities formed, were women, and the experience of speaking, interpreting and deciding in the circle was, for many, the first experience of public authority their society had offered them. Feminist theologians within and beyond the movement, among them the Cuban American scholar Ada Maria Isasi Diaz with her account of mujerista theology, the theology arising from the daily struggles of Hispanic women, pressed the school to recognise that the poor whose option it proclaimed were not an undifferentiated subject and that patriarchy structured the communities as surely as class did. The pressure produced some of the school's most honest internal argument, and it stands as evidence that the hermeneutic circle could correct its own practitioners: the method that taught communities to interrogate the structures around them could not permanently exempt the structures within them.

The decline of the network, when it came, had several causes that compounded one another. Episcopal appointments through the nineteen eighties and nineties placed many dioceses under bishops who withdrew encouragement, materials and clergy from the communities or redirected their energies toward purely devotional ends. The political openings of the same decades drew experienced organisers into parties, unions, municipalities and non governmental organisations, where their skills commanded salaries and their energies were absorbed. The Pentecostal churches, growing fast in the same neighbourhoods, offered the poor a religious home that made fewer demands on their time and promised more immediate consolations, and many community members or their children migrated there. And the communities' own success changed them: a group founded to win a water supply that has won it must find a second purpose or become a memory. By the end of the century the network that remained was a fraction of its former extent, though its alumni staffed a remarkable share of the continent's popular movements, unions and left parties, a diffusion of trained organisers that may prove the communities' most durable historical effect.

## The option for the poor: a structural commitment, not a charity

The preferential option for the poor is the movement's most successful export, a phrase that travelled from the Medellin and Puebla documents into the ordinary teaching vocabulary of the worldwide church, and its very success has blurred its original edge. In the theologians' usage the option is not a recommendation of generosity. It is a claim about where God stands and therefore about where the church must stand: with the poor, not as benefactor above them but as companion among them, and against the structures that make them poor. Its scriptural warrants were read as a pattern rather than a proof text: the Exodus, in which God hears the cry of an enslaved people; the prophets, for whom the treatment of the widow, the orphan and the stranger is the test of the covenant; the Magnificat's reversal of the mighty and the lowly; the judgement scene of Matthew's gospel, in which the nations are assessed by their treatment of the least. The option is preferential rather than exclusive, a formula the documents were careful to state: God's love is universal, and the preference names an order of attention and of solidarity, not a restriction of grace. And it is an option for the poor as subjects, which is the clause that separates it from philanthropy. The poor of the option are not the occasion of the church's virtue; they are the agents of their own liberation, and the church's task is accompaniment, a word the movement made technical: to walk with, at the pace of the accompanied, under their direction in the matters that are theirs.

### Is the option for the poor a form of charity?

No, and the distinction is the phrase's whole point. Charity relieves poverty's consequences and leaves the producing structures intact; the option commits the church to the poor as a class and a people, to their organisation, their agency and the transformation of the structures. A food parcel enacts charity; a land campaign accompanied by the church enacts the option.

The structural reading of the option explains both its power and the resistance it met. A commitment to charity costs the comfortable money; a commitment to the poor as agents costs them position, since the poor organised are the poor making claims, on land, on wages, on the budget and on the state. Bishops who proclaimed the option in the language of Puebla sometimes discovered that their own dioceses contained communities taking the proclamation literally, and the history of the following decades includes a long, quiet negotiation between the phrase as teaching and the phrase as practice. Rome's own handling of the phrase is the clearest index of its success: the 1986 instruction received the preferential option into the church's doctrinal vocabulary, glossed as primacy of a special form within the exercise of Christian charity, a formulation that honoured the commitment while relocating it within the virtue the theologians had distinguished it from. The relocation was not lost on the movement, and the difference between the two glosses, primacy in charity versus solidarity with a subject people, remains a live marker of the two readings of the movement that this article has tracked throughout.

## Institutionalised violence: the phrase that named an order

Among the Medellin documents' contributions, one phrase did more work than any other in the movement's subsequent vocabulary. The conference's document on peace observed that the continent's peoples lived under forms of injustice so systematic that the situation itself could be called institutionalised violence: violence not as an event but as a condition, embedded in the normal functioning of institutions, producing its toll of stunted lives and early deaths without any single act that a court could name. The phrase inverted the usual distribution of the word. Governments of the period reserved violence for the actions of their opponents; the bishops applied it to the order the governments defended, and drew the conclusion that the first violence, the violence of the structure, was the provocation to which other violences responded. The document did not license those responses; it condemned the spiral entire. But by naming the order itself violent it destroyed the moral asymmetry on which the national security states depended, the asymmetry between a peaceful order and the violent few who disturbed it.

### What distinguishes structural sin from a sum of personal sins?

Its location and its persistence. A sum of personal sins ends when the sinners repent; a structural sin is carried by arrangements, a land code, a labour regime, a pattern of policing, that produce unjust outcomes through people who may intend none of it. Such arrangements, the theologians held, are built by human decisions and are therefore morally chargeable.

The phrase travelled because it solved a problem of description that everyone working in the continent's poor districts recognised. The child dead of a treatable disease in a district without a clinic, the labourer crippled in a mine whose owners had declined every safety measure, the family evicted by a title they could not read: none of these deaths and losses appeared in any ledger of violence, and all of them were as systematic as a budget. Institutionalised violence gave the pattern a name and a moral address. Its critics objected, reasonably, that the concept dilutes the meaning of violence and smuggles a political programme into a moral category, and that a condition, unlike an act, has no agent to repent or to punish. The theologians' answer has been given above in the school's doctrine of structural sin: arrangements are made and remade by decisions, and the responsibility is real though distributed. The exchange matters beyond the period, because the phrase and its cognates passed from the conference documents into the general vocabulary of social criticism in the region, where they continue to do the work of insisting that an order be judged by its routine outcomes and not only by its spectacular crimes.

## Romero and the Salvadoran church: the movement under fire

The movement's ideas were argued in books and conferences, but they were tested in places where the argument carried mortal stakes, and no place tested them harder than El Salvador in the late nineteen seventies. The country's land structure was among the most concentrated on the continent, its politics dominated by an alliance of military governments and landed families, and its popular organisations, peasant leagues, unions, student groups and base communities among them, were met by escalating repression from security forces and from paramilitary bodies operating in their shadow. Church workers stood inside that confrontation by the logic of the option itself: catechists and community organisers were among the repressed, and priests who denounced the repression from the pulpit found their parishes watched and their colleagues threatened. In 1977 Oscar Romero, a conservative churchman by reputation, was appointed Archbishop of San Salvador, an appointment the country's rulers welcomed as a brake on the activist clergy. The killing of his friend, the Jesuit Rutilio Grande, who had organised peasants in the parish of Aguilares and was assassinated with two companions on the road to celebrate Mass, converted the new archbishop. Romero became the country's most prominent defender of the persecuted: his Sunday homilies, broadcast nationally, documented the week's abuses and named the disappeared; his archdiocese's legal aid office recorded what the state denied; and he wrote to the president of the United States asking that military aid to the Salvadoran government be halted, on the ground that it was being used against the people he served.

### Who was Oscar Romero, in the movement's own terms?

A bishop formed by the option under pressure of events. Romero was no theorist and began as a critic of its enthusiasms; the murder of Grande and the repression of his flock made him its witness. He was assassinated in March 1980, celebrating Mass in a chapel, and the movement counts him its martyr and proof of its seriousness.

The assassination is reported here as the brief of this article requires: factually, and without elaboration of its circumstances. Romero was shot on 24 March 1980 as he celebrated Mass, the day after a homily in which he had appealed to the soldiers of his country, in the name of God, to disobey orders to kill their brothers. His funeral in the cathedral plaza was itself attacked, and the civil war that followed consumed the country for twelve years. The Salvadoran church's ordeal did not end with its archbishop. In November 1989, soldiers of an elite battalion murdered Ignacio Ellacuria, the rector of the Central American University and one of the school's most original minds, together with other Jesuits of his community and two women who worked in their residence. Ellacuria had spent the war arguing, against both the insurgency's maximalists and the government's, for a negotiated settlement, and his university had made itself the war's unwelcome analyst. The killings were intended, on the evidence later assembled, to silence that analysis. Within the movement's memory the two events frame the Salvadoran chapter: a bishop killed at the altar for preaching the option, and a theologian killed in his house for practising the analysis. The church's later judgement on Romero is a matter of public record: he was beatified in 2015 and canonised in 2018, recognised by the institution that had once kept his cause waiting for decades.

What the Salvadoran chapter establishes for the assessment of the movement is the seriousness of its theology under the conditions the theology described. It is one thing to write of institutionalised violence in a study; it is another to document it weekly, by name, in a country where documentation was treated as subversion. Romero's homilies were, in form, exactly what the school's method prescribed: the week's praxis, the arrests, the disappearances, the strikes and the massacres, reflected upon in the light of the Sunday readings and returned to the congregation as judgement and as consolation. The archbishop who performed that reflection had not been trained in the school and did not use its vocabulary of mediation; he arrived at its practice by the road of pastoral fidelity. For the movement, that convergence mattered more than any book. It suggested that the theology was not an import imposed on the Latin American church but a name for what that church, at its most faithful, was already doing, and it is a large part of why the school's claims about the poor as theological subjects persuaded people who never opened Gutierrez.

## Priests in office: Nicaragua and the boundary question

If El Salvador tested the movement under persecution, Nicaragua tested it in power, and the test raised the boundary question the school had deferred since Camilo Torres: what may a priest do with a revolution that has won? The Sandinista movement that overthrew the Somoza dictatorship in 1979 included Christians in its ranks in significant numbers, formed in the base communities and the student movements, and its government included priests in senior office: Miguel d'Escoto as foreign minister, Ernesto Cardenal as minister of culture, Fernando Cardenal directing the literacy campaign and then education, and others in lesser posts. The priests defended their service with the movement's own logic. The option for the poor, they argued, does not expire when the poor's representatives form a government; accompaniment that stops at the palace gate is accompaniment of a cause only while the cause is losing. The Vatican's judgement was the opposite: priestly office and political office are incompatible, the priests were ordered to resign their posts, and their refusal produced a long standoff that included the celebrated public rebuke of Ernesto Cardenal by Pope John Paul II during the papal visit to Managua in 1983.

The episode deserves its place in an assessment of the theology rather than merely of church politics, because it exposed a genuine lacuna in the school's theory. The mediation distinction governed the borrowing of analysis; it said little about the assumption of power. The theologians' writings on the church and the political order assumed, in the main, a church accompanying popular movements from a position outside the state, and the Nicaraguan priests who entered the state were improvising beyond the theory, with arguments drawn from the option's logic rather than from any worked out doctrine of priestly office under a revolutionary government. Their critics within the movement, and there were such, worried that the church's critical distance, the freedom to judge every order including a friendly one, was being traded for portfolios. Their defenders replied that the distance had always been a luxury of the uncommitted. The argument was never resolved inside the school, and the Sandinistas' electoral defeat in 1990 closed the experiment before a resolution was forced. What remains is the question, which the school bequeathed to every successor movement: whether a theology of accompaniment can survive accompaniment's success, and what its practitioners owe a revolution once the revolution can give them ministries.

The Nicaraguan years also produced the period's most intense argument about the church itself, in the claim, heard from some Sandinista Christians and their supporters, that the authentic church was the iglesia popular, the people's church born from the base communities, set against a hierarchy aligned with the old order. Rome treated that claim as the gravest ecclesiological danger in the whole movement, a church within the church constituted by political commitment rather than by sacrament and office, and the 1984 instruction addressed it directly. The theologians of the school, Boff most prominently, had indeed argued that the church is born from below, from the faith of the people, as well as from above, and that the base communities were not a pastoral technique but an ecclesial reality with its own dignity. The distance between that argument and the slogan of a parallel people's church was real but easily collapsed in polemic, and much of the Vatican's case against the movement was built in the space the collapse created. Sorting the school's argued ecclesiology from the slogans attributed to it is one of the tasks the borrowing table's discipline exists to perform, and it is a sorting the assessment below attempts to maintain.

## The Vatican instructions: two documents, two tones

The Roman response to the movement arrived in two formal instructions from the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, issued in 1984 and 1986 under the authority of Pope John Paul II and the signature of the congregation's prefect, Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger. The documents are routinely described in summary as a condemnation followed by a softening, and the description is not false, but it misses what makes the pair worth reading as documents with arguments. The first instruction is a critique with a defined object: not the aspiration to liberation, which it affirms, and not the option for the poor, which it endorses in its own terms, but the use of Marxist analysis by certain theologies of liberation, which it argues cannot be purified of the ideology that produced it. The second instruction is a constructive statement of the church's own doctrine of liberation, which receives several of the movement's central themes, the option for the poor among them, into official teaching while fencing them with qualifications. Read together, the pair is the record of an institution absorbing a challenge by distinguishing, within it, what it would condemn, what it would correct and what it would claim as its own.

### What did the first instruction warn against, precisely?

The 1984 instruction warned that Marxist analysis arrives with an atheistic ideology inseparably attached: that class struggle becomes a law of all history, that sin is reduced to its social forms, that the church is reimagined as a political people, and that truth is subordinated to revolutionary practice. Its target was an unborrowable method, not the hunger for justice it honoured.

The argument of 1984 deserves the respect of exact statement, because it is the strongest form of the Roman objection this article must later answer. The instruction concedes the scandal that prompted the movement: the poverty of the continent, the urgency of liberation, the legitimacy of the theologians' indignation. It then argues that the instrument they chose is not an instrument. Marxist analysis, in the congregation's account, is a totalising conception of history in which the class struggle is the fundamental law, atheism is not an appendage but a premise, and every reality, religion included, is interpreted by its function in the struggle. To borrow the analysis is therefore to import the conception, whatever the borrower's intentions, and the consequences follow with logical necessity: salvation history collapses into political history, the Eucharist becomes a celebration of struggle, the poor cease to be persons and become a class bearing a messianic role, and the church's hierarchy is displaced by a people's church generated by commitment. The instruction's most quoted sentence warns that a tree is known by its fruits, and its practical demand was that theologians cease using concepts borrowed from Marx without submitting them to a critique the congregation believed could not stop short of abandoning them. This is not the document of an institution indifferent to the poor; it is the document of an institution that believed the remedy proposed would, in the end, betray the poor it claimed to serve, by subordinating their faith to a theory and their church to a party. The assessment of the movement stands or falls on whether that argument about inseparability is sound, and the argument is taken up in full in the objections section below.

### How did the second instruction differ in tone and judgement?

The 1986 instruction on Christian freedom and liberation wrote from inside the movement's own theme. It affirmed liberation as a central biblical reality, received the option into official teaching under careful glosses, accepted a qualified language of structures of sin, and warned chiefly against reducing liberation to a purely earthly programme.

The second instruction's constructive character changed the dispute's terrain. After 1986, no party to the argument could claim that Rome had rejected liberation as a theological theme; the theme had been dogmatically housed, with the Exodus as its paradigm and the option for the poor as its pastoral consequence. What remained in dispute was narrower and, in a way, more difficult: the permissible means of analysis, and the ecclesial standing of the communities and theologians who had carried the theme before Rome housed it. The movement's historians note the sequence without bitterness in their better moments: the themes travelled upward, from the base communities through the theologians and the episcopal conferences into the universal teaching office, while the carriers of the themes were disciplined. Whether that sequence is the normal metabolism of a doctrinal tradition, in which every living idea is first a disturbance and then a commonplace, or a specific injustice done to specific people, is a judgement this article leaves to the reader's weighing of the cases that follow. The cases begin with the school's most prominent theologian after Gutierrez.

## The silencing of Boff and the long institutional turn

Leonardo Boff's book on the church, published in Portuguese in 1981 as Igreja: Carisma e Poder, argued that the church's institutional order, its hoarding of sacred power in the ordained hierarchy, stood in tension with the liberation the church preached, and that the base communities embodied a different distribution of charism and authority. The doctrinal congregation summoned its author to Rome in 1984, examined him in a colloquy whose other principal was the congregation's prefect, and in 1985 imposed on him a period of obedient silence, a disciplinary measure suspending his teaching and publishing. The silencing became the movement's emblematic Roman encounter: the theologian of a church born from below, muted by the church governed from above, in a proceeding whose form illustrated his thesis even as its outcome refuted his hopes. Boff submitted to the measure, resumed writing when it lapsed, and in 1992, facing a second intervention, left the Franciscan order and the exercise of the priesthood, continuing as a lay theologian and writer. The school's other figures were handled by quieter instruments: investigations opened and left open, teaching permissions weighed, episcopal protectors replaced. Jon Sobrino received a notification concerning his Christological works decades later, in 2007, which criticised formulations while stopping short of condemnation. Gutierrez, notably, was never condemned, a fact his defenders cite and his critics explain by the care of his formulations; the founding text's threefold scheme of liberation, examined earlier in this article, had been built to withstand exactly the scrutiny it received.

The institutional turn against the movement was executed less by documents than by appointments. Through the nineteen eighties and nineties, the episcopal sees of Latin America were filled, vacancy by vacancy, with men formed in a more cautious mould than the generation of Medellin, and the continental episcopal council's own leadership had passed, already in the nineteen seventies, to figures openly critical of the liberation current. A diocese is governed by its bishop in the Catholic order, and a bishop unsympathetic to base communities could starve them of clergy, materials and legitimacy without issuing a single prohibition. The movement's organisational base contracted under that pressure in diocese after diocese, while its ideas, detached from their carriers, continued their upward journey into textbooks, seminary curricula in moderated form, and the church's official social vocabulary. The pattern is familiar from the history of other movements inside large institutions, and it is neither simple suppression nor simple victory. The institution declined to be transformed at the speed and in the direction the movement proposed; it also declined, in the end, to surrender the movement's central words, and it kept the words while retiring the analysis that had given them their edge.

To that institutional account, honesty requires adding the causes of decline that were not Rome's doing. The collapse of the socialist states of Eastern Europe at the end of the nineteen eighties removed, for many Latin Americans as for many others, the horizon within which a structural alternative to the existing order had seemed historically available, and the theology that had borrowed its analysis from the Marxist tradition felt the chill of that tradition's general crisis. The military regimes gave way to elected governments whose economic programmes, through the nineteen nineties, largely ratified the market order the movement had analysed as the problem, and popular energies migrated toward the immediate and the local. The Pentecostal expansion among the continent's poor, the most consequential religious fact of the period, drew millions from the Catholic popular world the base communities had served, offering healing, discipline and hope in a register the liberation parishes rarely matched. And the movement's own founding generation aged without a successor generation of equal weight formed in the same crucible of dictatorship, exile and episcopal encouragement. A movement is a mortal thing; the assessment of this one must weigh its ideas, which travelled, separately from its organisation, which did not survive the conditions that had created it, and the weighing is more respectful to both than a narrative of pure martyrdom or pure failure.

## The counter-example: what the movement does to the Marxist theory of religion

The movement's significance is usually assessed inside church history, but its sharpest theoretical interest lies on the other side of the borrowing, in what it does to the theory it borrowed from. The classical Marxist account of religion, descending from the young Marx's writings of 1844 and systematised by his followers, assigns religion two functions in a class society: consolation, the imaginary compensation for real suffering, summed in the famous description of religion as the opium of the people; and legitimation, the sanctification of the existing order and of the classes it serves. On that account, religion is expected to reconcile the oppressed to their oppression and to wither as its material basis is transformed. The full treatment of Marx's own, more ambivalent texts is the business of the companion study of [Marx on religion](/2021/06/01/marx-on-religion-explained/), and the present article takes the classical account in the form in which the movement's contemporaries held it. Against that account, liberation theology stands as a documented counter-example of a specific and damaging kind. Here was religion, in its most institutional form, a hierarchical church with a landed history, functioning as the organising grammar of resistance to the existing order: its texts supplying the indictment, its communities supplying the organisation, its clergy supplying, in some cases, the martyrs. The consolation function did not disappear, but it was not doing what the theory required of it. A theory that predicts religion's service to domination must explain a generation in which the continent's most effective popular organisation wore a crucifix.

### Which version of the Marxist theory of religion does the movement refute?

The functionalist version, in which religion necessarily consoles and legitimates. The movement does not touch the subtler observation, present in Marx's own sentence, that religion is also the sigh of the oppressed and a form of protest against suffering. It refutes the claim that protest is religion's accidental disguise rather than one of its standing possibilities, activated by determinate conditions.

The distinction in that answer is the analytical yield of the whole encounter, and it is the reason the series pattern this article instantiates is the counter-example pattern: a phenomenon a theory does not accommodate, whose value lies in specifying precisely which version of the theory it refutes. Liberation theology does not refute the claim that religion often legitimates domination; its own ideology critique of the colonial and oligarchic church asserts that claim more thoroughly than most Marxist writing on the region. Nor does it overturn the observation that religion consoles; the base communities consoled their members through funerals, illnesses and defeats as a matter of weekly routine. What it refutes is the necessity in the classical account, the inference from religion's frequent functions to its essence. Under conditions the movement itself specified, a church with a popular base, a clergy formed by proximity to the poor, a text read by the poor themselves, religion's protest function moved from the margin of the theory to the centre of the facts. A Marxism that cannot register that movement has mistaken a historical generalisation for a law, which is, by the standards of Marx's own method, the characteristic error of ideology.

### Why did a Marxist scholar treat the movement as a counter-example?

Because his own tradition's categories failed on his own continent. Michael Löwy, a Brazilian born Marxist sociologist working in France, argued in his 1996 study that the liberation current, which he named the Christianity of liberation, could not be explained as false consciousness or as clerical manipulation, and required Marxists to rebuild their account of religion and emancipation.

Löwy's treatment is the more telling for coming from inside the house. His category of the Christianity of liberation widened the lens from the theological school to the whole social movement, base communities, pastoral agencies, religious orders, sympathetic bishops and the theologians together, and he argued that this movement constituted the most significant popular mobilisation in the region since the nineteen thirties, a judgement he supported with the movement's role in forming the cadres of the Brazilian workers' party and unions, the Sandinista revolution's Christian base and the Salvadoran popular organisations. For the theory of religion, his conclusion was that the Latin American experience demonstrates an elective affinity, in the Weberian phrase he borrowed, between certain forms of religious culture and certain forms of resistance, an affinity as real as the affinity between other forms of religious culture and domination. Marxist analysis, to remain analysis, had to become a theory of that variation rather than a verdict on religion as such.

The philosophical bridge for such a rebuilding had been built earlier, and by a Marxist whose work the theologians also read: Ernst Bloch, whose principle of hope treated religion's utopian surplus, its images of exodus, kingdom and jubilee, as the stored imagination of a humanity not yet reconciled to its condition. Bloch's atheism was not in doubt, and neither was his judgement that the Bible contains a subversive inheritance that the churches had buried and that a materialism worthy of the name should claim rather than dismiss. The liberation theologians found in Bloch a Marxist who confirmed their own reading of their tradition's texts, and Marxist readers found in the theologians an unwelcome confirmation of Bloch. The full account of that strange alliance belongs to the study of [Ernst Bloch and Marxism](/2016/02/01/ernst-bloch-and-marxism/); here it is enough to register that the counter-example had been theoretically prepared from within Marxism itself, which is why the movement's challenge to the classical theory of religion could not be waved away as a misunderstanding by outsiders. The theory's own dissident wing had marked the place where the facts would arrive.

## Analogues elsewhere: the pattern repeats on other ground

A movement that claims to have found a general possibility, that faith can organise liberation rather than only console its absence, invites the comparative question, and the comparison was already available within the movement's own lifetime. Analogous theologies arose in several settings where a religious tradition confronted a structure of oppression its adherents could name, and their family resemblance to the Latin American school, alongside their independence from it, is evidence about the pattern's generality.

In South Korea, minjung theology emerged in the nineteen seventies under a military dictatorship, taking its name from the minjung, the mass of the people who are the subjects of history in Korean Christian reflection as the poor are in Latin American reflection. Its theologians, among them Ahn Byung-mu and Suh Nam-dong, read the Bible through the experience of the Korean people's suffering under successive dominations, developed the notion of the minjung as the bearer of a biography of han, the accumulated grief and resentment of the wronged, and stood with the labour and democracy movements against the regime, at the cost of dismissals and imprisonment. The Korean current borrowed less from Marxist analysis than its Latin American counterpart and more from the national narrative and the folk tradition, a difference that maps the different weight of Marxism in the two intellectual worlds; the series treatment of [Marxism in Japan and Korea](/2018/08/15/marxism-in-japan-and-korea/) gives the surrounding context. The structural identity with the Latin American pattern is nonetheless exact: a majority faith, a suffering people, a reading of the founding texts from underneath, and a church divided between its custodians and its witnesses.

### What did Korean minjung theology share with the Latin American current?

A method and a subject. The method was to read scripture from the experience of the oppressed and to treat that experience as a theological source; the subject was the minjung, the people in their suffering and their hope, standing where the poor stand in the Latin American texts. Both were disciplined by their churches and defended by popular movements.

In India, dalit theology arose in the nineteen eighties among Christian theologians of the communities stigmatised as untouchable, with Arvind Nirmal as its most cited founder, arguing that a theology for India must begin from the pathos of the dalits and that the dominant Indian Christian theology, formed by converts of high caste and by dialogue with classical Hindu philosophy, had been a theology of the oppressor in its silences. The Indian case is the closest structural analogue to the Latin American one and also its most instructive variation, because the structure to be analysed was caste, which the categories of class fit imperfectly and which the Indian Marxist tradition had itself debated for generations; the companion study of the [caste and class debate in India](/2018/02/15/caste-and-class-debate-india/) sets out that debate. Dalit theologians borrowed the liberation method, the reading from underneath, the insistence that theology is reflection on a people's struggle, while rebuilding its analysis on the ground of caste, and their work demonstrates both the portability of the method and the non portability of the specific analysis, which is precisely what the mediation distinction would predict: the instrument must be refitted to each reality, and the faith that wields it judges the refitting.

### How does dalit theology repeat the pattern on different ground?

By substituting the structure that organises its own people's suffering. Where the Latin American school analysed class and dependency, the dalit theologians analysed caste: a hierarchy sanctified by religion itself, which made their ideology critique sharper than the original, since the legitimating doctrines belonged to the religious world their converts had left and partly carried with them.

Beyond those two, the family extends in several directions, each member adapting the pattern to its ground. Black theology in the United States, announced by James Cone in the closing years of the nineteen sixties, read the gospel from the experience of black Americans and identified God's liberation with the liberation of the oppressed in terms Cone stated without mediation and with a polemical force the Latin Americans recognised as kin. In South Africa, the Kairos Document of 1985, signed by a broad group of theologians and church workers, distinguished a state theology blessing apartheid, a church theology preaching reconciliation without justice, and a prophetic theology that named the regime's order as sin, a taxonomy that is the Latin American ideology critique wearing South African dress. Feminist and womanist theologies across several continents applied the method to patriarchy inside and outside the churches. Palestinian theologians, Naim Ateek prominent among them, constructed a liberation theology for a people under occupation. The enumeration could continue, and its point is not completeness but weight: a pattern that reproduces itself, without central direction, wherever a scriptural faith meets a nameable structure of oppression and a generation willing to read from underneath, is not a Latin American accident. It is a standing possibility within the traditions involved, which the Latin American school was the first to theorise at full strength, and which any adequate theory of religion, Marxist or theological, must find room to explain.

## The objections from both directions, stated at full strength

The assessment of the movement now reaches the dispute on which everything else depends, and the dispute must be stated as its parties stated it, at full strength, before any answer is attempted. The mediation distinction claims that an analysis can be borrowed while its philosophy is refused. Two great objections deny the claim from opposite sides, and the symmetry between them is the most instructive fact in the whole controversy: each side argues that the separation is unstable, and each predicts that the borrower will slide, in time, toward the side's own position.

The Roman objection, in the form given it by the 1984 instruction, runs as follows. Marxist analysis is not a set of detachable techniques but a conception of the world, in which the struggle of classes is the motor and the truth of history, matter exhausts reality, and religion is a function of the relations of production, destined to disappear with them. The conception's parts imply one another: the analysis of exploitation presupposes the theory of history, the theory of history presupposes the materialism, and the materialism excludes the God of Abraham as firmly as it excludes the gods of the philosophers. A theologian who takes the analysis therefore takes, whether he knows it or not, a framework in which his faith has already been explained and assigned its place, and the pastoral consequences the instruction catalogued, the reduction of sin to structures, of salvation to revolution, of the church to a political people, of the sacraments to celebrations of struggle, are not abuses of the borrowing but its logic unfolding. The poor, in whose name the borrowing was made, are the final victims of the transaction, since they are offered, in place of the God who saves, a theory that uses their suffering as its evidence and their faith as its fuel. The objection concedes the borrower's sincerity and denies his possibility: the separation he asserts is one he cannot perform, because the instrument thinks, and it will think his theology for him if he lets it into the house.

The Marxist objection, pressed by critics within the tradition the theologians borrowed from, runs in mirror image. An analysis of society that locates the causes of poverty in relations of production and the dynamics of history in material interests cannot be sustained by a thinker who reserves, at the centre of the account, a transcendent agent whose action is exempt from the analysis. The liberation theologians' God, on this objection, is not an innocent addition to the analysis but a limit placed upon it: where the analysis would conclude that human beings make their own history under conditions they did not choose, and that their emancipation is their own work or nobody's, the theology interposes a salvation that arrives from beyond history and a church that mediates it. The result is an idealism with Marxist vocabulary, in which liberation is finally located in a conversion of hearts and a communion with God that the material analysis can neither locate nor verify, and in which the poor are asked to wait, once again, for a deliverance whose timetable is not theirs. The objection points, as its evidence, to the movement's own stopping places: its refusal of the conclusions about religion that its analysis implies, its retention of a hierarchy whose power its ecclesiology criticised but whose authority it obeyed, its silenced theologian submitting to the silencing. A materialism which exempts its user's deepest commitments from material explanation, the critics conclude, is not a mediation of materialism but its abandonment at the decisive point.

Between the two objections, the theologians' answer must now be given in its strongest available form. The answer is the mediation distinction itself, stated as a claim about what borrowed instruments are. An instrument of analysis, the answer runs, is a set of questions and categories whose warrant is their yield: class analysis is warranted where it explains what rival accounts leave unexplained, and its use commits the user to nothing beyond the explanations it actually delivers. The philosophy historically packaged with the instrument is a further set of claims, about reality as a whole, and those claims are neither delivered nor implied by the local explanations, any more than a physician's use of a thermometer commits him to the metaphysics of its inventor. Rome's objection proves too much: if every instrument carried its maker's whole philosophy, the church could not use Aristotle, whose God is not Abraham's, nor the historical criticism of scripture, whose pioneers were not believers, nor the dependency economics of a United Nations commission. The Marxist objection proves too much in the opposite direction: if every analysis must be totalised to be valid, then Marx's own practice fails the test, since Marx analysed the societies in front of him with categories he expected to be superseded and left the philosophy of the whole conspicuously underbuilt. The theologians' separation, on their account, is not a refusal to think but a refusal to totalise, grounded in their conviction that reality is larger than any analysis of it, and that the analysis serves a liberation whose deepest springs it does not exhaust.

The concession that honesty requires, and that this article's brief requires, must now be recorded without softening. Neither objection has been answered to its proponents' satisfaction. The Roman critics were not persuaded that the traffic between the mediations could be governed as Clodovis Boff proposed, and the history of some communities and some texts, in which the political reading did swallow the theological one, stands in the record as their evidence. The Marxist critics were not persuaded that a liberation whose final horizon is communion with God can be the self emancipation of the working class that their tradition describes, and the movement's obedience at the moments of discipline stands in the record as theirs. The separation the theologians asserted remains, at the level of theory, asserted rather than demonstrated: a distinction maintained in practice by communities and writers over decades, whose coherence as a piece of philosophy is still argued wherever the argument is taken up. That is not a verdict of failure. Many distinctions that organise real work, in theology and outside it, have exactly that status. But it is the accurate verdict, and an assessment of liberation theology that claims more for the mediation distinction than its authors could prove would repeat, in the school's favour, the error its critics committed against it: the substitution of a label for an argument.

## Scripture read from underneath: the biblical warrants

A theology that claims to borrow only its social analysis from outside must show that its theological substance comes from its own sources, and the liberation school devoted some of its most careful work to exactly that demonstration. The biblical reading at the movement's centre was not a decoration applied to conclusions reached elsewhere; it was, by the theologians' account, the origin of the option for the poor, which the analysis then served. The warrants were assembled as a pattern across the whole canon. The Exodus stands first, read not as a myth of national origin but as the political event in which God is revealed as the liberator of an enslaved labour force, taking the side of the oppressed against the empire that owned them, before any doctrine of God is stated. The legal codes of the Torah protect the widow, the orphan and the resident stranger with a repetitiveness the theologians treated as emphasis rather than accident. The prophets make justice to the poor the criterion of authentic worship, in passages, Amos on the festivals God hates, Isaiah on the fast God chooses, Micah on what the Lord requires, that the base communities could recite from use. The Magnificat sings of the mighty put down and the lowly lifted up. The ministry of Jesus is read as a practice before it is a doctrine: table fellowship with the excluded, the announcement of good news to the poor as the first line of his programme at Nazareth, and a death inflicted by the alliance of religious and imperial power. The judgement scene in the twenty fifth chapter of Matthew, in which the Son of Man identifies himself with the hungry, the imprisoned and the stranger, supplied the movement with its most uncompromising text: the treatment of the least is the treatment of Christ, and no account of salvation that brackets that identification survived contact with the communities' reading.

### What does the Exodus paradigm claim about God?

That God is known as a liberator in history. Before Israel confesses a creator it confesses a deliverer: the God who heard the cry of slaves and broke their bondage. The theologians built on the order of that knowledge, arguing that a people's experience of liberation is no illustration of a doctrine of God but its original source.

The reading had a discipline as well as a fire. The theologians were aware that a Bible ransacked for warrants becomes a mirror, and the better writers of the school subjected their own readings to the historical criticism they had learned in European faculties, distinguishing what the texts could bear from what the movement wished them to bear. The Exodus as paradigm, for instance, was defended not as a programme, ancient Israel's escape supplies no policy for a modern economy, but as a revelation of God's character and therefore of the direction in which faithfulness moves. Sobrino's Christology applied the same discipline to the gospels: the historical Jesus, his practice, his conflict and his execution, are the control on every later construction, and a Christology that cannot survive the cross as a political execution under empire has, on his account, already drifted into ideology. This exegetical seriousness is part of what separates the school from the devotional radicalism with which it is sometimes confused, and it is the ground of the school's reply when Rome charged it with reading scripture through Marx: the reading, the theologians answered, runs through the poor, whose presence in the text is not a Marxist discovery but a biblical one that comfortable centuries had learned not to see.

## A spirituality of liberation: prayer, wells and martyrdom

Movements of ideas are remembered by their books, but movements of people are held together by their practices of endurance, and the liberation current developed a spirituality as distinctive as its analysis. Gutierrez gave it its classic statement in a work of 1983 whose title, drawn from a saying attributed to Bernard of Clairvaux by way of the Cistercian tradition, announced its theme: We Drink from Our Own Wells. The book argues that a people engaged in liberation develops its own modes of prayer, celebration and mourning, and that these are not the fuel of the struggle but one of its fruits and one of its forms of knowledge. The spirituality of the base communities had recognisable marks: prayer that named the week's events and the community's dead; the celebration of the feasts of local martyrs and witnesses alongside the universal calendar; a reading of the passion in which the suffering of the people and the suffering of Christ interpreted one another without being confused; and an insistence, against every purely political account of the movement, that joy, fiesta and gratitude were theological acts and not respites from seriousness. The point matters for the assessment of the whole, because a spirituality is not an instrument that can be borrowed or a conclusion that can be totalised. The communities' prayer is the place where the Marxist objection, that the theology reserves an unanalysed sanctuary at its centre, meets its most concrete reply and its most concrete confirmation at once: the sanctuary exists, it is full of people, and what happens in it is the movement's reason for existing, which the analysis serves and does not replace.

### Why does the school treat martyrdom as a theological category?

Because its witnesses died for the content of the faith. A catechist killed for organising a cooperative and an archbishop killed for documenting repression died, on the school's reading, in witness to the God of life against the idols of death, its phrase for powers that demand human sacrifice. Martyrdom is no misfortune but evidence of what its faith was.

The vocabulary of idolatry in that answer deserves a sentence of expansion, since it is one of the school's original theological constructions and one of its clearest cases of analysis transposed into doctrine. Writers such as Hugo Assmann and Franz Hinkelammert argued that the structures the analysis described, the market absolutised, the national security state, function theologically as idols: powers that claim ultimacy and exact sacrifices, of the disappeared, of the starved, of the tortured, as the price of their maintenance. Against them the God of the Exodus and of Jesus is characterised as the God of life, whose worship is incompatible with the sacrifices the idols demand. The construction allowed the theologians to conduct their critique of the social order as an internal theological dispute, a contest between gods, rather than as a political programme with religious garnish, and it gave the base communities a language in which resisting an eviction and confessing the faith could be experienced, coherently, as the same act. Critics found the construction overheated and its application to economic systems indiscriminate; defenders replied that the biblical tradition's own polemic against idols was never a polite affair. The dispute is recorded here as part of the school's substance, another instance of the pattern this article has followed throughout: a borrowing transformed, in the using, into something the lender would not recognise as his own.

## The school's Christology: Jesus and the crucified peoples

Every theology is finally judged by its account of its centre, and the liberation school's centre was a Christology built, in Sobrino's phrase, from the underside. Its starting point was methodological: Christology must begin from the historical Jesus, from his practice and his fate, rather than from the dogmatic formulas read backward, because the formulas themselves claim to be about that history. Its material centre was the kingdom of God as Jesus announced it, good news to the poor, a reality that judges every present order and whose coming reorganises the values of the world. Its darkest category was the crucified peoples: the collectivities of the earth who bear in their bodies the sin of the world as Christ bore it, and who therefore, in a transposition the school defended from the suffering servant songs of Isaiah, mediate knowledge of God to the rest of humanity. And its account of the resurrection was correspondingly historical without being reductive: the resurrection is God's vindication of the crucified one and therefore the ground of hope for the crucified peoples, the warrant that the powers which crucify do not have history's last word.

### What is meant by calling the poor a crucified people?

It means that the suffering of the oppressed is not random misfortune but the product of identifiable powers, as the crucifixion was; that the victims are innocent of the fate imposed on them; and that God stands with them rather than with the powers. The phrase joins a Christological claim to an analytical one, the school's signature.

The Roman notification directed at Sobrino's work in 2007, which found errors and dangerous formulations in his treatment of the divinity of Christ and the uniqueness of his mediation, marks the point where the school's Christological concentration met its limit in the institution's judgement. Sobrino's defenders answered that the notification misread emphases as denials, and the exchange closed without condemnation of the author. The episode is included here because it completes the pattern established by the Boff case and the instructions: the institution policed the school's formulations at the points where they touched the doctrines of the church and the person of Christ, while receiving, in the same decades, the school's social emphases into its ordinary teaching. An assessment that treats the Roman response as mere politics cannot explain the precision of its targets; an assessment that treats it as mere theology cannot explain the appointments policy that accompanied it. Both registers ran together throughout, and the movement's history is unintelligible in either one alone.

## The church under the dictatorships: human rights as the option's second language

The movement's theology was formed in the years when most of the continent passed under military rule, and its practice acquired, under those regimes, a second language alongside the language of liberation: the language of human rights, documented, filed and defended case by case. The regimes justified themselves by a doctrine of national security in which the enemy was internal, subversion was a permanent condition, and the defence of western and Christian civilisation licensed the suspension of every legal restraint. The phrase deserves attention in an article about ideology critique, because the liberation theologians analysed the doctrine as precisely that: the ideology of an order, a story that converted the protection of a distribution of property into a sacred war and its torturers into civilisation's frontier guard. Against the doctrine, sectors of the church in several countries built the period's most durable institutions of resistance, and they built them from the option's own materials: parish networks, religious orders, and the trust of communities who would report a disappearance to a priest when they would report it to no one else.

In Chile after the coup of 1973, the archdiocese of Santiago under Cardinal Raul Silva Henriquez first sponsored an ecumenical committee for peace and then, when the regime forced that body's dissolution, replaced it with the Vicariate of Solidarity, an organ of the archdiocese itself and therefore harder to abolish. The Vicariate's lawyers filed writ after writ for the detained and disappeared, knowing most would fail, because the filing created a record; its social workers supported the families of the prisoners; its publications documented what the censored press could not; and its archive became, after the dictatorship, one of the evidentiary bases on which the country's truth commission worked. In Brazil, the archdiocese of Sao Paulo under Cardinal Paulo Evaristo Arns, together with the Presbyterian pastor Jaime Wright, secretly copied the military courts' own records to produce the clandestine report Brasil: Nunca Mais, a documentation of systematic torture assembled from the regime's paperwork and published as the regime ended. The Brazilian bishops' conference of those years, the most institutionally courageous in the region, defended land reform, indigenous peoples and the victims of the security apparatus in terms that owed their edge to the theology this article describes, even where individual bishops kept their distance from its school.

### Why did human rights work become the church's characteristic practice under the regimes?

Because it was the form the option took when every other form was criminalised. Organising could be treated as subversion and analysis as sedition, but the documentation of a disappearance, the visit to a prisoner, the support of his family were pastoral acts the regimes found hard to prohibit, preserving lives and the record on which later judgement depended.

The significance of this chapter for the assessment of the theology is considerable, and it cuts against two simplifications at once. Against the Roman simplification, in which the movement appears as a politicisation of faith that endangered the church, the human rights record shows the theology's adherents and their allies performing the church's most widely honoured work of the period, work for which the same institution later claimed credit. Against the romantic simplification, in which the movement figures as a revolutionary vanguard in clerical dress, the record shows its characteristic practice to have been legal, documentary and defensive, the filing of writs and the visiting of prisoners, under regimes whose violence it lacked the means and mostly the intention to answer in kind. The liberation church of the dictatorship years was neither a party nor an army. It was, in the phrase its members used, a voice for the voiceless, and the phrase's modesty should not conceal its cost: the voiceless were voiceless because speaking was punished, and the voice was supplied by people who knew the tariff and spoke anyway, in homilies, in filings and in the sheltering of the hunted. A theology is entitled to be judged, among other ways, by what its adherents did when doing was dangerous, and by that measure the school's account of itself, that faith and analysis together produce a particular kind of courage, is not the weakest of its claims.

## Christians for socialism: the Chilean experiment and the movement's left edge

The movement's left edge, the place where its adherents came closest to the fusion with Marxism that its method forbade, was Chile in the years of the Popular Unity government. A group of priests, many of them young and many working in the poblaciones of Santiago, issued in 1971 a declaration identifying themselves as Christians for socialism, and an international encounter under that name gathered in Santiago in 1972, drawing participants from across the continent and beyond. The Chilean Christians for socialism argued that the choice facing the continent's Christians was not between faith and revolution but between two revolutions, and that a Christianity which declined the socialist one had, by that decline, chosen the other side. Their texts pressed the school's shared premises harder than the school's centre would press them: the critique of capitalism became a commitment to a specific government's programme, and the analysis of the church's ideology became, in some mouths, an announcement that the institutional church was itself part of the apparatus to be dismantled.

The episode is instructive because it shows the mediation distinction under maximum load, and shows the school's centre holding it. Gutierrez and the movement's leading theologians maintained relations with the Chilean current and shared its diagnosis, but the school's characteristic positions, the church as the home of the struggle rather than its enemy, the refusal to identify liberation with any single political process, the insistence that the option outlasts every government that claims it, were stated in those years partly in answer to the Chilean temptation. The coup of September 1973 destroyed the experiment and scattered its participants into exile and clandestinity, and the argument about what it had been was never concluded. For the assessment, the Chilean chapter functions as the control case: it demonstrates that the slide Rome predicted, from borrowing to fusion, was a real possibility that real Christians enacted, and it demonstrates with equal clarity that the school's centre recognised the slide as a danger and argued against it from inside the same commitments. A distinction that is maintained against pressure from one's own side is not a fiction; it is a discipline, and disciplines are what distinctions look like when they are doing work.

## The church born of the people: the ecclesiological wager

Boff's silenced book deserves a section of its own, because its argument is the school's most consequential internal claim and the one that most alarmed the institution. The argument, stripped to its skeleton, is that the church's order is not exhausted by its hierarchy. The Spirit distributes charisms through the whole people of God; the base communities, in which lay people interpret scripture, organise their common life and celebrate their faith, are therefore not pastoral conveniences but church in the full sense, a church arising from the people themselves, from below as truly as the institution descends from above. Boff pressed the historical case: the concentration of sacred power in the clergy is a development with dates and causes, not an apostolic datum, and a church that preaches liberation while practising absolutism contradicts its message in its medium. The congregation's reply defended the given order as belonging to the church's divine constitution, and the colloquy of 1984 set the two ecclesiologies face to face in their least reconcilable forms.

### What did Boff mean by calling the base communities a new way of being church?

They were not a technique for delivering the church's message to the poor but a form of the church itself, in which the poor are subjects: interpreting the text, exercising charisms, generating ministries from their own ranks. The claim relocated ecclesial reality from the institution's centre to its base, which is why it drew the silencing.

The wager embedded in the argument is worth naming, because it explains the intensity on both sides. If the church can be born from the people, then the people's experience of faith, including their reading of their own oppression and their organisation against it, carries theological authority that no office can simply override, and the liberation theology's other claims, about the poor as subjects, about theology as the second act, follow from an ecclesial fact rather than from a methodological preference. If it cannot, then the communities are a programme, to be encouraged or wound down as pastors judge best, and the theology built on them is a literature after all. The institution's conduct across the following decades can be read as an attempt to concede the communities' value while denying the wager: to keep the groups as instruments of a pastoral strategy whose direction remains set from above. The communities' decline under that settlement, documented earlier in this article, is the practical verdict on the attempt, though whether the verdict condemns the settlement or the wager is a judgement on which the parties to this story remain, characteristically, divided.

## Where the theologians were formed: Europe, the worker priests and the Latin American return

The school's originality is sometimes exaggerated by its admirers into a creation from nothing, and the record of its authors' formation corrects the picture in a way that strengthens rather than weakens its interest. Gutierrez and his generation were formed in Europe, in the faculties of Louvain, Lyon, Rome and Paris, during the years when French and Belgian theology was undergoing its own renewal. They absorbed the nouvelle theologie's return to the sources, the historical consciousness of a Congar or a Chenu, and above all the French church's experiments in the world of labour: the worker priests who had taken factory jobs to evangelise a dechristianised proletariat, and the movement around Louis Joseph Lebret and his Economy and Humanism centre, which had made the study of underdevelopment a Christian discipline a decade before the theologians took it up. Gutierrez served as an adviser to Catholic student organisations in Latin America through the nineteen sixties, travelling the continent and meeting the generation of lay activists from whom the base communities' first organisers would come. The liberation theology that resulted is therefore not an import of European theology to Latin America, nor a purely indigenous growth. It is a return: European tools, a French concern for the working world and a Louvain historical method, carried home by Latin Americans and rebuilt under the pressure of a reality Europe had not supplied, in which the working world was a continent and the dechristianisation to be feared was not indifference but despair.

The same years produced, inside the Latin American clergy itself, the movements that formed the school's immediate constituency. In Peru, the National Office of Social Information and the circle of priests around it, known by the acronym ONIS, pressed the Peruvian church toward the positions Medellin would adopt. In Colombia, the Golconda group of priests issued statements of a radicalism that startled their bishops. In Argentina, the Movement of Priests for the Third World linked its members to the Peronist left and to the theology of the people, a distinctively Argentine current, associated with theologians such as Lucio Gera, which emphasised the people's culture and religiosity where the liberation school emphasised class analysis, a difference of accent that would matter for decades. In Brazil, the bishops of the northeast and the church's basic education movement, which taught literacy by radio with a method indebted to Paulo Freire, built the pastoral infrastructure the communities would inherit. Freire's pedagogy deserves its own sentence in any genealogy of the movement: his literacy work in Brazil, and his Pedagogy of the Oppressed, published as the nineteen sixties closed, supplied the concept of conscientisation and the practice of dialogue based education that the base communities absorbed as their native method, so that the school's theory of knowledge, learning by reflecting on one's own reality in common, had a pedagogical twin developed in the same years on the same ground.

### What did Freire's pedagogy contribute that theology alone could not?

A practice of knowing. Freire's circles taught adults to read by decoding the words naming their world: land, wage, hunger, vote, and thereby taught that their world could be read, questioned and rewritten. The communities took over the practice entire: the circle, the generative words, the refusal of the depositing teacher. Theology gave the practice its warrants; pedagogy gave it its hands.

The genealogy matters to the assessment because it complicates the story both camps preferred. Rome's account required the movement to be a Marxist import wearing a soutane; the formation record shows a movement whose sources were at least as French and Catholic as they were Marxist, and whose Marxist borrowings entered a structure already built by the council, by the worker priest tradition and by Freire. The romantic account required the movement to spring immaculate from the Latin American soil; the record shows its authors reading European theology in European libraries and its methods descending from a Belgian cardinal's youth movement. What the record actually supports is the school's own account of itself, the one this article has maintained: a Latin American construction, made from materials of several origins, under the pressure of a reality none of the materials had been designed for, and distinguished from its sources by the use to which the pressure put them.

## Three national shapes: Brazil, Peru and the isthmus

The school was a continental current, but it lived in national churches whose different situations gave it different shapes, and a reader who knows the three principal shapes knows the movement better than a reader who knows only its texts.

Brazil gave the movement its scale and its organisation. The Brazilian church was the continent's largest, its episcopal conference the most developed, and its northeast the region where bishops had first named underdevelopment a theological problem. The base communities reached their greatest density there, sheltered in many dioceses by bishops who regarded them as the church's future, and the Brazilian church's agencies for land, for indigenous peoples and for urban ministry gave the option for the poor an institutional machinery unmatched elsewhere. Brazil also gave the movement its most consequential political fruit: the organisers of the Workers' Party founded in 1980, and of the great union central that arose beside it, were formed in large numbers in the communities and the church's pastoral agencies, a transfer of trained cadres from the ecclesial to the political field that no other country's church performed on the same scale. Leonardo Boff's Franciscan province and the religious orders' networks made Brazil the school's publishing engine as well as its demographic base, and the Brazilian church's relative strength helps explain why the institutional turn, when it came, had to work there by appointments over decades rather than by any single act.

Peru gave the movement its founding text and its most rigorous mind, but a thinner organisation. The Peruvian church was poorer in resources than the Brazilian, its episcopate more divided, and its popular pastoral work concentrated in the parishes of Lima's shantytowns and in a few rural dioceses of the south. Gutierrez's own base was the parish and the institute he founded in Lima for the study of the theology he had named, and the Peruvian movement's character followed its founder's: intellectual, exact, and more exposed than the Brazilian to the charge that the school was a literature. Peru's political catastrophe of the nineteen eighties and nineties, hyperinflation, insurgency and counter insurgency, fell on the popular church with particular weight, since the Shining Path insurgency treated independent popular organisation, the communities included, as an obstacle to be terrorised, while the state's forces treated the same organisations as the insurgency's seedbed. Communities caught between two fires are a recurring figure in the movement's history, and the Peruvian case is its extreme form.

The Central American isthmus gave the movement its martyrs and its revolutionary entanglements. In El Salvador, as the earlier section recorded, the church of the option was persecuted as an enemy of the state. In Guatemala, the popular church of the highlands, where catechists organised among the indigenous communities, was destroyed in the counter insurgency of the early nineteen eighties with a thoroughness that left whole dioceses decapitated; the diocese of El Quiche closed its operations for a period because its agents were being killed faster than they could be replaced. In Nicaragua, uniquely, the popular church's side won its war, and the movement faced the problems of victory described above. The isthmus cases matter to the assessment because they are the ones where the theology's claims were cashed in lives, and where its distinction between accompaniment and insurrection was maintained, by most of its adherents, under pressures that make seminar discussions of the distinction look weightless. A catechist who continues to convene a community that the army has marked has performed the mediation distinction in the only laboratory that finally tests such things.

## The economics examined: what happened to the borrowed analysis

A borrowing is answerable for its performance, and the performance of the school's economics is the part of the record where sympathetic assessment must work hardest. The dependency analysis the theologians adopted in its strong form held that the periphery's development within the capitalist world economy was blocked in principle, that the centre's wealth and the periphery's poverty were two faces of one process, and that only a break with the system could open a path. The subsequent decades were unkind to the strong form. East Asian economies industrialised within the world economy at speeds the theory had no room for; Latin America's own import substituting industrialisation, which dependency writers had treated as a dead end, was succeeded not by liberation but by a debt crisis and a market turn that the theologians opposed without being able to prevent; and the socialist economies whose existence had made the break imaginable collapsed. Economists who had never been Marxists and Marxists who had never been dependency theorists converged on the judgement that the strong form had mistaken a historical configuration for a law.

### Did the collapse of the strong dependency thesis refute the theology built on it?

It damaged the theology's economics and left its theology standing, which is the answer the school's distinction predicts. The claims about God, sin and the option do not depend on any account of trade; the claims about why the poor are poor depend on the borrowed analysis, and where that analysis failed, the school's social teaching required, and partly received, revision.

The school's response to the damage was uneven, and the unevenness is itself informative. Some of its writers revised: the later Gutierrez wrote of the poor in a wider register, attending to culture, race and gender alongside class, and recast the enemy less as a system with an address than as an economy of exclusion; the school's engagement with the market order of the nineteen nineties produced a literature on the idolatry of the market that translated the old dependency polemic into a theological critique of neoliberalism, with Franz Hinkelammert's work the most systematic. Others held the analysis of 1971 as a settled possession and repeated it into a world it no longer described. The institutional church, meanwhile, absorbed the school's moral vocabulary while quietly retiring its economics, so that official documents could denounce exclusion and the idolatry of money in language indistinguishable from the theologians' while proposing remedies the theologians would have judged palliatives. For the assessment, the episode supplies the cleanest available test of the mediation distinction, and the test's result is double edged in exactly the way the distinction's defenders should welcome. The theology survived its economics, which suggests the two were genuinely separable in the direction the theologians claimed, faith first and analysis as its servant. But the school's slowness to revise the servant, and the comfort with which some of its texts went on citing a superseded analysis as though it were doctrine, suggests the traffic between the mediations ran, in practice, in both directions, and that the analysis had acquired for some of its users the untouchable status the theologians reserved for the faith. The distinction held as a structure; its discipline was imperfectly kept, which is the normal fate of distinctions and the reason the school's critics and defenders can both quote the record.

## Suspicion and retrieval: the school's way of reading everything

Running beneath the school's particular arguments is a habit of reading that deserves isolation, because it explains the movement's fertility and its characteristic errors better than any single doctrine. The habit has two movements. The first is suspicion: every received account, of society, of the church, of scripture's meaning, is interrogated for the interests it serves and the realities it conceals. The second is retrieval: beneath the received account, an older and more authentic stratum is recovered, the Exodus beneath the imperial church's Bible, the communities of the Acts beneath the Christendom of the dioceses, the option for the poor beneath the church's alliances with wealth. Suspicion without retrieval is mere unmasking, and the Marxist tradition from which the school borrowed the first movement sometimes stopped there; retrieval without suspicion is antiquarianism. The school's originality was to run the two movements as one operation, and to apply the operation impartially: to the oligarchy's economics and to the church's preaching, to the developmentalist's statistics and to the theologian's own tradition, and, in its best practitioners, to itself.

### What separates the school's suspicion from simple unmasking?

Its destination. Unmasking ends when the interest is exposed; the school's suspicion aims at retrieval, at recovering what the exposed idea had buried, a text, a practice, a memory of the poor's faith. The operation is complete only when something has been handed back. Where the school forgot its rule and merely unmasked, its writing thinned into polemic.

The habit's theological warrant, as the school stated it, lies in the tradition's own doctrine of sin as a power that deforms knowledge: if sin is structural, then the descriptions a structure gives of itself are among its products, and a theology that accepts them at face value has failed at the level of method before it reaches any conclusion. The habit's danger is the mirror image, and Rome's instruction named it with precision: a reader trained to find interests behind every doctrine will find them behind the doctrines he is bound to teach, and the suspicion, universalised, consumes the tradition that licensed it, leaving the theologian with a Bible he has explained and a church he has dissolved. The school's safeguard was its second movement: retrieval anchors the suspicion in a recovered content that the reader receives rather than manufactures. Whether the anchor holds is, once more, the standing question of the whole assessment, and it is fitting that the school's method and the school's risk should be the same operation viewed from two sides, since that identity is what made the movement, for thirty years, impossible for its church either to absorb quietly or to dismiss.

## The reception abroad: political theology, the North Atlantic reading and the feminist correction

The movement's influence outside Latin America ran through three channels, and each channel altered what it carried. In Europe, the school entered a conversation already under way: the political theology of Metz, the theology of hope descending from Bloch through Jurgen Moltmann, and the older French concern with the working world from which the school's founders had themselves learned. The Europeans received the Latin Americans as the practitioners of what they had theorised, and the exchange was genuine in both directions, though the Latin Americans were heard to remark that a theology of liberation written in a German study, however radical, answered to different pressures than one written within reach of a death squad. In North America, the school was read through the academy and the solidarity movements together: its texts entered seminary curricula, its exiles and visitors lectured widely, and its categories were taken up by theologians of the black, feminist and other liberation currents that the comparative section of this article surveyed. The North Atlantic reading characteristically prized the school's method and its politics while domesticating its ecclesial strangeness, treating it as a resource for progressive Christianity in general rather than as the discipline of a particular church under particular bishops, a translation whose losses the school's own writers noted with courtesy and precision.

The feminist correction, the third channel, was less a reception than a reckoning, and it has been touched already in the discussion of the communities. Feminist theologians inside the movement, among them the Brazilian Ivone Gebara and the Mexican born Elsa Tamez, whose biblical scholarship gave the school some of its most exact work on the texts of oppression, argued that the school's subject, the poor, had been imagined as male: that the analysis of class had been allowed to stand for the whole of oppression while the oppression of women, inside the communities, the church and the movement's own households, went unanalysed by the categories the school trusted. The correction was liberation theology applied to liberation theology, suspicion and retrieval turned on the school's own texts, and the school's willingness, partial and contested as it was, to undergo the operation is among the stronger evidences that its method was a discipline rather than a brand. A movement that teaches a church to interrogate itself will be interrogated by its own students; the school's history in this respect is not a scandal but a fulfilment, and the feminist liberation theology that resulted, with its attention to daily life, the body and the sustaining of existence as theological loci, is one of the parent movement's most living descendants.

## Violence and revolution: the question the school refused to evade

No question pressed on the movement harder, from every side, than the question of violence, and its handling of the question is a fair test of its seriousness. The theologians wrote in societies where violence was the ordinary instrument of order: the violence of the eviction and the death squad, of the prison and the disappeared, which their analysis named and their bishops had already, at Medellin, called institutionalised. Against that background, the school's treatment of revolutionary violence proceeded by distinctions it knew would satisfy nobody. It refused, first, to equate the violences: the violence that maintains an unjust order and the violence that resists it are not, in its moral accounting, the same act with different flags, and a theology that treats them as equivalent has already taken the order's side. It refused, second, to baptise the resistance: Gutierrez and the school's centre declined to commend armed struggle as a Christian vocation, treated the resort to arms as a tragedy within a tragedy, and located the church's own task in the construction of the popular organisation that might make transformation possible by other means. And it refused, third, to condemn the desperate in the abstract: the tradition it inherited, running through the scholastics to Paul VI's encyclical, recognised a right of resistance to manifest and prolonged tyranny, and the theologians would not pronounce, from safety, on the consciences of those who lived under the tyrannies they analysed.

### How did the school distinguish the violence of the order from the violence against it?

By origin and by end. The order's violence is structural and continuous, exercised to preserve a distribution of goods and power that produces death as a routine outcome; resistance violence is episodic and reactive, exercised to end that distribution. The distinction did not sanctify the second; it refused to let the first pass as peace.

The school's severest internal argument on the question concerned not the insurgent but the theologian: what the writer owes the fighter. When Christians of the communities joined armed movements, as some did in El Salvador, Guatemala and elsewhere, the school's priests and theologians accompanied them pastorally while mostly declining to theorise their choice as the movement's own, and the tension between accompaniment and endorsement ran through the movement's wartime history unresolved. Camilo Torres stood at the argument's origin as its permanent exhibit: the priest who had concluded that love of neighbour, to be efficacious, required revolution, and who had acted on the conclusion before the school existed to qualify it. The school's mature position can be stated as a refusal to let Torres be either its patron or its embarrassment. His diagnosis was theirs; his conclusion was his own, taken in a Colombia whose channels of peaceful change he judged, not without evidence, to be sealed; and the difference between the diagnosis and the conclusion is the difference between the theology and the insurgency, which the school maintained even when maintaining it cost the school the allegiance of the young.

## The religion of the people: popular piety defended

One of the school's least expected positions, and one of the most revealing, was its defence of the popular religion of the Latin American poor against the contempt of two sets of critics who agreed about nothing else. Secular Marxists of the classical school regarded the processions, the vows, the fiestas and the devotions of the popular church as the opium in its purest form, the consolation that reconciled the people to their misery. Modernising churchmen, including some who agreed with the liberationists on economics, regarded the same practices as a pre modern residue, magical and fatalistic, to be educated away in favour of a purified and social faith. The liberation theologians defended the devotions against both. Popular religiosity, in the school's reading, is the faith of the poor in the cultural forms the poor themselves created under conditions of domination: a reservoir of memory, dignity and hope, in which the people preserved, through centuries when every other institution belonged to their masters, a God who was theirs. The fiesta of the patron saint, the pilgrimage, the vow made at the shrine, are not the opposites of the base community's Bible study but its elder relatives, and the communities that lasted were characteristically those that wove the two together rather than demanding the people choose between their grandmothers' faith and their organisers' analysis.

### Why did the school defend practices that Marxist analysis classifies as consolation?

Because it had watched the practices work. The devotion that carried a community through a funeral, a drought and a persecution was not reconciling it to its oppressors; it was preserving its identity and hope in a vault the oppressors could not confiscate. The school concluded that the classification mistook the practice's function, reading it from outside the people who performed it.

The defence had a methodological root that connects it to the article's central theme. The theologians' refusal of the opium classification, in its crude form, was the refusal they had practised at the level of the borrowing table: a category of the analysis, religion as consolation, was tested against the reality it claimed to describe, the faith of actual communities, and was found to describe a part and to slander the whole. The school's procedure was the same one it applied to class and to dependency, take the instrument, test it on the ground, keep what it reveals and refuse what it flattens, and the fact that the procedure was applied to the analysis's own doctrine of religion is the strongest internal evidence that the borrowing was governed rather than servile. A borrower who corrects the lender's account of the borrower's own household has understood the terms of the loan exactly as the mediation distinction describes them.

The Puebla conference gave the defence institutional standing, treating popular piety as a treasure of the Latin American church and a form of inculturation to be evangelised from within rather than suppressed, and the theologians of the people's current in Argentina had made the point a system before Puebla met. The later history added an irony the school noted without pleasure: the devotions it had defended became, in the decades of Pentecostal growth, one of the fields of competition, as the new churches offered the poor a piety of healing and deliverance with an organisational energy the Catholic parishes rarely matched, and the popular religion of the continent began to change its denominational address while keeping its forms. The school's analysis of that change, where its surviving writers attempted it, applied its own categories faithfully: the people were seeking, in the new churches, the community, the recognition and the hope that the old structures had promised and, in too many places, ceased to deliver.

## The movement and the Marxist left: an alliance neither side could settle

The theology's relations with the organised Marxist left of its own continent form a chapter that both sides later found convenient to forget, the church because the alliance embarrassed its account of the movement as purely pastoral, the left because the alliance's terms were never the ones its orthodoxy prescribed. The facts are substantial. In country after country, the popular church and the Marxist organisations confronted the same regimes, sheltered the same fugitives and buried the same dead. Christians formed in the communities entered the parties and movements of the left in numbers that changed those organisations' character: the Sandinista front's Christian current, the Brazilian Workers' Party's base of community organisers, the Salvadoran insurgency's social base in the Christian communities of the countryside. Marxist intellectuals of the region, in turn, found themselves revising the inherited anticlericalism of their tradition in the presence of priests who quoted the dependency literature more exactly than the parties' own pamphlets did.

### What did the alliance with the left cost and give each side?

The church's popular sectors gained protection, organisation and a political horizon, and paid with their hierarchy's suspicion and with lives taken by regimes that treated the alliance as proof of subversion. The left gained a mass base, a moral vocabulary and disciplined organisers, and paid by admitting a constituency it could not command and could not explain.

The alliance's limits were as instructive as its warmth. The parties wanted the communities as a transmission belt and discovered that the communities' members distinguished, with the catechist's precision, between cooperation on a campaign and submission to a line; the theologians wanted the left's analysis and organisational seriousness and discovered that the parties' internal regimes reproduced, in miniature, some of the authoritarianism the theology existed to oppose. The Cuban question divided the alliance throughout: the island's revolution was the continent's standing proof that the order could be broken, and its church settlement, in which the churches had been pressed to the margin of society for a generation, was the standing warning of what a victorious left might do with its Christian allies. The school's writers handled Cuba with a discretion that bordered on evasion, praising the revolution's social achievements, regretting its religious policy in general terms and declining to make the island the test of their theory. The discretion is understandable and it is also a gap in the record: the one case where the movement's hoped for alliance held state power for decades is the case its literature examined least, and the assessment of the school is entitled to register the silence as the place where its suspicion and retrieval, so sharp elsewhere, were laid aside out of solidarity.

## The later condition: absorption, contraction and persistence

The movement's condition in the decades after its heroic period is best described structurally, as the brief for any honest account of it requires, because its institutional facts changed faster than its ideas and because the naming of officeholders would date what is meant to be a durable description. Three processes define the condition. The first is absorption: the movement's vocabulary completed its journey into the church's ordinary teaching, so that the option for the poor, the critique of sin's structures and the denunciation of an economy that excludes are heard, in moderated registers, from pulpits and documents that would once have disciplined the phrases' authors. Absorption of that kind is a real victory and a real defeat in one event: the words won, and the analysis that gave the words their content, the class structure, the dependency, the ideology critique turned on the church itself, did not travel with them. The second process is contraction: the organisational base in the communities shrank under the pressures already described, episcopal, political and Pentecostal, and the school's publishing and teaching presence contracted with it, its founding generation unreplaced at the same weight. The third process is persistence in altered forms: the method survived its movement, in the feminist and ecological theologies that descend from it, in the dalit and minjung currents that parallel it, in the pastoral practice of communities that never learned its name, and in the formation of organisers across the continent's unions, parties and movements, where the communities' alumni carried its habits of reading and meeting into institutions that have never heard of Clodovis Boff.

### Has the movement disappeared?

No, though the form in which it was a mass movement has passed. Its language was absorbed by the institution it challenged, its organisation contracted, and its method dispersed into successor theologies and popular movements' habits. Disappearance describes its headlines, not its fate. The record shows transformation, the normal fate of movements that succeed in part.

The question of the school's standing with Rome completed its own arc within the period this article can describe historically. The theologians who had been investigated were, in the fullness of the institution's slow time, variously received, honoured and cited; the founder of the school lived to see his work treated as a classic of the church's theology rather than as a problem for its police, and the archbishop whose cause had waited was raised to the altars. This article names no serving official and describes no pending cause; it records the arc because the arc is itself evidence in the assessment, evidence that the institution's considered judgement, delivered at its characteristic pace, fell nearer to the second instruction's reception of the movement than to the first instruction's alarm. Institutions rarely retract; they absorb, and the absorption is the retraction's practical form.

## A balance sheet: what the movement proved and what it did not

The assessment can now be gathered, and it should be gathered in the graded form the borrowing table taught: row by row, claim by claim, rather than in a single verdict on a label.

The movement proved, first, that the separation at its centre could be lived. For roughly three decades, across a continent, communities and writers used an analysis while refusing its philosophy, and produced work, organisation and witness that neither a simple conversion to Marxism nor a simple refusal of it would have produced. The proof is practical rather than demonstrative, and this article has recorded the concession that belongs with it: the distinction's coherence as philosophy remains asserted rather than demonstrated, and neither of its great objectors withdrew. But a distinction that organises thirty years of serious practice has passed a test that many demonstrated distinctions fail.

It proved, second, that the classical Marxist theory of religion required revision, and it supplied the revision's materials: a case, documented at scale, in which religion functioned as the grammar of emancipation, together with the specification of the conditions, a popular church, a text read from underneath, an analysis that named the structure, under which the protest function of religion displaces its consoling and legitimating functions. Marxist scholarship of quality, Löwy's above all, registered the proof and rebuilt accordingly; the rebuilding is part of the movement's permanent effect on the tradition it borrowed from, a rare case of the borrower amending the lender.

It did not prove, third, the economics it borrowed. The strong dependency thesis failed the test of the following decades, and the school's social programme, insofar as it rested on that thesis, failed with it. The movement's defenders are entitled to the reply that its theology was never its economics, and this article has given that reply its full force; they are not entitled to forget that the movement promised the poor an analysis of their poverty, and that the analysis, at its most confident, was wrong about the world economy's possibilities in ways that mattered to the strategies built on it.

It did not achieve, fourth, the transformation of its own church at the depth its ecclesiology proposed. The church received the option and declined the wager: the words entered the teaching, the communities were not enthroned as the church's constitutive form, and the distribution of sacred power that Boff challenged survived its challenger. Whether that outcome represents the institution's wisdom or its refusal is the theological form of the question this article has declined, throughout, to adjudicate; the historical form of the answer is simply that the church changed its language more than its structure, and that the movement changed the church's language more than any other current of its century.

And it left, fifth, a method whose travels are not finished. The pattern, read the founding texts from the position of those the order crushes, analyse the order with the best instruments available, refuse to let the instruments become the faith, is reproducible wherever the conditions recur, and it has recurred, in Korea and India, in South Africa and in the feminist and ecological currents of the school's own continent. The movement's final significance may be that it demonstrated the pattern at full strength first, under the hardest conditions, and left the demonstration in a literature exact enough to be argued with. Arguments that can be continued are the immortality available to schools of thought, and by that measure the school of Gutierrez, the Boffs, Segundo, Sobrino and the unnamed catechists of the communities is in no danger of the disappearance its obituarists periodically announce.

## The theologians read Marx: the founder distinguished from the inheritance

A detail of the borrowing that repays attention is the school's preference, within the Marxist tradition, for Marx himself over the systems built in his name. The theologians' Marx was the critic of political economy and the analyst of fetishism, the writer who had shown how the products of human labour come to rule their producers as alien powers, a figure whose critique of idolatry the school could set, with a straight face, beside the prophets'. The Mexican theologian Jose Porfirio Miranda pressed the preference furthest in his Marx and the Bible, arguing that Marx's critique of religion was aimed at a religion of oppression foreign to the biblical faith, and that the God of Exodus and the prophets stands on Marx's side of the argument against the gods of the empires. Dussel, in a long engagement with Marx's manuscripts, argued that the later Marx's ethics of the living labourer supplied resources the orthodox tradition had buried. The school's mainstream kept a cooler distance than Miranda's, but the direction of reading was common: the founder's critique was treated as an instrument of the first rank, while the codified doctrine of the Marxist states, with its established atheism and its party catechism, was treated as one more ideology to be suspected.

### Why did the school prefer the early and the critical Marx to the codified doctrine?

Because the codified doctrine arrived as a system with its philosophy attached and its conclusions pre drawn, the form in which the school could not use it, while the critical Marx arrived as unmasking method, an account of fetishism and exploitation whose findings could be tested. The preference is the mediation distinction operating inside the Marxist tradition.

The preference also carried a risk the school's Marxist critics were quick to name: that a Marx selected for his compatibility is a Marx defused, his critique of religion trimmed to the verses that spare the reader's own altar. The theologians' answer returned to their standing rule, that every borrowing is judged by its yield on the ground it was borrowed for, and the ground in this case was the continent's poverty, about which the critique of political economy had more to say than the doctrine of the party schools. The answer does not close the question, and the question's persistence is recorded here as part of the pattern: at every level of the borrowing, from the table's rows to the choice of texts within Marx, the same dispute recurs, whether the separation the school practised was discernment or evasion. The recurrence is not a defect of this article's organisation. It is the shape of the subject, a hall of mirrors in which one distinction is reflected at every scale, and a reader who has grasped the distinction at any one scale has the key to all the others.

## Misreadings corrected

The assessment is incomplete without a direct engagement with the errors that dominate summary coverage of the movement, since those errors are the competition this article exists to displace.

The first misreading holds that the liberation theologians were Marxists. The borrowing table refutes it row by row: the school adopted instruments of analysis and refused the philosophy and the atheism that define the Marxist position as a position, and its members paid for the refusal in suspicion from the Marxist organisations themselves. The second misreading holds that the Vatican condemned the movement outright. The record shows two instructions of differing tone, the second substantially positive toward the movement's central theme, a silencing directed at one theologian's ecclesiology, investigations that stopped short of condemnation for the school's founder, and the reception of the option for the poor into official teaching. Condemnation names something simpler and more total than anything in that record. The third misreading holds that the movement was a literature, a fashion of the seminaries. The base communities refute it: the school was the reflection of a mass practice, and its decline is unintelligible except as the decline of that practice's organisation. The fourth misreading holds that the movement has disappeared. The structural account given above refutes it: absorbed in vocabulary, contracted in organisation, dispersed in method, the movement underwent the fate of partly successful movements, which is transformation rather than extinction. The fifth misreading, the gentlest and the most corrosive, holds the option for the poor to be a call to charity with a Spanish accent. The option's whole content, as the school and the conferences stated it, is the refusal of that reduction: the poor as subjects, the structures as the address, and solidarity, not alms, as the form of the church's presence.

Each misreading survives because it is cheaper than the truth it replaces. The label Marxist saves the labour of the borrowing table; the label condemned saves the labour of reading two instructions; the label literature saves the labour of visiting a community; the label disappeared saves the labour of tracing a dispersal; the label charity saves the labour, and the cost, of the option itself. An article in this series is written against cheapness of exactly that kind, and the reader who has followed the argument to this point possesses the materials to refuse all five labels in their simple forms and to state, in their place, the graded account the record supports.

## A note on sources and their languages

The documentary base of this article is unevenly available to the English reader, and the asymmetry should be stated as plainly as the school's own writers would wish. The founding texts are translated: Gutierrez's book exists in a standard English version, as do the principal works of Leonardo Boff, Sobrino and Segundo, the two Vatican instructions, and the great conference documents of Medellin and Puebla. The reader who wishes to test this article's account against the school's own statements can do so in English for every major claim. Below that level, the record closes. The base community materials, the catechetical guides, discussion pamphlets, community newspapers and pastoral letters in which the movement's daily life was conducted, are principally in Spanish and Portuguese and largely untranslated. The regional episcopal documents beneath the great conferences, the proceedings of the pastoral institutes, the internal bulletins of the religious orders and the archives of the human rights vicariates are in the same condition. Much of the movement's own publication, the journals and collections through which the school argued with itself, circulated in the continent's languages and reached the English reader, if at all, in selections made by others. The consequence for scholarship is structural: the English language literature over represents the school's treatises and under represents its practice, which is one reason the misreading of the movement as a literature is so persistent in English. The reader is warned, accordingly, that the movement's centre of gravity lies further toward the parish hall and the mimeographed pamphlet than the translated shelf suggests, and that the untranslated record, when it is worked, has consistently confirmed the picture the treatises give while thickening it beyond recognition.

## The bishops of the option: the movement's protectors

The school's survival inside its institution depended, at every critical moment, on bishops who were willing to spend their authority on it, and the type deserves a portrait because it has no equivalent in movements that live outside churches. The exemplary figure of the first hour was Helder Camara, Archbishop of Olinda and Recife in Brazil's poor northeast, who had named the spiral of violence, the violence of injustice answered by revolt answered by repression, in an address that carried the analysis around the world before the theologians had systematised it, and whose diocese became a shelter for the pastoral experiments the military regime harassed. In Mexico, Sergio Mendez Arceo, Bishop of Cuernavaca, opened his diocese to the continent's exiles and its experiments alike, defended the base communities against their critics and stood as the Mexican episcopate's solitary public voice for the positions Medellin had adopted. In Ecuador, Leonidas Proano, Bishop of Riobamba, built his diocese among the indigenous communities of Chimborazo, learned their language, returned church lands, and was investigated by his own government and his own church for the crime of treating the Indians as subjects. In Mexico again, Samuel Ruiz, Bishop of San Cristobal de las Casas in Chiapas, spent four decades building a diocesan church of indigenous catechists and deacons, mediated between the state and the Zapatista insurgency when it erupted in 1994, and retired under sustained pressure from those who judged his diocese a state within the state.

### What did a sympathetic bishop actually protect?

The space in which the practice could occur. A bishop's protection meant clergy assigned rather than withdrawn, materials approved rather than confiscated, a diocese's legal and moral standing placed between the communities and the police, and his signature on the documents that gave its positions institutional weight. Its books could be written without him; its communities could not be kept.

The type's importance for the assessment is that it defeats, from inside the record, the picture of the movement as a clergy of the study arrayed against a hierarchy of the throne. The Latin American church of the period contained, at every level, both the movement and its opposition, and the struggle between them was conducted in the institution's own currencies: appointments, documents, budgets, and the slow accumulation of precedent. The bishops of the option were not theologians manqué; several of them regarded the school's Marxist borrowings with reserve and said so. What they shared with the school was the pastoral judgement that the church of the poor districts could not preach patience to the evicted, and that judgement, made by men who held the institution's own authority, is what converted a theological current into a continental fact. When the appointments policy of the following decades replaced the type, diocese by diocese, with administrators of a different judgement, the movement's contraction followed as a matter of institutional arithmetic, and the school's writers understood the mechanism perfectly, since it was the one their own analysis predicted: structures, including the church's, outlast the intentions of the individuals who staff them, which is why the school had insisted from the beginning that conversion, to be real, must reach the structures too.

## Behind the school: the social doctrine it radicalised

The liberation theologians presented their work, accurately, as a break with the church's settled ways of speaking about society, but the break was made from inside a tradition that contained more resources for it than the school's rhetoric sometimes admitted. The modern social doctrine of the Catholic Church runs from the encyclical Rerum Novarum of 1891, which defended the worker's right to organise and a just wage while defending private property against socialism, through the corporatist speculations of the nineteen thirties, to the council's pastoral constitution and Paul VI's teaching of the nineteen sixties. Two elements of that inheritance matter for the genealogy. The first is the doctrine that property carries a social mortgage: ownership, in the tradition's own terms, is stewardship, the goods of the earth are destined for all, and a distribution that leaves the majority destitute is not an unfortunate outcome but a disorder in the institution itself. John Paul II's later phrase for the principle, that private property is under a social mortgage, was taken up by the theologians as proof that their economics had a magisterial root, whatever Rome thought of their analysis. The second element is the tradition's method of moral judgement on systems: the same teaching office that condemned communism as intrinsically wrong had condemned, in the nineteen thirties, the dictatorship of money and the international imperialism of capital in language the liberationists could quote without altering a syllable.

### How did the school stand to the older social doctrine, heir or rebel?

Both, by its own account and in fact. It inherited the doctrine's ends, the universal destination of goods, the dignity of labour, the condemnation of an economy ruled by accumulation, and rebelled against its method, which had proceeded by deduction from principles and address to rulers, substituting induction from the poor's reality and address to the poor themselves as agents.

The comparison clarifies what was actually new in the school, and it is not the conclusions about property, most of which can be footnoted to the encyclicals by a competent advocate. The novelties are three. The first is the starting point: the older doctrine deduced the just society from human nature and addressed its conclusions to those with power to enact them, while the school began from the poor's experience and addressed the poor as the agents of the change, a difference in the doctrine's subject as consequential as any difference in its content. The second novelty is the analytical machinery: the older doctrine's economics was moralised description, while the school's was the borrowed analysis, with its structures, its mechanisms and its account of why good intentions at the top had failed for a century. The third novelty is the doctrine of the church itself: the older teaching imagined the church as society's tutor, standing above the struggle and instructing the parties, while the school imagined it as a participant, converted by its own option, standing inside the struggle on a stated side. Rome's resistance concentrated, intelligibly, on the second and third novelties, where its own prerogatives were engaged; its reception of the school concentrated on the first, where the school had radicalised an inheritance Rome itself owned. The pattern of the whole Roman encounter, alarm at the method and the ecclesiology, absorption of the moral substance, is visible in embryo in this genealogy.

## The kingdom and history: the school's eschatology

The theological nerve of the whole construction, and the point where its critics pressed hardest, is its account of the relation between God's kingdom and human history. Classical eschatology held the kingdom at history's end and outside its processes; the political theologies of the nineteen sixties had begun to ask whether that placement had not served, in practice, to evacuate history of theological meaning and to leave its injustices unjudged until too late. The liberation school's answer was careful and, in its carefulness, characteristic. Historical liberations, the school held, are not the kingdom; no revolution, however just, is the reign of God, and the identification of the two is the idolatry the school's doctrine of idols exists to refuse. But historical liberations are not unrelated to the kingdom either: they are its anticipations, signs and partial realisations, growths of the kingdom in Gutierrez's phrase, in which something of the promised justice becomes actual within history and is received by faith as a pledge of the whole. The formula guards both flanks. Against the conservative charge that the school had collapsed heaven into a political programme, it maintains the distance between every achievement and the promise. Against the Marxist charge that the promise consoles for the achievement's absence, it maintains that the promise is already at work in the achievements, judging them, exceeding them and refusing to let any of them be worshipped as final.

### Why does the school refuse to identify any revolution with the kingdom?

Because the identification is, in its own vocabulary, idolatry: the absolutising of a historical work, which then demands the sacrifices idols demand and forbids the criticism faith owes every human order. The refusal protects the revolution's victims from the revolution's theology and protects the faith from becoming any government's chaplain, including a friendly government's.

The eschatology had a pastoral consequence that the base communities embodied and that pure theory might have missed. A people taught that its struggles anticipate the kingdom without exhausting it possesses a doctrine for defeat. The community whose campaign failed, whose organiser was killed, whose cooperative was dissolved by decree, was not required to conclude that God had failed or that the struggle had been meaningless; the kingdom's non identity with any campaign meant that no campaign's failure was the promise's failure, and the communities' capacity to persist, to bury their dead and reconvene, drew on that doctrine as on a well. The Marxist movements of the continent, whose eschatologies were immanent and whose promises were therefore hostage to outcomes, repeatedly shattered on defeats from which their theory offered no recovery except the next offensive. The church of the poor, armed with a hope that no outcome could falsify because no outcome had been identified with it, proved in place after place the more durable organisation. Observers who attribute that durability to the consolations of religion have the fact right and the analysis inverted: the consolation in question was not a compensation for the struggle but one of its instruments, and it functioned, in the record, as the classical theory of religion least expects consolation to function, as a reason to continue.

## The organisational ecology: orders, institutes and journals

A school is carried by institutions or it is not carried at all, and the liberation current's carriers formed an ecology worth mapping, since the map explains both the movement's reach and its vulnerability. The religious orders supplied its shock troops and many of its minds: Jesuits in the universities and the Salvadoran parishes, Franciscans in Boff's Brazil, Dominicans, Maryknoll missioners from the United States whose order's publishing house carried the school's texts into English, and the sisters of a dozen congregations whose presence in the poor districts exceeded the priests' and whose role the movement's male literature chronically understated. The pastoral institutes supplied its training: centres in several countries where catechists, community animators and clergy studied the method, learned the analysis and returned to their districts with materials and with one another's addresses. The journals supplied its nervous system: reviews in Spanish and Portuguese in which the school's arguments were first printed, its internal disputes conducted and its documents circulated, together with the great European theological reviews that carried its work into the faculties. The publishing houses of the religious orders and the episcopal conferences supplied its distribution, mimeographed and printed, into the communities themselves.

### What did the religious orders contribute that dioceses could not?

Personnel with a double loyalty that cut the school's way. A religious answers to his order as well as to his bishop, is formed in an international body with its intellectual traditions, and can be sustained in conflict by a worldwide fraternity. The orders gave the movement mobility, universities and a share of martyrs out of proportion to their numbers.

The ecology's vulnerability was the mirror of its design. Every carrier in the map answered, in the end, to the institution the movement sought to convert: the orders to their Roman superiors and to the congregation for religious, the institutes to their funding and their bishops, the journals to their censors and their markets, the communities to their parishes. When the institution's weather changed, the ecology transmitted the change to every level at once, which is why the contraction, when it came, was continental and simultaneous rather than local and staggered. A movement carried by a party would have had a headquarters to defend; a movement carried by an ecology had a climate to endure, and climates are not negotiated with. The school's historians have drawn the moral in the school's own terms: a theology that taught the primacy of structures over intentions was itself undone, as an organisation, by a structure, the appointment system of its church, operating exactly as its analysis said structures operate, impersonally, cumulatively and regardless of the merits of the individuals involved. There is a bleak satisfaction in the fit, and the school's survivors have not failed to notice it.

## The Argentine divergence: the theology of the people

One internal alternative accompanied the school from its early years and has outlasted it in influence, and an assessment that omits it leaves the map of Latin American theology with a false simplicity. In Argentina, a current associated with the theologians Lucio Gera and Rafael Tello developed, alongside and partly against the liberation school, a theology of the people. Its starting point was not class but the pueblo, the people as a historical and cultural subject, bearer of a wisdom and a religiosity of its own; its analysis attended less to the structures of dependency than to the culture of the people and its colonisation; and its pastoral conclusion was the evangelisation of culture rather than the organisation of communities for structural change. The Argentine current shared the school's option for the poor and its suspicion of liberal individualism, and it differed in its judgement of Marxist analysis, which it regarded as a foreign grid that misdescribed a people whose unity, faith and national feeling were the realities a theology should begin from.

### What divides the theology of the people from the liberation school?

The unit of analysis. The school begins from class and asks how the poor are positioned in a structure; the Argentine current begins from the people as a cultural and historical whole and asks how its faith and wisdom are to be honoured and purified. The division reproduces the older argument between class politics and national populism.

The divergence matters beyond taxonomy for two reasons. The first is that it supplied the school's most intimate criticism: not Rome's charge that the analysis was unborrowable, nor the Marxists' charge that the faith was unassimilable, but the charge, from theologians who shared the school's streets, that its borrowed categories had led it to undervalue the people's own religion, the very popular piety the school elsewhere defended, and to imagine the poor as a class in formation where the people experienced themselves as a people already formed. The second reason is the current's later reach: the theology of the people formed the intellectual world of the Argentine church from which, in the following century, the universal church received a pope, and its categories, the people, the culture, the peripheries, entered the church's highest teaching in that pontificate's vocabulary. No serving official is named here, as the article's discipline requires, and the fact of transmission is stated without the name: a current that the liberation school regarded as its conservative sibling became, by the institution's slow routes, the idiom in which the option for the poor was finally spoken from the church's summit, while the school's own idiom, of class and mediation, remained in the footnotes. The irony completes the pattern of absorption described throughout this article, and it is the pattern's most elegant instance: the institution took the movement's substance twice over, in two vocabularies, and credited neither.

## The conquest remembered: the school's history of its own church

A movement that reads the present from underneath will eventually read the past from underneath, and the liberation school's historiography is the part of its work that most disturbed the institution's self image. The Latin American church was born in conquest: its first evangelisation rode with the Spanish and Portuguese empires, its missions were instruments of colonial order as well as of faith, and its wealth was accumulated within the economy of encomienda and mine. The school's historians, Dussel preeminent among them, wrote that history without the anaesthesia of the traditional accounts. They recovered the dissenting tradition inside it, the Dominican Antonio de Montesinos preaching at Santo Domingo in 1511 that the Indians were men with souls, Bartolome de las Casas spending a long life in the defence of the peoples his countrymen were destroying, and they treated Las Casas as the first theologian of liberation in the Americas, the founder of the line in which the school consciously stood. The recovery was retrieval in the school's exact sense: suspicion applied to the triumphalist history, and beneath it a buried tradition of protest handed back to the present as a warrant.

### What did the approach of the conquest's five hundredth year force the school to say?

In 1992, five centuries after the first landfall, the school, with the indigenous movements beside it, refused the language of celebration. Its theologians spoke of an invasion and an evangelisation bound together, of a debt unpaid and a memory to be confessed, and the episcopal conference of that year, at Santo Domingo, heard the demand for pardon the school's history had prepared.

The historiography mattered to the movement's present tense in a precise way. The school's claim that the church must be converted by its option for the poor required an account of what the church was being converted from, and the conquest history supplied it: an institution that had arrived as the chaplaincy of an empire and had never fully audited the inheritance. The base communities' indigenous and mestizo members heard in that history an explanation of their own church's divided face, the priest who baptised and the priest who blessed the landlord, and the school's willingness to tell the story whole was a principal source of its credibility among people who had inherited the story's costs. Critics replied that the school's history was as selective as the triumphalism it replaced, gathering the prophets and omitting the schoolmen, the hospitals and the universities, the orders that learned the languages and recorded the cultures the conquest was destroying. The reply has force as far as it goes; the school's history was a retrieval, and retrievals are arguments. But the argument's direction, toward the victims' side of the record, was the school's method applied to the past, and its critics' counter selection does not refute the method. It confirms that the past, like the present, is read from somewhere, and that the school had chosen its somewhere on stated grounds.

## The indigenous correction: peoples who were not a class

The school's encounter with the indigenous peoples of the continent administered to its categories the most instructive correction they received from any quarter. The analysis of class, borrowed and refined, described the indigenous of the Andes, of Mesoamerica and of Amazonia as peasants, and in the description something essential escaped: these were peoples, with languages, territories, memories and gods of their own, whose oppression was not only the extraction of their labour but the denial of their existence as peoples, and whose resistance aimed not only at a fairer distribution within the nation but at the recognition of nations the official nation had buried. Indigenous theologians and pastoral workers, in the Andes, in Mexico and in Brazil's indigenous missionary council, pressed the correction from inside the movement: an option for the poor that could not see a people was an option with a European blindfold, and the God of the Exodus, they argued, had spoken to their ancestors before the missionaries arrived, in the people's own spiritual traditions, which a theology of liberation must approach not as idolatry to be extirpated but as an elder revelation to be heard.

### What did indigenous theology add that the school's framework lacked?

A subject older than the class structure. The indigenous current taught that the poor are not only positioned in an economy but rooted in peoples, that land is not only a factor of production but a mother and a territory of memory, and that a liberation which dissolves those roots in the name of progress repeats the conquest with intentions no better.

The correction's reception inside the school followed the pattern of the feminist correction before it: resistance, argument, and a partial, genuine revision. The later writings of the school's surviving masters attend to culture, ethnicity and the earth in registers the founding texts did not possess, and the school's account of the poor widened from a class to a company of subjects, indigenous, black, women, the discarded of every order, whose unity is not a shared position in production but a shared experience of being treated as nobody. Whether that widening strengthened the theology or dissolved its analytical edge is a live question among its interpreters, and it is the form in which the school's oldest dispute, between the precision of the borrowed analysis and the breadth of the faith's attention, continued into its later life. The borrowing table's discipline suggests the answer the school's own method would give: each widening is a new row to be graded on its yield, and the grading, as always, is the work rather than the slogan.

## The method at work: one valley through the three mediations

Abstract accounts of method persuade few readers, and the school's method is best exhibited in the shape in which it was actually used: a pastoral team, a priest, two sisters and the elected animators of a dozen communities, confronting the situation of a single valley and working it through the three mediations in order. The example that follows is composite, assembled from the documented practice of many teams, and it is offered as the article's demonstration piece, the place where the borrowing table's rows can be watched doing their work.

The valley's facts, as the team's socio-analytic mediation assembled them, were these. The valley floor belonged to a small number of families and one company, growing export crops with seasonal labour; the hillsides belonged, in plots too small to feed their holders, to the descendants of the valley's original communities, who descended each year to sell their labour on the floor their grandparents had owned entire. A single buyer set the price of the hillside's coffee; a single store, owned by the same interest, sold the hillside its food on credit against the harvest; the municipality's offices were staffed by the floor's nominees; the school's calendar was arranged around the floor's need for hands. The analysis named the relations without rhetoric: a land structure, a monopsony, a debt mechanism, a captured state, and it predicted, correctly, that a campaign addressed to any one of them in isolation would be absorbed by the others. Nothing in the assembly was Marxist in the sense of the philosophy refused at the table's lower rows. Every category in it, class, exploitation, the state as an instrument, the ideology of the arrangement's naturalness, came from the rows the school had adopted, and the team's faith had not yet been consulted.

The hermeneutic mediation began where the analysis ended, in the communities' own reading. The texts that the season's lectionary and the communities' choices brought forward were the Exodus, the Naboth story, in which a king's desire for a smallholder's vineyard is arranged by law's forms into theft and murder, and the parable of the labourers in the vineyard read against the valley's own hiring ground at dawn. The readings were not applications pasted onto the analysis; they were the communities' discovery that their book had been describing their valley for some time, and the discovery did to the valley's ideology, the arrangement's appearance as nature, what no pamphlet had done: it made the arrangement look recent, human and therefore changeable, by setting it inside a story where God had opinions about vineyards. The practical mediation followed as the school's method requires, in acts proportioned to the communities' strength: a cooperative store to break the credit mechanism at its weakest point, a legal campaign on the titles of one hillside, a catechist school to train the valley's own interpreters, and a decision, taken with open eyes, about which confrontations to decline. Some of the acts succeeded and some were broken, and the team's reports record both with the same plainness. What the example demonstrates is the article's thesis in miniature. The analysis told the truth about the valley and could not, by itself, have convened a single meeting; the faith convened the meetings yet could never, unaided, have found the monopsony. The mediation distinction is the name for the discipline by which the two were kept in one room without either being asked to impersonate the other.

### Why does the school insist the practical mediation comes last in order and first in intention?

Because the construction exists for its practice, while the practice, to be more than impulse, must wait on the analysis and the reading. The order of the mediations is the order of work, analysis, interpretation, act; the order of dignity is the reverse, since the act is why the other two are undertaken and the test by which they are judged.

## The school argues with itself: the second generation and the widening of the subject

The school's internal history after its founding decade is a record of its categories being widened under pressure from its own students, and the record deserves summary because it distinguishes a living school from a founded sect. The first pressure came from the communities' women, whose correction has been described: the subject of the theology, the poor, acquired a sex, and the analysis acquired patriarchy as a structure it had to learn to see. The second came from the indigenous and black movements, whose correction has also been described: the subject acquired peoples and races, and the analysis acquired the colonial wound as a category beside the economic one. The third came from the earth itself, as Boff in his later work and the theologians of ecology within the school argued that the cry of the poor and the cry of the earth are one cry, and that an analysis of exploitation which stopped at the factory gate had missed the metabolism between the human order and the natural one on which the order stands. The fourth came from the school's own political defeats, which forced a reckoning with the category of hope: the later Gutierrez writes of the poor's spirituality, of gratuitousness, of a love that is not exhausted by efficacy, in a register the founding text's urgency had crowded out, and the shift is the school's own verdict that a theology which began as reflection on praxis had to learn, from the praxis's failures, what the praxis was for.

### What distinguishes the school's widenings from a simple drift of fashion?

Their source and their discipline. Each widening arrived as a correction from a constituency the school claimed to serve, women, indigenous peoples, black movements, the movements of the earth, and each was tested by the school's standing operation, suspicion and retrieval, before it was admitted. Drift follows the audience. These followed the subjects, the direction the school's method prescribes.

The widenings pose, in aggregate, the question with which this article's balance sheet already grappled: whether a theology whose subject keeps widening and whose analysis keeps being corrected retains an identity, or whether the school's later form is a diaspora of its founding energies into other movements. The school's own answer would be that identity was never located in a list of conclusions but in a method and an option, and that a method which cannot be corrected by the people it serves has become the kind of system the school was founded to refuse. The answer is consistent, and it leaves the school's achievement where this article has located it throughout: not in a system that survived intact, but in a discipline of reading, analysing and accompanying that survived its system's defeats and entered the permanent equipment of the traditions it touched, the church's teaching, the Marxist theory of religion, and the practice of communities that have never heard its name.

## The new humanity: the school's anthropology against the revolutionary new man

The second level of liberation in the founding text, the project of a new humanity, contains the school's theological anthropology in embryo, and setting it beside its secular contemporary clarifies both. The revolutionary movements of the continent spoke, in those years, of the new man: the human type to be formed by the revolution, unselfish, disciplined, collectivist, whose creation Che Guevara had placed at the centre of socialism's moral economy. The theologians shared the diagnosis behind the phrase, that the existing order forms a human type fitted to itself, acquisitive, competitive and resigned in the poor, and that liberation must therefore be anthropological as well as structural, a change in what people are and not only in what they own. They refused, however, the revolutionary version's implication that the new humanity is a product to be manufactured by the movement that possesses the correct line. In the school's account, the new humanity is received before it is achieved: it is the humanity of the resurrection, given as gift and promise, and historical work participates in it without producing it. The difference sounds scholastic and is anything but. A new man who is manufactured can be manufactured by terror, and the century's revolutionary states had supplied the demonstration; a new humanity that is received sets a limit to every manufacturer, since no party and no church is the author of the humanity it serves, and the poor themselves, in their faith and their fiestas, already carry more of it than their liberators' programmes acknowledge.

### How does the school's anthropology limit its own politics?

By denying the movement ownership of its goal. If the new humanity is God's gift and the kingdom's content, then no organisation, party or church embodies it, every embodiment stands under judgement, and the poor's own humanity, already present and already dignified, is the movement's datum rather than its raw material. The limit is the anthropology's political cash value.

The anthropology also grounded the school's resistance to a temptation inside its own camp: the treatment of the poor as a collective messiah. Rome's first instruction had charged the movement with transferring to the poor, as a class, the role of the suffering servant and therewith a kind of collective sinlessness, and the charge found enough careless texts to be worth answering. The school's considered position was finer than the charge. The poor are the option's addressees not because they are virtuous, the theologians insisted against their own romantics, but because they are wronged, and because God's partiality in the scriptures attaches to the wronged as such; the poor are also sinners, also capable of domination in miniature, also in need of the liberation they are said to mediate. A theology that forgets the second half of that sentence will build a cult where it meant to build a church, and the school's best writers, who had lived among the poor rather than admiring them at a distance, rarely forgot it. The distinction between the poor as the gospel's privileged address and the poor as an idealised subject is one more row for the borrowing table's discipline, and one more place where the school's practice was more exact than the slogans of its friends.

## The school in the university: scholarship as a second front

The movement's relation to the academy was double from the start, and both halves belong in the record. The school's authors were university men, formed in Europe's faculties, holding chairs, directing institutes, editing the reviews in which Latin American theology became a scholarly discipline with its own canons and controversies. Their work created, within a generation, a theological literature of a volume and rigour that the continent had not previously possessed, and it placed Latin America, for the first time in the history of Christian thought, in the position of an exporter of theology to the older centres: European and North American faculties assigned the school's texts, translated its journals' debates and sent their students to learn from the communities the texts described. The reversal was noted at the time with justified pride, as a small decolonisation within the republic of letters.

### What did the academy give the movement, and what did it threaten?

It gave rigour, memory and a second audience: the discipline of sources, the archive of its development, and readers in the older centres whose attention protected the movement when its continent's institutions turned cold. It threatened domestication: the conversion of reflection on dangerous practice into a field of study, with careers built on exegesis of texts whose authors expected organisers.

The threat was not hypothetical, and the school's internal critics named it early: a theology of liberation can be taught, examined and cited in perfect safety by institutions that would dismiss a catechist for practising it, and the safer it becomes the more completely it has been translated from the second act of a commitment into the first act of a career. The school's defence was built into its method: the insistence that the theology is reflection on a praxis the reader must share, so that the academic reader is not offered a spectacle but an examination of conscience. Whether the defence worked is a question the enrolment figures of theology faculties cannot answer, and this article records it as the domestic form of the standing question that has organised the whole assessment. The separation the school asserted, between a faith that commits and an analysis that serves, has its academic analogue in the separation between a scholarship that studies liberation and a scholarship that belongs to it, and the second separation, like the first, is maintained in practice by some, asserted by many and demonstrated by none. The school's honesty about the first entitles it to the reader's patience about the second.

## Why this subject belongs in a series on Marxism

An article on a theological movement requires, in a series devoted to Marxism, a closing word of placement, and the word can now be given with the evidence assembled. Liberation theology belongs to the history of Marxism in Latin America for three reasons that no narrower framing can accommodate. It is, first, the most consequential use made of Marxist analysis on the continent outside the Marxist organisations themselves: more Latin Americans encountered the categories of class, exploitation and ideology in a catechist's circle than in any party school, and the encounter formed organisers whom the continent's politics is still spending. It is, second, the site of the most exact negotiation the Marxist tradition has anywhere conducted with a living religion: not the negotiation of the pamphlet, in which religion is dismissed, nor of the united front, in which it is used, but a negotiation at the level of method, in which each side's categories were tested against the other's reality and each side emerged amended, the theology with a social analysis it had lacked, the Marxism with a theory of religion its founder's subtler sentences had promised and its orthodoxy had forgotten. And it is, third, the standing counter-example by which the region's Marxism must grade its own claims about the superstructure: a case in which the realm of ideas, organised by an ancient institution among the poorest people of a continent, acted on the base with a force no honest materialism can wave away, and which therefore disciplines every future use of the word reflection. The series that houses this article exists to record what Marxism did in the world and what the world did to Marxism. On both sides of that ledger, few entries are larger than the one written, in Spanish and Portuguese, by the theologians of the poor.

## Inside the Medellin documents: what the bishops actually wrote

The conference of 1968 has been invoked so often, by every party to the later disputes, that its documents risk becoming a flag rather than a text, and an assessment of the movement should spend a section inside them. The bishops produced sixteen documents, and their centre of gravity is not where later polemic usually places it. The document on justice grounds the conference's social teaching in the tradition this article has already traced, the destination of all goods for all people, the dignity of work, the demand for structural reform of landholding, taxation and trade, and it addresses its demands in the first instance to the continent's own elites and governments rather than to any foreign power. The document on peace contains the conference's most quoted and most contested reasoning: having described the continent's situation as one of injustice amounting to institutionalised violence, it warns that the temptation to violence grows where peaceful change is blocked, cites the inherited doctrine that resistance to manifest tyranny can be legitimate, and simultaneously commits the church to the active non violence of those who work for justice without hatred. The document devoted to the church's own poverty turns the analysis inward with a frankness that still startles: the bishops commit their own institution to a poverty of style and of fact, to the denunciation of the scandal given by the church's wealth amid the people's misery, and to solidarity practised as proximity rather than as administration. The documents on education and on joint pastoral planning supply the movement's instruments: education as conscientisation, the awakening of the person's critical awareness of his world, and the parish restructured around small communities in which the laity share responsibility.

### Which Medellin document did the movement's later history most depend on?

The poverty of the church document, because it made the option a matter of the institution's own life rather than only of its teaching. A church that had promised to be poor with the poor gave its critics a standard by which to judge its palaces, schools and alliances, and the school's ecclesiology is that promise developed into a system.

Read whole, the documents support neither of the simple inheritances later claimed for them. They are not a revolutionary manifesto: they defend private property under its social mortgage, reject violence as a method while analysing its causes, and address the continent's rulers as men capable of reform. They are not a conservative text either: they name the existing order violent, commit the church to the poor against the structures that impoverish them, and authorise the laity's organised presence in the world in terms the older church would not have used. What they are is a hinge, a text written at the moment when an institution formed in one world addressed a continent that had already become another, and their ambiguity is the ambiguity of that moment rather than a failure of drafting. The movement and its opponents both descend from Medellin because Medellin contained them both in embryo, and the subsequent history, Puebla's confirmations, the instructions' distinctions, the appointments' slow reversal, is the history of the embryos being separated by force of circumstance. The school's claim to be Medellin's faithful heir is therefore exactly as strong, and exactly as contestable, as its opponents' counter claim, and the contest is settled, where it is settled at all, not by the documents but by the use each party made of the standard the documents set: the church's own life judged by its proximity to the poor.

## The opposition inside the continent: the other Latin American church

The movement's opponents were not only in Rome, and an account that casts the story as a prophetic periphery against a curial centre misses the extent to which the argument was Latin American on both sides. The episcopal council itself changed hands early: the Colombian bishop Alfonso Lopez Trujillo, elected its secretary general in 1972 and later its president and a cardinal, became the continent's most effective organiser of the resistance to the liberation current, arguing in print and in the council's machinery that the theology was a foreign ideology's colonisation of the Latin American faith and that the church's true path lay through evangelisation and the defence of the family and of life. Around that position gathered a real constituency: bishops who had watched neighbouring dioceses polarise, religious orders that judged the school a threat to their vocations and their unity, lay movements of the continent's Catholic middle classes for whom the church of the option appeared as the church of a politics they feared, and the military regimes' own Catholic ideologues, who supplied the resistance with its least reputable and most energetic allies.

### What was the strongest Latin American argument against the school?

That it divided the church along the continent's conflicts and destroyed the one institution that crossed them. The church's unique social gift is communion, a table where both widows can kneel; a church that takes sides in the class struggle forfeits the standing to reconcile, leaving the continent's hatreds with no arbiter and its poor with one more party.

The argument deserves its place in the assessment because it is not a Roman import and because the school's answer to it reveals the school's deepest commitment. The answer was that the communion being defended was, in the concrete conditions of the continent, a fiction maintained at the poor's expense: the table at which both widows knelt stood in a church built by one widow's husband's rents, its sermon counselled the other widow to patience, and its peace was the peace the Medellin document had already named as institutionalised violence wearing a liturgical dress. A reconciliation that does not pass through justice, the school held, is the opium in its ecclesial form, and the church's unity is worth having only as the unity of a converted people, not as the armistice of an unconverted one. The counter argument replied that the school's justice had a party's address and a decade's shelf life, while the church's communion had buried a hundred parties and would bury this one. Both arguments are stated here at the strength their proponents gave them, because the dispute between them is not closed by the events this article records: it is the permanent argument of a church that claims to be both universal and committed, conducted in Latin America in those decades at an intensity, and with a quality of mind on both sides, that the universal church has rarely matched. The reader who understands why neither side could concede has understood the movement's setting better than the reader who has merely chosen between them.

## The option travels upward: the school's language in the universal teaching

The decades after the instructions supply a coda to the Roman story that the assessment should record, since it bears directly on the question of who, in the end, persuaded whom. The encyclical Sollicitudo Rei Socialis of 1987, issued for the twentieth anniversary of the development encyclical the young theologians had radicalised, spoke of structures of sin in terms the school recognised, while attaching the qualifications the second instruction had prepared: structures are rooted in personal sin and are changed by conversion as well as by reform. The same text received the option for the poor into the universal church's programme in its own voice. The encyclical Centesimus Annus of 1991, written in the aftermath of the socialist states' collapse, performed the period's most delicate balancing act: it refused the Marxist collectivism whose failure it recorded, refused with equal explicitness a capitalism in which the market is the whole of the moral law, and restated the social mortgage on property and the priority of labour in language the Latin American school could have footnoted to its own tradition. Neither document cites the theologians; the institution's metabolism does not work by citation. But the trajectory is legible to any reader who sets the documents of 1967, 1984, 1986, 1987 and 1991 in a row: the themes the school had forced, the structural dimension that sin had acquired, the option for the poor, the critique of an economy that excludes, travelled steadily from the suspect margin toward the teaching centre, shedding at each stage the analytical machinery that had carried them and acquiring at each stage the qualifications that made them safe. Whether the shedding represents the themes' purification or their defusing is the last form of the standing question, and it is left, as the school's own history leaves it, to the judgement of readers who will have to live with the answer in institutions the article cannot foresee.

### What did the universal teaching keep, and what did it leave behind?

It kept the moral substance: the option, the social mortgage on property, structural sin, the refusal of both collectivism and the absolutised market. It left behind the analysis: class structure as the continent's key, the dependency framework, ideology critique turned on the church, and the ecclesiology of the church from below. The division is the absorption pattern in inventory form.

## The city as a theological place: ministry in the shantytowns

The movement's characteristic geography deserves separate treatment before the assessment closes, because the theology was formed not only in a continent but in a kind of place, and the place shaped its categories. The place was the shantytown, the belt of self built housing rising around every Latin American city of the period, populated by migrants from the countryside who had carried their villages' faith into the city's edge and found neither the parish structures of the village nor the services of the city waiting for them. The church that followed them there, in the persons of sisters who settled in the districts, priests who celebrated in chapels the residents had built, and lay catechists drawn from the streets themselves, encountered a Christianity already present and already organised by need: the neighbours' associations, the mothers' clubs, the communal kitchens of the crisis years, the rotating funds and the burial societies. The base community, in the city, was rarely a creation; it was the recognition and the theological naming of an organisation the poor had already built to survive, and the school's claim that the poor are subjects rather than objects was, in the shantytowns, not a thesis but a report.

### Why did the shantytown, rather than the factory, become the movement's emblematic site?

Because the period's poor were concentrated there, not in the industrial workplaces the classical analysis expected. The migrant there was a peasant yesterday, a builder's labourer in the morning, a street vendor in the afternoon and unemployed in the statistics, and a theology addressed to him had to begin from the neighbourhood and the household.

The urban setting corrected the borrowed analysis in a way the school's theorists were honest enough to record. The classical categories presumed a proletariat concentrated by production and disciplined by the factory; the shantytown held a population the economists of the period called marginal, whose relation to production was intermittent and whose solidarity ran through kinship, neighbourhood and devotion rather than through the point of production. A theology that waited for the classical subject would have waited in an empty chapel. The school instead theorised the subject it found, the poor as a people in their neighbourhoods, and in doing so produced, almost without noticing, its most durable amendment to the tradition it borrowed from: the relocation of the revolutionary subject from the factory floor to the territory of daily life, an amendment that the continent's later movements, of the landless, of the unemployed, of the neighbourhoods themselves, would take up as their own. The borrowing table's rows were graded, in the end, not only by theologians in their studies but by a geography that refused to match the lender's map, and the grading held because the school had built the refusal into its method from the first: the reality judges the instrument, and the instrument that cannot find the poor where the poor actually are is returned, however distinguished its pedigree, as unfit for the work.

## The movement in the Cold War: Washington reads the theology

The school's significance was registered, in its own time, by an audience its authors had not sought: the foreign policy establishment of the United States, for which Latin America was a hemisphere to be held and any doctrine that organised the poor was a strategic fact. The registration began early. A report prepared for President Nixon in 1969, after Nelson Rockefeller's troubled tour of the continent, identified the Catholic Church, changed since Medellin, as an institution no longer reliably aligned with the established order and potentially a centre of revolutionary sentiment. The judgement was repeated in sharper form in 1980 by the Committee of Santa Fe, a group of conservative policy intellectuals whose programme for a new inter American policy urged that the United States counter liberation theology explicitly, on the ground that the doctrine supplied the insurgencies of Central America with their moral infrastructure. The document is worth the historian's attention precisely because it took the theology as seriously as the theologians did: where Rome worried about the faith's purity and the Marxists about the analysis's completeness, Washington worried about the base communities as organisation, and set about supporting, with funds and favour, the ecclesial and political forces arrayed against them.

### Why did a theological current attract the attention of strategists?

Because it organised the constituency every Cold War party in Latin America needed and none could command: the poor majority in their own institutions, under leaders formed by a doctrine naming the existing order unjust. Strategists read the communities as infrastructure, and in their own terms were not wrong; their error was reading that faith as a disguise.

The geopolitical framing damaged the movement in ways its members felt daily and its historians must record without exaggeration. Under the national security regimes, the accusation that a catechist was a communist was not a debating point but a death sentence's first draft, and the accusation drew plausibility, in the eyes of the officers who acted on it, from the strategic literature that had classified the theology as an enemy instrument. The school's writers replied, with justice, that they had been denounced as Marxists by the strategists for borrowings their church was simultaneously investigating and their Marxist contemporaries found insufficient, a triangulation that might have amused them in a safer world. The episode completes the article's map of the movement's receptions: read as theology by its school, as heresy by its congregation, as ideology by its strategists and as consolation's refutation by its Marxist students, the same body of work was four different objects to its four audiences, and an assessment that adopts any one audience's object as the whole will misdescribe the other three. The discipline this article has practised, grading each claim at the level where it was made, is the only known protection against that error, and it is offered, in closing the body of the article, as the method's final vindication: the movement that taught its readers to grade borrowings row by row is best honoured by readers who grade the movement the same way.

## Reading the school: the principal texts in their order

A movement carried by books as well as by communities deserves a reader's map of the books themselves, and the map has a natural order, since the school's literature was built as an argument in stages. The foundation is Gutierrez's A Theology of Liberation of 1971, which must be read first because every later text is a commentary on its settlement: the definition of theology as the second act, the three levels of liberation, the rejection of developmentalism and the irruption of the poor are all stated there with an economy the expansions never quite recover. Beside it belongs the same author's 1983 volume of spiritual reflection, on drinking from the community's own wells, and his reading of the book of Job, published in the middle nineteen eighties, in which the theologian of structural sin confronts the suffering that no structure explains and finds, in the speeches of a blameless sufferer, the school's doctrine of gratuitous love. The two Gutierrez volumes, the programme and the prayer, are the school's alpha and its omega, and a reader who knows both knows why the movement cannot be reduced to either.

### In what order should a new reader approach the school's books?

Begin with the founding text, for the programme; pass to the ecclesiology and method of the Boff brothers, for the construction; then to Sobrino for the Christological centre and Segundo for the hermeneutics; and end with the founder's spiritual writings, where the school explains the construction's purpose. The order follows the argument's logic from act to reflection and back.

The construction's second storey is the work of the Boff brothers. Leonardo Boff's Jesus Christ Liberator, published in 1972, rebuilt Christology on the historical Jesus before Sobrino gave the enterprise its Salvadoran depth, and his Church: Charism and Power of 1981 carried the school's argument into the church's own order, with the consequences this article has recorded. Clodovis Boff's Theology and Praxis, published in the final years of the nineteen seventies, is the school's methodological summa, the source of the three mediations and the most rigorous statement of the epistemology on which the borrowing table rests; it is the least read of the major texts in English and the one a serious student can least afford to skip. Segundo's The Liberation of Theology, of the middle nineteen seventies, supplies the hermeneutic circle and the school's theory of ideology in its most philosophical form. Sobrino's Christology at the Crossroads, of the same years, and his later Jesus the Liberator, give the school's centre its mature statement: the kingdom, the crucified peoples and the resurrection read from El Salvador. Around these stand the works this article has cited in passing, Assmann's political economy of the church, Dussel's histories and his philosophy of liberation, Miranda's reading of Marx against the Bible, Tamez's biblical scholarship, Gebara's feminist reconstruction, each extending the settlement onto ground its founder had not surveyed. A literature of that architecture, programme, method, centre and extensions, is not the residue of a mood. It is the deposit of a school, and it is as a school, with a school's virtues of system and a school's vices of repetition, that posterity will read it.

## The testimony literature: the poor write in the first person

The school's method implied a literary consequence that arrived in the nineteen eighties and carried its argument further than any treatise travelled. If the poor are the subjects of theology, their own speech is a theological source, and the genre that institutionalised that speech was the testimonio: the book length testimony of a single witness, recorded and edited by an educated collaborator, in which a life stands for a people's condition. The genre's most famous instance appeared in 1983, the account of Rigoberta Menchu, a K'iche' Maya woman from the Guatemalan highlands whose family was destroyed by the counter insurgency and whose narrative, taken down by the anthropologist Elisabeth Burgos, moved from a village's customs and catechists through land conflict and army terror to exile and organisation. The book was read around the world, its author received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1992, and a later controversy over the literal accuracy of particular episodes became, itself, an education in the genre's terms: a testimony claims the truth of a people's experience in a representative life, and it is judged, by its serious readers, as witness rather than as affidavit. The liberation theologians recognised the genre as their own method wearing another dress. The catechist's circle had always begun from a life read aloud; the testimonio simply gave the circle a printing press, and the questions it raised, whose voice edits, whose memory governs, what a representative life may compress, are the hermeneutic circle's questions translated into the politics of publishing.

### What does a testimony claim that a treatise cannot?

Presence. A treatise argues that the poor are subjects; a testimony enacts it, placing the reader before a speaker whose authority is her life rather than her credentials, and whose generalisations arrive carrying the weight of the particular dead. The school held that theology begins there, in the encountered witness, and the genre is that conviction bound.

The testimony literature also returned the movement's argument to its senders with interest. Readers in Europe and North America who had consumed the theology as an idea encountered, in the testimonies, the catechists, the cooperatives and the massacres as a narrated world, and the solidarity movements that sustained the Central American opposition through the nineteen eighties were staffed disproportionately by people whose conversion had been literary before it was political. Within Latin America, the genre outlasted the school's institutional fortunes: the truth commissions of the post war years built their reports from testimony, the Chilean and Peruvian and Guatemalan records alike resting on the archived voices of witnesses whose statements were taken, in form after form, by the same patient method the vicariates had pioneered. A theology that had taught a continent to treat the speech of the poor as evidence ended by supplying the continent's justice with its archive, which is a posterity few schools of theology can claim and none of the school's founders predicted in terms.

## The long argument with modernity: development, progress and the school's suspicion of both

One further strand of the school's thought deserves recovery before the personas, because it explains the movement's tone toward its own century and connects its theology to arguments far beyond the church. The founding generation had been formed, as this article recorded, inside the postwar cult of development: the belief, shared by Washington, Moscow and the continent's own planners, that history moves by stages toward an industrial modernity whose benefits patience and technique would universalise. The liberation theologians were among the earliest intellectuals anywhere to refuse the cult's central consolation, the claim that the misery of the periphery is a stage on a journey rather than a product of the journey's organisation. Their refusal was theological before it was economic. A history that justifies present suffering by future arrival is, in the school's vocabulary, an ideology in the strict sense, a story the beneficiaries tell; and the God of the Exodus, who hears the cry of slaves in the present tense, authorises no such deferral. The point gave the school's critique its peculiar reach. It could address the developmentalism of the Alliance for Progress and the developmentalism of the socialist states with the same instrument, since both asked the poor to wait inside a story whose ending the poor had not written, and both treated the waiting as science.

The refusal also separated the school from a temptation inside its own inheritance, the temptation of the church's older social doctrine to baptise whichever modernity was on offer provided its morals were supervised. The school's writers insisted that the question is not whether the church blesses or curses the modern world but whether the modern world's own account of itself can survive interrogation from underneath, from the districts where its costs are invoiced. That interrogation, conducted with borrowed instruments and an unborrowed faith, is the movement's whole method stated at the scale of an epoch, and it is why the school's texts retain their address to readers who share neither its faith nor its politics. Every generation is offered a story in which its arrangements are a stage; the school's permanent question, addressed to the offer, is the one the base communities learned to ask of every text placed before them: who is telling this story, whose suffering does it schedule, and what would it look like read from the place where the schedule is served. A movement that teaches its readers to ask those three questions of every orthodoxy, including eventually its own, has built something more durable than a position. It has built a habit of suspicion in the service of hope, and habits, unlike positions, can be inherited by people who have never heard the names of their inventors.

## Periods of the movement: a short chronology in prose

For the reader's orientation, the movement's life divides into four periods, each with its own centre of gravity. The first, formation, runs from the council's close to the founding text of 1971: the years of the pastoral experiments, the priests' movements, Medellin and the drafting of the programme. The second, expansion, runs through the nineteen seventies: the school's construction, the communities' multiplication, Puebla's confirmation and the first martyrs, the years in which the movement was simultaneously a literature, an organisation and a target. The third, confrontation, occupies the nineteen eighties: the two instructions, the silencing, the Central American wars at their height, the priests in office in Nicaragua and the human rights church under the dictatorships, the decade in which every question the movement had deferred was asked at once and at gunpoint. The fourth, dispersal, runs from the socialist collapse through the century's end and beyond the point where summary can follow it: absorption of the vocabulary, contraction of the base, migration of the method into successor theologies and popular movements, and the slow rehabilitation of the founders in the institution's esteem. The periods overlap and their borders are arguments rather than facts; a history that begins the confrontation in 1979 with Puebla's warnings, or the dispersal in 1985 with the silencing, can be defended from the record. What the scheme secures is the shape: a movement formed in hope, expanded in organisation, confronted in power and dispersed in victory's strangest form, the victory of its words over its organisation. Few movements are granted so legible an arc, and fewer still survive the granting with their questions intact.

The periods also map, with uncomfortable neatness, onto the borrowing table's fortunes. In formation, the instruments were gathered; in expansion, they were used at full stretch and their yield was greatest; in confrontation, their ownership was disputed by the two objectors this article has staged; in dispersal, the instruments passed into hands their borrowers never met, the feminist and indigenous correctors, the organisers of parties and unions, the drafters of the church's own documents, each grading the inheritance by the only test the school itself recognised, what it explains and what it builds on the ground where it is used. A reader who keeps the four periods and the six rows in view together possesses the article's whole argument in portable form, and can test any summary of the movement, including the summaries offered by its friends, against the two exhibits without further assistance.

One caution belongs to the scheme itself. Periods are conveniences imposed on a reality that was lived, in any given year, at several of the scheme's stages simultaneously: a diocese expanding its communities in 1986 while its neighbour buried catechists, a theologian in dispersal's exile writing expansion's most confident texts. The school's own method, which began every inquiry from the concrete situation rather than from the schema, would insist on the caution, and the insistence is honoured here by recording it. The arc is real, and it is a summary, and the difference between those two judgements is the difference the whole article has been written to teach.

## For students and exam candidates

The examinable core of this subject is a distinction, and answers that reproduce it exactly earn their marks before they add anything else: liberation theologians used Marxist social analysis as a socio-analytic mediation, an instrument for explaining poverty, while refusing the materialist philosophy and its atheism, and the whole controversy, with Rome on one side and Marxist critics on the other, turns on whether that separation is coherent. Build every answer on the borrowing table's rows rather than on the label Marxist or not Marxist, since the graded treatment of the adopted instruments, class and exploitation, ideology critique and dependency, against the refusal of historical materialism and atheism is what distinguishes an analytical answer from a summary. Keep the institutional facts in their order: Medellin in 1968, the founding text of 1971, Puebla's confirmation of that option in 1979, the two instructions of 1984 and 1986 with their differing tones, and the silencing of Boff in 1985. For revision, the borrowing table repays being kept where it can be reviewed and annotated over time; the study notes collected at [VaultBook's Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are built for exactly that kind of structured return to a framework, and candidates preparing on the Indian examinations for which this material also appears can test their command of the surrounding syllabus against the previous years' questions gathered at [ReportMedic's UPSC previous year question explorer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html). Avoid the two errors that markers see most: calling the theologians Marxists without qualification, and calling the movement condemned without reading the second instruction.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The mediation distinction is the most teachable item in this article, because it gives a seminar a precise and portable example of a distinction students usually handle only as a slogan: the difference between borrowing an instrument and adopting a framework. Set the borrowing table before the group and require each row to be argued separately, with the objection column answered from the theologians' own texts before any verdict is allowed; the exercise disciplines the habit, transferable to every other borrowing in intellectual history, of grading adoptions one by one. The two objections then stage themselves: assign the Roman case from the 1984 instruction and the Marxist case from the tradition's own critics, and let the seminar discover that the objections are mirror images, each predicting the borrower's slide toward the objector. The base communities supply the seminar's second hour, since they convert the methodological question into a sociological one: whatever the distinction's status in theory, a mass practice maintained it for decades, and students should be asked what kind of evidence practice is for a distinction's coherence. Close with the counter-example problem for the Marxist theory of religion, which returns the group to the series' larger concerns with a question no one in the room will settle: what a theory owes a fact it did not predict.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three cautions govern serious work on this subject. The first is documentary: the movement's record is split between a translated shelf of treatises and an untranslated ocean of pastoral materials, conference proceedings and community publications in Spanish and Portuguese, and accounts built on the shelf alone will reproduce the error of treating a mass movement as a literature; the asymmetry is structural and should be declared in any work that cannot correct it. The second caution is disciplinary: the Roman documents must be read as arguments, with the 1984 instruction's inseparability thesis stated at its full strength, and not as episodes in a suppression narrative, just as the theologians must be read as theologians and not as politicians in vestments; the subject is destroyed from either side by the refusal to take its ideas seriously as ideas. The third caution is numerical: the movement's scale, in communities, adherents and clergy, is a field of contested and interested estimates, and the discipline this series applies, characterising the network as very extensive on the authority of the sociological literature and asserting no counts, is recommended to any researcher who does not wish to launder a party's arithmetic into a fact. The live questions for new work are identifiable: the communities' alumni inside the continent's parties and unions, the untranslated archive of the human rights vicariates, and the comparative study of the liberation pattern's recurrences on other continents, each of which promises evidence the existing literature, rich as it is, does not yet contain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is liberation theology?

A theological movement that arose among Latin American Christians from the end of the nineteen sixties, built on a single redefinition: theology is critical reflection on the practice of liberation, undertaken in the light of faith. It reads reality from the position of the impoverished majority, commits the church to stand with them preferentially, and explains their condition through structures rather than misfortune. Gustavo Gutierrez's book of 1971 gave it its programme, the Medellin bishops' conference of 1968 its institutional opening, and the base ecclesial communities its local embodiment across the continent. Its questions remain theology's own throughout: God, sin, salvation, and the nature of the church.

### Q: How does liberation theology use Marxism?

As a toolkit for social explanation, taken up deliberately and refused as a creed. From Marxism the theologians took the categories that expose how poverty is manufactured: class position, exploitation, the critique of ideology, the dependency of the periphery. What they would not take was the system's philosophy, its materialism and its denial of God, which they held to answer different questions from the ones the categories answer. Their technical name for the arrangement was mediation, the analysis standing between faith and society as an instrument of knowledge. Both Rome and orthodox Marxists replied that instruments of that power cannot be quarantined from the frameworks that produced them, and the argument between the school and its two sets of critics is the controversy's centre.

### Q: What is the preferential option for the poor?

A commitment, adopted by the Latin American bishops at Medellin and confirmed at Puebla, that the church is required to stand with the poor in a definite order of solidarity: not as a benefactor distributing relief from above, but as a companion sharing their situation and supporting their own organisation and agency. Its warrants are scriptural, the Exodus, the prophets, the Magnificat and Matthew's scene of judgement, and its content is structural rather than charitable. To opt for the poor is to oppose the arrangements that make them poor, which is why the phrase travelled from conference documents into the worldwide church's teaching while the analysis attached to it remained contested. The option is preferential, not exclusive: it orders attention and loyalty without restricting grace.

### Q: What are base ecclesial communities?

The movement's local cell: a small circle of neighbours, in a poor urban district or a village, meeting regularly around the Bible. A passage was read, the group's own conditions were examined in its light, and the meeting closed with a decision: a cooperative organised, a claim filed, a well dug. Lay animators, most of them women, kept the circles running between the visits of priests who served many circles at once. From the late nineteen sixties the circles multiplied across the continent until the sociological literature describes the resulting network as very extensive; precise counts are contested and none is asserted here. The communities were the theology's mass form, the place where its reading method and its social analysis were practised weekly by the people they concerned.

### Q: Did the Vatican condemn liberation theology?

The record is one of distinction, not blanket condemnation. Rome's doctrinal congregation published two instructions, one in 1984 and one in 1986. The earlier document warned against theologies it judged to have absorbed Marxist analysis uncritically, arguing that the analysis drags an atheistic philosophy behind it and bends sin, salvation and the church out of shape. The later document built the church's own theology of liberation, welcomed the option for the poor under careful glosses, and accepted a qualified speech about structures of sin. Discipline fell on individuals, most visibly the silencing imposed on Leonardo Boff in 1985; the school's founder was never condemned. Condemnation of the movement as such is a summary the documents do not support.

### Q: Is liberation theology still active?

Its condition is best described as transformation rather than survival or disappearance. The mass organisation of the base communities contracted under episcopal appointments that withdrew encouragement, under the migration of organisers into parties, unions and civic bodies, and under competition from fast growing Pentecostal churches in the same neighbourhoods. At the same time the movement's language was absorbed into the church's ordinary teaching, its method passed into successor theologies, feminist, ecological, indigenous and others, and its former members carried the habits of reading and meeting they had learned into the continent's popular movements. The school's founding generation was not replaced at equal weight, and its sharpest analytical claims were retired by the institution that adopted its vocabulary. A movement that succeeds in part characteristically ends in that divided condition.

### Q: Who was Oscar Romero?

Oscar Romero was Archbishop of San Salvador from 1977 until his death three years later. Appointed in the expectation that he would restrain an activist clergy, he was changed by the murder of his friend Rutilio Grande and by the repression his archdiocese documented week by week: his Sunday homilies, carried by radio across the country, named the disappeared and judged the violence by the gospel, and his legal aid office built the record the state refused to keep. In March 1980 he was shot while saying Mass, one day after calling on soldiers to disobey orders to kill. The movement venerates him as its martyr; the universal church beatified him in 2015 and canonised him three years after that.

### Q: Does liberation theology refute the opium of the people argument?

Partly, and the partition matters. Where the classical argument says religion consoles the oppressed and sanctifies their oppression, the Latin American record shows faith doing the opposite at scale: scripture supplying the indictment of an order, communities supplying its organised opposition, clergy among its victims. The theologians themselves wielded the argument's second half, ideology critique, against their own church's history of blessing oligarchies. What survives of Marx's formula is its subtlest strand, religion as protest, the groan of a suffering creature against the conditions that make it groan; the movement can be read as that strand organised. What falls is the inference from religion's frequent service to domination toward a necessity written into faith itself.

### Q: What is institutionalised violence?

The Medellin bishops' name for an order whose ordinary workings maim and kill without anyone striking a blow: land concentrated until the landless starve beside export harvests, labour regimes that consume the labourer, districts abandoned by medicine and law. Calling the condition violence, rather than reserving the word for riots and insurgencies, reversed the period's official bookkeeping, in which the state kept the peace and only its enemies broke it. The theologians housed the idea in the doctrine of structural sin: arrangements people build and keep choosing are morally chargeable even when no single operator intends the outcome. Critics answer that a condition cannot repent; the school's reply is that conditions are rebuilt daily by decisions, and decisions have authors.

### Q: Are there liberation theologies outside Latin America?

Yes; the pattern proved portable. Korean theologians of the nineteen seventies formed minjung theology around the suffering and hope of the people under dictatorship. In India, dalit theologians took the pathos of the castes stigmatised as untouchable as their starting point and rebuilt the social analysis around caste. James Cone's black theology in North America, the prophetic theology of South Africa's Kairos Document, mujerista and feminist theologies, and a Palestinian theology of liberation each worked the same seam on different ground: a scripture read from below, a named people as the reading's subject, and the strongest available account of the structure that crushes them. The analyses diverge as the structures do; the method is the family likeness.

### Q: How does the mediation distinction grade a borrowing from Marxist analysis?

It grades element by element rather than by label. A borrowing is examined for what it is, an instrument of social knowledge or a claim about the whole of reality; for its warrant, the explanations it actually delivers on the terrain it was borrowed to serve; and for its jurisdiction, whether it is being kept at the level of social analysis or allowed to govern the theology's foundations. Class analysis, exploitation, ideology critique and dependency pass the grading as instruments, answerable to evidence. Historical materialism as a philosophy and atheism fail it, not because they are Marxist but because they answer questions the theology holds to be already answered from its own sources. The borrowing table in this article is the distinction applied, and the disputes about the movement are, at bottom, disputes about whether the grading can be enforced in practice.

### Q: What should a student separate between borrowing an instrument and adopting a framework?

Three things. First, the questions each answers: an instrument answers questions about how a society works, a framework answers questions about what reality is. Second, the warrant each carries: an instrument is justified by its yield, case by case, and can be laid down where it stops explaining; a framework is adopted as a whole and judges its user before the user can judge it. Third, the consequences of error: a mistaken instrument produces a mistaken analysis, which evidence can correct, while a mistaken framework reorganises every conclusion downstream. Students who keep the three separations distinct can state the liberation theologians' procedure exactly, and can also state, without confusion, why Rome and the Marxist critics each denied that the separations could hold in practice.

### Q: Why did Rome object to the borrowing even where it shared the conclusions?

Because the quarrel was about method, not conclusions. Rome's 1984 instruction shared the movement's horror at the continent's poverty and endorsed standing with the poor; what it denied was that the analysis the theologians borrowed could be quarantined from the philosophy that generated it. In the congregation's reading, the Marxist account of society arrives as a total system: struggle between classes as history's engine, religion as a byproduct awaiting dissolution. Admit the analysis, the argument ran, and the system follows it through the door, recasting sin as structure alone, salvation as political success and the church as the people's party. The objection therefore survived every agreement on particulars, since it concerned what the borrowed instrument would do to the borrowers over time. The school answered that instruments are graded by their yield; the congregation was never persuaded.

### Q: How does the borrowing table order the exchanges?

It sets the adoptions first and the refusals after, six rows in all, and gives each row four entries: the element of the Marxist tradition, the theologians' decision, the reason they offered, and the objection the decision drew. Class analysis, exploitation, ideology critique and dependency stand in the adopted rows; the materialist philosophy in its total form, and its atheism, stand in the refused rows. The sequence is the argument: a reader who begins at the top watches an instrument being assembled, and a reader who reaches the bottom watches its limits being drawn, and neither reader can honestly collapse the two movements into a single verdict about whether the theologians were Marxists. The table is this article's central exhibit because it forces the grading of the borrowing to happen element by element.

### Q: What separates treating the theology as serious theology from treating it as politics in religious dress?

By the company its claims keep. An account that treats the movement as politics in costume asks about alliances, funding and effects, and never has to decide whether its doctrines are true, or even what they are. An account that treats it as theology argues with the doctrines themselves: a God disclosed as liberator before he is confessed as creator, sin lodged in arrangements as well as in wills, the poor installed as subjects of the church's knowledge, earthly liberations received as pledges of a kingdom they do not exhaust. Those propositions carried the movement's practice, and they, not the costumes, are what its friends defended and its critics engaged. The same courtesy is owed in reverse: the Roman instructions are arguments about how ideas behave, and they shrink when read as mere acts of discipline.
