A farmer who carries a harvest to a market yard in most of India performs an act of faith in a number. The number is the minimum support price, announced with ceremony before the sowing seasons, printed in newspapers, debated in Parliament and treated in public argument as though it were a floor written into law beneath every transaction in farm produce. When the auction at the yard opens below that number, as it does for most crops in most districts in most seasons, the farmer discovers the fact that this article exists to state precisely: there is no law behind the number. No Act of Parliament creates the minimum support price, prescribes how it is calculated, obliges any buyer to pay it, or gives the farmer who is paid less any remedy arising from the announcement itself. The price is an executive decision, taken by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs on the recommendation of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, a body that is itself not a statutory body but an attached office of the agriculture ministry created by a government resolution. The most argued-about figure in Indian agriculture is, in the strict legal sense, an announcement.

![Minimum support price and the procurement framework, showing why the announced price carries no statutory obligation - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-108.webp)

That single fact reorganises every other question in the debate. It explains why a farmer paid below the announced price has no cause of action founded on the price. It explains why the demand that survived the farm laws agitation and the repeal of the three farm laws of 2020 was not a demand to enforce an existing entitlement but a demand to create one that has never existed. It explains why governments can promise the price in one season and under-deliver it in the next without breaking any legal obligation, because the only obligation the announcement creates is political. And it explains why the argument about a legal guarantee has run for years without producing a bill: converting an announcement into an entitlement requires answering drafting questions of genuine difficulty, about whom the duty binds, in what form, for which crops, in what quantity, at whose cost and with what consequence when the guaranteed price sits above the market price for an entire harvest. Those questions are set out in the design table later in this article, because they are the substance that the slogan conceals.

This article works through the whole arrangement in the order a reader needs it. It states what the announcement legally is and what it is not. It explains who recommends the price, who decides it, and how the figure is built from cost concepts whose names, A2, A2 plus FL and C2, carry the entire weight of the fiercest dispute in the field, the dispute about whether the Swaminathan Commission's recommended margin of fifty per cent has been delivered or quietly redefined. It describes who actually buys at the announced price, through the Food Corporation of India, the state agencies, the decentralised procurement scheme and the price support operations for pulses and oilseeds, and why that buying is concentrated in a handful of states and two crops. It then turns to the remedies question in the form a rights and remedies article must: what a farmer who is paid less can actually enforce, through contract, through the market regulation statutes, through the one neighbouring price, the fair and remunerative price for sugarcane, that does rest on a statutory instrument, and through the experiments states have run with bonuses and deficiency payments. It closes with the guarantee debate stated at full strength on both sides, because the stance of this series is that a contested question deserves its strongest advocates before it deserves a verdict.

## An announcement, not an enactment

Begin with the negative, because the negative is the article. Search the statute book for the minimum support price and the search fails. There is no Minimum Support Price Act. There is no chapter of any other Act that fixes the price, names the crops, or prescribes the margin over cost. The Essential Commodities Act 1955, which is the statute most often assumed to stand behind agricultural prices, does something quite different: it empowers the government to issue control orders regulating production, supply, distribution and trade in essential commodities, and prices can be touched through that machinery in particular cases, but the support price announcements do not issue under it and draw no force from it. The state agricultural produce marketing statutes regulate the yards in which produce is sold, the licences of those who trade there and the fees the market committees charge, and they too are silent about any duty to transact at the announced price. The support price floats free of the entire body of enacted law. It exists because the executive announces it, season after season, on advice, and it would cease to exist, without any repeal, amendment or judicial proceeding, if a Cabinet chose not to announce it.

The institutional chain that produces the number is administrative from end to end. The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices prepares recommendations for each season, kharif and rabi, after studying costs of cultivation collected through a comprehensive scheme of cost studies, consulting state governments, farmers' organisations and other interests, and weighing the factors its terms of reference prescribe, which include the cost of production, changes in input prices, the parity between prices paid and prices received by farmers, demand and supply, inter-crop price parity, the effect on industrial cost structures and on the cost of living, and the likely implications for the economy's price level. The commission's recommendation then travels to the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, the committee of the Union Cabinet charged with economic decisions, which takes the final decision and is free to accept, raise or moderate what has been recommended. The announcement follows. At no point in that chain does a legislature vote, a statute speak, or a legal duty crystallise on any buyer in any market yard in the country.

### Is the minimum support price a legal right a farmer can enforce?

No. The announcement creates no right in any farmer and no duty in any buyer, public or private. It binds neither the trader who bids at the mandi nor, in strict law, the state itself, whose procurement agencies buy under schemes and administrative arrangements rather than under a statutory command issued to them for each farmer's benefit.

The character of the recommending body deserves emphasis, because it is the second most common error in public writing on this subject, after the error of treating the price as statutory. The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices began life in January 1965 as the Agricultural Prices Commission, constituted by a resolution of the Government of India, and was renamed to its present form in 1985. It is an attached office of the Department of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare in the Ministry of Agriculture. An attached office is a creature of executive organisation, not of legislation: it has no statutory charter, no powers conferred by Parliament, no tenure protection written into an Act, and its advice has no legal force even on the government that receives it. Successive governments have treated the commission's recommendations with respect, and departures from them are politically costly, but political cost is not legal obligation. When the committee notified in July 2022 to examine the support price system was asked, among its terms of reference, to address the autonomy of the commission, the question itself conceded the point: an advisory office staffed and housed by the ministry whose policies it advises on is an instrument of the executive, and both the price and the advice behind it belong wholly to the executive branch.

Compare this with the prices in Indian law that do carry legal force, because the comparison isolates exactly what is missing. Where Parliament or a competent legislature intends a price to bind, it says so in a statute or in a statutory instrument, attaches a consequence to non-payment, and names the authority that will enforce it. The fair and remunerative price for sugarcane, examined in detail below, is fixed under a control order issued under the Essential Commodities Act, and a sugar mill that fails to pay it faces interest on arrears and recovery proceedings under that order. Wages under the minimum wages legislation are enforceable by claim before an authority the statute names. The support price has none of this architecture: no instrument, no consequence, no forum. A farmer who is paid less than the announced price stands, in relation to that announcement, exactly where a citizen stands in relation to a target in a plan document. The number describes an intention of the state. It does not describe an obligation of anyone.

It follows that litigation founded on the announcement itself has nowhere to go. Courts asked to convert the announcement into an entitlement run into the settled position that price fixation and economic policy lie within the executive's domain, and that a recommendation by an advisory body, followed by a Cabinet decision of a general character, does not generate an individual right that a writ court can enforce against a private buyer or compel the state to honour by unlimited purchase. The grievances that do reach courts in this field are of different kinds: challenges to procurement operations conducted arbitrarily, disputes about grading and rejection of grain at purchase centres, claims under scheme guidelines by farmers who were within a procurement operation and were wrongly turned away. Those claims arise from the schemes and from ordinary public law duties of fairness in administration. None of them arises from the price. The distinction matters for everything that follows in this article, because the remedies a farmer actually has are remedies attached to transactions, to schemes and to contracts, never to the announced figure itself.

## Where the figure comes from: costs, margins and the commission

The announced price is built on cost accounting, and the accounting categories are not technical decoration. They are the battleground on which the largest dispute in Indian farm policy has been fought for two decades, so they must be stated exactly. The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices works with cost concepts developed for the cost of cultivation studies. Cost A2 covers the expenses a cultivator actually pays out in cash and kind: seed, fertiliser, manure, pesticides, hired human labour, hired and owned machinery and bullock labour charged at working rates, irrigation charges, land revenue and interest on working capital, together with rent paid for leased-in land. Cost A2 plus FL adds to A2 the imputed value of the family's own labour, the work the farm household performs without paying itself a wage. Cost C2 is the comprehensive measure: it takes A2 plus FL and adds the imputed rental value of owned land and the interest on the value of owned fixed capital, so that it prices not only what the farmer spends but what the farmer's own assets could have earned elsewhere. C2 is always substantially higher than A2 plus FL for the same crop in the same season, because land rent and capital interest are large items in most farm budgets, and the gap between the two measures is the ground on which the implementation dispute stands.

The commission recommends a price for each crop after projecting these costs for the coming season, and its reports express the margin the recommended price carries over each measure, so that the recommendation can be read against A2 plus FL and against C2 alike. The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs then decides. Two features of this process are worth holding firmly. The first is that the recommendation is crop by crop and state-weighted: costs differ across states, and the commission builds all-India projections from state cost data, so the single announced number for a crop is an average-based figure that may sit above the costs of an efficient producer in a low-cost region and below the costs of a producer on poor land with dear inputs. The second is that the announcement precedes the season it governs, kharif prices announced before the monsoon sowing and rabi prices before the winter sowing, because the instrument's original purpose, examined below, was to influence what farmers chose to plant. A price announced after harvest would be a compensation scheme. A price announced before sowing is a signal, and a signal is what the support price, in its design, has always principally been.

The list of crops covered by the announcement is fixed by the mandate given to the commission, and it is shorter than public argument usually assumes. The support price is recommended and announced for twenty-two mandated crops: fourteen kharif crops, among them paddy, jowar, bajra, maize, ragi, tur, moong, urad, groundnut, sunflower seed, soyabean, sesamum, nigerseed and cotton; six rabi crops, wheat, barley, gram, lentil, rapeseed and mustard, and safflower; and two commercial crops, copra and raw jute, which together with the fourteen kharif and six rabi entries make up the twenty-two crops the commission's mandate covers across the cereal, pulse, oilseed and commercial categories. Sugarcane stands apart: for it, the corresponding figure is the fair and remunerative price, recommended by the same commission and approved by the same Cabinet committee, but resting, once fixed, on a statutory order rather than on announcement alone, a difference whose consequences occupy a later section of this article. For every other crop a farmer may grow, and the great majority of horticultural produce, vegetables, fruits, milk, eggs and livestock products among them, there is no support price at all. The instrument covers a defined list of field crops, and even for crops on the list, as the procurement sections will show, the announcement is only as real as the buying machinery that stands behind it in a particular state.

### How is the minimum support price calculated?

It is projected from cost of cultivation studies that classify a farm's expenses as A2, meaning paid-out costs, A2 plus FL, which adds the imputed value of family labour, and C2, which further adds imputed rent on owned land and interest on owned capital. The commission recommends a margin over these measures and the Cabinet committee decides the final figure.

The factors the commission must weigh extend beyond cost, and stating them matters because critics who treat the price as a pure cost-plus formula misdescribe the exercise. The terms of reference require a view on the need to provide incentives to producers for adopting improved technology and developing a production pattern broadly in the light of national requirements, on the likely effect of the price on the rest of the economy, particularly on the cost of living and on industrial costs and competitiveness, on inter-crop parity, so that relative prices steer cropping patterns in intended directions, and on the terms of trade between the agricultural and non-agricultural sectors. The price is therefore an administered judgment dressed in cost accounting, not the output of a formula that yields one correct answer. Two implications follow. A recommendation can be defended and attacked on several independent grounds at once, which is why the debate is so difficult to close. And the choice of the cost measure against which the margin is publicly benchmarked, A2 plus FL or C2, is itself a political decision about how the achievement will be described, which is exactly the terrain of the next section.

## How the costs are known: the cultivation studies and their critics

Behind the cost concepts stands a data machine that is rarely described and often contested. The cost figures the commission projects begin in the Comprehensive Scheme for Studying the Cost of Cultivation of Principal Crops, a statistical operation run since 1970-71 through the Directorate of Economics and Statistics with state agricultural universities and research institutions, under which a rotating sample of farm holdings in selected blocks records its operations, inputs and outputs through the season in prescribed schedules. From those records the costs are compiled state by state and crop by crop, and the commission projects them forward to the coming season using price indices for inputs and labour, because the recommendation must be made before the season whose costs it purports to measure. Every stage of that chain has its professional critics, and their criticisms matter because a price defended as cost-based inherits the strengths and weaknesses of the costs. The sample frames age between renewals, so the holdings studied may drift from the population they represent; the projection method assumes that the coming season's input prices will move with the indices chosen, which drought, fuel prices and wage movements can falsify; the treatment of family labour, owned land and management input within the concepts is conventional rather than self-evident, so that two honest accountants can build different C2 figures for the same fields; and state governments, which generate their own estimates through their agricultural departments and statistical bureaus, regularly produce figures above the commission's, so that the consultation stage features rival cost books for the same crop in the same state. None of this makes the exercise worthless: the cost studies are the most sustained empirical record of Indian farm economics in existence, and the commission's published reports expose their data to scrutiny in a way few price-setting processes anywhere attempt. It does mean that the announcement's apparent precision, a single figure to the rupee, rests on estimation layers that a reader should know are there. A statute that fixed a formula in law would freeze one set of conventions into the legal text and transfer the quarrel from the consultation room to the courtroom, where the question in case after case would become whether the state's accountants had applied the statutory concept correctly. That prospect, rarely mentioned in the guarantee debate, is one of the quieter reasons drafting a workable formula statute is harder than demanding one.

## The decision above the recommendation: the Cabinet, the states and the calendar

Between the commission's report and the announcement stands the decision of the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, and that interval is where the instrument's executive character is most visible. The committee is not bound by the recommendation, and the record of announcements includes seasons in which the approved price exceeded the recommended figure, seasons in which it matched, and seasons in which the announcement was delayed beyond the point at which it could influence the sowing it was meant to guide. Because the decision is an act of government rather than the application of a rule, it absorbs every pressure that bears on the government of the day: the fiscal position, the inflation readings of the preceding months, the electoral calendar, and the lobbying of state governments, several of which submit their own cost computations to the commission and publicly demand prices above the recommended level for the crops their farmers grow. State governments participate in the commission's consultations as a matter of course, and chief ministers write to the Centre pressing their farmers' case, but participation is not a share in the decision: the price is the Union's to announce, even though agriculture is a state subject and the buying, in large part, is done by state agencies with state staff. That asymmetry, a Union announcement executed substantially by state machinery, recurs throughout this field and returns in the guarantee debate, where the question of which level of government a statute would bind cannot be separated from the question of which level would pay.

The calendar of announcement carries its own politics. Kharif prices are due before the monsoon, and a delayed announcement is widely read as a signal of fiscal anxiety or of disagreement between the agriculture ministry and the finance ministry over the inflationary consequence of a generous increase. The commission's reports are published and are documents of real analytical substance, setting out the cost projections state by state, the margin the recommended price carries over each cost measure, and the commission's reasoning on parity and demand; a reader who wants to know what the price for a crop is built on will find the material there, in a form that the announcement itself, a bare number in a press release, never supplies. The discipline this article recommends to any reader of an announcement is therefore to read it against the report: the margin over A2 plus FL and over C2, the states whose costs the all-India figure leaves above the line, and the crops for which the announcement, though made with full ceremony, will be followed by no procurement operation of any size. An announcement read that way is an informative document about the government's intentions. An announcement read as a promise is a misunderstanding waiting for a harvest.

## The fifty per cent dispute: one recommendation, two cost measures

The National Commission on Farmers, constituted in November 2004 and chaired by M. S. Swaminathan, submitted five reports between December 2004 and October 2006 on the agrarian distress of that period. Its best-known recommendation, stated in its final report, was that the minimum support price should be at least fifty per cent more than the weighted average cost of production, and the commission's own framing, both in the report and in Swaminathan's repeated public restatements of it, made plain that the cost in question was the comprehensive cost, C2, including the imputed rent on land and interest on capital. The formula entered political language as C2 plus fifty per cent, and in that form it became a standing commitment that farmer organisations demanded and opposition parties promised. Swaminathan's reasoning, stated in the report, was that the net take-home income of farmers should be comparable to that of salaried employees, and that a price built on anything less than comprehensive cost would systematically understate what farming actually costs the family that practises it, because the family's land and labour are real contributions even when no cash changes hands.

The government's answer, when it came, adopted the fifty per cent figure and changed the measure. In the Union Budget for 2018-19, the Finance Minister announced that the support prices for crops would be fixed at at least one and a half times the cost of production, honouring what the government described as a promise made to farmers. When the kharif prices for 2018-19 were approved by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs in July 2018, the official statements expressed the achievement as a return of at least fifty per cent over the cost of production, with the cost measure used being A2 plus FL, the paid-out costs together with imputed family labour, excluding the imputed rent on owned land and interest on owned capital that C2 carries. On that measure the claim of delivery was arithmetically supportable for the season's announcements. On the Swaminathan measure it was not, and Swaminathan said so himself at the time, describing the July 2018 announcement as a welcome step that nevertheless fell short of the recommended level, and illustrating the gap from the commission's own concepts: the margin over C2 for the principal crops, on his computation using CACP cost figures, ran well below fifty per cent. Both sides of this dispute state their arithmetic correctly. They are measuring against different denominators, and every public assertion that the recommendation has or has not been implemented, which does not specify the measure, is empty.

The dispute is worth one more level of analysis, because it is the cleanest example in this field of how an executive instrument escapes accountability. A statutory formula, whatever its merits, would have fixed the measure in law and made the argument a question of compliance that courts and auditors could test. An announcement-based system leaves the measure to the announcer, so the standard against which delivery is judged can be chosen after the figure is known. Farmer organisations, including the Samyukt Kisan Morcha in the charter it carried through the 2020 to 2021 agitation, continued to demand C2 plus fifty per cent as one element of the wider guarantee demand. Governments of both national parties have, in office, worked with the narrower measure. The honest statement of the position is that the fifty per cent margin exists as a benchmark over A2 plus FL by declared policy, that C2 plus fifty per cent exists as a recommendation of 2006 that no government has adopted, and that the distance between the two is not a rounding difference but a structural gap equal to the imputed rent of land and the return on farm capital across the entire cropped area of the country.

## Why the minimum support price began as an incentive

The design becomes intelligible once its origin is recovered, because the support price was never conceived as a welfare entitlement. It belongs to the food crisis of the mid-1960s, when drought, dependence on imported grain under concessional arrangements with the United States, and the arrival of high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice confronted the government with a single practical problem: how to persuade cultivators to adopt unfamiliar seed, heavier fertiliser doses and new practices whose returns were uncertain. The Agricultural Prices Commission was created in January 1965, and the Food Corporation of India was set up in the same month under the Food Corporations Act 1964, as the two halves of one design. The commission would announce prices in advance of sowing generous enough to make the new technology worth the risk, and the corporation would stand in the market to buy at those prices, so that the announcement carried a buyer behind it for the crops the state needed. The price was an inducement instrument inside a food security strategy. Its beneficiary, in the design's own logic, was the national grain stock as much as the individual cultivator.

The dependence behind that crisis deserves a sentence of its own, because farmers and ministers of that generation carried its memory into the design. India in the mid-1960s imported grain on concessional terms, principally from the United States under its Public Law 480 programme, in quantities that made the country's food supply a function of another government's shipping decisions, a condition remembered in the phrase ship to mouth. Successive drought years, war on two fronts within the decade, and a foreign exchange position that could not support commercial imports at that scale made domestic production a matter of state survival rather than of agricultural policy in the ordinary sense. The price instrument of 1965 was therefore forged under strategic pressure, and its designers, the administrators and economists of the food ministry and the Planning Commission of the day, measured its success by a single test: whether the godowns filled. An instrument judged by that test has no need of legal enforceability, because the state's own hunger is the enforcement. The later expectation that the same instrument should also serve as an income right enforceable by each of tens of millions of sellers loads it with a function its designers never built into it, which is not an argument against the new function but is the explanation of why the machinery fits it so badly.

That origin explains the features of the arrangement that otherwise look like defects. The list of crops is confined to the staples and commercial field crops the state had reason to encourage or procure. The buying machinery was built where the surplus was, in the irrigated wheat and rice belts, and not spread evenly across the country. The price is announced before sowing because it exists to steer sowing. And no legal right was created because none was needed for the design to work: a farmer persuaded by an assured buyer does not need to sue the buyer. What changed across the following half-century was not the design but the meaning attached to it. As procurement deepened in a few states and as farm incomes outside the procurement belt stagnated, the announcement came to be heard as a promise of income justice rather than as a production incentive, and the demand for a legal guarantee is precisely the demand to complete that transformation, to convert an instrument of production policy into an instrument of entitlement. Whether that conversion is wise is the contested question this article addresses in its final sections. That it is a conversion, and not the enforcement of something that already exists in law, is not contested at all by anyone who has read the statute book.

### Why did the support price start as an incentive rather than a right?

Because it was designed in the food crisis of the mid-1960s to persuade cultivators to adopt new high-yielding varieties whose returns were uncertain. An advance price, backed by a state buyer, removed the risk of the new technology. The instrument served a production strategy for national food security, and such instruments do not need to create legal rights.

## Who actually buys: the procurement machinery

An announced price with no buyer behind it is a press release. The support price has meaning in a farmer's life exactly to the extent that some agency stands ready to purchase the crop at that price, in the farmer's district, within the harvest window, at grades the farmer's produce can meet. This section describes that machinery, because its shape explains the geography of the entire debate.

The principal buyer is the state itself, acting through procurement operations. For wheat and paddy, the Food Corporation of India and the agencies of state governments purchase grain at the announced price for the central pool, the stock from which the public distribution system and other public programmes are supplied. The Food Corporation of India is a statutory corporation, established under the Food Corporations Act 1964 and functioning from January 1965, and its statute charges it, in the language of its functions provision, with undertaking the purchase, storage, movement, distribution and sale of foodgrains and other foodstuffs, subject to the directions of the Central Government. Two features of that mandate matter legally. The corporation is a creature of statute for its existence and functions, but no provision of its Act obliges it to buy any particular farmer's grain, in any particular quantity, at the announced price. Its purchasing is conducted under the procurement policy and the season's arrangements settled by governments, including the levy, custom milling and agency arrangements through which paddy is converted to rice for the pool. A farmer turned away from a purchase centre has grievances that arise, if at all, from the terms of the operation that excluded the grain, from grading decisions and from administrative fairness, and not from any statutory promise that the corporation would buy. The one institution in this field that Parliament did create is thus an instrument of procurement policy, not the guarantor of a price right.

Procurement runs through the regulated market yards for most crops in most states, since it is in the mandis that arrivals are aggregated, weighed, graded and auctioned within a supervised framework, and the state marketing statutes that constitute those yards, their committees, licensing and fee structures are examined in [the article on the APMC laws and agricultural marketing](/2014/03/15/apmc-act-agricultural-marketing-india/), which owns that framework. The point for this article is narrower: the support price operates inside a market architecture built by state law, and its practical force depends on whether a procurement agency is present and purchasing in that yard in that season. Where the agency is present with funds, staff and storage, the announced price becomes the effective floor of the auction, because no trader needs to be compelled to match a bid the state is already making. Where the agency is absent, the announcement competes in the auction as information only, and the auction clears where supply and private demand put it.

The geography of buying is therefore the geography of the instrument's real existence. Procurement of paddy and wheat is heavily concentrated in Punjab and Haryana, which built dense networks of purchase centres, market yards, commission agency and storage across the decades in which the central pool depended on their surpluses, and extends in strength through Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh for paddy and through Uttar Pradesh for wheat, with the decentralised procurement arrangement adding a second structure in states that chose to run their own operations. Under the decentralised procurement scheme, introduced in 1997-98, a state government itself procures, stores and distributes grain for the public distribution system within the state, retaining the surplus or meeting the deficit against the central pool by arrangement with the Food Corporation of India, and states from Madhya Pradesh to Odisha, West Bengal, Chhattisgarh and others adopted it in varying measure. The design was intended to widen procurement beyond the traditional belt and to cut the transport and handling costs of moving grain into and out of a single national pool. Its spread is real but uneven, and outside the procurement states the typical farmer growing paddy or wheat sells to private trade at prices formed locally, with the announced price acting, at most, as a reference in negotiation.

For pulses, oilseeds and copra, physical purchase has historically been thin, and the machinery is different in kind. The Price Support Scheme, operated through central nodal agencies of which the National Agricultural Cooperative Marketing Federation, NAFED, is the principal, authorises procurement of notified pulses, oilseeds and copra at the announced price when market prices fall below it, with the expenditure and losses borne by the Central Government under the scheme's terms. Operations under the scheme are episodic and quantity-limited rather than open-ended: a state proposes, the Centre sanctions a quantity, agencies buy through the season, and the stock is later disposed of in the market. In September 2018 the Union Cabinet approved an umbrella programme, the Pradhan Mantri Annadata Aay Sanrakshan Abhiyan, known as PM-AASHA, which restructured this side of price support into three components. The first continued the Price Support Scheme with modifications for pulses, oilseeds and copra. The second, the Price Deficiency Payment Scheme, dropped physical purchase altogether for the oilseeds it covered and instead paid registered farmers who sold in notified market yards the difference between the announced price and the price their produce actually fetched, directly into their bank accounts. The third, a pilot of private procurement and stockist participation, allowed states to engage private stockists in selected districts to purchase at the announced price when the market fell below it, on terms under which the state compensated the private buyer within prescribed limits. Each component is an executive scheme, created by Cabinet decision and guidelines, revocable and alterable by the same authority, and none of them converts the announcement into a legal entitlement of the farmer against anyone. They are best understood as three different ways for the state to spend money in support of a price it has announced, each with its own administrative burden and its own failure modes, and the differences among them matter to the guarantee debate, because the deficiency payment design in particular is one of the forms a statutory guarantee might take.

The concentration point must be stated plainly, since the brief for this article and the record both insist on it. For the majority of the twenty-two mandated crops, and for farmers in the majority of states, procurement at the announced price touches only a small part of what is grown and sold. Pulses and oilseeds are bought in sanctioned windows and quantities. Cotton is procured through the Cotton Corporation of India when prices fall below the announced level, in operations that wax and wane with the market. Jute has its own corporation and intermittent operations. The crops for which the announcement is routinely and massively backed by purchase are paddy and wheat, and the states where that purchase is a dependable feature of the harvest are a minority of the Union. Estimates discussed in the policy literature, including material placed before parliamentary committees over the years, have consistently put the share of farmers who actually sell to procurement agencies in the single digits or low double digits as a proportion of all cultivators, and the share of output procured at a similar order for most crops outside the two staples. A reader who keeps that distribution in mind will understand why the guarantee demand is pressed most intensely by the organisations of the procurement belt, whose members know the instrument in its working form and fear its dilution, and why the same demand sounds abstract to a groundnut grower in a district where no agency has bought in living memory. The debate is regional in its intensity because the instrument is regional in its operation, and that is a fact about machinery, not about the sincerity of either side.

## The name in the record: tenancy and the farmer the registers cannot see

One further structural feature of the machinery must be recorded, because it decides who the remedies in this article can reach. Procurement registration, deficiency payment enrolment and scheme participation generally run on documents, and the master document is the record of rights in land. The cultivator who farms leased land on an oral arrangement, the sharecropper whose name appears in no record, the widow cultivating land still recorded for a deceased husband, the son farming a father's holding in a joint family that has never partitioned on paper: these are the cultivators for whom the machinery's gates are hardest to pass, because the operation that would pay the support price, or the scheme that would pay the difference, must first be persuaded that the person presenting the crop is the person entitled to be paid for it. State practice varies: some procurement systems accept tenancy evidence or village certification in place of title, others have created cultivator registration of various kinds, and the deficiency payment designs, which pay against a recorded sale rather than against ownership, can reach a tenant who can register and sell in a notified yard more readily than a purchase centre can. The pattern is nonetheless consistent enough to state as a design law of this field: every instrument in the support price family pays the documented, and the least documented cultivators, who are disproportionately tenants, sharecroppers and women farming family land, stand at the end of every queue the instrument creates. A guarantee statute would inherit this filter unless it deliberately legislated against it, by recognising the cultivator rather than the owner as the right-holder and by building enrolment on cultivation rather than on title. The demand's documents speak of all farmers; the registers the state keeps cannot see all farmers; and the distance between those two phrases is the distance within which a future guarantee would either succeed as a right or fail as another scheme for the already recorded. For the individual tenant farmer, the practical consequence follows the theme of this article: the remedies available are those attached to provable transactions, so a written tenancy arrangement, however informal, and sale records in the cultivator's own name are worth more than any argument about the announcement.

## The thin end of the list: pulses, oilseeds, cotton and jute

The contrast between the two staples and the rest of the list is sharp enough to deserve its own section, because it is here that the announcement's limits are most exposed. Pulses and oilseeds are the crops where India runs structural deficits, importing a substantial share of its edible oils and a variable but significant quantity of pulses, and they are the crops where price support, if it worked as announced, would do the most for import substitution. Their procurement history is a history of episodes. When pulse prices spiked sharply in the middle of the last decade and the retail price of tur dal became a national political grievance, the Union government moved to build a buffer stock of pulses through procurement at the support price, an admission in action that the announcement alone had not called forth either production or arrivals on the scale required. Operations for pulses under the Price Support Scheme expand in years of low prices and contract in years of high prices, which is the reverse of a standing institution: the farmer contemplating a pulse crop cannot know, at sowing, whether a sanctioned operation with funds and a quantity ceiling will exist at harvest, nor whether the ceiling will be exhausted by earlier arrivals from other districts. Oilseeds present the same pattern with an additional complication, since the deficiency payment component of PM-AASHA was built principally for them, so that in states that chose that component the support for an oilseed farmer takes the form of a payment claim with registration and verification attached, rather than a buyer at the yard.

Cotton and jute, the two commercial crops carried in the mandate in the standard enumeration alongside copra, show a third pattern, the specialised corporation. The Cotton Corporation of India, a public sector undertaking, undertakes support price operations for raw cotton when market prices fall below the announced level, buying through market yards in the cotton states and disposing of the stock to mills and exporters thereafter; in years of heavy operation it has been among the largest single buyers in the Indian cotton market, and its interventions demonstrate that a determined agency can hold a floor under a commercial crop for a season without any statutory duty compelling it. The jute economy, concentrated in West Bengal, Assam and Bihar and tied to the statutory packaging requirements that oblige specified commodities to be packed in jute, has its own corporation and its own intermittent operations, in a market where the demand side is itself substantially a creature of law. Copra, the dried coconut kernel of the southern states, is procured episodically under the Price Support Scheme when prices sag. The lesson common to all three patterns is that where the state wishes a floor to exist for a season, it can manufacture one with money, agencies and instructions, entirely within executive power; and that the floor so manufactured lasts exactly as long as the money and the instructions do. Nothing in these episodes required a statute, and nothing in them created rights. They are the strongest available evidence for this article's central distinction, because they show the ceiling of what announcement plus machinery can do, and it is considerably less than the word guarantee promises.

## Inside a procurement operation: what the farmer meets at the purchase centre

The support price becomes real or unreal at the purchase centre, and the texture of that encounter explains why farmers in the procurement belt defend the system with a passion that puzzles observers who know only its legal character. A paddy or wheat farmer in a procuring district registers for the operation, in the states that run registration systems, before or during the season, recording land and crop details against which purchases are later checked. The harvested grain is brought to a purchase centre or a market yard on an allotted day, where it is tested against the fair average quality specifications prescribed for the season: limits on moisture, on foreign matter, on damaged, discoloured and immature grains, and on admixture of inferior varieties. Grain within specification is accepted, weighed, bagged and receipted at the announced price. Grain outside specification may be rejected outright, or accepted in some operations with a value cut proportionate to the deficiency, and the grading decision at the centre is the single most consequential discretionary act in the farmer's season, because it converts the announced price from a fact into a negotiation. Appeals against grading lie within the operation, to supervisory officers of the agency and the district administration, and the farmer's practical protection is the presence of competing purchase points, the publicity of the specifications, and the records the operation generates.

Around this core cluster the arrangements that decide who in fact captures the price. In Punjab and Haryana, procurement historically ran through the commission agent, the arhtiya, whose shop in the yard served as the farmer's point of sale, whose credit had often financed the crop, and whose commission on procurement operations was built into the incidentals the state paid; reforms moving payment directly into farmers' bank accounts have repeatedly collided with this structure, because the arhtiya is at once a service provider, a creditor and a political constituency. Paddy adds a further layer, since what the pool requires is rice: the state procures paddy and has it milled by rice mills under custom milling arrangements, against an out-turn ratio fixed for the season, so the farmer's counterparty experience is of a paddy purchase while the system's accounts are kept in rice. Payment timelines have been prescribed in operation after operation, and their breach is the commonest grievance of the procurement belt, pursued through the administrative channels described in the remedies section of this article. Storage closes the circle: grain accepted faster than it can be moved accumulates in yards and covered plinths, and the visible mountains of bagged wheat and paddy in the procuring states are the physical form of an open-ended purchase obligation for two crops. A reader who has seen that landscape understands both halves of the guarantee debate at once: the relief of the farmer whose grain was lifted at the announced price, and the fiscal and logistical anxiety of the official who must find covered space, working capital and eventual issue for every quintal the obligation draws in.

## What the announcement does even where nobody buys

It would be an error, having established that the announcement binds nobody, to conclude that it does nothing where no agency purchases. The announced price works on the market through expectation, and its effects are visible in at least three channels that do not depend on procurement. It anchors negotiation: a farmer and a trader bargaining at the farm gate or in an unregulated transaction both know the number, and the trader's offer is disciplined by the seller's knowledge of what the state says the crop is worth, imperfectly and unevenly, but in a direction no serious observer denies; market prices for the mandated crops cluster in relation to the announcement rather than in ignorance of it. It steers planting beyond the procurement belt as well, because the announcement is the state's most public statement of which crops it wishes to encourage, and area decisions respond to relative announcements even among farmers who will sell to private trade. And it supplies the reference against which other instruments are written: deficiency payment schemes measure their payments from it, state bonuses are declared as additions to it, crop diversification programmes argue against its pull toward paddy and wheat, and election manifestos across parties commit to its enhancement. The announcement is thus best characterised not as a nullity outside the procurement states but as a signal with real economic effects and no legal ones, a combination that is precisely the source of the political difficulty. A signal can be strengthened or weakened by degrees and judged by results; a right is binary, held or breached. The state's preference for the signal and the farmers' demand for the right are preferences for different kinds of instrument, and the debate between them is not resolvable by evidence about prices alone, which is why it has survived every change of government and every committee appointed to compose it.

## From the purchase centre to the ration shop

Procurement exists because the state needs grain, and the destination of the procured grain completes the legal picture. The central pool feeds the public distribution system, whose entitlements were placed on a statutory footing by the National Food Security Act 2013, and the reader who wants the entitlement side of that settlement should follow [the guide to the National Food Security Act 2013](/2014/05/15/national-food-security-act-2013-guide/) and the record of [the National Food Security Act and the public distribution system in operation](/2014/06/01/nfsa-pds-implementation-record/), which own the distribution framework. The connection matters to this article for one structural reason: the food subsidy, the difference between what the state spends acquiring, moving, storing and issuing grain and what it recovers at issue prices, is the fiscal shadow of the support price. Every increase in the announced price raises the economic cost of the grain the state distributes, and the same population that gains as producers from a higher price includes the households that depend on the distribution system as consumers. When the guarantee debate is costed, this is the ledger in which the cost appears, and the trade-off is internal to rural India rather than a simple transfer from an urban exchequer to a rural countryside. A guarantee extended to all crops and all produce would not merely add a new expenditure; it would interact with the largest food programme in the world, whose stocks, storage and distribution costs are already driven substantially by the two-crop procurement the announcement supports.

## The levies on the operation: what the states charge the buyer of last resort

Procurement is also taxed by the governments that host it, a fact that surprises readers who imagine the operation as a single state's act of purchase. A procurement agency buying grain in a regulated yard pays, in addition to the support price, the incidentals of the operation: the market fee levied under the state marketing statute, charges for handling, weighing, stitching and loading, the commission payable where sale runs through commission agents, and, in the largest procuring state, a rural development charge levied on the transaction's value. These incidentals are a significant component of the economic cost of grain to the Food Corporation of India and the Centre, and they convert the procurement operation into a revenue stream for the producing state's own budget and market institutions, a stream whose size grows with the support price itself. The political economy that results is visible in the positions states take in the wider debate: a high-volume procuring state has fiscal reasons, quite apart from its farmers' incomes, to defend and enlarge the operation within its borders, and the Shanta Kumar committee's treatment of bonuses belongs to the same picture of state-level additions loading cost onto a national pool. For the guarantee debate the levy structure carries a specific lesson. Any statute that obliged purchase at the guaranteed price would have to decide the treatment of these incidentals too, whether they ride on top of the guaranteed price as a public cost, are capped, or are abolished for guarantee transactions, because a guarantee of the price that leaves the deductions and the levies untouched guarantees a number that the seller, net of the operation's charges, may still not receive. The table's enforcement row assumes a price; the operation's accounts would supply the price's footnotes.

## Stocks, carrying costs and the critique from within the system

The stocks the system accumulates have repeatedly exceeded the buffer norms the government prescribes for the central pool, and the excess is not an abstraction: grain held beyond need deteriorates, occupies storage that must be hired, and carries interest and handling costs that inflate the economic cost of every quintal eventually issued. The critique of this structure from within the state's own advisory machinery is a standing feature of the field, and its most systematic statement came from the High Level Committee on the restructuring of the Food Corporation of India, chaired by the former food minister Shanta Kumar, which reported in January 2015. The committee recommended, among its central proposals, that the corporation hand over procurement operations in the surplus states with mature systems to the state agencies themselves and concentrate its own operations on the eastern states where procurement was weak; that the coverage of the food security legislation be revisited so that subsidy was better targeted; and that cash transfers substitute for physical grain distribution in large cities where banking and market infrastructure made them workable. It also addressed the price side directly in one respect that matters here, treating state bonuses over the announced price as a distortion that the Centre should discourage because they draw grain across state lines and load the pool with stocks acquired at above-announcement cost. The committee's significance for this article is not any single recommendation but the standing fact it represents: the system's own reviewers describe a procurement structure that is too concentrated, too costly and too rigid, at the same time as the political debate demands that the same structure's central promise be universalised by statute. A guarantee debate that does not absorb the Shanta Kumar critique is arguing about a price while ignoring the machine that would have to honour it.

The Finance Commission and the Comptroller and Auditor General have, in their respective examinations of food management over the years, added the fiscal dimension of the same critique, questioning the growth of carrying costs and the gap between the economic cost of grain and the prices at which it is issued. None of these institutional critics proposes abolishing the support price; the announcement's role in stabilising production and in anchoring market expectations is accepted across the official literature. What they contest is open-endedness, the conversion of a price signal into an unlimited purchase commitment without a corresponding decision about stocks, subsidy and cropping consequences. That distinction, between announcing a price and underwriting a market, is the distinction the guarantee debate repeatedly blurs, and it is preserved in the design table below as the difference between the first and second rows.

## What a farmer can actually enforce

This is the section the framework of this article exists for, and it must be written without consolation. A farmer who sells below the announced price and asks what the law gives back must be told, in order, what does not exist, and then what does.

What does not exist is any claim founded on the announcement. There is no provision under which the difference between the price received and the support price can be recovered from the buyer, from the market committee or from the state, on the ground that the announcement promised it. There is no offence of buying below the support price in any central law, and a trader who bids low commits no illegality by that fact alone. There is no authority before whom a complaint of underpayment relative to the announcement can be filed with a prospect of an order for the difference. Anyone who advises a farmer otherwise is inventing a remedy, and the prevalence of the belief that such a remedy exists is itself one of the costs of conducting a major economic instrument by announcement: the language of a minimum, borrowed from the vocabulary of enforceable wage law, promises a floor that the machinery does not supply.

What exists are the ordinary and the neighbouring remedies, and they are worth mapping carefully because they are real within their limits. The sale of produce is a contract, and the ordinary law of contract and of sale of goods governs it: a farmer cheated on weight, on grading deductions not agreed, or on payment promised and withheld has the remedies of a seller, including suit for the price and complaint mechanisms that several state marketing statutes provide for disputes arising in the market yard, where the market committee's dispute processes are usually the practical first forum. Where the transaction is infected by fraud, the criminal law's cheating provisions are available in principle, though their fit with hard bargaining is poor and their use in price disputes is rare. Within a procurement operation, the farmer's position is stronger: the terms of the operation, the specifications for fair average quality grain, the purchase centre's duty to issue receipts and the payment timelines the operation prescribes create administrative obligations whose breach can be taken up through the department, the district administration and, in cases of arbitrary exclusion, the writ jurisdiction of the High Court, which supervises the fairness of state action even where no substantive entitlement exists. A farmer whose grain meeting specification is refused while inferior grain from another source is accepted at the same centre has a public law complaint about unequal treatment. A farmer whose complaint is only that the state did not buy at all, or bought too little, has none that a court will convert into a purchase order.

Practical discipline follows from the legal position, and it is the part of this article a farmer reader can use. The evidence of a transaction decides most disputes that can be decided, so the receipts issued at the yard, the weighbridge slips, the auction records and the bank trail of the payment received are the documents on which any later claim, contractual, administrative or under a deficiency payment scheme, will stand or fall. Registration is the gateway to most scheme benefits: the price deficiency payment designs, state and central, pay only registered farmers who sell in notified yards within notified windows, and the Madhya Pradesh experience examined below shows both the value of registration and what happens to the unregistered. Keeping sale records in a durable, retrievable form is therefore not clerical fussiness but the substance of the farmer's legal position; where records exist in spreadsheets and scanned documents, they can be opened and checked without specialised software through [a browser-based viewer for Excel, Word and PowerPoint files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which is the sort of unglamorous step that decides whether a genuine grievance can be proved. The general position described in this article is information about the framework, not advice on any individual matter, and a farmer with a live dispute should take advice on the facts of that dispute, because limitation periods and the rules of the forum are unforgiving and a meritorious claim can be lost to delay alone.

One neighbouring price shows what enforceability looks like when the state means it, and it is the comparison every discussion of this subject needs. The fair and remunerative price for sugarcane is recommended by the same Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices and approved by the same Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, and up to that point its biography matches the support price exactly. It then diverges. The price is fixed in the Sugarcane (Control) Order 1966, an instrument issued by the Central Government under the Essential Commodities Act 1955, which also renamed the earlier statutory minimum price for cane as the fair and remunerative price with effect from the 2009-10 season. Because the order is delegated legislation under a statute, its terms bind: a sugar mill is legally obliged to pay the fixed price for cane supplied to it, the order prescribes the period within which payment must be made and interest where payment is delayed, and arrears are recoverable through the machinery the order and state law provide. Several states announce a still higher state advised price for cane, and the obligation questions around those announcements have generated their own litigation and their own fiscal strain on mills, which is itself instructive. The lesson is not that cane farmers are pampered; cane pricing has produced chronic arrears, mill sickness and repeated judicial intervention. The lesson is that India already possesses the legal technology to make a floor price bind, has used it in one crop for decades, and has chosen not to use it for the twenty-two support price crops. The absence of a statute behind the support price is not an oversight awaiting correction by discovery. It is a settled, repeated institutional choice, and the guarantee debate is the argument about reversing it.

## Reading an announcement like a user: the distance between the price and the payment

For the farmer reader, the practical section of this article is the one that measures the distance between the announced number and the money that reaches the bank account, because that distance, and not the announcement, is what a season's income is made of. The first component of the distance is grade. The announced price is the price for grain of fair average quality meeting the season's specifications; produce that fails on moisture or admixture is rejected or discounted, and the specifications are set by the purchasing side. The second component is deduction: charges that appear between the auction price and the payment, for handling, for bagging, for market fee and commission where the sale runs through the yard, some lawful under the marketing statute and the operation's terms and some surviving by custom, so that a farmer comparing the payment with the announcement must net the two against each other before concluding anything about compliance. The third component is time: payment timelines prescribed on paper and payment arriving weeks after delivery transfer the cost of the state's working capital to the seller, at the interest rate of the seller's own borrowings, which for a farmer who has taken crop credit or the arhtiya's advance is not a bookkeeping matter. The fourth component is access: the purchase centre's distance, the day allotted for arrivals, the quantity the centre can absorb, and the point in the season at which sanctioned quantities are exhausted, all decide whether the announced price was available to a particular farmer on the day the crop had to move. None of these components involves the law of the support price, because there is none; all of them involve the terms of schemes, the marketing statute, the law of contract and the administration of the operation, which is where this article has consistently located the farmer's real legal world.

The same user's reading applies at one remove to the policy reader. The figures that circulate after each announcement, the percentage increase over the previous season, the margin over cost, describe the number in isolation. The measures that describe the instrument are different: how many farmers sold to an agency, in how many districts an operation ran, what share of the marketed surplus of each crop was purchased, and at what total cost to the exchequer including the carrying cost of the stocks created. Those measures are published irregularly, in answers to Parliament, in the reports of the agencies and in official reviews, and a reader equipped with them can perform the test this article proposes for every claim in the field: ask not what the price is, but where it was paid, to how many, and out of what machinery. Claims that survive that test are the minority, and they are the ones worth building policy on.

## The writ route and its limits

Because the remedies section above mentions the High Court's writ jurisdiction, its scope and its boundaries deserve a fuller statement, since the writ is the remedy most often imagined by readers who discover that no statute supports them. Under Article 226 of the Constitution, a High Court may issue writs for the enforcement of fundamental rights and for any other purpose, and procurement operations, being state action, are amenable to it. What the writ court supervises is the legality and fairness of the administration: a purchase centre that departs from the operation's own specifications to one farmer's detriment, a grading decision taken without applying the prescribed standard, a payment withheld contrary to the scheme's terms, an exclusion from registration that the scheme does not authorise. These are real protections, and procurement litigation in the producing states includes a steady run of such cases. What the writ court will not do is manufacture the underlying entitlement. It will not direct the state to open procurement in a district where no operation was sanctioned, will not fix a quantity the state must buy, and will not convert the announcement into a decree for the difference between the price obtained and the price announced. The doctrine of legitimate expectation, which in Indian public law protects a person against the unfair withdrawal of a settled practice or representation in some circumstances, has not been carried to the point of obliging the state to purchase at announced prices, because the announcement is addressed to the market at large as policy and the courts treat price policy as the executive's province absent a statutory standard against which to measure it. The writ route is therefore best understood as policing the boundaries of operations that exist. The guarantee debate is, at bottom, an argument about whether an entitlement should be created that would give the writ something larger to enforce, and the farmers' organisations' insistence on a statute rather than on assurances reflects a correct understanding of exactly this boundary.

## The state experiments: bonuses, deficiency payments and attempted prohibitions

Between the announcement that binds nobody and the guarantee that does not exist, the states have run a series of experiments that fill the middle ground, and each experiment teaches something about the design problem.

The simplest instrument is the bonus. State governments have at various times announced a payment above the support price for a crop procured in the state, paddy in Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh being the recurring examples, funded from the state budget and paid to farmers who sold through the state's procurement operation. A bonus is an executive act of the same legal species as the announcement itself: it creates no enduring entitlement, it lasts for the season and the crop for which it is declared, and its legal incidents are those of the scheme that pays it. Its effects, however, are instructive for the guarantee debate. A bonus in one state draws produce across borders from neighbouring states whose farmers seek the higher effective price, distorting the procurement geography and the accounts of both states, and the Union government has at intervals pushed back against bonuses precisely because they disturb the central pool's economics and the parity among states. The episode of the bonus demonstrates that even a modest, funded, above-announcement payment by an intermediate government can destabilise the wider system when it is not coordinated, which is a small-scale preview of the coordination problem a national guarantee would have to solve between the Centre and the states.

The most instructive experiment is the deficiency payment in working form. Madhya Pradesh launched the Bhavantar Bhugtan Yojana in October 2017, initially for eight kharif crops chosen because procurement for them was thin, principally oilseeds and pulses together with maize. A farmer registered for the scheme, sold the crop in a notified market yard within the notified window, and the state paid into the farmer's bank account a sum computed from the gap between the support price and a modal price, the modal price being drawn from the average of prevailing mandi prices within the state and in other states where the crop traded. Where the farmer's actual sale price sat between the modal price and the support price, the payment made up the difference to the support price; where the sale price fell below the modal price, the payment was capped at the gap between the modal price and the support price, so the scheme insures against the market's general level rather than against a bad bargain of the individual seller. The design's purpose was transparent: to give the farmer the support price's benefit without the state taking delivery of a single quintal, avoiding purchase, storage and disposal altogether. Its observed difficulties were equally instructive and were documented in the scheme's own reviews and in independent study, including analysis by the Indian Council for Research on International Economic Relations: traders, knowing the state would top up the farmer's return, had a reduced incentive to bid up to the support price, and in some markets prices appeared to be quoted lower in the knowledge that the deficiency would be paid by the exchequer, so that part of the transfer leaked to the buying side; registration, windows and verification generated exclusions and delays; and the modal price mechanism imported into the payment the very market weakness the scheme existed to correct. The scheme was subsequently carried into the national Price Deficiency Payment Scheme component of PM-AASHA in modified form, with the state experiment as its acknowledged model. For the design table below, Bhavantar is the single most valuable exhibit in Indian experience: it is the closest the country has come to operating a guarantee without procurement, and its record shows that the difficulties of a guarantee are not exhausted by the fiscal question, because a payment design changes the behaviour of the market it measures.

The third experiment is the attempted prohibition, and it belongs to the federal argument. During the agitation against the farm laws, the Punjab Legislative Assembly in October 2020 passed amendment bills addressed to the central farm statutes which proposed, among other things, that purchase of produce below the support price within the state be made punishable. Bills of that character, amending the operation of central legislation within a state, require the assent of the President under the repugnancy procedure of Article 254 to prevail within the state, and they never became operative law. The episode is recorded here not for its effect, which was nil, but for what it reveals about the geography of legal power in this field: a state that wishes to make the support price binding by prohibition must either legislate within its own marketing competence, where its writ runs to its own yards and licensees, or seek the Centre's assent to override a parliamentary scheme, and the politics of the second route are the politics of asking the author of a policy to authorise its contradiction. No state marketing statute in general operation converts the support price into a floor below which purchase is an offence, and the brief for this article directs, correctly, that the position be described in that durable form rather than by listing a shifting set of state measures. The durable form is this: remedies below the announcement must be found in contract, in the regulatory law of the market yard, in scheme entitlements where a scheme applies, or in a specific statutory instrument of the sugarcane kind. A general offence of underpayment relative to the support price does not exist in Indian law.

### What can a farmer do after being paid below the support price?

The farmer cannot recover the difference by pointing to the announcement, because it creates no claim. What can be pursued is the transaction itself: unpaid price under the sale contract, cheating on weight or grade through the market committee's dispute process, and refusal or discrimination inside a procurement operation through administrative complaint and, in a clear case, writ jurisdiction.

## The comparative mirror: floor prices elsewhere

India's choice to carry its principal farm floor price by announcement rather than by law looks stranger in comparative light, because the other large agricultural systems that have operated floor prices have generally carried them in legislation and have spent decades managing the consequences the design table predicts. The United States built its support system in farm legislation renewed by Congress in successive farm bills, under which, for long periods, the federal government stood ready to lend against stored crops at a loan rate that functioned as a floor, taking the crop itself in settlement where the market stayed below it; the accumulation of public stocks and the fiscal exposure that followed drove reforms toward deficiency payments, under which the Treasury paid producers the gap between a legislated target price and the market price on recorded production, and later toward insurance-based instruments. The European Community's common agricultural policy operated intervention buying at legislated prices for cereals and dairy for a generation, produced the celebrated surpluses of the nineteen-eighties, and was reformed step by step toward decoupled payments precisely because an enforceable floor above market levels obliged the public authority to become the buyer, the storekeeper and the exporter of last resort. The comparative record does not decide the Indian question, since India's small holdings, thin markets and food security dependence differ from both cases, but it establishes one proposition beyond reasonable dispute: where a floor price is made law, the state does in practice end up owning the consequences listed in the table, in stocks, in payments or in both, and legislatures that have enacted floors have spent much of their subsequent history negotiating the exits. A legislature enacting a guarantee in India would not be writing on a blank slate; it would be re-entering a room whose furniture other countries have been moving out of for forty years, and it should enter with its eyes open about why they left.

## The demand that survived the repeal

The political history of the guarantee demand can be told briefly here, because its legislative history belongs to a neighbouring article. The three farm laws of 2020, which restructured agricultural marketing without writing any protection of the support price into their text, provoked the year-long agitation at Delhi's borders whose settlement is recorded in [the account of the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021](/2014/04/15/farm-laws-repeal-act-2021-explained/), where the surviving demand sentence of that episode belongs. The demand that survived the repeal, after the statutes were withdrawn and the encampments lifted, was the demand the agitation had carried alongside repeal from its early months: a statutory guarantee that the support price would be a legal entitlement. The repeal disposed of the laws the farmers opposed and left the price exactly where it had always been, in executive hands, which is why the demand outlasted the victory. The wider protest and what it demonstrated about the legislative process, the costs of enacting contested economic law without deliberative stages and of conceding under street pressure without settling the underlying question, are treated in [the analysis of the farmers' protest and its legislative lessons](/2014/05/01/farmers-protest-legislative-lessons/), which owns the political dimension of the episode.

The government's institutional answer to the surviving demand was a committee, promised in the Prime Minister's address of 19 November 2021 that announced the repeal, repeated in the Agriculture Secretary's letter of 9 December 2021 on which the agitation was suspended, and notified by the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare on 12 July 2022 under S.O. 3211(E). It was chaired by Sanjay Agrawal, the former Agriculture Secretary who had signed the December letter. Its remit, as notified, was to suggest ways of making the support price more effective and transparent, to promote natural farming, to suggest changes in cropping patterns in the light of the country's changing needs, and to address the autonomy of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices. Three seats were reserved for nominees of the Samyukt Kisan Morcha, the coalition that had conducted the agitation, to be added when the names were received. The names never came. The Morcha rejected the committee within days of its notification and declined to nominate representatives, on grounds it stated publicly: the terms of reference asked how the support price might be made effective and transparent but did not ask the committee to consider a legal guarantee, which was the demand itself; and the committee's composition, beginning with its chairman, was drawn from the official and advisory world whose arrangements the demand challenged. The committee met without the Morcha, organised its work through sub-groups, and governments answering Parliament in later sessions described its meetings as continuing and its subjects as under deliberation. No bill embodying a guarantee followed, and the support price continued to be announced season by season on the same administrative footing as before 2020. The demand returned to the street in February 2024, when farmer organisations marched on Delhi again behind the legal guarantee as their first demand and were halted at the Shambhu border between Punjab and Haryana, in a smaller repetition of the earlier encampment. That recurrence is the political measure of the committee route: a question referred to a body whose terms exclude the question has not been answered, and unanswered questions in this field retain their capacity to mobilise.

The demand's precise content is worth stating, because it is more specific than the slogan under which it marches. The Samyukt Kisan Morcha's charter framed the guarantee as a law ensuring the support price, computed on the comprehensive cost measure, for all crops and all farmers, so that the demand bundles three separate enlargements into one: the conversion of the announcement into an entitlement, the extension of effective coverage beyond the two staples and the procurement belt, and the substitution of C2 for A2 plus FL as the statutory cost base. Each enlargement multiplies the fiscal and administrative consequences of the others, and a legislature could in principle enact any one without the rest; the political function of the bundled demand is that it cannot be half-met without the shortfall being visible. The demand also entered party competition in explicit statutory form: the Indian National Congress carried a promise of a legal guarantee of the support price into the general election of 2024 as a manifesto commitment, which marks the point at which the demand ceased to be only a movement's charter and became a legislative programme competing for a mandate, still without a published draft bill against which the design questions in this article's table could be tested. The absence of the draft, from movement and parties alike, remains the most telling fact in the whole debate: the demand has been formulated with precision about the price and the cost base, and with silence about obligation, enforcement and the glut year, the three matters on which any enacted version would stand or fall.

Why does the demand insist on a statute, rather than on larger procurement, higher announcements or better schemes, all of which the executive could deliver without legislation? The insistence is itself legally literate, and it deserves to be understood rather than dismissed as maximalism. Everything the executive can deliver without legislation it can also withdraw without legislation, as the history of bonuses given and withdrawn, schemes sanctioned for a season and allowed to lapse, and procurement quantities fixed year by year demonstrates. The farmers' organisations learned from the farm laws episode itself that executive assurances, including written assurances given in negotiation, bind politically at most. A statute is demanded because a statute is the one instrument that binds the state as well as the citizen, that survives a change of ministry, that can be pleaded in a court, and that can be amended only by the public process of a legislature. The demand for a legal guarantee is therefore not a demand for a bigger number. It is a demand to move the number from the category of policy to the category of law, and the whole of the difficulty lies in what that move requires the number to become.

## What a legal guarantee would have to decide

A guarantee statute cannot be drafted from a slogan, because the slogan "legal guarantee of MSP" is compatible with at least three fundamentally different statutes, and its advocates do not always distinguish among them. The first is a duty to buy: the state, through its agencies, must purchase whatever is offered at the announced price, which converts procurement from a policy operation into an open-ended obligation. The second is a prohibition: no person may purchase covered produce below the announced price, enforced by penalty, which converts the price into a floor on private trade without obliging the state to buy anything. The third is a duty to pay the difference: the state must compensate the seller for the gap between the announced price and the price actually realised in a lawful sale, which is the deficiency payment generalised into an entitlement. These are different laws with different costs, different enforcement machinery and different effects on the market, and a legislature choosing a guarantee must choose among them before it reaches any question of detail. The table below sets out the design questions any of the three statutes must answer, with the strongest objection that attaches to the choice at each point. It is the findable artifact of this article, and it is offered as the drafting agenda without which discussion of a guarantee remains at the level of competing assertions.

| Design question a guarantee statute must answer | The choices open to the draftsman | The strongest objection to the choice |
|---|---|---|
| Whom does the obligation bind? | The state as buyer of last resort; every private buyer as a condition of lawful trade; or the state as payer of the difference after a private sale | A duty on the state alone is open-ended; a duty on private buyers alone is enforceable mainly against the organised trade that is easiest to regulate and easiest to drive away |
| Is it an obligation to buy, or a prohibition on buying below the price? | An obligation to buy guarantees a transaction; a prohibition guarantees only a term of transactions that still occur | A prohibition cannot compel a buyer to appear, and a floor above the market clearing price can shrink the volume purchased rather than raise the price received |
| Which crops, and what quantity? | The twenty-two mandated crops; a narrower list of staples; or all agricultural produce, and either unlimited quantity or a per-farmer or per-acre ceiling | Extending the guarantee to all produce multiplies the fiscal exposure beyond any credible estimate, while ceilings reintroduce registration, verification and exclusion disputes |
| Who bears the cost? | The Union budget; state budgets; a shared formula; or the buyer, through the price itself, with the consumer behind the buyer | Each bearer shifts the burden to a different constituency, and the food subsidy ledger means the Union's cost interacts with the distribution system the same rural households depend on |
| What is the enforcement mechanism? | Criminal penalty for underpayment; a civil claim for the difference before a named authority; an administrative payment on proof of sale; or market-yard supervision with licence consequences | Criminal enforcement against thousands of dispersed transactions invites selective use and evasion, while administrative payment depends on the registration and price data whose weakness the state experiments exposed |
| What happens when the guaranteed price exceeds the market price for an entire harvest? | The state absorbs the whole marketable surplus; trade migrates outside regulated channels; or the guarantee is suspended or capped by a fiscal escape clause | A guarantee that can be suspended in exactly the glut conditions it exists for loses its credibility, and one that cannot be suspended obliges the state to become the market |

Two further dimensions of the design problem deserve statement before the table's lessons are drawn, because they are the dimensions on which costing exercises in this debate most often mislead. The first is the difference between the price of the guarantee and the cost of the guarantee. A guarantee's price is visible and small to state: the announced figure for a crop in a season. Its cost depends on behaviour that the guarantee itself changes: how much produce is diverted to the assured channel, how far private buyers withdraw or shade their bids in the knowledge that the state stands behind the price, how cropping patterns shift toward guaranteed crops and away from unguaranteed ones, and how much of the recorded production against which a deficiency is paid represents genuine marketings by the farmer in whose name it is recorded. Costing studies in the published debate, including work by economists at the Indian Council for Research on International Economic Relations comparing physical procurement with deficiency payment designs, have generally found the payment designs cheaper than open-ended purchase for the same coverage, because the state pays a difference rather than a price and never takes delivery; but every such estimate is hostage to its assumptions about diversion and bid behaviour, and the Madhya Pradesh record, in which part of the transfer appeared to leak to the buying side through lower bids, is the standing warning that realised cost and realised benefit can part company. The second dimension is time. A guarantee is demanded most intensely in glut years, when prices collapse, and it is in glut years that its cost peaks, its storage fills and its enforcement is hardest; in scarcity years the guarantee costs little because the market clears above it. A fiscal instrument whose liabilities are at their maximum exactly when farm distress dominates politics and revenue is under strain is a counter-cyclical commitment of a particularly demanding kind, and a statute that ignores this, for instance by leaving the finance ministry to find the money season by season, has postponed its hardest question rather than answered it. The escape-clause row of the table exists for this reason: every guarantee design yet operated anywhere has needed either a cap, a quantity limit, a suspension power or a funding rule for the glut year, and the credibility of the guarantee is precisely the credibility of whatever the statute says will happen then.

Three observations discipline the use of this table. The first is that every row is a genuine choice on which reasonable legislatures could differ, so the absence of a draft guarantee bill is not by itself evidence of bad faith on anyone's part; the design problem is hard, and it is harder in India than in a smaller or richer agricultural economy because of the number of crops, the number of sellers, the thinness of the state's presence in most markets and the fiscal weight of the existing food programme. The second is that the three candidate statutes fail in different ways, and the failure modes are known in advance from domestic experience: the duty to buy fails through storage, deterioration and fiscal exhaustion, as the history of open-ended procurement of two crops already strains the system; the prohibition fails through evasion and the migration of trade beyond the regulated yards; the deficiency payment fails through the depression of the market price it measures, as the Bhavantar record shows, unless its price reference is constructed independently of the transactions it compensates. A serious guarantee bill would be recognisable by the defences it builds against its own failure mode, and the absence of such defences in public drafts and manifestos is the tell that the slogan is still doing the work. The third observation concerns the beneficiaries. A price guarantee, in any of the three forms, pays in proportion to what a cultivator markets. The poorest rural households, landless labourers, tenants on oral arrangements, marginal holders who consume most of what they grow and buy food in the market for part of the year, market little or nothing and are net buyers of food. A guarantee raises their incomes not at all and may raise the prices they pay. That distributional fact does not refute the guarantee; the incomes of surplus-producing farmers are a legitimate object of policy and their insecurity is real. It does mean the guarantee cannot be defended as a measure for the rural poor as such, and that its fiscal cost would compete, within a finite budget, with the distribution system and employment programmes on which the rural poor principally rely. Neither side of the public debate habitually makes this admission, the advocates because it complicates the moral claim, the opponents because it complicates theirs; the record requires it.

## Reform short of a guarantee: the middle programme

Between the announcement as it stands and the guarantee as demanded lies a programme of reforms that neither camp emphasises, because it gives neither the slogan it wants, and it deserves record here because parts of it are repeatedly recommended by the field's own institutions. The first element is the status of the commission itself. Proposals to place the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices on a statutory footing, with defined tenure, independent staffing and a duty laid on the government to explain departures from its recommendations to Parliament, have circulated for decades and surfaced again in the terms of reference of the July 2022 committee, which was asked to address the commission's autonomy. A statutory commission would not make the price binding, and its supporters should not pretend otherwise; what it would do is relocate the argument about each season's figure from the press release to a documented, answerable process, and make the choice of cost measure a matter on which the government must state reasons. The second element is coverage honesty: a published account, season by season, of how much of each crop was actually purchased, in which districts, for how many sellers, laid before Parliament with the announcement's own documents, so that the distance between the announced list and the operating list becomes a statistic of record rather than a finding of researchers. The third element is the steady extension of the machinery that works, decentralised procurement in states willing to run it, pulses and oilseeds operations sanctioned before sowing rather than after prices collapse, and deficiency payment designs rebuilt on the Madhya Pradesh lessons with reference prices insulated from the transactions being compensated. None of this satisfies the demand for a right, and the movement that wants a right is entitled to say so. But a debate that cannot distinguish this programme from the status quo, or the guarantee from this programme, is not yet a legislative debate; it is a contest of banners, and the farmers in whose name it is conducted are owed the distinctions.

## The case for the guarantee, and the case against, at full strength

For the guarantee, the strongest statement begins from the character of the announcement itself. A price proclaimed by the state, around which farmers are invited to plan sowing, borrowing and investment, and which the state then honours only where its machinery happens to reach, is an assurance structured to be withdrawn at will and under-delivered without consequence, and the economist and former commission chairman Ashok Gulati has argued in his published writing on the subject that the system's credibility requires the state either to back its announcements with real purchasing and payment arrangements or to stop pretending that the announcement is a floor. The farmer organisations' case, pressed by the Samyukt Kisan Morcha through its charter and its 2024 mobilisation, adds the planting-certainty argument: agricultural decisions are irreversible once the seed is in the ground, input credit is taken against the expected price, and a cultivator who cannot know whether the announced price will be available at harvest is being asked to bear a risk that the state's own announcement created. The Morcha's representatives also point to the existing fact that for paddy and wheat in the procurement states the state already does, in practice, guarantee the price by purchase, so that the demand generalises an arrangement the exchequer already sustains for two crops and a few regions, and the inequality of the present distribution, in which a Punjab wheat grower enjoys a working guarantee and a Maharashtra pulse grower enjoys a poster, is itself an argument of fairness for extension. Underneath these arguments sits the dignity claim that runs through the Swaminathan report's comparison of farm incomes with salaried incomes: that a society which fixes enforceable minimum wages for labour should not regard an enforceable floor for the produce of the labourer's own farm as an exotic demand.

Against the guarantee, the strongest statement is fiscal and structural, and it too must be given without caricature. An obligation to buy everything offered at the announced price, across twenty-two crops, would require procurement, transport, storage and disposal on a scale for which no credible costing exists, and the attempts at costing made in the policy debate diverge so widely, depending on assumptions about how much produce would be diverted to the state, that the honest statement is that the exposure is unknown and potentially very large; economists who have examined the deficiency payment route, including the researchers who studied the Madhya Pradesh experiment, caution that even the cheaper designs transfer a significant share of their value to traders when reference prices are drawn from the markets being compensated. The prohibition design faces the objection from first principles of trade: a floor price that forbids sale below a level above the market's valuation does not raise the valuation, and the predictable consequences are unsold produce, distress sales outside the regulated channels where no record exists, and enforcement directed at a vast number of small transactions by the weakest arm of the state. There is also the production distortion: a guaranteed price for the mandated crops, especially one built on the C2 measure, would lock cropping patterns to the guaranteed list, aggravating the water-intensive paddy cultivation in the north-western aquifer belt that agricultural policy has spent decades trying to moderate, a point pressed by the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices itself in its own price policy reports, which have repeatedly cautioned against open-ended procurement of water-intensive crops in ecologically stressed regions. And the constitutional-political objection completes the case: agriculture and agricultural marketing sit in the State List, so a Union guarantee statute would have to be built on the Union's trade and foodstuffs powers in the Concurrent field, would displace state systems that vary enormously, and would reproduce, in the name of the farmers' movement, the centralisation that the same movement resisted in 2020.

The consumer belongs in this argument too, and both camps recruit and neglect the consumer in turn. Every increase in the support price of a staple transmits, through the market and through the economic cost of distributed grain, into the price of food for a population the majority of which buys more food than it sells, including the rural landless and the urban poor. The commission's own terms of reference oblige it to weigh the effect of its recommendations on the cost of living, an instruction that exists because the framers of the instrument understood the price to be a transfer decision as much as an incentive. Defenders of generous prices answer, with reason, that the distribution system insulates the poorest consumers by issuing grain at prices fixed under the food security framework, so that the transmission runs largely to the exchequer rather than to the ration shop queue; critics answer, also with reason, that the insulation is itself a fiscal cost that grows with the support price, and that households outside the distribution net, migrants and the unregistered among them, face the market price unprotected. The honest position, which this article adopts, is that the support price is a choice about the distribution of income among farmers, consumers and taxpayers, made annually by the executive without a legislative debate on its distributive merits. The demand for a statute is, among other things, a demand that this distributive choice be taken where the Constitution puts such choices, in a legislature, on the record, with the losers named. That is a constitutional argument of real force, and it stands independently of whether the statute demanded would be good economics.

One more constraint belongs in the record before the verdict, because it limits what any Indian guarantee statute could promise even if Parliament enacted one tomorrow in the fullest form: the international trade discipline. India's support programmes are measured, in the World Trade Organization's Agreement on Agriculture, against limits on trade-distorting support calculated by comparing administered prices with fixed external reference prices from a base period in the nineteen-eighties, a methodology under which the arithmetic gap attributed to India's rice and wheat programmes runs large even where the actual fiscal outlay is what it is, because the reference prices are frozen in a different economic era. India has defended its food security procurement under the peace clause agreed at the Bali ministerial conference, which shields public stockholding programmes for food security from challenge subject to conditions and notifications, and has invoked that protection for its rice programme; the underlying dispute about the methodology, pressed by the United States and other exporters on one side and by India and the developing country groupings on the other, remains unresolved. The significance for the design table is concrete. A statutory guarantee extended across crops, at prices benchmarked to comprehensive costs, would enlarge the measured support under the contested methodology for every covered crop, multiplying India's exposure in the one forum where the support price already generates litigation risk for the state. This is not a decisive argument against a guarantee, and India has consistently and publicly rejected the proposition that its food security programmes should be trimmed to a formula built on outdated reference prices. It is a further design question, of the kind the table exists to expose: a guarantee statute would have to be drafted with an eye to Geneva as well as to the mandi, and the draftsmen would inherit a negotiation their predecessors have been conducting, without conclusion, for a decade.

The verdict this article defends follows from the legal analysis rather than from either campaign. The present arrangement is indefensible in its rhetoric: a state that calls a number a minimum support price, and harvests the political credit of a promise, while maintaining no machinery that makes the number a minimum for most of the farmers who hear it, is conducting policy by ambiguity, and the ambiguity is not accidental but load-bearing. The remedy, however, is not necessarily the guarantee in its slogan form, because the design table shows that each statutory form of the guarantee carries failure modes that would fall hardest on the farmers least able to absorb them. The remedy that the record supports is honesty of instrument, in whichever direction a legislature chooses to move: if the price is to remain an announcement, it should be announced as what it is, a procurement intention for specified crops in specified operations, with the reach of those operations published as plainly as the price; if it is to become a right, the bill that creates it should be judged by the six design questions in the table, and a bill that does not answer them should be recognised as the slogan in statutory dress. What the subject cannot survive indefinitely is the present condition, in which the word minimum performs in public the work that only machinery can perform in a market yard.

One closing observation belongs to the reader rather than to either camp. The support price debate is usually conducted as though the question were the size of a number, and generations of argument have been spent on margins, percentages and cost measures as if the figure were the policy. This article has tried to show that the figure is the least of it. The policy is the apparatus around the figure: an advisory commission without a statute, a Cabinet decision without a duty, a buying machine built for two crops and a few states, a set of schemes that begin and end by executive instruction, and a body of remedies that attaches to everything except the promise itself. Whoever wishes to change what the support price does in a farmer's life, in either direction, will have to legislate about the apparatus, and the six questions in the design table are the agenda for that legislation whenever a Parliament is ready to take it up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is minimum support price legally binding in India?

No. No statute creates the minimum support price or obliges any buyer, public or private, to pay it. The price is announced by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs on the recommendation of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, which is itself an attached office of the agriculture ministry rather than a statutory body. The announcement is an executive act that states the price at which government agencies intend to procure covered crops where procurement operations run. A sale below the announced price breaches no provision of law by that fact alone, and the difference cannot be recovered from the buyer or the state on the strength of the announcement.

### Q: Who decides the minimum support price?

The recommendation is prepared by the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, which studies costs of cultivation, consults state governments and farmers' organisations, and weighs the factors in its terms of reference, including inter-crop parity, demand and supply and the effect on the wider economy. The decision is taken by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, the Cabinet committee responsible for economic matters, which may accept or modify the recommendation. The commission began as the Agricultural Prices Commission in January 1965, was renamed in 1985, and functions as an attached office of the agriculture ministry. Its advice binds no one, and the Cabinet's decision creates an announcement, not a legal obligation.

### Q: For which crops is a minimum support price announced?

The announcement covers the twenty-two crops in the mandate of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices: the kharif group including paddy, jowar, bajra, maize, ragi, tur, moong, urad, groundnut, sunflower seed, soyabean, sesamum, nigerseed and cotton, the rabi group of wheat, barley, gram, lentil, rapeseed and mustard and safflower, and commercial crops including copra and raw jute in the standard enumeration. Sugarcane is treated separately through the fair and remunerative price. Fruits, vegetables, milk and most other produce have no announced support price at all, so the instrument reaches a defined list of field crops and not the whole of agricultural output.

### Q: What is the difference between A2 plus FL and C2 cost in support price calculation?

A2 counts the costs a cultivator actually pays out: seed, fertiliser, hired labour, machinery, irrigation, land revenue, interest on working capital and rent for leased-in land. A2 plus FL adds the imputed value of the farm family's own labour. C2 adds, further, the imputed rental value of owned land and interest on owned fixed capital, making it the comprehensive cost and always the largest of the three figures for the same crop. The distinction decides the largest argument in the field, because a margin of fifty per cent over A2 plus FL is a much smaller price than a margin of fifty per cent over C2.

### Q: What did the Swaminathan Commission recommend on price?

The National Commission on Farmers, chaired by M. S. Swaminathan and reporting between 2004 and 2006, recommended that the minimum support price be at least fifty per cent more than the weighted average cost of production, with the cost understood comprehensively, in the C2 sense that includes imputed rent on land and interest on capital. Its reasoning was that farmers' net take-home income should be comparable to the incomes of salaried employees. The recommendation was never adopted in that form. The Union Budget for 2018-19 committed to prices at one and a half times the cost of production, and the government implemented the commitment using the narrower A2 plus FL measure, which is why the implementation of the recommendation remains disputed.

### Q: Who actually buys crops at the support price?

Government agencies buy, where buying happens at all. The Food Corporation of India and state government agencies procure paddy and wheat for the central pool in the states with developed procurement systems, and state agencies procure under the decentralised procurement arrangement in states that run their own operations. For pulses, oilseeds and copra, central nodal agencies such as NAFED procure under the Price Support Scheme in sanctioned quantities when market prices fall below the announcement. Private traders are never obliged to buy at the support price; they buy at auction prices, which the presence of a purchasing agency may hold at the announced level in a procurement centre and which elsewhere are formed by the local market.

### Q: What would a legal guarantee for support price mean?

It would mean a statute converting the announced price from an executive statement into an enforceable obligation, and it could take three distinct forms: a duty on the state to purchase all produce offered at the price, a prohibition on any buyer purchasing below the price enforced by penalty, or a duty on the state to pay sellers the difference between the announced price and the price actually realised. Each form requires the statute to specify the crops and quantities covered, who bears the cost, the enforcement machinery and what happens when the guaranteed price exceeds the market price for a whole harvest. No such statute has been enacted, and the demand for one is a demand to create a right that does not yet exist.

### Q: Can a farmer sue if paid below the support price?

Not on the basis of the announcement itself, because it creates no right that a suit could enforce and no duty in the buyer that a court could hold breached. A farmer's claims, where they exist, arise from the transaction: recovery of an agreed price wrongfully withheld, complaints about weight, grading or deductions through the market committee's processes under the state marketing law, or challenges to arbitrary treatment inside a procurement operation, such as refusal of conforming grain, through administrative channels and the High Court's writ jurisdiction. Fraud in a transaction can attract the criminal law, but hard bargaining at a low price is not an offence, and no court can order a buyer to have paid the support price.

### Q: How many times a year is the minimum support price announced?

Announcements are made crop season by crop season, principally in two rounds. The kharif prices are announced before the monsoon sowing for the fourteen kharif crops, and the rabi prices before the winter sowing for the rabi group, with the fair and remunerative price for sugarcane settled for the sugar season on its own cycle. The timing is part of the instrument's design: a price meant to influence planting decisions must precede them. Each announcement follows the same institutional path, a recommendation from the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices built on projected costs for the coming season and a decision by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs.

### Q: What is the fair and remunerative price for sugarcane, and how does it differ from the minimum support price?

It is the price fixed for sugarcane supplied to sugar mills, recommended by the same commission and approved by the same Cabinet committee, but it differs from the support price in the one respect that decides enforceability: once fixed, it is written into the Sugarcane (Control) Order 1966, an instrument issued under the Essential Commodities Act 1955, which replaced the earlier statutory minimum price for cane with the fair and remunerative price from the 2009-10 season. The order obliges mills to pay it, prescribes the time for payment and interest on delay, and arrears are recoverable. Sugarcane thus shows that a floor price binds when it is carried by a statutory instrument, and that the support price's weakness is a matter of legal form, not of administrative capacity.

### Q: Why is procurement concentrated in a handful of states?

Because the machinery was built where the food security strategy of the 1960s needed surplus grain, in the irrigated wheat and rice belts of Punjab and Haryana, and because procurement infrastructure, purchase centres, storage, working capital, market yards and experienced agencies, accumulates where it has operated longest. Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Uttar Pradesh built substantial operations later, and the decentralised procurement scheme spread state-run buying further, but for most crops in most states the announced price is not backed by a purchasing agency at the harvest. The concentration explains the regional intensity of price politics: the instrument is a working institution in a few states and an announcement elsewhere.

### Q: What is the decentralised procurement scheme and which states use it?

Under the decentralised procurement scheme, introduced in 1997-98, a state government procures grain itself for the public distribution system within the state instead of routing the operation through the Food Corporation of India, storing and distributing the grain against its own allocation and settling the surplus or deficit with the central pool by arrangement. States including Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, West Bengal and others adopted it in varying measure and at varying times for paddy or wheat. The design aimed to extend procurement beyond the traditional belt, to reduce the double handling of grain moved into and out of a national pool, and to give states a direct stake in the quality and cost of their own operations.

### Q: What is PM-AASHA and what are its three parts?

PM-AASHA, the Pradhan Mantri Annadata Aay Sanrakshan Abhiyan approved in September 2018, is the umbrella programme through which the Union supports prices for pulses, oilseeds and copra. Its first part continues the Price Support Scheme, under which central nodal agencies procure sanctioned quantities at the support price when the market falls below it, with the Centre bearing the expenditure and losses. Its second part, the Price Deficiency Payment Scheme, pays registered farmers who sell in notified yards the difference between the support price and the realised price, without the state taking delivery. Its third part piloted private procurement by stockists at the support price in selected districts on compensated terms. All three are executive schemes, and none creates a legal entitlement.

### Q: How did Madhya Pradesh's Bhavantar scheme pay farmers the price difference?

The Bhavantar Bhugtan Yojana, launched by Madhya Pradesh in October 2017 for eight kharif crops with thin procurement, paid registered farmers who sold in notified market yards the gap between the support price and a modal price drawn from mandi prices within the state and in other states where the crop traded. If the farmer's sale price fell between the modal price and the support price, the payment completed the return to the support price; if it fell below the modal price, the payment was capped at the modal gap, so the scheme insured the market's general level rather than an individual bargain. Its record, including evidence that traders bid less keenly when the state topped up returns, shaped the national Price Deficiency Payment Scheme that followed it.

### Q: Why do state governments announce bonuses over and above the minimum support price?

A bonus lets a state government raise the effective return to its farmers for a season, display responsiveness to farm distress within its borders, and compete for produce, at a cost carried on the state budget and without any legislation. Paddy bonuses in states such as Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh are the recurring examples. The instrument's limits are those of its legal character: it lasts only for the crop and season announced, it is payable only through the procurement operation it attaches to, and it can distort the wider system by drawing produce across state borders toward the higher effective price. The Union government has at intervals resisted bonuses for exactly that reason, since they disturb the economics of the central pool and parity among states.

### Q: Does the minimum support price apply when a farmer sells outside a mandi?

The support price does not apply, in the sense of binding anyone, to any sale anywhere, inside a mandi or outside it, because it is an announcement and not a term imposed by law on transactions. What changes outside the mandi is the surrounding framework. A sale inside a regulated yard takes place under the state marketing statute, with recorded auction, weighment and the market committee's dispute processes available. A sale at the farm gate or to a village trader rests on the parties' bargain alone, and where a deficiency payment scheme operates it will usually require sale in a notified yard as the condition of payment, precisely because the yard generates the price record the scheme needs. The announcement follows the farmer as information in both places and as law in neither.

### Q: Can a state make buying below the minimum support price an offence?

No state marketing statute in general operation does so, and the attempts show the difficulty. A state can regulate its own market yards and licensees under its agriculture and marketing entries, but a prohibition addressed to central legislation's field, or one that cuts across inter-state trade, runs into the repugnancy procedure of Article 254 and requires the President's assent to prevail. When the Punjab Assembly in October 2020 passed amendment bills proposing to punish purchase below the support price within the state, those bills depended on that assent and never became operative law. A state can, however, spend its own money above the announcement through bonuses and deficiency payments without any such obstacle, which is why state action in this field takes fiscal rather than penal form.

### Q: What happens to the grain the government procures at the minimum support price?

Procured paddy and wheat enter the central pool, or the stocks of a decentralised procurement state, and are held as buffer and operational stocks against the needs of the public distribution system, the welfare programmes supplied with grain by the state and the food security reserve. The grain is milled where it is paddy, moved to deficit regions, stored in godowns and covered storage, and issued over the following seasons at the issue prices the distribution framework prescribes. The costs of acquisition at the support price, together with procurement incidentals, transport, storage and interest, form the economic cost of grain, and the gap between that cost and the recovery from issue prices is the food subsidy carried in the Union budget.

### Q: Why is it so difficult to write a workable law guaranteeing the minimum support price?

Because the slogan conceals three different statutes, a duty on the state to buy, a prohibition on private purchase below the price, and a duty to pay the difference, and each fails in a characteristic way. Open-ended purchase collides with storage, deterioration and fiscal limits already strained by two crops. A prohibition cannot summon buyers into a market priced above its valuation and pushes trade outside the regulated channels where records exist. Deficiency payment depends on a reference price drawn from the very market being compensated, which traders can bid down in the knowledge that the state will top up, as the Madhya Pradesh experiment showed. A draft that does not defend itself against its own failure mode is not yet a workable law.

### Q: Why do farmer organisations insist on a law rather than accept assurances, bonuses and schemes?

Because everything the executive can give without a statute it can withdraw without one, and the record of this field is a record of withdrawals and lapses: bonuses announced for a season and discontinued, procurement quantities fixed year by year, scheme windows that open late or close early, and written assurances in negotiation that bind politically at most. A statute is the one instrument that binds the state itself, survives a change of ministry, can be pleaded before a court or the authority the statute names, and can be altered only by a legislature in public. The insistence on legislation is therefore not a preference for a bigger announcement; it is a judgment, formed by experience, about which promises in this field have historically been kept.
