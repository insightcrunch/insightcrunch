The Motor Vehicles Act 1988 is the statute that decides who may drive on an Indian road, on what conditions a vehicle may earn its living there, and who pays when a journey ends in death or injury. Almost every Indian family interacts with the first two of those functions through a licence, a registration certificate or a bus permit. The third function is the one that matters most and is understood least, because it operates in a specialised tribunal, under a method of calculation that the Act itself never sets out, at the worst moment of a family's life. This article explains all three functions from the text of the Act as Parliament enacted it, and then explains the judicial method that the text left to the courts.

The Act is Act 59 of 1988. It received the assent of the President on 14 October 1988 and came into force on 1 July 1989, replacing the Motor Vehicles Act 1939, which had governed Indian road transport for fifty years. Its long title describes it as an Act to consolidate and amend the law relating to motor vehicles, and consolidation is the right word for most of it: the licensing, registration and permit chapters carry forward the architecture of the 1939 Act in modernised form. The compensation chapters are different in kind. There the 1988 Act assembled, in one statutory scheme, a compulsory insurance obligation, a liability without fault for fixed sums, and a dedicated tribunal with power to award just compensation, and that assembly is the reason the Act is read less as a traffic code than as India's principal personal injury statute.

Three disciplines run through everything that follows, and the reader should hold them from the start. The article states the Act as enacted in 1988, because the restructuring carried out by the amendment of 2019, with its substituted penalty figures and its reworked compensation provisions, belongs to [the article on the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019](/2020/08/01/motor-vehicles-amendment-act-2019/), whose questions this pillar does not take. Where a provision of the 1988 text was changed by the intermediate amendment of 1994, most importantly the addition of an optional structured formula for compensation, the article says so and dates the change. And the article is written clinically about claims, because it will be read by people who have lost a family member in a road accident: it describes the process and the calculation, it describes no incident, and it closes with a short statement of the limits of what a general account of the law can do for a person with an actual claim.

## From the Act of 1939 to the Motor Vehicles Act 1988

The 1988 Act was the third general statute on the subject. The Indian Motor Vehicles Act 1914 was a short wartime measure. The Motor Vehicles Act 1939 was the first mature code, and its half century of operation built the institutions the 1988 Act inherited: licensing and registering authorities, stage carriage permits, state transport undertakings, and, through an amendment of 1956, the Claims Tribunals themselves, which entered Indian law as an addition to the 1939 Act rather than as an invention of 1988. Liability without fault also arrived by amendment of the older Act, in 1982, at sums below those the 1988 Act would enact. The compensation scheme of the present Act is therefore best understood as a consolidation and enlargement of machinery that had been growing inside the 1939 framework, attached to a licensing and permit code whose outlines the 1939 Act had already settled.

What the 1988 consolidation changed, beyond renumbering and modernisation, can be stated under four heads. It raised the no fault sums to twenty five thousand rupees for death and twelve thousand rupees for permanent disablement. It restated the insurance chapter so that the insurer's obligation to satisfy awards and the limited grounds of defence stood in a single section, Section 149, in place of the dispersed provisions that had generated conflicting readings. It liberalised the grant of stage carriage permits on routes outside nationalised schemes and of all India tourist permits and national permits for goods carriages, recording in its statement of objects a judgment that the permit system's scarcity had itself become a source of rent and delay. And it provided for deterrent punishment for the offences it singled out, for standards for components and anti pollution devices, for the administration of the solatium scheme for hit and run cases through the General Insurance Corporation, and for road safety councils at the Centre and in the states. Section 217 repealed the 1939 Act and saved what had been done under it, so that licences, registrations and permits continued without a break in continuity.

The Act works through subordinate legislation to an extent the bare chapter headings conceal. The Central Motor Vehicles Rules 1989, made by the Centre under the rule making powers in sections 27 and 110 and their companions, prescribe the forms of the licence and the registration certificate, the tests of competence to drive, the syllabus of the learner's test, the construction and equipment standards in detail, and the procedure for national permits. Each state makes rules under sections 28, 65, 96 and 111 and the other state rule making heads, covering the conduct of licensing and registering authorities, permit fees and procedure, and the procedure of the Claims Tribunals under Section 176. A practitioner reading a dispute about a licence condition or a permit fee will therefore move constantly between the Act and two layers of rules, and a reader of this pillar should hold the layering in mind: the Act fixes the entitlements and the forums, and the rules fix most of the paper.

The Act also carries an institutional layer directed at prevention rather than compensation, which its statement of objects recorded and its closing chapters contain: road safety councils at the Centre and in the states and districts, charged with advising on and coordinating the measures by which accidents are to be reduced. These bodies make no awards and grant no licences, and their presence in a statute otherwise built of prohibitions and forums is a reminder that the 1988 Parliament understood the compensation scheme, however elaborately the courts would later build on it, as the second best outcome: the first was the accident that did not happen, and the licensing, fitness and traffic chapters are its instruments.

One further structural point completes the outline. The Act binds the Republic's own fleet as well as the citizen's: Section 60 provides for the registration of vehicles belonging to governments, and the insurance chapter's exemptions for government vehicles are conditioned on the authority either carrying its own risk under the section's terms or establishing the fund the section contemplates. The state that licenses, registers and permits is itself a road user inside the scheme, a duality that recurs in the compensation chapters, where state transport undertakings are among the most frequent respondents before the tribunals their own governments constitute.

## The three things the Act does

The first thing the Act does is control entry. No person may drive a motor vehicle in a public place without a licence granted under Chapter II, no motor vehicle may be used in a public place unless it is registered under Chapter IV, and no transport vehicle may be used to carry passengers or goods for hire or reward unless a permit under Chapter V authorises that use in that place. Entry control is the Act's oldest function, inherited from the legislation of 1914 and 1939, and it is administered almost entirely by state authorities: licensing authorities and registering authorities appointed by state governments, working under rules framed by the Centre in the Central Motor Vehicles Rules 1989 and by each state under its own rule making power.

The second thing the Act does is regulate conduct and condition. Chapter VII prescribes standards for the construction, equipment and maintenance of vehicles, Chapter VIII controls traffic through speed limits, weight limits, traffic signs and driving regulations, and Chapter XIII backs the whole code with offences and penalties. This regulatory middle of the Act is the part a driver encounters at a checkpoint, and it is the part whose penalties the 2019 amendment most heavily revised, which is why this article states the enacted figures with care and flags them as the enacted figures wherever they appear.

The third thing the Act does is allocate the cost of accidents. Chapter X imposes liability without fault for fixed sums where death or permanent disablement results from the use of a motor vehicle. Chapter XI makes third party insurance compulsory and obliges the insurer, within defined limits and subject to defined defences, to satisfy awards made against the insured. Chapter XII constitutes Motor Accidents Claims Tribunals to adjudicate claims, gives them a summary procedure, and empowers them to award just compensation. The three chapters together create a compensation system that does not depend on the solvency of the individual driver who caused the harm, and the design choice embedded in them, that the loss should be spread through compulsory insurance rather than left where it falls, is the most consequential policy decision in the Act.

### What must a person hold before driving lawfully on a public road?

A driving licence effective for the class of vehicle being driven, granted under Chapter II after the tests and conditions that chapter prescribes. The vehicle itself must be registered under Chapter IV and, if it is a transport vehicle, covered by a permit under Chapter V, and a policy of third party insurance must be in force for its use.

## The federal shape of the Act

Road transport sits in the Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule, and the Act bears the marks of that placement throughout. Parliament legislates the framework: the definitions in Section 2, the chapter architecture, the tribunal scheme and the insurance obligation are uniform across India. The states administer the framework and fill much of its content. State governments appoint the licensing and registering authorities, constitute the State Transport Authority and the Regional Transport Authorities that grant permits, constitute the Claims Tribunals under Section 165, and frame rules on the matters the Act leaves to them. The Centre frames rules on construction, equipment and interstate matters, and retains specific powers, including the power under Section 88 to frame the scheme for national permits that let a goods carriage work across state boundaries on a single authorisation.

This division explains two features of the Act that puzzle readers who expect a traffic code to be uniform. The first is that procedure at the counter varies between states while the entitlements do not: the licence, the registration certificate and the permit are national documents in form, issued by state machinery. The second is that the Act repeatedly legislates through authorities rather than through courts. Permits are granted or refused by transport authorities, licences are suspended by licensing authorities subject to appeal, and accident claims are decided by tribunals rather than by the civil courts, whose jurisdiction Section 175 expressly bars. The Act is, in this respect, part of the wider pattern by which specialised statutory forums displaced the ordinary courts across Indian regulatory law, a pattern the [guide to infrastructure and transport legislation](/2020/06/01/infrastructure-laws-india-guide/) traces across the sector.

The chapters also differ sharply in how much they leave open. The licensing and permit chapters are dense with detail, because Parliament intended the counter level rules to be visible in the statute. The compensation chapter, by contrast, states a liability and a forum and a standard, just compensation, and says almost nothing about how the standard is to be converted into a sum of money. That silence was not an oversight that later Parliaments corrected. It persisted through the amendments of 1994 and 2000, and the method that fills it was built by the Supreme Court over three decades, in decisions this article sets out in their sequence. The namable claim of this pillar is therefore precise: the statute created the forum and the courts created the sum, and anyone who reads only the Act will know where a claim is heard and almost nothing about what it is worth.

## Licensing of drivers: Chapter II

Chapter II, sections 3 to 28, is built on a single prohibition. Section 3 provides that no person shall drive a motor vehicle in any public place unless he holds an effective driving licence issued to him authorising him to drive the vehicle, and that no person shall drive a transport vehicle, other than a vehicle hired for his own use or rented under a scheme the Act recognises, unless the licence specifically entitles him to do so. The licence is therefore class specific: authority to drive a motorcycle is not authority to drive a heavy goods vehicle, and the endorsement structure of Section 10, which prescribes the form and contents of the licence by class of vehicle, is what makes the prohibition administrable.

Section 4 fixes the age thresholds. As enacted, a person below eighteen years could not drive a motor vehicle in a public place, with a limited exception permitting a person of sixteen to drive a motorcycle with an engine capacity not exceeding fifty cubic centimetres, and no person below twenty years could drive a transport vehicle. The gradient is deliberate: the Act treats the professional carriage of passengers and goods as a responsibility that begins later than private driving, and it supports the gradient with additional conditions, including the requirement that a transport vehicle driver's licence depends on holding a licence for a light motor vehicle for a qualifying period and on producing a medical certificate.

The learner's licence, in sections 7 and 8, is the supervised antechamber to full licensing. Section 7 restricts the grant of a learner's licence for a transport vehicle, and Section 8 provides for the grant of a learner's licence to a person who satisfies the licensing authority on the matters the rules prescribe, including knowledge of the rules of the road and traffic signs, after the prescribed fee and, in the case of the categories the section specifies, a preliminary test. The full driving licence follows under Section 9, on the applicant passing the test of competence to drive that the section and the Central Motor Vehicles Rules prescribe. Section 6 supports the structure by restricting the holding of multiple licences and by disqualifying a person whose licence has been revoked from obtaining a fresh one during the period the section specifies.

Duration and renewal were fixed by Section 14 as enacted. A licence to drive a vehicle other than a transport vehicle remained effective for twenty years from issue or renewal, or until the holder attained fifty years of age, whichever was earlier, and then for five years at a time on renewal. A transport vehicle licence remained effective for three years. Section 15 governs renewal and Section 16 the revocation of a licence on grounds of disease or disability. These periods matter to the pillar's purpose because they were among the provisions the later amendments altered, and a reader comparing the enacted text with any later statement of licensing periods will find different numbers: the figures in this paragraph are the Act as passed in 1988.

The disciplinary machinery of the chapter is in sections 17 to 22. Section 19 empowers the licensing authority to disqualify a holder from driving, or to revoke the licence, on grounds that include habitual criminal or drunken conduct, use of the vehicle in the commission of a cognisable offence, dangerous driving that endangers the public, and failure to stop after an accident, after giving the holder an opportunity of being heard. Section 20 empowers a court convicting a person of an offence under the Act to disqualify him from holding a licence, Section 21 suspends the licence in the situations it specifies pending proceedings, and Section 22 provides for suspension or cancellation on conviction for the serious offences it names, including causing death by dangerous driving. Section 28 gives the state government rule making power over the chapter. An appeal against the orders of a licensing authority lies to the appellate authority the Act prescribes, keeping licensing discipline, like everything else in this Act, inside the administrative hierarchy rather than in the courts in the first instance.

### How long did a driving licence last under the Act as enacted?

A licence for a vehicle other than a transport vehicle lasted twenty years from issue or renewal or until the holder turned fifty, whichever came first, and renewed thereafter in five year periods. A transport vehicle licence lasted three years. Section 14 fixed both periods, and later amendments changed them, so the figures here are the 1988 settlement.

The making of the licence is as regulated as its holding. Section 8 requires the applicant for a learner's licence to produce a medical certificate in the prescribed form where the category requires one and to pass the preliminary test the rules prescribe on the rules of the road, traffic signs and signals and the duties of a driver when involved in an accident. Section 9 conditions the full licence on a test of competence, conducted by the licensing authority or the person it authorises, covering the applicant's ability to drive safely, knowledge of the mechanisms of the vehicle and understanding of the regulations, and it disqualifies an applicant whose previous licence was revoked until the period the section states has run. Section 26 obliges every state government to maintain a State Register of driving licences in the prescribed form, recording the particulars of licences issued and renewed and the classes of vehicle each holder is authorised to drive, so that the licence is backed by a public register against which its authenticity and endorsements can be checked. The Central Government's rule making power in Section 27 covers the form of application, the fees and the conduct of the tests. The apparatus is deliberately bureaucratic: competence is certified once, recorded permanently, and withdrawable for cause, and the register is what makes the withdrawal effective across the country.

The chapter also governs the edges of the licence. Section 5 makes the owner of a motor vehicle responsible where a person who does not satisfy the conditions of sections 3 and 4 is allowed to drive it, so that the licensing prohibition binds the fleet operator as well as the driver. Section 11 provides for additions to a licence, by which a holder qualified on one class of vehicle obtains authority for another class on satisfying the prescribed conditions, and Section 12 regulates the licensing and regulation of schools or establishments imparting instruction in the driving of motor vehicles, bringing driving instruction itself inside the regulatory perimeter. Section 13 makes a licence granted under the Act effective throughout India. Section 17 governs the orders the licensing authority may pass and the endorsement of disqualification and other particulars on the licence itself, and Section 23 states the effect of disqualification, under which the licence is suspended or its holder is barred from obtaining one, according to the nature of the order. Section 24 requires the endorsement of any conviction or order on the licence and its transmission between authorities, so that the licence carries its holder's disciplinary history with it. The scheme's coherence lies in this accumulation: the licence is not a one time permission but a continuing record, against which competence, class, validity and discipline are all readable at the roadside.

The interaction between licensing and liability is indirect but real, and it is worth stating precisely because it is so often misstated. Driving without an effective licence is an offence, and permitting it is an offence by the owner. In compensation law, the same fact operates differently: the absence or invalidity of the driver's licence does not reduce the claimant's right against the insurer, because the insurer's defence under Section 149(2) founded on the driver's licence is a defence between insurer and insured, which the pay and recover doctrine resolves by payment to the claimant and recovery from the owner. The licensing chapter therefore disciplines the driver and the owner without diminishing the protection Chapter XI gives the stranger on the road, and the separation of those questions, discipline in one forum and protection in another, is characteristic of the Act's design.

## Licensing of conductors: Chapter III

Chapter III, sections 29 to 38, performs for the conductor of a stage carriage what Chapter II performs for its driver. Section 29 requires that no person shall act as a conductor of a stage carriage unless he holds an effective conductor's licence granted or renewed under the chapter, and that no person who does not hold such a licence shall be employed or permitted to act as a conductor by the owner of the vehicle. The conductor is defined by function in Section 2, as the person engaged in collecting fares from passengers and regulating their entry into and exit from the stage carriage, and the chapter subjects that function to the same apparatus of grant, age and qualification conditions, renewal, revocation for misconduct, and appeal that governs drivers.

The chapter is short and easily passed over, and its inclusion tells the reader something about the world the Act was consolidating. The stage carriage conductor handled the cash of a public transport undertaking, dealt continuously with the travelling public, and was the point at which fare disputes, overcrowding and passenger safety converged in daily operation. Parliament in 1939, and again in 1988, treated that role as licensed work rather than as a matter left to the employer's judgment. The provision on the conductor's duties and conduct, and the offences attached to refusal of duty, complete a scheme in which every person exercising a public function on a public service vehicle holds a state granted authority to do so.


The chapter's disciplinary provisions mirror the driver's scheme at a smaller scale. The licensing authority may revoke or suspend a conductor's licence where the holder's conduct falls within the grounds the chapter states, after hearing him, and the licence must be surrendered in the situations the chapter prescribes, with an appeal to the authority the Act designates. Section 38 reserves the state governments' rule making power over the chapter. The conductor's licence has diminished in practical prominence as fare collection has been reorganised in many undertakings, and the automated and conductorless services of the largest cities operate under state level arrangements, but the chapter remains the statutory statement that the person who takes the public's fares and manages the public's entry to a public service vehicle holds a licence to do so, liable to be taken away for misconduct. It is a small chapter performing the same work as Chapter II: it converts an employment relationship into a licensed public function.

## Registration of vehicles: Chapter IV

Chapter IV, sections 39 to 65, makes registration the identity system of the vehicle fleet. Section 39 states the prohibition in absolute terms: no person shall drive any motor vehicle, and no owner shall cause or permit a vehicle to be driven, in any public place or in any other place unless the vehicle is registered in accordance with the chapter and the certificate of registration has not been suspended or cancelled, and the vehicle carries a registration mark displayed in the prescribed manner. Section 41 prescribes how registration is made, on application to the registering authority within whose jurisdiction the vehicle is or the owner resides or carries on business, and Section 40 addresses the place of registration. The registration certificate that results is the document against which ownership, transfer, hypothecation and fitness are recorded, and the chapter's machinery exists to keep that record continuous through the vehicle's life.

The principal events of that life are each given a provision. Section 47 requires a new registration mark to be assigned when a vehicle registered in one state is kept in another state for a period exceeding twelve months. Section 48 provides the no objection certificate that makes interstate transfer of registration orderly, by requiring the authority of the state of original registration to certify that it has no objection, so that tax and offence records follow the vehicle. Section 49 obliges the owner to report a change of residence or place of business. Section 50 governs transfer of ownership, requiring the transferor to report the transfer within fourteen days and the transferee to apply within thirty days for the transfer to be recorded, and Section 51 makes special provision for vehicles held under hire purchase, lease or hypothecation, recording the financier's interest in the certificate so that the person in possession cannot deal with the vehicle as though unencumbered. Section 52 restricts alteration of a vehicle's particulars from those recorded at registration, with a controlled exception that later became significant for fuel conversion, and Section 53 empowers the registering authority to suspend a registration where the vehicle is in a condition dangerous to the public or is used in contravention of the Act, with Section 55 providing for cancellation in graver cases.

Two provisions carry weight in daily operation. Section 56 requires a transport vehicle to hold a certificate of fitness, issued after inspection, and provides that a transport vehicle shall not be deemed validly registered unless it carries such a certificate: registration alone does not put a truck or a bus lawfully on the road, and the fitness certificate is the point at which physical condition enters the registration system. Section 59 empowers the Centre to fix the age limit beyond which a motor vehicle shall not be registered or its registration renewed, a power left in reserve by the 1988 text. Section 62 requires information about stolen and recovered vehicles to move between the police and the registering authority. Taken together the chapter performs a function easy to underestimate: it makes every vehicle on the road answerable to a record, and it is that record, produced after an accident, that identifies the owner and the insurer on whom the compensation chapters operate.

Three further provisions of the chapter deserve record. Section 43 provides for temporary registration, valid for the period the section specifies, to carry a new vehicle from the place of purchase or manufacture to the place of registration. Section 45 empowers the registering authority to refuse registration or renewal in the circumstances the section states, including where the vehicle is reported stolen or its particulars do not tally, and Section 46 makes registration under the chapter effective throughout India, which is the registration counterpart of the licence's national effectiveness. Section 57 provides an appeal against the orders of a registering authority. Section 58 makes special provision for the registration of transport vehicles and trailers, and Section 61 applies the chapter to trailers drawn by motor vehicles. The chapter closes with Section 65, the state governments' rule making power over registration. The pattern repeats the licensing pattern: a national entitlement, state administered machinery, a record that follows the subject matter, and discipline by suspension and cancellation inside the administrative system, subject to appeal.

Registration also performs an evidentiary function in accident litigation that its drafting barely acknowledges. The certificate of registration identifies the owner, and ownership under the Act has been read by the courts by reference to the person in whose name the vehicle stands registered, so that the registration record ordinarily fixes the person answerable as owner for the liabilities of Chapters X and XI, whatever private arrangement about the vehicle may exist between individuals. Where a vehicle has been sold but the transfer not recorded under Section 50, the registered owner has repeatedly been held to remain the owner for the purposes of third party liability, a line of authority that gives the duty to report a transfer in Section 50 its practical teeth: the record is not a formality but the public statement of who answers for the vehicle.

## Control of transport vehicles: Chapter V

Chapter V, sections 66 to 96, is the permit system, and it begins where registration ends. Section 66 provides that no owner of a motor vehicle shall use or permit the use of the vehicle as a transport vehicle in any public place, whether or not it is carrying passengers or goods, except in accordance with the conditions of a permit granted or countersigned by a Regional or State Transport Authority authorising the use of the vehicle in that place in the manner in which it is used. Registration identifies the vehicle; the permit authorises its commercial work, and the distinction is the chapter's foundation. A private car needs registration and insurance. A bus, a taxi, a truck or a school vehicle needs, in addition, a permit that specifies what it may carry, where, and on what conditions.

Section 67 reserves to the state government a general power to control road transport in the public interest, having regard to the advantages offered to the public and the desirability of preventing congestion and deterioration of road conditions, and to issue directions to the transport authorities accordingly. Section 68 constitutes the authorities themselves: a State Transport Authority for each state and Regional Transport Authorities for the regions into which the state is divided, each with a composition that combines official members with persons the section recognises as having relevant knowledge or experience, and each with the separation between the appellate and granting functions that the section's provisos preserve. Sections 69 to 71 govern applications and the procedure for stage carriage permits, including the considerations the authority must weigh and the representation procedure that lets existing operators and affected persons be heard.

The permit categories are the Act's vocabulary for the organisation of road transport, and each is defined by the work it authorises. A stage carriage permit, granted under Section 72, authorises a motor vehicle adapted to carry more than six passengers to pick up and set down passengers at successive stages along a route for separate fares: this is the bus. A contract carriage permit, granted under Section 74, authorises the vehicle to carry passengers under a contract for the use of the vehicle as a whole, for a fixed or agreed rate, without stopping to pick up or set down passengers not included in the contract: this is the hired taxi or the chartered coach. Section 76 deals with private service vehicle permits for vehicles used by firms and institutions for their own staff. Goods carriage permits authorise the carriage of goods for hire or reward, and Section 84 prescribes the general conditions attaching to all permits, including compliance with the Act, maintenance of the vehicle in a fit condition and observance of the conditions as to routes, areas, fares and freight that the permit embodies. Section 81 fixes the currency of a permit at five years, with renewal, and Section 87 empowers the grant of temporary permits for limited periods and specified occasions. Section 86 empowers the authority that granted the permit to cancel it, or to suspend it for a period, where any of its conditions is breached or the vehicle is used in a manner not authorised, after hearing the holder.

The procedure for the stage carriage permit shows the chapter's administrative law character most fully. Under Section 70 the application must state the route or area, the type and seating capacity of the vehicle, the frequency of service proposed and the timetable where the authority requires one, and under Section 71 the Regional Transport Authority, in considering the application, must have regard to the objects of the Act, shall refuse a permit for an illegal purpose, and must consider the extent to which additional services are required in the public interest, the financial stability of the applicant, the applicant's record as an operator and the interests of the public generally, with a preference the section structures between applicants. Applications are published and representations heard, so that the grant of a bus route is a public act done in the open, in which existing operators may oppose the newcomer and the travelling public's need is the stated criterion. The contract carriage procedure in sections 73 and 74 is lighter, because a contract carriage competes for contracts rather than for routes, and the grant turns principally on the applicant and the vehicle. Renewal under Section 81 is not automatic: the authority may renew the permit or refuse it for the reasons the Act recognises, and the permit holder's compliance record under Section 84 is the material on which refusal ordinarily rests. The system in operation therefore grades commercial road transport by the public exposure of the work: the closer the service comes to carrying the general public on fixed routes, the heavier the apparatus of publication, hearing and preference that surrounds its authorisation.

### What separates a stage carriage from a contract carriage?

The fare structure and the stopping pattern. A stage carriage picks up and sets down individual passengers at successive stages along a route, each paying a separate fare. A contract carriage is engaged as a whole vehicle under one contract for an agreed sum and may not stop to collect passengers outside that contract.

The chapter's most distinctive mechanism is Section 88, which governs the use of a vehicle under a permit outside the region or state in which the permit was granted. A permit granted in one state is not valid in another unless it is countersigned by the transport authority of the other state, or unless it falls within a scheme the section itself creates. Sub sections of Section 88 build the national permit system for goods carriages, under which a single permit, supported by authorisation fees paid to the states in which the vehicle will work, authorises operation across multiple states or throughout India, and a parallel scheme authorises tourist vehicles to operate across states on a single permit. The countersignature and the national permit together were the 1988 Act's answer to a structural problem of Indian federalism: transport is commercially national and constitutionally state administered, and without a statutory bridge every interstate journey would have required a fresh grant in every state crossed. Section 90 provides the revision remedy against orders of the transport authorities, and Section 89 an appeal, again keeping permit disputes inside the administrative system in the first instance.

The national permit machinery in Section 88 shows the countersignature idea carried to its conclusion. A national permit is granted by the authority of the home state for a goods carriage to operate in the home state and in other states specified in the permit, against payment of an authorisation fee which is then distributed among the states concerned in the manner the rules prescribe, replacing the older practice by which the operator sought countersignatures state by state and paid each state's taxes at its border. The permit must be used in at least the minimum number of states the scheme specifies, the vehicle must carry the distinguishing mark the rules prescribe for national permit vehicles, and the authorisation is annual within the five year currency of the permit itself. All India tourist permits apply the same logic to passenger vehicles engaged in tourist promotion, authorising operation across states on a single grant. Both schemes were the 1988 settlement of the oldest grievance of Indian road transport, that a vehicle lawful in its home state became unlawful at an invisible line, and both explain why the chapter spends so much of its length on validity beyond the granting region: in a union of states with state administered transport, the law of movement across internal borders is not an appendix to the permit system but one of its central problems.

The conditions that attach to every permit under Section 84 repay attention, because breach of a condition is the trigger for both the cancellation power in Section 86 and, in the case of use for a purpose the permit does not allow, the insurer's defence under Section 149(2). The holder must maintain the vehicle in accordance with the Act, must not use it in contravention of the permit or the Act, must comply with the conditions as to fares and freight where they are fixed, must carry and produce the permit and the certificate of fitness as required, and must keep to the route or area the permit specifies. Section 82 provides for the transfer of a permit on the death of the holder to the person succeeding to the possession of the vehicle, and with the authority's permission in other cases, and Section 83 for the replacement of a vehicle under an existing permit by another of the same nature. Section 85 prescribes the general form of permits, and sections 93 to 95 deal with the licensing of agents and canvassers in the sale of tickets for travel by public service vehicles, and with the state rule making power on stage and contract carriages. Section 94 bars the jurisdiction of civil courts over questions of the grant of permits that the Act commits to the transport authorities, completing the chapter's administrative closure.

The permit system's economic character should be stated plainly, since the Act's text assumes it. A permit for a remunerative route is a scarce entitlement created by the state, and the procedure of Sections 70 and 71, with publication of applications and hearing of representations, was the classic administrative law apparatus for distributing scarcity under a duty of fairness. The liberalisation the 1988 Act recorded did not abolish that apparatus; it narrowed the fields in which scarcity was policy, for goods carriages most of all, where national permits were intended to make authorisation close to automatic on payment of the composite fee. The tribunal of scarcity survived in passenger transport, where route permits on nationalised and other routes continue to be contested property, and the case law on the grant and renewal of stage carriage permits is correspondingly voluminous.

## State transport undertakings, the vehicle and the road: Chapters VI to VIII

Chapter VI, sections 97 to 108, preserves the place of the state as an operator. It provides that where a state transport undertaking proposes to run a road transport service on a route or in an area to the exclusion, complete or partial, of private operators, the undertaking prepares and publishes a scheme under Section 99, objections are heard, and the state government approves the scheme, whereupon permits are granted or refused in conformity with it and compensation is addressed under Section 105 for permits curtailed or refused renewal in consequence. Section 98 gives the chapter overriding effect. This nationalisation machinery was the inherited settlement of 1939, by which the public bus services of the states were built, and by 1988 it coexisted with the licensing liberalisation that the Act's statement of objects recorded for permits on routes outside the nationalised schemes. The chapter is retained here in outline because it explains the structure of Indian bus transport, in which the stage carriage permits described above operate in the spaces the schemes leave.

Chapter VII, sections 109 to 111, states the Centre's control over the object itself: every motor vehicle shall be so constructed and maintained as to be at all times under the effective control of the person driving it, the Centre may make rules on dimensions, equipment, safety glass, signalling devices, brakes and pollution control devices, and the state governments hold the residuary rule making power. Chapter VIII, sections 112 to 138, controls traffic in motion. Section 112 empowers the fixation of maximum and minimum speed limits, Section 113 the fixation of limits on weights and the prohibition of use in a public place while overloaded, Section 116 the erection of traffic signs by the state government or the authority it authorises, Section 117 the determination of parking places and halting stations, Section 118 the making of driving regulations, and Section 129, as enacted, the requirement that a person driving a motorcycle in a public place shall wear protective headgear conforming to the prescribed standards, with an exception the section stated for a person who is a Sikh wearing a turban. Section 134 imposes the duties that matter most in this pillar's third subject: where a person is injured or property damaged in an accident, the driver shall secure medical attention for the injured person unless impracticable for the reasons the section gives, and shall report the circumstances to the police. Section 136 provides for the inspection of a vehicle involved in an accident. These chapters are the regulatory background against which the compensation chapters operate: they define the standards of conduct and condition whose breach generates liability, and several of their violations are the same conduct that Chapter XIII criminalises.


The fitness regime that links the registration chapter and the construction chapter deserves separate notice, because it is the point at which the Act's concern with the machine is enforced in time rather than at manufacture. The certificate of fitness under Section 56 is issued after inspection by the prescribed authority or by an authorised testing station, a category the 1988 Act's objects expressly contemplated so that the inspection capacity of the state was not the bottleneck of the system, and it is granted for the period the rules prescribe, shorter for older vehicles, so that a transport vehicle's physical condition is re-certified at intervals across its life. A vehicle used without a subsisting certificate is, by the same section, treated as not validly registered, and its use also breaches the permit conditions drawn from Chapter V, so that a single lapse of maintenance exposes the operator to the registration consequence, the permit consequence and, in the event of an accident, an argument on the insurer's defences confined, as Section 149 requires, to such of them as the specified conditions cover. The construction standards of Section 109 and the Centre's rules on brakes, steering, tyres, lights, safety glass and emission devices complete the scheme from the other side, fixing the condition in which the vehicle must be kept, while the inspection regime verifies it. Neither half works without the other, and the pairing explains the Act's characteristic method in its regulatory chapters: a standard stated in the statute or the central rules, a state administered verification, and a consequence that operates on the document rather than only on the person.

The traffic chapter's detail rewards a closer reading than its position in the middle of the Act usually earns, because it is the chapter that writes the standard of the careful road user against which negligence is later judged. Section 112 empowers the state government and the authorities it notifies to fix speed limits by notification, and fixes the liability of a driver who exceeds them, while Section 113 addresses the physical dimension of the load, empowering limits on the weight carried and prohibiting the use of a vehicle or trailer that is loaded in breach. Section 115 empowers the state government to restrict the use of vehicles in specified areas or on specified roads for the purposes it states, including the safety of the public and the condition of a road or bridge, and Section 118 empowers the Centre to make driving regulations, the rules of the road that govern overtaking, junction conduct, signalling and the duties owed by and to other road users. Section 119 obliges every driver to obey traffic signs and the directions of a police officer regulating traffic, Section 121 requires signals to be given by the driver on the occasions it specifies, Section 122 prohibits leaving a vehicle in a dangerous position, Section 123 prohibits riding on the running board or the bonnet of a vehicle, Section 125 prohibits obstructing the driver, Section 126 requires a stationary truck to be secured against moving, Section 127 provides for the removal of vehicles abandoned or left unattended in a public place, Section 128 prohibits a second person on a two wheeled motorcycle except on a proper seat behind the rider, and Section 130 imposes the duty to produce the licence and the certificate of registration to a police officer in uniform on demand. Section 131 imposes a duty on the driver to take reasonable care at a level crossing, Section 132 obliges the driver to stop when required by the police or where an accident has occurred involving an animal in the circumstances stated, Section 133 obliges the owner to give information as to the identity of the driver where an offence or an accident is alleged, and Section 135 provides for schemes of research and investigation into the causes of accidents and for the production of a vehicle for examination. Breach of these provisions is not merely penal: in a claim under Chapter XII the breach of the traffic code is the standard material from which the tribunal infers negligence, and the provisions on speed, signs and position on the road are cited in awards with a frequency that makes Chapter VIII, in practice, the Act's definition of careless driving.

## Liability without fault: Chapter X

Chapter X, sections 140 to 144, is short, and in the enacted text it did one thing. Section 140 provides that where death or permanent disablement of any person has resulted from an accident arising out of the use of a motor vehicle, the owner of the vehicle shall, or the owners of the vehicles shall jointly and severally, be liable to pay compensation in respect of that death or disablement in accordance with the section. Section 140(2), as enacted, fixed the sums: twenty five thousand rupees in respect of the death of any person and twelve thousand rupees in respect of the permanent disablement of any person. Section 140(3) provides that the claimant shall not be required to plead and establish that the death or disablement was due to any wrongful act, neglect or default of the owner or of any other person, and Section 140(4) provides that the claim shall not be defeated by the wrongful act, neglect or default of the person who died or was disabled, nor the quantum reduced on the basis of that person's share of responsibility. Section 143 extends the provisions of the chapter to claims for compensation in respect of death or permanent disablement of the same nature made under the Workmen's Compensation Act 1923, so that the no fault floor is not lost merely because the claim travels under the employment statute, and Section 141, as noted, preserves the larger claim under any other law subject to adjustment of the sum paid under the chapter. Section 142 defines permanent disablement for the chapter by a list of injuries: permanent privation of sight or hearing, privation of any member or joint, destruction or permanent impairing of the powers of any member or joint, and permanent disfiguration of the head or face. Section 141 preserves the right to claim compensation under any other law, subject to adjustment of the no fault sum, and Section 144 gives the chapter overriding effect.

The character of the provision deserves exact statement, because it is routinely misdescribed in both directions. It is not a compensation scheme: the sums were modest even in 1989 values, and the section's function was to put a floor of immediate, incontestable relief under the bereaved or disabled household while the real claim, the fault based claim for just compensation under Chapter XII, was prepared and heard. Nor is it a mere interim payment in the procedural sense, because it requires no proof of any person's fault and cannot be defeated by the victim's own contribution to the accident, which no interim remedy in a fault claim could survive. It is a statutory acknowledgment that the use of motor vehicles imposes risks on the public for which the owner, and through Chapter XI the owner's insurer, answers without inquiry into blame. The fixed sums were raised by the amendment of 1994, with effect from 14 November 1994, to fifty thousand rupees for death and twenty five thousand rupees for permanent disablement, and the same amendment gave the chapter a companion that is dealt with below in the discussion of how compensation is calculated, because it changed the structure of claiming rather than the floor.

The design choice of a fixed sum without proof of fault is not unique to road transport in Indian statute law, and the comparison nearest to hand is the regime of [the Railways Act 1989 and passenger rights](/2020/08/15/railways-act-1989-passenger-rights/), where Parliament likewise attached compensation to an untoward incident on a no fault footing and left the scheduled sums to subordinate legislation. In both statutes the legislature preferred speed and certainty over individualised assessment for the first layer of relief, and in both the interesting questions of quantification were pushed upward, in the railway case to the schedule and in the motor case, as will appear, to the courts.

In practice the no fault chapter works alongside the fault claim rather than instead of it. A claimant files the application under Section 166 and claims the Section 140 amount within it, and the tribunal deals with the no fault liability at an early stage because it turns on proof of the accident, the death or the permanent disablement, and the insurance particulars, and on nothing else. The amount so paid is then adjusted against the final award, as Section 141 contemplates, so that the family receives the floor early and the balance when the fault claim is decided. The courts protected this working arrangement in two directions: they refused to let an insurer defeat the no fault payment by raising the defences of Section 149(2) that belong to the fault inquiry, and they refused to let the dismissal of a fault claim for want of proof of negligence extinguish a no fault entitlement that never depended on negligence. The chapter's small sums therefore do a job out of proportion to their size in the system's daily operation: they are the only money in the entire scheme that is designed to arrive before the argument begins.

The treatment of permanent disablement under Section 142 also shows the chapter's character. The definition proceeds by a closed list of grave injuries, loss of sight or hearing, loss of a member or joint, destruction or permanent impairment of the powers of a member or joint, and permanent disfiguration of the head or face, and it does not attempt any graduated scale of partial disablement. A claimant whose injury is serious but outside the list receives nothing under this chapter and is remitted to the fault claim, where disablement is assessed on evidence without a list. The boundary is crude and was drawn for speed: the chapter trades completeness for certainty, paying a fixed sum on a short list of unmistakable outcomes, and leaves every harder question to the tribunal's inquiry under Chapter XII.

## Insurance of motor vehicles: Chapter XI

Chapter XI, sections 145 to 164, converts the liabilities the Act creates into liabilities an insurer must stand behind. Its centre is Section 146: no person shall use, or cause or allow any other person to use, a motor vehicle in a public place unless there is in force in relation to the use of the vehicle a policy of insurance complying with the requirements of the chapter. The exceptions are narrow and institutional, for vehicles owned by governments and specified authorities where the section permits the risk to be carried otherwise. Driving or permitting use without such a policy is an offence under Section 196, and the insurance obligation is therefore not a term of private prudence but a condition of lawful use, enforced by the criminal law.

Section 147 prescribes what the policy must cover. It must be issued by an authorised insurer and must insure the person or classes of persons specified against liability incurred in respect of the death of or bodily injury to any person, including, subject to the section's structure, passengers in the situations it names, and damage to the property of a third party, caused by or arising out of the use of the vehicle in a public place. As enacted, the section limited the insurer's liability for damage to third party property to six thousand rupees, while liability for death and bodily injury carried no monetary ceiling in the policy the chapter required. The policy need not cover the liabilities the section enumerates as exclusions, including liability in respect of death or injury to employees arising out of and in the course of employment beyond the extent to which the Workmen's Compensation Act 1923 would impose it, and contractual liabilities. The distinction between the cover the chapter compels, habitually called third party or Act only cover, and the wider cover an owner may buy against damage to his own vehicle and other risks, is a distinction between statute and market: the chapter compels the former and is indifferent to the latter, except that what the chapter compels defines what a claimant can reach.

Section 149 is the chapter's enforcement provision and one of the most litigated sections in the Act. It obliges the insurer, where a judgment or award is obtained against a person insured under the chapter in respect of a liability the policy covers, to pay to the person entitled to the benefit of the decree the sums payable under it, notwithstanding that the insurer might in other circumstances be entitled to avoid or cancel the policy, provided the insurer had the notice of the proceedings that the section requires. Section 149(2) then states the only grounds on which the insurer may defend the action, and the enumeration is the point: a breach of a specified condition of the policy, namely conditions excluding use for hire or reward where the policy does not cover such use, use for organised racing and speed testing, use for a purpose not allowed by the permit where the vehicle is a transport vehicle, use without a side car attached where the vehicle is a motorcycle, or liability as excluded by the section's other heads; the condition excluding driving by a named person, by a person not duly licensed, or by a person disqualified during the period of disqualification; and the avoidance of the policy by material non disclosure or false representation. Section 150 preserves the rights of third parties against insurers on the insolvency of the insured, Section 155 addresses the effect of the insured's death on the cause of action, Section 156 the effect of the certificate of insurance, and Section 157 the transfer of the certificate with the vehicle, deeming the policy to move with a transfer of ownership so that the transferee is not uninsured by the transfer itself.

Sections 161 to 163 address the case in which the vehicle cannot be identified. Section 161 provides for compensation in hit and run motor accidents, in which the identity of the vehicle cannot be ascertained in spite of reasonable efforts, and Section 162 provided for a scheme for the payment of such compensation, administered through the General Insurance Corporation under the solatium arrangements the Act continued, with Section 163 requiring the owner or the insurer, where the identity later emerges or the case proves not to be a hit and run case, to support the refund mechanism the section prescribes. The detail of that scheme, and the figures attached to it, sit in the territory of the amendment article in this series, because the 2019 restructuring reworked these provisions; the architecture, a fund answering where no defendant can be found, was the 1988 Act's own.

Four features of the required cover complete the picture. The first is the position of passengers. Section 147 as enacted required the policy to cover death or bodily injury to any person, and the section's provisos and the tariff practice of the period produced prolonged litigation about persons carried in goods vehicles, who were neither fare paying passengers nor third parties on the road. The amendment of 1994 answered part of that dispute by expressly including the owner of the goods, or his authorised representative, carried in a goods vehicle within the required cover, and the remainder of the field was worked out by the courts on the distinction between the cover the statute compels and the wider cover a premium has actually bought. The second feature is the employee position: the required policy need cover liability for death or injury to an employee of the insured arising out of and in the course of employment only to the extent the Workmen's Compensation Act 1923 would impose on the employer, except that the section requires full cover for the driver of the vehicle and, in a public service vehicle, the conductor and the person examining tickets. The third feature is the certificate: Section 145 and the sections that follow build the scheme around the certificate of insurance, whose issue is the evidence that the statutory policy exists, whose production to the police and to a purchaser is provided for, and which Section 157 deems transferred with the vehicle so that the benefit and the burden of the policy follow the machine. The fourth feature is reciprocity and information: the chapter makes provision for vehicles coming from reciprocating countries and imposes duties, in sections 151 and 158 and their neighbours, to furnish information about insurance and to produce the certificate where an accident has occurred, so that the claimant's first practical question, who insures this vehicle, has a statutory route to an answer.

The duty in Section 149 is also conditioned by a notice discipline that protects the insurer's participation rather than the insured's convenience. The insurer is entitled to notice of the bringing of the proceedings through the court or the tribunal, and the section gives the insurer the right to be made a party and to defend on the enumerated grounds where that notice has been given. The design is a bargain: the insurer loses the general freedom to avoid the policy against the third party, and in exchange receives an assured place in the litigation and a closed list of defences, each of which goes to a matter the insured controlled: the use to which the vehicle was put, the person allowed to drive it, and the honesty of the proposal on which the policy was issued. A defence that succeeds does not end the claimant's journey, because of the recovery structure already described; it reallocates the loss, after payment, to the person whose breach created the defence.

### Can an insurer simply refuse to honour an award against its insured?

No. Section 149 obliges the insurer to satisfy a judgment against the insured on a liability the policy covers, and confines the insurer's defence to the grounds Section 149(2) enumerates. Where a ground is established, the statutory structure and the case law direct payment to the claimant with recovery from the insured, rather than refusal at the claimant's expense.

The enumerated defences repay itemisation, because their closed character is the section's whole point and because each corresponds to a failure the insured controlled. A policy condition may exclude use of the vehicle for hire or reward at a time when it is not covered for such use, for organised racing and speed testing, for a purpose not allowed by the vehicle's permit where the vehicle is a transport vehicle, or, in the case of a motorcycle, use without a side car attached where the policy is written on that footing; it may exclude driving by a person expressly named in the policy, by a person not duly licensed, or by a person disqualified from holding a licence and during the disqualification; and the insurer may avoid the policy itself where it was obtained by non disclosure of a material fact or by a representation of fact false in a material particular. Nothing else is a defence against the third party: not a breach of a condition as to the vehicle's repair or loading that is not within the specified conditions, not the driver's want of care, not the insured's failure to cooperate where the section's notice machinery has run, and not an exclusion clause drafted wider than the section permits. An insurer relying on a specified defence must prove it, and must also prove, in the licence cases, that the breach was fundamental and within the insured's control, a qualification the Supreme Court read into the section in the line of cases that produced the pay and recover settlement, so that a technical or involuntary breach does not move the loss from the insurer at all.

The doctrine that paragraph states in general terms is the pay and recover doctrine, and its history shows the courts completing the statute's design. The enacted Section 149 itself contained a limited version: in the situations it specified in sub sections (4) and (5), an insurer that had paid a claimant despite a defence founded on breach of a policy condition was entitled to recover the amount from the insured. The Supreme Court generalised the principle in National Insurance Company Limited v Swaran Singh, decided in 2004, holding that where the insurer proves a breach of a policy condition, including the classic case of a driver holding no effective licence, the tribunal may direct the insurer to pay the compensation to the claimant and recover it from the owner, so that the third party victim is not defeated by a dispute between insurer and insured to which he was a stranger. The doctrine is a genuine judicial innovation with a statutory root: it protects claimants, at the cost, which this article addresses in the counter reading below, of leaving the insurer to pursue an owner who is frequently insolvent.

Once the award is made, the chapter's concern is that it should turn into money. Section 149 obliges the insurer to deposit the amount payable under the judgment or award in the tribunal as required, and tribunal practice under Section 168 directs the insurer, owner or driver held liable to deposit the awarded sum within the period fixed, commonly thirty days from the award, with interest under Section 171 running on default in substance if not in form. Where the person liable does not deposit, Section 174's certificate to the Collector converts the award into a demand recoverable as an arrear of land revenue, attaching the coercive machinery of revenue recovery, attachment and sale of property, to a private law entitlement without a fresh suit. Sections 150 and 155 close the two remaining gaps in satisfaction: the claimant's rights survive the insured's insolvency, because the statute transfers the insured's rights against the insurer to the third party on insolvency, and they survive the insured's death, because the cause of action and the insurer's liability are preserved notwithstanding the death of the person insured. The chapters on insurance and tribunals were drafted, in other words, against the three classic ways a judgment becomes worthless, the defendant without assets, the defendant dissolved, and the defendant dead, and each has its answer in the text.

## The Claims Tribunals: Chapter XII

Chapter XII, sections 165 to 176, creates the forum. Section 165 empowers the state government to constitute, by notification, Motor Accidents Claims Tribunals for such areas as may be specified, for the purpose of adjudicating upon claims for compensation in respect of accidents involving the death of, or bodily injury to, persons arising out of the use of motor vehicles, or damage to the property of a third party so arising. The tribunal consists of a single member, who must be or have been a judge of a High Court, a district judge, or qualified for appointment as one, and the state may appoint additional members where the volume of work requires. The tribunal is therefore a judicial forum staffed by judicial officers, not an administrative board, and that choice of staffing explains both the quality of its output and the appellate structure that reviews it.

Section 166 governs the application. An application for compensation may be made by the person who has sustained the injury, by the owner of damaged property, by the legal representatives of a person who has died, or by an agent authorised by any of them, and Section 166(4) obliges the tribunal to treat a report of an accident forwarded to it by the police as an application for compensation. The application may be made to the tribunal having jurisdiction over the area in which the accident occurred or, at the claimant's option, to the tribunal within whose local limits the claimant resides or carries on business or the respondent resides. As enacted, Section 166(3) imposed a limitation period of six months from the occurrence of the accident, with a power in the tribunal to entertain a later application for sufficient cause up to a further six months; the amendment of 1994 deleted that limitation provision, so that the enacted text's strict window belongs to the history of the pillar and the application must still be made and pursued like any other claim, but without that particular statutory clock. The array of parties in a claim follows the scheme of the liability chapters. The application names the driver whose negligence is alleged, the owner of the vehicle, who answers for the use of the vehicle and for the driver's acts in its use, and the insurer with whom the vehicle was insured on the date of the accident, served with notice through the tribunal so that the insurer may take the defences Section 149 allows. Proof of the insurance particulars comes from the certificate of insurance and the registration record, which is one reason the documents listed in this article matter at the threshold: a claim in which the insurer is misidentified or the policy period misstated invites adjournments before the merits are reached. Where two or more vehicles are involved, the owners and insurers of each are impleaded and the tribunal apportions liability between them according to the negligence it finds, the claimant recovering the whole from any of those held liable in the proportions the award fixes. Where the vehicle was being driven by a person other than the owner, the owner's liability is the liability the Act fastens on the person who put the vehicle on the road, and the driver's separate liability in negligence runs beside it. The tribunal's award then specifies, as Section 168 requires, by whom the sum is payable, which in the ordinary insured case directs the insurer to deposit the whole, leaving the insurer and the insured to any recovery contest between themselves. Section 167 addresses overlap with the Workmen's Compensation Act 1923, requiring an election where compensation for death or injury is claimable under both statutes. Section 168 states the operative duty: on receipt of an application, the tribunal shall, after giving notice to the insurer and an opportunity of being heard to the parties, hold an inquiry and make an award determining the amount of compensation which appears to it to be just, specifying the person or persons to whom it is payable and the person by whom it is payable, whether insurer, owner or driver.

Procedure is deliberately lightened. Section 169 empowers the tribunal to follow such summary procedure as it thinks fit, clothes it with specified powers of a civil court for taking evidence on oath, enforcing attendance and compelling the production of documents, and deems it a civil court for the purposes the section names. Section 170 gives the insurer a controlled escape from the limited defences of Section 149(2): where the tribunal is satisfied that there is collusion between the claimant and the person against whom the claim is made, or that that person has failed to contest the claim, it may direct that the insurer be impleaded and, once impleaded, the insurer has the right to contest the claim on all or any of the grounds available to the person against whom the claim is made. Section 171 empowers the tribunal to award interest on the compensation from a date not earlier than the date of the claim, at a rate it considers fit. Section 173 provides an appeal to the High Court against the tribunal's award, subject to the conditions the section imposes, including the deposit requirement for an appellant against whom money is payable. Section 174 provides that where any amount is due from a person under an award, the tribunal may, on application, issue a certificate for the amount to the Collector, who shall recover it in the same manner as an arrear of land revenue, which is the Act's way of giving the award a public recovery engine without sending the claimant to execution proceedings. Section 175 bars the jurisdiction of civil courts over any question relating to a claim that a tribunal is empowered to determine, and Section 176 reserves the state governments' rule making power over the chapter's procedure.

The election in Section 167 deserves a fuller statement, because it governs a large class of workplace road deaths. Where a person is killed or injured in circumstances that would support both a claim before the tribunal and a claim under the Workmen's Compensation Act 1923, as where a driver or a conductor dies in the course of employment, the claimant must choose the forum, and compensation may not be recovered under both. The choice is consequential and effectively irreversible: the workmen's compensation route pays on a statutory scale tied to wages and age without proof of the employer's fault and ordinarily pays less, while the tribunal route pays just compensation on the judicial method but requires the negligence of a driver to be established, which, where the deceased was himself the driver at fault, may be unavailable against anyone but an insurer answerable on the policy. Advisers therefore examine, before electing, whose negligence can be proved and what the deceased's income was, since the higher the income the wider the gap between the two measures. The section is the point at which the Act acknowledges that its compensation scheme overlaps an older labour welfare scheme and resolves the overlap by election rather than by accumulation.

Three features of the tribunal's working deserve emphasis for a reader assessing the design. The first is the composition of the award. Section 168 requires the award to specify the person or persons to whom compensation is payable and the person by whom it is payable, and tribunal practice under the section apportions the award among dependants, records the shares of minors to be held in deposit until majority, and directs the insurer to deposit the amount within the period the award fixes, so that the award is a working instrument of distribution and not only a declaration of liability. The second is interest. Section 171 leaves the rate and the date from which interest runs to the tribunal's judgment, and awards habitually carry interest from the date of the application, which gives the respondent who delays a price for delay and partly compensates the claimant for the years the claim consumes; the rate is discretionary and is one of the points most often varied in appeal. The third is the appellate discipline in Section 173: an appellant against whom an amount is payable must, under the proviso to that section as enacted, deposit twenty five thousand rupees or fifty per cent of the amount awarded, whichever is less, before the appeal is entertained, a threshold that filters appeals filed for postponement while leaving the High Court's corrective jurisdiction over liability and computation intact.

The forum's accessibility is part of its statutory character rather than an accident of practice. The application is made on the prescribed form with a fee that the state rules fix at modest levels, the police report route in Section 166(4) converts the state's own record of the accident into a claim for a family that files nothing, the tribunal's summary procedure frees it from the full procedural code that governs a civil suit, and the bar in Section 175 prevents the same claim from being re-agitated in a civil court by a respondent seeking a slower forum. Set against that design is the reality addressed later in this article, that contested claims still take years; the distance between the design and the outcome is produced by the volume of claims, by the contest on income and disability that the calculation method invites, and by appeals, and not by any defect in the forum's formal accessibility.

The pattern of committing a defined class of money claims to a specialised forum, and barring the ordinary courts, is one this series has examined in other fields, and it is instructive to compare the design choice with the one made for homebuyers, examined in [the comparison of the real estate regulator and the consumer forum](/2019/02/15/rera-vs-consumer-protection-homebuyers/): in both fields the legislature judged that a generalist forum had failed a defined class of claimant, and in both the displacement of the civil courts is the measure of that judgment. For accident claimants, the forum question has a second layer, because free and competent legal help for a person entitled to it is itself a statutory service, and a claimant who cannot afford representation may approach the legal services institutions whose framework [the article on the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987](/2021/06/15/legal-services-authorities-act-1987/) explains, rather than face the insurer unrepresented. A family assembling a claim will ordinarily need the registration certificate, the insurance policy, the first information report and the documents generated by it, the post mortem report or the medical records and disability evidence, and the proof of the income of the person who has died or been injured, and it should keep the complete set, including the presentation files and scanned schedules in which insurers and hospitals circulate such records, in one ordered file, for which [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) is a practical tool. The documents are not a formality: on them, as the next section explains, the size of the award turns.


## The statute created the forum and the courts created the sum

Section 168 directs the tribunal to award the compensation that appears to it to be just, and neither the section nor any other part of the Act says how a monetary value is to be placed on a lost life or a ruined body. In death cases, the economic core of the claim is dependency: the support the deceased would have provided to the household over the remainder of a working life. To value that support, a tribunal needs the deceased's income, an allowance for the expenses the deceased would have consumed personally, an allowance for the growth the income would have shown, and a period over which the support would have run, shortened to reflect that a lump sum paid at once can itself earn returns. Every one of those choices is a potential source of divergence between benches, and for the first two decades of the Act's operation the tribunals of India diverged on all of them, producing awards for materially identical losses that differed several times over between districts. The standardisation of the method, component by component, is the work of the Supreme Court.

The multiplier as a device was taken up and disciplined first. In Uttar Pradesh State Road Transport Corporation v Trilok Chandra, decided in 1996, the Court worked with the multiplier method under which the annual dependency is multiplied by a figure chosen by reference to the age of the deceased, and it corrected the errors by which tribunals had applied multipliers drawn from unsuitable comparators, holding that the multiplier could not exceed eighteen. In Sarla Verma v Delhi Transport Corporation, decided in 2009, a bench of the Court systematised the whole computation. Sarla Verma prescribed the deduction for the personal and living expenses of the deceased by reference to the household: where the deceased was married, one third where the number of dependent family members is two to three, one fourth where the number is four to six, and one fifth where the number exceeds six, while for a bachelor the deduction is one half, subject to the qualifications the judgment stated for bachelors supporting parents and siblings. Sarla Verma also fixed the multiplier by age bands, running from eighteen for the youngest working ages through sixteen for a deceased aged thirty one to thirty five, fifteen for thirty six to forty, thirteen for forty six to fifty, eleven for fifty one to fifty five, nine for fifty six to sixty, seven for sixty one to sixty five, and five above sixty five, and it confined the addition for future prospects, as the judgment then stood, by reference to the nature of the deceased's employment.

Two components remained contested. The first was future prospects for those outside permanent employment: in Rajesh v Rajbir Singh, decided in 2013, a bench gave the addition generously to the self employed and those on fixed wages, and for the conventional heads awarded sums materially above the older scale, while in Reshma Kumari v Madan Mohan, decided in the same period, a larger bench took a more confined view and referred the conflict for authoritative settlement. The second contested component was the conventional heads themselves, the standard sums for loss of estate, loss of consortium and funeral expenses, which older practice had kept at token levels. The reference produced the Constitution Bench decision in National Insurance Company Limited v Pranay Sethi, delivered on 31 October 2017. Pranay Sethi settled the computation on the following terms. In determining income, an addition for future prospects shall be made of fifty per cent of the actual salary where the deceased had a permanent job and was below forty years of age, thirty per cent where the deceased was between forty and fifty, and fifteen per cent where the deceased was between fifty and sixty, with actual salary read as salary less tax. Where the deceased was self employed or on a fixed salary, the addition is forty per cent below forty years, twenty five per cent between forty and fifty, and ten per cent between fifty and sixty, with established income read as income less tax, and no addition beyond sixty in either case. The conventional heads were standardised at fifteen thousand rupees for loss of estate, forty thousand rupees for loss of consortium and fifteen thousand rupees for funeral expenses, with the amounts to be enhanced by ten per cent every three years. Deduction for personal expenses and the selection of the multiplier were to follow Sarla Verma. In a subsequent decision, Magma General Insurance Company Limited v Nanu Ram, decided in 2018, the Court held that loss of consortium is not confined to the spouse: a parent who loses a child and a child who loses a parent may each be awarded parental and filial consortium, so that the conventional head answers the relationship lost and not only the marital one.

The burden of proof in this jurisdiction matches its purpose. A claim under Section 166 is not a prosecution: the claimant must establish the involvement of the vehicle, the negligence or rashness that grounds fault, and the quantum, on the preponderance of probabilities, and the Supreme Court in Bimla Devi v Himachal Road Transport Corporation, decided in 2009, held that the tribunal is not to apply the standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt, that claimants cannot be expected to prove a road accident with mathematical precision, and that the tribunal should take a holistic view of the evidence, including the police record. Contributory negligence, where proved, reduces the award in proportion to the deceased's or the claimant's share in the fault, which stands in deliberate contrast with the no fault chapter, where Section 140(4) forbids any such reduction. In injury cases the same architecture applies with different content: the tribunal assesses the medical expenses proved, the loss of earnings during treatment, the loss of future earning capacity by reference to the functional disability the medical evidence establishes rather than the bare percentage in a certificate, and compensation for pain, suffering and loss of amenities, and the multiplier method is applied to the annual loss so found. The point of substance for the reader is that none of this law is in the statute book. It is in the law reports, it is binding on every tribunal, and it is the reason two claims with the same facts documents are argued, in practice, as arguments about age bands, employment character and deduction fractions under four decisions.

The standardisation achieved by this sequence should not be mistaken for unanimity at every step, and the contested points are part of the record. Reshma Kumari v Madan Mohan, decided in 2013 by a bench of three judges, accepted the Sarla Verma multiplier table and confined the addition for future prospects to cases in which the deceased had a permanent and stable income, and it was the tension between that confinement and the generosity of Rajesh v Rajbir Singh, decided in the same year, that made the Constitution Bench reference necessary. Pranay Sethi resolved the tension in favour of extension, holding that the principle of standardisation, once accepted for the multiplier and the deduction, could not rationally stop at the salaried, and that a self employed person's income is as capable of growth as a salary. The dissenting strain in the litigation, pressed by insurers, was that standardisation by judgment removes the flexibility a discretionary standard requires and converts rules of thumb into a tariff the legislature never enacted. The Constitution Bench answered that a standard of just compensation applied by hundreds of tribunals without a method had produced the very inequality the standard existed to prevent, and that certainty in the components is what makes settlement possible in the great majority of cases that never reach a hearing. That exchange is the deepest statement available of what the courts did in this field and why: they treated the absence of a statutory method not as a delegation of discretion to each tribunal but as a gap that precedent was required to fill uniformly.

Apportionment within the award follows the same preference for structure. Where the deceased leaves a widow, children and parents, the tribunal apportions the dependency among them by reference to their dependence on the deceased, protects the shares of minors by directing deposit until they attain majority, and treats the amounts under the conventional heads as attached to the relationships they name, consortium to the spouse and, after Magma, to parents and children, and loss of estate to the estate. Interest under Section 171 runs on the whole and is the tribunal's instrument for the period between application and deposit. None of these directions is in the Act either: they are the working law of the tribunal, settled by appellate practice, and a claimant reading only Chapter XII would not suspect their existence.

Injury claims use the same architecture with a different centre of gravity. Where the claimant survives, the pecuniary losses are the expenses of treatment proved by bills and records, the earnings lost during the period of treatment and recovery, and, where the injury leaves a lasting impairment, the loss of future earning capacity. The decisive assessment is the third. A medical board or a treating doctor certifies a percentage of disability, but that percentage describes the body, whereas the loss the Act compensates is the loss of the capacity to earn, and the courts have repeatedly directed tribunals to translate the medical percentage into a functional disability by reference to the claimant's occupation: a given impairment of a limb may end the earning life of a driver or a manual worker and barely touch the earnings of a person whose work is sedentary. The annual loss so found is then capitalised with the multiplier for the claimant's age, with future prospects added on the Pranay Sethi principles, and the non pecuniary heads, pain and suffering, loss of amenities of life, and the expenses of future treatment and attendance where proved, are awarded in addition. The discipline is identical in form to the death computation and different in its evidence, which is why injury awards vary more widely between tribunals than death awards do.

Two recurrent questions of the death computation should also be recorded, because they account for a large share of the appeals. The first is the income of a deceased who left no documents: the self employed worker, the daily wage earner, the homemaker. The tribunal must fix a notional income on such material as exists, the minimum wages notified for the relevant class of work in the relevant state being the most common anchor, and the courts have corrected assessments that valued a homemaker's services at trivial sums, holding that the work of managing a household and raising children is economic contribution to be valued with the same seriousness as wage labour. The second is the selection between the age of the deceased and the age of the claimants for the multiplier where the claimants are elderly parents: Sarla Verma adopted the age of the deceased as the basis, Pranay Sethi endorsed that basis, and the older practice of choosing by the claimants' age where it produced the lower figure was displaced. A reader who understands these two points, and the table that follows, understands the ground on which almost every contested award is fought.

## How the sum is built

The table below assembles the components of a death award in the order in which a tribunal constructs it, on a worked example: a deceased aged thirty five, in a permanent job, with an established annual income of six lakh rupees after tax, survived by a spouse and two children, so that the dependent family members number three. The example is illustrative of method only: every figure in an actual award must be proved or established on the evidence in that claim.

| Component | Rule that supplies it | Worked figure |
|---|---|---|
| Established annual income | Income as proved, less tax, per Sarla Verma and Pranay Sethi | 600000 rupees |
| Addition for future prospects | 50 per cent, permanent job and below 40 years, Pranay Sethi | 900000 rupees after addition |
| Deduction for personal and living expenses | One third, dependent family members two to three, Sarla Verma | 600000 rupees annual dependency |
| Multiplier | 16, for age 31 to 35 years, Sarla Verma table | 600000 multiplied by 16 |
| Loss of dependency | Multiplicand multiplied by multiplier, Section 168 just compensation | 9600000 rupees |
| Loss of estate | Conventional head standardised by Pranay Sethi | 15000 rupees |
| Loss of consortium | Conventional head standardised by Pranay Sethi, extended by Magma | 40000 rupees |
| Funeral expenses | Conventional head standardised by Pranay Sethi | 15000 rupees |
| Total before interest | Sum of dependency and conventional heads, interest under Section 171 additional | 9670000 rupees |

Three cautions attach to the table. The first is that the conventional head figures are the amounts standardised in 2017, subject to the enhancement of ten per cent every three years that Pranay Sethi directed, so the figures in a later award are the standardised figures as enhanced for the period. The second is that the multiplier is selected by the age of the deceased, not of the claimants, following the basis Sarla Verma prescribed and Pranay Sethi endorsed. The third is that the worked income is deliberately round and proved income in real claims is the single most contested element, particularly for the self employed, the informally employed and homemakers, where tribunals must fix a notional income on the material available and appellate courts have repeatedly intervened to correct assessments that priced a household's lost labour at negligible values.

## Offences and penalties: Chapter XIII, as enacted

Chapter XIII, sections 177 to 210, supplies the penal backing of the code, and it is the chapter on which later amendment has fallen most heavily, so the figures in this section are the figures of the Act as enacted in 1988 and no others. Section 177 is the general penalty clause: whoever contravenes any provision of the Act, or of any rule, regulation or notification made under it, for which no penalty is separately provided, shall be punishable for a first offence with a fine which may extend to one hundred rupees, and for a second or subsequent offence with a fine which may extend to three hundred rupees. The smallness of the figure is itself information: the enacted Act's general clause was inherited from an era of modest fines, and specific offences carried specific figures above it.

The driving offences cluster at the chapter's head. Section 180 penalises permitting a vehicle to be driven by a person disqualified or unlicensed, Section 181 penalises driving in contravention of Section 3 or Section 4, Section 183 penalises driving at a speed exceeding the maximum allowed, Section 184 penalises driving dangerously, for a first offence with imprisonment which may extend to six months or with a fine which may extend to one thousand rupees or with both under the enacted text, and Section 185 penalises driving under the influence of drink or drugs, where the alcohol in the blood exceeds the limit the section prescribes, for a first offence with imprisonment which may extend to six months or a fine which may extend to two thousand rupees or both, and for a second offence committed within three years with imprisonment which may extend to two years or a fine of three thousand rupees or both. Section 187 penalises offences in relation to an accident, including failure of the duties Section 134 imposes, and Section 190 penalises the use of a vehicle in an unsafe condition. Section 192 penalises the use of a vehicle without registration, Section 194 the carrying of loads in excess of the permitted weight, with a fine structure that adds an amount for every tonne of excess load, and Section 196 penalises driving a vehicle without the insurance Section 146 requires, with imprisonment which may extend to three months or a fine which may extend to one thousand rupees or both, as enacted. Section 199 addresses offences by companies, Section 200 empowers the composition of specified offences by the officers and on the terms the section and the state notifications prescribe, Section 206 empowers a police officer in uniform to take charge of a licence in the circumstances specified, and Section 207 empowers authorised officers to detain a vehicle used without a registration certificate or permit, or in contravention of permit conditions, until the contravention is remedied as the section provides.

The remainder of the chapter is machinery for enforcement, and it should be noticed because it shapes behaviour at the roadside. Section 178 penalises travelling in a stage carriage without a proper pass or ticket and the contravention of its conditions, Section 179 penalises disobedience of orders and obstruction of authorised persons and refusal to give information, Section 186 penalises driving while mentally or physically unfit, Section 188 penalises the abetment of the speeding and dangerous driving offences, Section 189 penalises racing and trials of speed without the written consent of the state government, Section 197 penalises taking and driving a vehicle without the owner's authority, and Section 198 penalises unauthorised interference with a stationary vehicle. Section 202 empowers a police officer in uniform to arrest without warrant a person who commits specified offences in his presence where the name and address cannot be ascertained or the person refuses to give them, Section 203 provides for breath tests for alcohol, Section 204 for laboratory tests, and Section 205 creates a presumption of unfitness to drive in the circumstances it states. Section 209 restricts the institution of prosecutions for certain offences to complaints by the officers and authorities the section names, and Section 210 requires courts sentencing a person for an offence under the Act to state in writing the reasons where the minimum sentence, where one is prescribed, is not imposed. Two procedural provisions complete the enforcement picture. Section 206 empowers a police officer in uniform, where an offence under the Act has been committed in his presence and the officer has reason to believe the person will not appear to answer a summons, to take charge of the licence held by that person and forward it to the court, the licence being returned when the matter is disposed of; the same section and Section 207, already noticed, extend the power of detention to the vehicle itself where it is used in the grave transport offences those sections specify. Section 208 provides for the summary disposal of cases in which the accused pleads guilty: the court may accept the plea transmitted in writing, convict on it and pass sentence in the accused's absence, sparing the driver of a minor offence a court attendance across distances. Read together, sections 200, 206, 207 and 208 reveal the chapter's administrative realism. The offences are numerous, mostly minor, and committed by a mobile population that a summons may never reach; the Act therefore arms the enforcing officer with the document and the vehicle as security for the process, offers composition for the lesser defaults, and offers a postal guilty plea for the rest, reserving the full trial for the offences, dangerous driving, drunken driving and the accident offences, where the public interest in adjudication is real.

Compounding under Section 200 is the chapter's settlement valve. The offences the section specifies, which are the lesser regulatory offences rather than the dangerous and drunken driving core, may be compounded by such officers or authorities and for such amount as the state government may by notification direct, either before or after the institution of the prosecution, and where an offence is compounded no further proceeding shall be taken against the person in respect of it, and a vehicle seized in connection with it is released on the terms the section provides. The design preference is visible: the chapter's everyday breaches, the documentary and regulatory defaults that make up the bulk of roadside enforcement, are to be closed at the roadside or the office for a composition amount, reserving prosecution for the conduct that endangers life. The composition amounts, like the fines, were set in the enacted scheme at levels the later amendment substantially raised, and they too belong to the figures a reader must take from the text of the period rather than from any later statement of penalties.

The chapter thus combined low fines with meaningful coercive powers over the document and the vehicle, a combination characteristic of the period: the state's grip lay in the licence, the certificate and the power to detain, rather than in the size of the fine.

Two observations place the chapter within the pillar's argument. The first is that the penal chapter and the compensation chapters pursue different objects and do not substitute for one another: a prosecution under Section 185 punishes the driver and compensates nobody, and a claim under Section 166 compensates the household and punishes nobody, and the same accident routinely generates both, in different forums, under different standards of proof, without either proceeding determining the other. The second is that the enacted penalty levels were the ones Parliament thought deterrent in 1988, and the scale of their replacement by the 2019 amendment, which substituted substantially higher figures across the chapter and added offences the 1988 text did not contain, is the measure of how far the policy assessment of deterrence moved in thirty years. That substitution is the amendment article's subject and is not reproduced here, because a pillar that imported the later figures would misdescribe the text it exists to explain.

## The option added in 1994: compensation by formula

The amendment of 1994, with effect from 14 November 1994, inserted into Chapter X a provision the enacted text did not contain, and the insertion changed the menu of claiming. Section 163A provides a special scheme of compensation on a structured formula basis: the owner of the motor vehicle or the authorised insurer shall be liable to pay, in the case of death or grievous hurt due to an accident arising out of the use of the vehicle, compensation as indicated in the Second Schedule to the legal heirs or the victim, as the case may be, and a claimant who chooses this route shall not be required to plead or establish that the death or grievous hurt was due to any wrongful act or neglect or default of the owner or of any other person. The Second Schedule supplies the formula: it proceeds on the deceased's annual income, applies a multiplier graded by age on a scale running higher than the judicial table, deducts one third for the expenses the deceased would have spent on himself, and adds the conventional sums the Schedule names for funeral expenses, loss of consortium, loss of estate and medical expenses. Section 163B gives the claimant the option to file the claim under Section 140, under Section 163A or under Section 166, but not under more than one of them in the end, so the formula route is an election against the fault route and the no fault route alike.

The formula did not displace the judicial method, for reasons the Supreme Court itself exposed. In Deepal Girishbhai Soni v United India Insurance Company Limited, decided in 2004, the Court treated Section 163A as a self contained code creating a distinct remedy based on a pre structured formula, available without proof of fault, and not as an interim stage of a Section 166 claim. Claimants still found the Schedule's internal arithmetic difficult: its table, read on its own terms, produced results that varied erratically with age and income, and in the Trilok Chandra line of reasoning and in later decisions the courts observed that the Schedule's multiplier table contained errors, so that in practice the formula route served claimants whose losses were modest or whose proof of fault was doubtful, while the fully argued fault claim under Section 166, valued by the Sarla Verma and Pranay Sethi method, served the larger losses. The 2019 restructuring of these provisions, which withdrew the formula and substituted fixed compensation amounts in a new arrangement, belongs to the amendment article; the pillar records the 1994 option because for twenty five years it was part of the claiming landscape and because its failure to standardise what it promised is the strongest evidence that the standardisation this field needed had to come, as it came, from the courts.

### Why did the statutory formula not replace the judicial method?

Because the Second Schedule's own table was defective and produced inconsistent results across age and income, as the courts repeatedly observed. Claimants with larger losses did better under the fault claim valued by the judicial method, and claimants used the formula mainly where proof of fault was doubtful or the loss was small.

The smallest part of the tribunal's docket, and the part that explains its design most plainly, is the claim for damage to property. Section 165 extends the tribunal's remit to damage to the property of a third party arising out of the use of a motor vehicle, and Section 166 gives the owner of the property the application. The claim is for the cost of repair or the value of what was destroyed, proved by invoices and assessment, and it carries none of the apparatus of the death computation. Its significance is structural: Parliament judged that even a dented boundary wall or a destroyed cargo should be recoverable in the same summary forum against the same insured fund, rather than by a civil suit against a driver, and the compulsory policy's property head, capped in the enacted text at six thousand rupees, marks the limit of the fund's answerability for such claims, the excess remaining a personal liability of the owner. A property claim above that figure therefore teaches the shape of the whole scheme in miniature: a forum designed for speed, a liability channelled to an insurer, a statutory ceiling at the point where Parliament thought the spread of loss should stop, and a residue left on the wrongdoer personally.

## The standard the tribunal applies: just compensation

Section 168's operative words, that the tribunal shall determine the amount of compensation which appears to it to be just, were carried into the 1988 Act from Section 110B of the 1939 Act, and the phrase had already accumulated a jurisprudence before consolidation. The courts read the standard as neither the narrow indemnity of the law of tort measured with forensic parsimony nor an open bounty: in the line of authority running through the decisions of the 1970s and 1980s, the Supreme Court repeatedly held that compensation in fatal accident cases must be just in the sense of being adequate to the loss of the dependants, assessed with sympathy for the claimant's position but on evidence, and that the tribunal is not bound by the pleadings' arithmetic where the material on record supports a higher or lower sum. Two practical consequences flow from that reading and survive into the standardised era. The first is that the tribunal may award more under a head than the application claimed, where the evidence establishes it, because the duty is the tribunal's and is not exhausted by the claimant's drafting. The second is that the standardisation of components did not displace the standard: Sarla Verma and Pranay Sethi prescribe the method by which the standard is ordinarily met, and the residual discretion of Section 168 remains for the features of a case the method does not capture, of which the treatment of a deceased who was the family's only earner at an advanced age, or of dependants with disabilities of their own, are standing examples. The statute's single word therefore carries both the rule and its safety valve, which is why the pillar treats the judicial method as construction of the section rather than as legislation beside it.

## The two sides of the compensation settlement, and the delay between them

The settlement this article has described is not costless, and the stance of this series requires the two positions on it to be stated at strength. The insurers' position begins from the structure of the product. Third party cover is compulsory, its pricing has been a regulated tariff matter for most of the Act's life, and the insurer cannot select its risks: every vehicle that takes a policy must be covered on the regulated terms, and the liabilities that follow are fixed not by the contract but by the tribunals applying a judicial method whose components have moved in one direction. The addition of future prospects for every class of earner, the extension of consortium beyond the spouse, and the standardisation of conventional heads at levels above the older token sums each enlarged awards, and insurers point out that the motor third party segment has run at a loss for years, with claim ratios persistently above the premium collected, so that the cost of the settlement is carried by the pool of policyholders and, where the tariff lags the awards, by the insurer's solvency margins. On this view, articulated across the industry's representations to the regulator and in litigation over the tariff, the system's tension cannot be removed by exhortation: either premiums follow awards upward, or the promise the statute makes to claimants is financed by eroding the companies expected to keep it.

The claimants' position answers on the same ground. An award is not a windfall: it is the capitalised value of support a household has lost for the whole of a working life, and the older practice, which priced that loss with parsimonious multipliers, no addition for the growth of earnings, and token sums for the loss of a spouse's or a parent's society, systematically undercompensated precisely the households least able to bear it, the young earner whose income would have risen, the self employed worker whose earnings no salary slip recorded, and the homemaker whose work no market priced. Future prospects and the conventional heads are, on this view, not generosity but correction: they align the award with the loss the household actually sustained, and the fact that awards rose when the corrections were made measures the size of the earlier shortfall rather than any excess in the settlement. The tension between the positions is real, and it is managed, where it is managed, through the premium and the tariff rather than through the award, because the statute fixes the claimant's entitlement and leaves the price of the promise to be settled between the industry and its regulator.

The premium side of that settlement deserves the same precision. For most of the Act's life the third party premium was fixed under a tariff administered first by the Tariff Advisory Committee and then, after the insurance regulator was created, by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority through notified premium orders, revised from time to time by vehicle class. A tariff priced below the claims cost is not a subsidy from the insurer in any lasting sense: it is a transfer among policyholders and across years, and its correction arrives as steep premium revisions that fall on every vehicle owner, including the prudent. Insurers make the further point that the tribunal method prices the individual claim with care while the tariff prices the class in aggregate, so the system contains no point at which the individual award is tested against the pool that funds it. Claimants answer that the pool is the statutory design, that Parliament chose insurance precisely so that losses would be spread rather than calibrated to each defendant's means, and that the industry's remedy for an inadequate tariff lies in the tariff process and in the management of fraud and inflated claims, which both sides agree impose real costs on the pool, rather than in resisting the method by which genuine losses are measured. The disagreement is therefore narrower than the rhetoric around it suggests: it is about the speed at which premiums may follow awards, and about who carries the interval.

Between entitlement and payment stands delay, and honesty requires it to be recorded. The tribunal's procedure is summary in design, but claims contest the involvement of the vehicle, the negligence, the income and the disability in sequence, insurers exercise the appeal Section 173 gives them, and a claim that is fought can take years from accident to realisation, during which the household that the no fault sum was meant to tide over is sustained by very little. The responses the system has built are administrative and settlement based rather than statutory: pre litigation and tribunal annexed mediation, the settlement of claims at Lok Adalats where insurers place batches of cases for negotiated disposal on standardised terms, and the duty the police report route in Section 166(4) performs in starting claims for families who never reach a lawyer. None of these alters the governing law, and none amounts to a guarantee of speed; they are the working compromise between a method of calculation elaborate enough to be fair and a claimant population that cannot wait for perfection.

One interface remains to be stated, because families encounter it at once and the Act does not mention it. A death on the road ordinarily generates proceedings outside this Act altogether: an investigation and prosecution for rash and negligent driving and for causing death by negligence under the general criminal law, in which the state is the prosecutor and the standard of proof is the criminal standard. The tribunal claim and the prosecution run on their own tracks. The criminal court's conclusion does not bind the tribunal, which decides involvement and negligence for itself on the civil standard, and an acquittal in the prosecution, which establishes only that guilt was not proved beyond reasonable doubt, is not an answer to the claim. The police papers generated by the criminal process, the first information report, the charge sheet, the site plan and the mechanical inspection report, form the evidential foundation on which most claims are built, and the tribunal treats them, as Bimla Devi directs, as material to be weighed in the whole and not as conclusions that decide the claim. A family therefore faces two proceedings arising from the same event, in two forums, answering two different questions, and the Act's silence about the criminal track should not be read as its absence from the family's experience.

That is the state of the settlement the 1988 Act produced. Parliament supplied a tribunal, an insurance compulsion, a floor of liability without fault, and a standard expressed in one word, just. It did not supply the arithmetic. The arithmetic came from the courts, in a sequence that runs from Trilok Chandra through Sarla Verma to the Constitution Bench in Pranay Sethi, and it is as fixed and as citable as any schedule, with the distinctive feature that its authority is precedent rather than enactment. The series has repeatedly found the operative content of a statute located outside the statute, in rules, in notifications and in practice. Here the operative content sits outside the statute and outside the rules, in a line of decisions, which is a distinct and under noticed location for the law that governs a very large number of claims, and it is the reason this pillar has spent more words on four judgments than on any chapter of the Act it exists to explain. This article describes the law; it is not advice on any person's claim, and a person with a claim should consult a lawyer or a legal services authority.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Motor Vehicles Act 1988 govern?

The Act governs the licensing of drivers and conductors, the registration of motor vehicles, the control of transport vehicles through permits, standards for construction, equipment and traffic, compulsory third party insurance, liability for road accidents and the tribunals that decide accident claims. It is Act 59 of 1988, in force from 1 July 1989, and it replaced the Motor Vehicles Act 1939. Its three functions are entry control over drivers and vehicles, regulation of conduct and condition on the road, and allocation of the cost of accidents through insurance and compensation.

### Q: What licences and permits does the Motor Vehicles Act require?

A driver must hold a driving licence for the class of vehicle driven, granted after a learner's licence stage, and a conductor of a stage carriage must hold a conductor's licence. Every vehicle must be registered and carry its registration mark, and a transport vehicle must in addition hold a fitness certificate. A transport vehicle used to carry passengers or goods for hire or reward must be covered by a permit, of which the principal kinds are the stage carriage, contract carriage, goods carriage and national permits.

### Q: Is third party insurance compulsory?

Yes. Section 146 provides that no person shall use, or allow another to use, a motor vehicle in a public place unless there is in force a policy of insurance complying with Chapter XI. The policy must cover liability for death or bodily injury to third parties and, within the limit the Act as enacted prescribed, damage to third party property. Using a vehicle without such a policy is an offence under Section 196. Wider cover for the owner's own vehicle is a matter of contract, not of the Act's compulsion.

### Q: How does a motor accident claim work?

The injured person, the owner of damaged property, or the legal representatives of a person who has died apply under Section 166 to the Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal for the area of the accident or, at the claimant's option, for the area where the claimant or the respondent resides. The tribunal gives notice to the insurer, holds an inquiry under its summary procedure and makes an award of just compensation under Section 168. Fault must be established on the preponderance of probabilities, and the insurer satisfies the award subject to the defences and the recovery rights the Act gives it.

### Q: How is motor accident compensation for a death calculated?

The tribunal establishes the deceased's income after tax, adds a percentage for future prospects at the rate the Constitution Bench in Pranay Sethi fixed for the deceased's age and employment type, deducts the fraction for personal and living expenses that Sarla Verma prescribed by the number of dependants, and multiplies the result by the multiplier Sarla Verma's table assigns to the deceased's age. Standard sums for loss of estate, loss of consortium and funeral expenses are then added, together with interest under Section 171.

### Q: What defences can an insurer raise?

Only those Section 149(2) enumerates: breach of a specified policy condition, such as use for a purpose the policy or the permit does not allow, or driving by a person named or specified in the condition, a person not duly licensed, or a person disqualified, and avoidance of the policy for material non disclosure or false representation. The insurer cannot refuse to satisfy an award on other grounds. Where the tribunal finds collusion or a failure to contest, Section 170 lets the insurer contest on every ground open to the insured.

### Q: What does a Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal decide?

It adjudicates claims for compensation in respect of accidents involving death or bodily injury to persons, or damage to the property of a third party, arising out of the use of motor vehicles. Constituted by the state government under Section 165 and presided over by a person of district judge rank or qualification, it decides who is liable, insurer, owner or driver, and what sum is just compensation, and its award is recoverable as an arrear of land revenue and appealable to the High Court under Section 173.

### Q: What offences and penalties does the Motor Vehicles Act create?

Chapter XIII penalises, among other conduct, driving without a licence or in breach of age conditions, driving at excessive speed, dangerous driving, driving under the influence of drink or drugs, using a vehicle without registration, overloading, and driving uninsured, with a general penalty clause in Section 177 for contraventions that no specific penalty covers. The figures stated in this article are those of the Act as enacted in 1988; the penalties were substantially revised by the amendment of 2019, which the amendment article in this series owns.

### Q: Where can a motor accident claim be filed, and who may file it?

Section 166 allows the injured person, the owner of damaged property, the legal representatives of a deceased person, or an authorised agent to apply. The application may be filed before the tribunal for the area where the accident occurred or, at the claimant's choice, where the claimant resides or carries on business, or where the respondent resides. A police report of the accident forwarded to the tribunal must be treated by it as an application for compensation under Section 166(4).

### Q: What is the multiplier method used to value a death claim?

The multiplier method values lost dependency in two steps. The tribunal first finds the annual dependency: the deceased's income after tax, increased by the fixed addition for future prospects and reduced by the deduction for personal and living expenses. It then multiplies that annual figure by a multiplier chosen from the table in Sarla Verma by the deceased's age, which converts a stream of future support into a fair lump sum that accounts for the earning power of money paid at once.

### Q: What did the Constitution Bench decide in Pranay Sethi?

In National Insurance Company Limited v Pranay Sethi, decided in 2017, a Constitution Bench standardised the calculation of death compensation. It fixed the addition for future prospects at fifty, thirty and fifteen per cent for a deceased in a permanent job in the age bands below forty, forty to fifty and fifty to sixty, and at forty, twenty five and ten per cent for the self employed or those on fixed salary. It standardised loss of estate at fifteen thousand rupees, loss of consortium at forty thousand rupees and funeral expenses at fifteen thousand rupees, rising by ten per cent every three years.

### Q: What is the pay and recover principle in motor insurance?

Pay and recover is the direction by which a tribunal orders the insurer to pay the compensation to the claimant even where the insurer has proved a breach of a policy condition, such as the driver holding no effective licence, and then to recover the amount from the insured owner. Section 149 contained a limited statutory version, and the Supreme Court generalised the principle in National Insurance Company Limited v Swaran Singh in 2004. Its purpose is that a dispute between insurer and insured should not defeat the third party the compulsory policy exists to protect.

### Q: What standard of proof applies before a Claims Tribunal?

The civil standard, the preponderance of probabilities, and not the criminal standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt. In Bimla Devi v Himachal Road Transport Corporation the Supreme Court held that claimants cannot be expected to prove a road accident with mathematical precision and that the tribunal should assess the evidence as a whole, including the police record of the accident. Negligence sufficient for the claim may therefore be inferred from the circumstances proved, even where the same facts would not sustain a conviction.

### Q: Can an award of a Claims Tribunal be appealed?

Yes. Section 173 gives an appeal to the High Court to any person aggrieved by an award of a Claims Tribunal, subject to the conditions the section imposes. An appellant against whom an amount is payable must deposit the sum the section prescribes before the appeal is entertained, which prevents the appeal from being used purely to postpone payment. The High Court in appeal examines both liability and the correctness of the computation, including the income found, the deduction made and the multiplier selected.

### Q: What time limit did the Act as enacted place on filing a claim?

As enacted, Section 166(3) required the application to be made within six months of the accident, and allowed the tribunal to entertain an application up to six months later where it was satisfied that the applicant was prevented by sufficient cause from applying in time. The amendment of 1994 deleted that sub section, so claims under the Act after that deletion are not governed by that statutory window. The originally enacted limitation is part of the history of the provision and explains many decisions on delayed claims from the Act's first years.

### Q: What does a third party policy cover, and what does it leave out?

The policy Chapter XI compels covers the insured's liability for death or bodily injury to any person arising out of the use of the vehicle in a public place, and liability for damage to a third party's property up to the limit the Act as enacted fixed at six thousand rupees. It does not, by the Act's compulsion, cover damage to the insured vehicle itself or the insured's own losses, and it need not cover the employee liabilities and contractual liabilities Section 147 excludes. Wider cover is available by contract at an additional premium.

### Q: Which documents does a claimant need to support a claim?

The claim turns on documents: the first information report and the police papers generated from it, the registration certificate and the insurance policy identifying the vehicle and its insurer, the post mortem report in a death case or the treatment records and disability certificate in an injury case, and proof of the deceased's or the injured person's income, such as salary records, employer certification or income tax returns. Where income documents do not exist, the tribunal fixes a notional income on the material available, which usually results in a lower award.

### Q: How does the claimant's own negligence affect the compensation?

In a fault claim under Section 166, contributory negligence reduces the compensation in proportion to the claimant's or the deceased's share of responsibility for the accident, where that share is proved. The reduction is a rule of the fault inquiry and follows the apportionment the tribunal makes on the evidence. It has no application to the liability without fault under Section 140, because Section 140(4) expressly provides that the no fault claim shall not be defeated, nor its amount reduced, by the wrongful act, neglect or default of the person who died or was disabled.

### Q: How is compensation worked out when the injured person survives with a disability?

The tribunal awards the medical expenses proved, compensation for pain, suffering and loss of amenities, and the loss of earnings during treatment. For the future, it assesses the annual loss of earning capacity by applying the percentage of functional disability the medical evidence establishes to the injured person's income, adds future prospects on the principles Pranay Sethi laid down, and multiplies by the multiplier for the injured person's age. The percentage in a disability certificate is evidence of, but not identical to, the loss of earning capacity, which depends on the work the person did.

### Q: When may an insurer contest a claim on grounds beyond Section 149?

Ordinarily the insurer's defence is confined to the grounds Section 149(2) lists. Section 170 enlarges that position where the tribunal is satisfied that there is collusion between the claimant and the person against whom the claim is made, or that that person has failed to contest the claim. In either case the tribunal may direct that the insurer be impleaded as a party, and the insurer then has the right to contest the claim on all or any of the grounds available to the person against whom the claim was made, including negligence and quantum.
