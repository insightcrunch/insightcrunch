In classrooms across the United States, a diagram on a wall or a page in a textbook explains how a bill becomes law through a sequence of clean boxes and arrows. A member introduces a proposal, a committee studies and amends it, one chamber debates and passes it, the second chamber repeats the process, a conference reconciles any differences, and the President signs the finished text. The diagram has the virtue of order. Every step follows the step before it, every measure receives the same treatment as every other measure, and the outcome appears to turn on the merits of the proposal and the votes of the members elected to judge it. Generations of students have memorized that sequence, reproduced it on examinations, and carried its logic into adult citizenship as the standard account of American lawmaking.

The misconception the diagram plants is not that those steps exist. Each step exists, and each enacted law passes through some recognizable version of the constitutional sequence at its end. The misconception is that the sequence describes the process that decides outcomes, as though introduction set a measure on a track and the track carried the measure forward unless a majority voted it down. Practice runs in the opposite direction. Most measures never move at all, and movement itself is a scarce resource that gatekeepers grant or withhold. A proposal that no committee chair schedules, that no leader places on a calendar, that no senator allows to proceed by consent, and that no larger measure carries as cargo has no path, whatever its support among members and whatever its performance would be in a vote that never occurs. The textbook sequence names the formal stations. It omits the decisions that determine whether a measure reaches any station beyond the first.

The central claim of the account that follows can be stated plainly: bills do not become law, vehicles do. A freestanding proposal, introduced with a number and a title and referred to a committee in the ordinary way, faces an attrition rate so severe that enactment through independent travel along the textbook route has become the exception rather than the pattern. The measures that reach the President in significant numbers arrive attached to, folded into, or carried by something larger: an appropriations package assembled under a funding deadline, an authorization renewed on an annual cycle, a consolidated act whose defeat would carry consequences far beyond any single provision inside it. The text that becomes law often began life as a separate bill, and its language may survive the journey nearly intact. The journey, however, belonged to the vehicle. Understanding lawmaking in practice means following the vehicles, the gates in front of them, and the officials who control passage through those gates, rather than following the biography of an individual bill from introduction onward.

Four determinants, working in sequence and in combination, decide whether a measure becomes law. The first is a committee chair's scheduling decision, because referral sends nearly every proposal to a committee and only the chair's choice to hold a hearing or a markup converts a referral into consideration. The second is leadership control of floor time, because a committee product that leadership never schedules for the floor of its own chamber remains a report rather than a law in waiting, and floor time in both chambers is allocated as a political resource rather than distributed by entitlement. The third is Senate consent, because the Senate conducts almost all of its business through unanimous consent agreements, so a single objection can raise the cost of action beyond what most measures can bear and the prospect of sixty votes for cloture stands behind every negotiation. The fourth is attachment to a measure that must pass, because attachment transfers the deadline, the constituency, and the momentum of the carrier to cargo that could never have generated those assets on its own. A measure strong on one determinant and weak on the others stalls. A measure carried by all four moves with a speed that the textbook sequence cannot explain.

The numbers behind that claim belong to a completed Congress, and the 116th Congress, which sat from 2019 to 2021, supplies the statistical frame for the sections that follow. Members introduced 16,602 bills during that Congress, and 344 public laws were enacted during the same period, a conversion of about 2 percent, with the qualification that a few public laws arrive as joint resolutions rather than bills, so the two counts do not divide exactly. The enacted share was also narrower in substance than the raw figure suggests, because commemorative enactments occupied a substantial portion of the statute book; the Congressional Research Service found that in the five Congresses examined in its report on commemorations, almost 18 percent of all statutes enacted were post office naming acts alone. The large remainder of introduced measures, including proposals with dozens of cosponsors and proposals commanding genuine majority sympathy, ended without a floor vote in either chamber. Attrition on that scale is not a failure of effort or drafting. Attrition is the system working as its gatekeepers direct it, and the gates deserve examination one at a time.

The examination begins with the constitutional floor, the short list of requirements in Article I that every enacted law satisfies and that no practice may waive: passage by both chambers in identical form, presentment to the President, and signature, expiration of the presentment period without a veto, or an override by two thirds of both Houses. From that floor, the account turns to the arithmetic of attrition and the distance between introduction and enactment, then to the committee filter, where referral and a chair's control of the agenda end most measures quietly and without a recorded decision. The House floor gate follows, with its three distinct routes of special rules, suspension of the rules, and unanimous consent, each route sorting measures by importance and controversy before a single vote is cast. The Senate's consent regime comes next, including the hold, the objection, and the costly formal fallback of cloture under Rule XXII. Attention then shifts to the resolution of differences between the chambers, where amendment exchange between the Houses displaced the formal conference committee across a documented decline, and to the vehicles that carry modern lawmaking, from consolidated appropriations packages to the annual defense authorization, closing with the discharge petition, the one formal device by which a House majority can seize a measure from a committee that refuses to release it. Each stage reframes the textbook step of the same name, and together the stages support a single reading of Congress: the formal route remains open, the gates along that route remain guarded, and passage belongs to measures that secure a carrier, a schedule, and consent before the question of majority support is ever formally put. That reading also explains failure without recourse to conspiracy or to accident.

## The Constitutional Floor Beneath Every Route

Every enacted federal law satisfies a short list of constitutional requirements. No statute escapes that list, and no statute satisfies it by a shortcut the Constitution fails to recognize. Article I sets the source of legislative power, reserves to each chamber the authority to govern its own proceedings, and fixes the steps that turn a text passed by Congress into law. Between the introduction of a measure and the completion of those final steps, the Constitution says remarkably little. Committees, scheduling decisions, consent practices, amendment strategies, and the choice of a legislative vehicle all developed inside space the Constitution left open. An account of lawmaking that begins and ends with the constitutional text therefore explains the conditions every law met and almost nothing about the contest that determined whether a particular proposal reached those conditions at all.

Article I, section 1 provides the foundation in a single sentence of vesting language. All legislative powers granted by the Constitution are vested in a Congress of the United States, consisting of a Senate and a House of Representatives. Federal legislative power is placed in Congress as an institution, not in a committee, a party, a leader, or an individual member. A committee recommendation carries no legal force by itself. A leadership agreement carries no legal force by itself. Even a vote in one chamber carries no legal force by itself. Each act matters because chamber rules and political practice give it effect inside a process that ends, if it ends successfully, in action by the two Houses as bodies. The vesting language also divides the institution into two distinct chambers, each with its own membership, terms, officers, and rules. A text approved by one chamber has completed only part of the constitutional requirement. Section 1 answers the question of where legislative power resides. It does not describe how Congress organizes the work of legislating, how a proposal is examined before a vote, or how disagreements inside a chamber are settled.

The distinction between a grant of power and a procedure for using it runs through the whole of Article I. A reader searching the Constitution for the committee system finds no committee system described. That absence does not make the devices unofficial. It makes them products of congressional choice, adopted under a separate constitutional provision and sustained by precedent inside each chamber. A device created by chamber rule can be shaped by chamber rule, interpreted through chamber precedent, and applied differently in the House and the Senate without any difference in the constitutional status of the two chambers. The Constitution supplies the floor.

Article I, section 5 supplies the authority for that construction. Each House may determine the rules of its proceedings. The sentence is brief, and its consequences are extensive. The House writes House rules and the Senate writes Senate rules, and neither chamber writes rules for the other. A procedure valid in one chamber may have no counterpart, no name, and no effect in the other. Quorum requirements and a small number of other constitutional provisions constrain proceedings, but the daily order of business, the recognition of members, the referral of measures, the structure of debate, and the conditions for amendments rest on authority each chamber exercises for itself. Section 5 therefore does more than authorize housekeeping. It locates the decisive procedural law of Congress inside Congress.

That location explains why so much of the law of lawmaking never appears in constitutional text at all. A House rule adopted at the opening of a Congress, a Senate precedent established by a ruling and an appeal, a consent practice repeated until it acquired the force of expectation, and a leadership custom governing which measures reach the floor all operate in the space section 5 reserves. The familiar statement that a measure must pass both Houses thus conceals how different passing can mean in practice, because each House defines the path to passage for itself. Procedure cannot be read off the Constitution. Procedure has to be learned chamber by chamber, rule by rule, and precedent by precedent.

Against that broad grant of procedural self government, Article I, section 7 imposes the hard requirements that define the constitutional floor. Its provisions are mandatory in form and narrow in scope, and both qualities deserve emphasis. Section 7 does not offer a suggested sequence or a default pattern that Congress may set aside for convenience. A measure that fails a section 7 requirement has not become law, however broad its support in one chamber. It fixes the endpoints with precision and leaves the route between introduction and those endpoints to the arrangements described in section 5. Mandatory and narrow is the combination that gives the rest of the process its character. The endpoints cannot be waived. The middle can be organized in more ways than any diagram can conveniently show.

The first hard requirement is passage by both Houses. A bill must be passed by the House of Representatives and by the Senate before it can be presented to the President as a measure ready for approval or veto. Passage by one chamber, recorded in that chamber's journal, remains an intermediate political event. It creates a text for the other chamber to accept, amend, or ignore. The absence of a constitutional deadline for the second chamber is itself consequential. A measure passed by one chamber can wait, and waiting can defeat it as surely as a recorded vote against it, because a Congress ends and unfinished business ends with it.

The second hard requirement sharpens the first: the two chambers must pass the same text. Bicameral passage means passage of identical language, word for word, in the form finally enrolled. Agreement on policy is not enough. Agreement on purpose, on a framework, or on most provisions is not enough. If the Senate amends a House passed measure, the House must accept that amended text in its amended form, or the disagreement must be resolved through further action until a single text commands passage in both chambers. The identical form requirement is the point at which the independence protected by section 5 meets its limit. Each chamber controls its own proceedings, but neither chamber can legislate alone, and neither can deem its preferred version to be the version of Congress. A law records the intersection of two separate acts of passage on one document. Every device for resolving differences between the chambers exists because that intersection has to be produced, and producing it can be the hardest part of the entire undertaking.

Section 7 then requires presentment. Every bill passed by the House and the Senate is presented to the President before it becomes law. Presentment is more than delivery as a courtesy. It is the act that starts the presidential decision the Constitution prescribes. Before presentment, Congress prepares the agreed text in enrolled form. The passed text is enrolled on parchment standard paper by the originating chamber, signed by the Speaker and by the President of the Senate or the President pro tempore, and then presented to the President. The signatures attest to that copy, and presentment delivers it for a decision that only the President can make under section 7: approval and signature, return with objections, or the passage of the prescribed period without signature under conditions that either produce a law or produce a pocket veto.

Approval is the simplest outcome. If the President approves a presented measure, the President signs it, and the measure becomes law. Congressional passage supplied the text, enrollment authenticated it, presentment delivered it, and presidential approval enacted it. By the time a text reaches presentment, the questions that decided its fate have generally been answered elsewhere: whether a committee reported it, whether leadership scheduled it, whether the Senate could proceed to it, whether the two chambers could converge on one version, and whether that version could hold its supporting coalition through final passage. Presentment certifies the result of that contest. It does not describe it. The return veto introduces the opposite presidential answer. If the President does not approve, the President returns the measure with objections to the chamber in which it originated. That chamber enters the objections in its journal and proceeds to reconsider the measure. A veto is not a silent erasure. It is a formal message identifying the grounds of disapproval, placed before the originating chamber and then before the other chamber if reconsideration succeeds there.

Time governs the presidential decision through the ten day rule. A presented measure becomes law without a signature if the President neither signs nor returns it within ten days, Sundays excepted, after presentment. The clock runs from presentment, not from passage, which reinforces the significance of enrollment and delivery as constitutional events in their own right. Once the period runs, inaction has a legal consequence assigned in advance: enactment without signature, provided Congress remains in a posture in which return would have been possible. That proviso leads directly to the pocket veto, the one form of presidential disapproval Congress cannot override.

The pocket veto arises when adjournment prevents return. If Congress, by adjournment, prevents the return of a measure within the ten day period, the measure does not become law. Where return is possible and the President allows ten days, Sundays excepted, to pass without action, the measure becomes law unsigned. Where adjournment has made return impossible within the period, inaction defeats the measure, and no reconsideration follows because no objections were returned for either chamber to reconsider. The pocket veto is therefore absolute in effect even though it consists of silence. A measure presented late in a session carries a risk embedded in the calendar itself, and disputes over whether an adjournment prevented return have turned on the precise arrangements Congress made for receiving messages during a recess.

Repassage over a veto is the final hard requirement section 7 supplies, and it is deliberately demanding. If, after reconsideration, two thirds of the originating House agree to pass the measure, it goes with the objections to the other House, by which it is likewise reconsidered, and if approved by two thirds of that House, it becomes law. The threshold is two thirds of both Houses, not two thirds of a joint sitting and not a simple majority reinforced by insistence. Each chamber must reach the supermajority separately, under its own rules, in sequence. A coalition sufficient to pass a measure once may not be sufficient to pass it again at a higher threshold against a stated veto. The historical arithmetic shows how rarely that obstacle has been overcome: of more than 1,400 regular vetoes recorded by the Secretary of the Senate through 2008, 110 were overridden. The figure is a measure of the height of the floor at that point. Presidents negotiate before a veto in the knowledge that a credible veto threat can move a text toward terms the President can sign.

One further requirement in section 7 allocates an advantage at the starting point of a defined class of measures. Bills for raising revenue originate in the House of Representatives, though the Senate may propose or concur with amendments as on other bills. Origination gives the House the first move on revenue measures, but it does not give the House control over the final content of revenue legislation. A revenue proposal can begin in the House in a short form and acquire extensive content through Senate amendment, provided the origination requirement is satisfied by the vehicle that carries it. A formal rule allocates a starting position. Practice inside the open middle determines how much advantage that starting position confers in a given contest, and the choice of vehicle can matter as much as the identity of the chamber that moved first.

Taken together, sections 1, 5, and 7 establish a framework that is exact at three points and open between them. Section 1 answers where legislative power lies. Section 5 answers who governs procedure inside each chamber. Section 7 answers what must happen for a passed text to become law, and what must happen for Congress to enact a text over presidential objection. None of the three sections answers the questions that decide most legislative contests. Which proposals receive serious examination, and in what order? Who decides that a measure reaches the floor, and under what limits on debate and amendment? How does the Senate proceed when a single objection can block a request for consent? When the two chambers pass different texts, who negotiates the single text that identical passage requires, and through what mechanism? Which expiring authority, appropriations deadline, or broadly supported measure becomes the vehicle that carries provisions unable to advance alone? The Constitution is silent on each question, not because the questions are minor, but because the answers were left to rules, precedents, offices, and practices that Congress developed for itself.

That silence defines the middle of the process, and the middle is where lawmaking is actually decided. A committee that declines to report a proposal has not violated section 7; section 7 was never reached. A leader who declines to schedule a reported measure has not breached a constitutional timetable; no such timetable exists for scheduling. A senator whose objection prevents action by consent has exercised a possibility created by Senate practice under section 5, not an exception carved out of a constitutional rule of passage. A provision enacted inside a consolidated appropriations act or an annual defense authorization satisfied the same floor as a provision enacted as a freestanding measure, even though the political path that carried it bore little resemblance to separate consideration and separate presentment for that provision standing alone. In each case, the constitutional requirements were either never engaged or fully satisfied at the end, while the outcome turned on arrangements the Constitution does not describe.

The classroom diagram of lawmaking has value because the endpoints it shows are real. Introduction, passage by both Houses, presentment, and signature or override are genuine features of the system, and a law that skipped any required endpoint would not be a law at all. The diagram fails as an explanation when its sequence is mistaken for the typical path of a significant measure. Most measures do not move at all. Movement is the exception that gatekeepers, schedulers, negotiators, and coalition builders produce against a default of inaction, and producing it usually requires a route the diagram does not depict: a text attached to another text, a difference settled by exchanged amendments, a proceeding governed by consent rather than by a contested motion, or a special procedure that limits the amendments a chamber may consider. None of those routes appears in the constitutional text. All of them operate under the authority the constitutional text granted each chamber over its own proceedings.

The reframing that the following sections develop begins from that contrast. The formal requirements of Article I are a floor every law must satisfy, not a description of the path any particular law travels. Beneath that shared ending, the paths diverged so widely that the shared ending explains little about why one measure arrived and another measure, similar in subject and equal in formal eligibility, never came close. Explaining arrival requires attention to the middle: to committees as gates, to leadership as a scheduling power, to consent as the operating system of the Senate, to vehicles as the carriers of modern legislation, and to the points at which a proposal can stop without ever receiving a final vote against it. The floor is constitutional. The path is congressional. A serious account of how a bill becomes law in practice holds both statements together and refuses to let the clarity of the first obscure the complexity of the second.

## The Arithmetic of Attrition: Introduction Is Not a Path

Introduction of a bill looks, on a chart, like a departure. A member files text, the clerk assigns a number, the title enters the record, and an arrow points toward committee. The arrow lends the act a sense of motion that the act itself does not possess. Filing a measure is closer to publishing a position than to launching a process. It places a member, a party faction, or an organized constituency on record with a specific remedy for a specific problem. It announces priorities to voters in a district or state, to interest organizations that score legislative activity, to donors who track particular issues, and to colleagues who may be pressed later to join a coalition. None of that requires passage, and much of it loses value if passage is treated as the only purpose.

A proposal introduced with no expectation of enactment can succeed completely on its own terms. It can define the sponsor as the owner of an issue inside a caucus. It can force rival members to explain why their names are absent from a popular cause. It can give an advocacy organization a concrete text to organize around rather than a general complaint to circulate. It can stake out the opening position for a negotiation that will end, if it ends at all, in language far narrower than the filed draft. It can also serve as a marker for a future Congress, preserving negotiated language in public form while sponsors wait for a different committee chair, a different majority, or a different legislative vehicle. A member who introduces the same text session after session is often not failing repeatedly at the same task. The repeated filing is the task, a way of keeping a claim visible until institutional conditions change.

The classroom sequence from introduction to committee to floor to the other chamber to the President describes a permitted route. It does not describe a schedule, an entitlement, or a default. No rule requires a committee to hold a hearing on a referred measure. No rule requires a markup, a report, or a vote in committee simply because text has been filed. No rule requires House or Senate leaders to allot floor time to a measure that a committee has reported. Each stage after introduction depends on a decision by a gatekeeper who controls scarce time and who gains nothing automatically from advancing another member's priority. Introduction is therefore not the first step of a journey in any reliable sense. It is admission to a waiting room whose doors open from the other side.

### What share of introduced bills become law?

In the 116th Congress, 16,602 bills were introduced and 344 public laws were enacted, a conversion of about two percent. A few public laws arrived as joint resolutions rather than bills, so the two counts did not divide exactly, but the scale of attrition was unmistakable in either calculation.

Two percent is not a rounding error in an otherwise functioning pipeline. It is the normal output of a system in which filing is inexpensive and every later action is rationed. Committee agendas are finite. Floor hours are finite. Attention from leadership staff, legislative counsel, and budget analysts is finite. Against those limits, the supply of filed text is effectively unlimited because a sponsor pays no institutional price for adding one more proposal to the queue. The imbalance between unlimited supply and rationed consideration is the arithmetic in the section title. Attrition is not an accident that befalls a few unlucky bills after a fair hearing for all. Attrition is the default outcome, and advancement is the exception that requires affirmative intervention at several points by actors other than the sponsor.

Referral, the first event after introduction, illustrates the point. In the House, the Speaker refers a measure to committee, guided by the Parliamentarian's advice on the jurisdiction fixed by House rules and precedent. In the Senate, referral likewise proceeds on the Parliamentarian's advice to the presiding officer, under Senate jurisdictional practice. Referral sounds clerical, and in a routine case the jurisdictional answer is settled enough that the act attracts no notice. Yet referral decides which chair acquires the power to ignore the measure, which staff will judge its drafting, which affected interests will be heard first if it moves at all, and which rival committee may claim a piece of the subject. A favorable referral can place a proposal before a sympathetic chair. An unfavorable referral can bury it among priorities that the receiving committee has no intention of displacing.

Referral also frequently fragments a measure rather than assigning it to a single destination. A proposal that touches health financing, taxation, and federal lands, or defense procurement and environmental review, can be referred to more than one committee, in whole or in part, sometimes at the same time and sometimes in sequence. Multiple referral reflects the genuine overlap of committee jurisdictions, but its practical effect is to multiply the points at which movement can stop. Each committee of referral holds a claim on the portion within its jurisdiction. One committee may act while another declines, leaving text incomplete. Committees may bargain over boundaries before either invests in hearings. The sponsor of a measure sent to several committees does not have several paths to the floor. The sponsor has several gatekeepers, each able to withhold the clearance that a clean report requires.

Even a single referral confers no momentum. A committee chair decides whether a measure merits a hearing slot against competing subjects that the chair, the majority leadership, and the committee majority actually intend to advance. Subcommittee referral adds another discretionary layer. Staff may examine a filed proposal, consult affected agencies, and conclude that the drafting is workable, and the measure can remain untouched because no one with agenda power has a reason to spend a markup slot on it. Death in committee is usually silent. There is no dramatic vote, no floor speech, and no formal rejection that a sponsor can appeal. The measure simply receives no further action before the Congress ends, at which point unenacted proposals expire and any revival must begin with a new filing, a new number, and a new referral.

The composition of the small enacted share deepens the lesson. A substantial part of what clears every gate is commemorative or local in character rather than programmatic. The Congressional Research Service report Commemorations in Congress, numbered R43539, found that across the five Congresses it examined, almost 18 percent of all statutes enacted were post office naming acts alone. Those enactments did nothing except attach a name to a federal facility, usually at the request of the member representing the district where the facility stood. In the 113th Congress, commemorative enactments of all kinds numbered 46, about 21 percent of that Congress's enactments. A reader who imagines the 344 public laws of the 116th Congress as 344 exercises in national policymaking has therefore misunderstood the output as well as the input.

Commemorative measures succeed for reasons that expose, by contrast, why substantive measures stall. A facility naming imposes no meaningful cost, creates no regulatory burden, distributes no scarce federal funds among competing claimants, and ordinarily draws no organized opposition. A proposal to restructure a health program, rewrite a tax provision, or authorize a contested weapons system presents the opposite profile at every point. It creates identifiable losers, invites agency and industry resistance, requires budgetary room, and gives opponents several venues in which delay alone is victory. The enacted share is padded, in statistical terms, by the measures least representative of legislative conflict. Strip away the commemorative and purely local layer, and the conversion rate for significant national policy falls well below the headline two percent.

That adjustment matters because two percent can sound like a filter that strains out weak drafts and passes strong ones. The filter does not primarily test drafting quality or even majority support in the country or in a chamber. It tests access to agenda control. A technically flawed measure backed by a chair and by leadership can be repaired in markup, in a manager's amendment, or in negotiations between the chambers, because actors with power have decided that the subject will move. A carefully drafted measure with broad public approval can receive no hearing because the chair of the committee of referral opposes its premise or because leadership prefers to keep a divisive subject off the floor in a given Congress. Quality helps at the margins, and unworkable text gives gatekeepers a convenient reason for inaction, but access decides which measures get the chance to be improved.

Cosponsorship is the signal most often mistaken for progress against that barrier, and it deserves direct treatment. A cosponsor list records sentiment. It is not a procedural event. Adding a name does not amend text, does not discharge a committee, does not reserve floor time, does not satisfy any rule, and does not oblige the cosponsor to defend the measure when a vote would carry a political cost. A member can cosponsor scores of proposals in a Congress at no expense beyond a staff instruction, and many do so precisely because cosponsorship is an inexpensive way to register sympathy with a cause, a constituency, or a colleague. The list measures how many members were willing to be associated with a filed text at the moment of signing. It does not measure intensity, priority, or willingness to spend bargaining capital, and those unmeasured qualities are the ones that determine scheduling.

For that reason, neither committee chairs nor party leaders count cosponsors when deciding what to schedule. A chair weighing a markup agenda looks at the priorities of the committee majority, the position of leadership, the state of negotiations with the other chamber, the budgetary consequences, and the demands of members whose support the chair needs on other matters. A long cosponsor list attached to a measure outside that calculation changes none of it. Leadership offices making floor decisions weigh the same factors at chamber scale, together with the risk that debate on one subject will consume time reserved for appropriations or another must-pass item. A measure with several hundred cosponsors can be left unscheduled for two full years, while a measure with a handful of names can move within weeks because a chair adopted it. A cosponsor total, by contrast, triggers nothing at all.

Bipartisan cosponsorship carries slightly more information than a list drawn from one party, since it suggests that a floor vote, if one occurred, might not divide strictly on party lines. Even that signal is weaker than it appears. Members of the minority party can endorse a majority party proposal in the knowledge that endorsement will never be tested, and members of the majority can endorse a minority proposal as a courtesy that costs nothing because the committee of referral will take no action. A cosponsor list also says nothing about the Senate, where a single determined objection to a consent request can hold a measure that passed the House with an overwhelming margin. Sentiment recorded on paper in one chamber does not relax a single veto point in either chamber. It is evidence that a subject has friends. Friends are not a schedule.

Companion bills operate in the same register of signal rather than mechanism. A companion is a measure filed in the other chamber, usually in identical or closely similar form, by a sponsor who supports the same policy. Filing a companion shows that an idea has an identified advocate on both sides of the Capitol, allows committees in each chamber to examine the subject in parallel, and gives staff in the two chambers a common text around which to negotiate differences early. What a companion does not do is create any procedural connection between the two filings. Each measure is referred separately, considered separately, and required to satisfy the passage requirements of its own chamber in identical form before presentment, under Article I, section 7. One companion can advance while the other is never taken up, and the advance of one creates no obligation and no shortcut for the other.

The history of a typical serious proposal therefore runs through companions and refilings rather than through a single continuous ascent. A measure filed in one Congress expires at the end of that Congress. If the sponsor or a successor takes up the cause again, the text is reintroduced in the next Congress with a new number, a new referral, and a new cosponsor list assembled from the start. Changes in committee membership, party control, and chairmanships mean that the reintroduced measure faces a different set of gatekeepers from the ones that ignored its predecessor. Sponsors adjust the text between Congresses, narrowing a provision that drew agency opposition, dropping a title that created a jurisdictional dispute, or adding language negotiated with a committee staff that had signaled objections in private. Reintroduction Congress after Congress is not an eccentricity of stubborn members. It is the normal biography of an idea that has not yet found its moment or its vehicle.

That biography often ends in a way that the introduction statistics cannot capture. When the moment arrives, the policy in a long-refiled proposal may not be enacted under its own number at all. Its operative language can be folded into an appropriations act, an annual defense authorization, an omnibus measure, or another text that leadership has committed to move on a deadline. The original bill remains, in the formal count, one of the thousands introduced and never enacted, even though its substance became law. Counting bills therefore understates the success of ideas and overstates the importance of the individual filings that carry them. The unit that completes the process is rarely the unit that began it.

Vehicles explain the apparent paradox of a Congress that enacts only about two percent of its bills and yet produces consequential law in most sessions. Leadership does not move thousands of freestanding measures through every veto point one at a time. Leadership assembles a small number of texts that must pass because a fiscal year is ending, an authorization is expiring, or a deadline carries consequences for inaction, and contested policy travels inside those texts under the protection of the deadline. From the sponsor's perspective, reintroduction over several Congresses is often a holding strategy for exactly that outcome. The filed text keeps the language current, the coalition identified, and the claim to authorship established until a vehicle with room for the provision passes through the chamber.

None of this makes introduction meaningless. It makes introduction legible. Read correctly, a filing is evidence about positioning, priorities, and coalition claims, offered by a member who knows that passage is improbable and who files anyway because the other functions are worth performing. Read incorrectly, as the first mile of a road that most measures will travel, a filing generates false expectations and false verdicts. Reporters who ask why a popular measure went nowhere are usually asking the wrong question, because popularity was never the currency in which floor time was purchased. The institution's statistics correct the grammar.

The base rate is the correction to keep in view. When roughly ninety-eight of every hundred filed bills in a completed Congress produce no law, the interesting analytical question is never how a bill becomes law in the abstract. The permitted route is settled, short enough to print on a poster, and identical for measures that pass and measures that disappear. The interesting question is what distinguishes the small set that receives a vote at all. A measure moves when a committee chair adopts its subject as committee business, when leadership assigns it scarce floor time or space inside a must-pass text, when the other chamber's gatekeepers see advantage in taking it up, and when no actor positioned to block it judges the cost of objection worth paying. Absent that alignment, a measure has a number, a referral, perhaps a long list of cosponsors, perhaps a companion across the Capitol, and nothing else. Those ornaments are the record of an intention. The alignment is the lawmaking.

## The Committee Filter: Where Measures End Quietly

Introduction creates an illusion of movement. A sponsor files a text, the Clerk records a number, the measure receives a title, and the record shows a referral to one or more committees. Each step looks procedural and neutral, and each step can mislead an observer who equates filing with progress. Referral does not advance a proposal toward passage. Referral assigns the proposal to a gatekeeper, and the gatekeeper decides whether any further step occurs at all. In the House and in the Senate alike, the committee stage separates the small set of measures that receive sustained institutional attention from the far larger set that receives a number, a referral, and silence.

The sorting function is real, and the scale of the intake makes some sorting unavoidable. The 116th Congress, which ran from 2019 to 2021, recorded 16,602 bills introduced and 344 public laws enacted, a conversion of about 2 percent, with the qualification that a few public laws arrive as joint resolutions rather than bills, so the counts do not divide exactly. No chamber could hold hearings on every filed text, and no floor could debate every proposal that a member chose to introduce. A system that divides labor by subject, assigns measures to panels with jurisdiction over health, taxation, defense, agriculture, transportation, or the judiciary, and expects those panels to screen the intake serves a practical need. The filter answers that need, and the filter also confers power on the members and staff who control the screen.

Jurisdiction determines the destination, and the destination often determines the outcome. A proposal that touches several subjects can fall under the jurisdiction of more than one committee, and the parliamentarian, acting under chamber rules and precedents, advises the presiding officer on referral. The broader [committee system and its markup practice](/2022/03/01/committee-system-and-markups/) explains why that advice matters far beyond clerical sorting. One committee may have a chair eager to act on a subject, another may have a chair determined to block action, and a third may have a crowded agenda that leaves no room for a measure regardless of merit. The same text, referred to different panels, can therefore face entirely different prospects before any hearing occurs and before any member casts a vote on substance.

### Where do most bills die?

Most bills die in committee, without a hearing, without a markup, and without a floor vote. Referral assigns a measure to a committee, the chair declines to schedule action, and the proposal remains in that assignment for the remainder of the Congress, unreported and unconsidered elsewhere.

Death in committee leaves almost no trace in the public record beyond the referral itself. No roll call records opposition, no debate transcript preserves arguments against the text, and no formal rejection gives a sponsor a decision to contest. A measure that a committee votes to table at least generates an action. A measure that a committee never takes up generates nothing, and nothing is difficult to challenge. Supporters can count cosponsors, circulate letters, and issue statements, but none of those activities compels a committee to act. The two year life of a Congress sets the limit. When a Congress ends, measures that received no action expire with that Congress, and a sponsor who persists must introduce the text again in the succeeding Congress and begin the referral process again from the start.

The chair stands at the center of the filter. A committee chair controls the agenda of the committee, subject to chamber rules, committee rules, and the views of the majority party that selected the chair. The chair decides which measures receive hearings, which witnesses appear, which proposals advance to markup, and which subjects occupy the limited meeting time that a committee can schedule in a session. Staff assigned to the majority prepare options for the chair, leadership communicates priorities to the chair, and the chair weighs those signals against the preferences of committee members and the demands of other business. A chair who favors a proposal can move the proposal through hearing and markup with speed. A chair who opposes a proposal, doubts the drafting, or simply ranks the subject below other business can prevent any movement by declining to place the measure on an agenda.

Silence from a chair is a decision that needs no vote and leaves no record to appeal. No rule requires a chair to explain inaction on a particular measure, and no appeal procedure allows a sponsor to force a hearing before a panel that has declined to schedule one. The decision takes the form of an omission, and omissions resist the usual tools of legislative accountability. A recorded vote can be defended, attacked, and remembered. An unscheduled hearing cannot be quoted. A markup that never occurs produces no amendment votes and no minority statement. A sponsor confronted with silence faces a practical problem as well as a political one, because the absence of action gives outside groups little to organize against and gives reporters little to describe. The measure has not been defeated in any visible sense. The measure has been withheld from every arena in which defeat or passage could occur.

Hearings illustrate the gatekeeping function with particular clarity, because a hearing is both an information gathering exercise and a signal of intent. A committee that schedules a hearing on a proposal announces that the subject has entered the active agenda, invites testimony from agencies, affected interests, and subject specialists, and creates a record that later drafting can draw upon. A committee that holds a hearing on a general subject without taking up a specific text sends a different signal, one of exploration without commitment. A committee that holds no hearing at all withholds both signals and both resources. Most filed measures receive no hearing, and the absence of a hearing matters beyond symbolism. Without testimony, questioning, and staff follow up, a proposal lacks the tested factual record that helps drafters identify unintended effects, conflicting statutes, and administrative obstacles before floor consideration begins.

Staff level screening performs much of the quiet work that precedes a chair decision. Committee staff review referred measures for jurisdictional fit, drafting defects, duplication of measures already under consideration, conflict with existing law, and compatibility with the agenda that the chair and the majority have set. Counsel examine definitions and cross references. Policy staff consult agency views, budget estimates where relevant, and the positions of members on and off the committee. Communications staff assess how a proposal might be received outside the chamber. Screening of that kind sorts measures into rough categories long before a member meeting occurs: measures ready for action, measures that need redrafting, measures that duplicate a preferred vehicle, and measures that the majority has no intention of advancing. A sponsor may never learn which category captured a particular text, and staff screening leaves even less of a record than chair inaction.

Markup marks the point at which consideration becomes textual and specific. In a markup session, members sit with a printed text, the chair or a designee explains the measure section by section, and members offer amendments that the committee votes to adopt or reject. For measures that reach that stage, markup is the one place where the text is genuinely rewritten line by line in an open committee proceeding, with each change debated on the record and each adopted amendment altering the document that will go forward. A manager amendment can replace large portions of a filed text. A substitute amendment can replace the text entirely and confront the committee with a different measure under the same number. Votes at markup reveal alignments that cosponsorship lists conceal, because a member who signed a measure in general form must decide on particular language, particular exceptions, and particular costs.

Few measures reach markup, and reaching markup guarantees nothing beyond a committee decision. A committee can order a measure reported favorably, report a measure with an adverse recommendation in rare practice, report a measure without recommendation, or fail to act after debate begins. A measure can also lose at markup when members adopt amendments that strip the central provision or when the committee declines to order the text reported at all. The distinction between debate and reporting matters because only a reported measure moves to the next stage in an orderly way. Discussion, however extended, leaves the text inside the committee. A vote to report sends the text outward, toward the calendar, toward leadership scheduling, and toward the possibility of floor action. Markup therefore functions as a second gate inside the first one. Referral opens the committee door. Markup decides whether anything passes through the far side of the room.

The difference between a bill that is reported and a bill that is merely filed captures the central distinction of the entire process. A filed measure expresses the intent of the sponsor and any cosponsors. A filed measure may attract attention, define a negotiating position, satisfy a constituency, or stake out language for a later Congress. A reported measure carries the judgment of a committee, expressed through a vote, embodied in an amended text where amendments were adopted, and explained in a written report. The reported text is the text that leadership schedules, the text that floor amendments address, and the text that the other chamber receives after passage. Filing requires a member and a form. Reporting requires a majority of a committee, assembled and voting, and that requirement explains why so many filed measures never acquire the status that would allow the rest of the process to begin.

The committee report deserves attention separate from the vote that produces the report, because the report travels farther and lasts longer than the markup session itself. When a committee orders a measure reported, staff and counsel prepare a document that describes the purpose of the measure, explains each section, records the votes taken, presents cost estimates where the Congressional Budget Office has supplied one, states the views of dissenting or additional members who choose to file views, and sets out changes to existing law where the measure amends a statute. Agencies charged with administering an enacted law read the report for guidance on purpose and scope. Courts asked to interpret ambiguous statutory language consult the report as part of the legislative history of the enactment. Scholars and journalists reconstructing a decision return to the report because the report preserves the reasoning that the committee chose to place on the record at the moment of action.

Multiple referral adds a further filter that a single referral does not impose. A measure whose provisions fall under the jurisdiction of two or more committees can be referred to each of those committees, and chamber practice distinguishes among joint referral, concurrent consideration, and sequential referral. Under a sequential referral, one committee acts first and a second committee receives the measure afterward, often under a time limit set by the Speaker in the House. Each committee in the sequence holds the same gatekeeping power over the portions within the jurisdiction of that committee, and each chair in the sequence can delay or halt progress by withholding action. A measure that survives the first panel may stall in the second, and a measure reported with amendments by two panels can give leadership conflicting texts that require negotiation before floor scheduling becomes practical.

The counterweight that any honest account of the filter must honor is that committee expertise genuinely shapes the text of measures that do move. Members who serve on a committee across several Congresses develop knowledge of a program, an industry, a weapons system, a tax provision, or a regulatory scheme that general debate cannot replicate. Staff who remain with a committee through changes in party control carry institutional memory about drafting choices that failed, compromises that held, and agency practices that a statute must address. Hearings bring agency officials and outside specialists into a record that drafters consult. Markup forces choices about definitions, deadlines, penalties, and funding formulas that floor debate, conducted under time limits and amendment restrictions, rarely examines with the same patience. A measure reported after serious committee work often reaches the floor in a form materially different from the filed version, and the differences frequently reflect problems identified in testimony, errors corrected by counsel, and objections answered by amendment.

Expertise and gatekeeping coexist in the same office and in the same proceeding, and separating the two functions proves difficult in practice. A chair who blocks a measure may act from political calculation, from substantive conviction that the drafting is unsound, from a judgment that agencies cannot administer the proposal, or from a mixture of motives that no outside observer can disentangle. A committee that reports a measure after extensive revision may have improved the text and advanced the agenda of the majority in a single act. The filter is substantive as well as political because the same hearing that tests a proposal also decides whether the proposal deserves further time, and the same markup that refines language also determines which provisions survive refinement. Readers who treat the committee stage as a mere obstacle miss the drafting work that occurs inside the stage. Readers who treat the committee stage as a neutral seminar miss the power that decides which subjects receive drafting work at all.

Comparison with the floor clarifies why the committee stage carries such weight in the full process. Floor action in the House generally occurs under structured conditions set in advance, and floor action in the Senate generally proceeds under agreements negotiated among leaders and consent requests that any single senator can block. Neither arena rewards extended line by line examination of a long text. Committees possess the staff, the jurisdiction, and the meeting format suited to that examination, and committees decide which texts receive the examination before either floor sees the result. A measure denied committee examination arrives at later stages, if the measure arrives at all through an unusual route, without the vetting that reporting supplies. A measure granted committee examination arrives with a record, a report, and a coalition of members who have already voted on the text once and who can defend the text in wider debate.

The quiet character of committee death also shapes public understanding of Congress as an institution. Citizens who follow a proposal through news coverage encounter announcements of introduction, statements of support, and counts of cosponsors, followed by silence that coverage rarely explains. The silence reads as mystery or drift, when the underlying reality is a sequence of decisions and nondecisions inside a referral. A chair declined to schedule a hearing. Staff screening placed the text in a category that received no further work. Another measure absorbed the committee calendar. A second referral added a committee that never acted. None of those steps produces a headline, and together those steps account for the fate of the overwhelming majority of measures introduced in a Congress. The classroom diagram that moves a proposal smoothly from introduction to committee to floor omits the decisive fact that the committee arrow points, for most measures, toward a terminus rather than toward the next chamber.

Understanding the filter changes how a reader interprets the measures that emerge from committee with a report. Emergence signals more than agreement with a purpose. Emergence signals that a chair chose to spend agenda time, that staff screening found the text workable or made the text workable, that a majority of a committee accepted a recorded vote, and that the institution produced a document explaining the choice. Emergence also signals that leadership, or at least the committee majority, judged the measure worth the floor time and the political exposure that floor consideration brings. A reported measure has survived the most selective stage of the process, the stage at which selection occurs without debate that the public can watch and without votes that the public can count. What survives that stage enters the visible process carrying advantages that a filed measure never possesses, and what fails to survive leaves the process at the point where failure is hardest to see.

## The House Floor Gate: Rules, Suspension, and the Managed Calendar

A committee report changed a measure without moving the measure a single step closer to passage by operation of law. The report certified that a committee with jurisdiction had examined the text, held hearings or a markup or both, voted to order the measure reported, and placed a written explanation before the House. The report gave colleagues information, gave opponents language to quote against the proposal, and gave supporters a record to defend. The report conferred no right to consideration on the floor, no date for debate, no guarantee of amendments, and no promise that the chamber would ever vote on the text in the form the committee approved. Between the committee room and the floor stood a separate gate, and a measure that cleared one gate in convincing fashion could wait at the other gate for months or for the remainder of a Congress without a hearing on the floor at all.

That separation explained much of the practical difference between introduction and lawmaking in the House of Representatives. Introduction put a proposal into the record, referral put the proposal before specialists, and a favorable report put the proposal into a queue. None of those steps compelled the institution to spend its scarcest resource, which was time on the floor under conditions that allowed a decision. Floor time was finite, because debate, amendment, and voting consumed hours that could not be recovered, and because every hour assigned to one measure was an hour denied to another measure or to an appropriations text. A leadership that controlled the assignment of floor time therefore controlled, in a large proportion of cases, the outcome that floor time would eventually produce. For the ordinary reported measure, the scheduling decision came before any other floor decision and often settled the question that a later vote appeared to answer. The route selected also shaped the substance, because a text written for a closed process differed from a text written to survive open amendment, and a text written to attract a two thirds vote differed from a text written to hold a simple majority.

The Committee on Rules held a jurisdiction unlike the jurisdiction of a legislative committee that examined a subject such as agriculture, banking, or armed services. A legislative committee produced measures within a policy field. The Rules Committee produced the conditions under which measures from many policy fields would be considered, and the committee also held jurisdiction over the standing rules themselves. Composition reinforced function. The majority party held a pronounced advantage in seats on the Rules Committee, an advantage larger in proportion than the advantage the majority ordinarily held on most other standing committees, and the Speaker held a central role in shaping the majority membership of the committee. The leadership needed a committee that would meet on short notice, consider a text that had reached a late stage of negotiation, and report a resolution that the majority could adopt on the floor. A member who appeared before the committee to request that an amendment be made in order received a decision that could end the amendment's practical life, whatever support the amendment might have attracted in a freer process.

The product of the committee was called a special rule, although the formal vehicle was a simple resolution of the House that, once adopted, operated as an order governing consideration of a named measure. A special rule ordinarily specified the stage at which consideration would begin, the text that would serve as the base text, the time allotted for general debate and the members who would control that time, the amendments that would be in order and the time allotted to each such amendment, the points of order that would be waived, and the manner in which the final question would be put. The details varied with the measure, but the function remained constant. The special rule displaced the default order, substituted a tailored order for a particular occasion, and expired after the occasion had passed. Article I, section 5 of the Constitution supplied the underlying authority, in the provision that each House could determine the rules of its proceedings.

### What does a closed rule do to a bill?

A closed rule brings a measure to the floor for a set period of debate with no floor amendments permitted, except amendments the rule itself makes in order or prints in advance, so members vote the reported text up or down without reshaping the text during floor consideration.

Adoption of the rule was itself a vote, taken before general debate began in earnest, and adoption committed the House to the terms the rule contained. A member who opposed a particular provision but supported the measure in general could not isolate the provision for a separate vote unless the rule allowed such a vote. A member who had drafted a corrective amendment and secured support for the amendment in committee corridors had no floor vehicle for the proposal under a closed rule. Opposition therefore had to aim at the rule itself, at the previous question on the rule, or at final passage, and each of those votes bundled together objections that an amendment process would have separated into distinct questions. Bundling also held bargains together. Under a closed rule, the text that leadership and committee managers had assembled entered the floor with a protection that no single provision could easily lose, because a member who disliked one concession could not remove that concession without defeating the entire measure or defeating the rule. Leadership could therefore promise wavering supporters that a negotiated balance would reach a vote intact, and the closed rule converted that private assurance into a public procedural commitment adopted by majority vote.

Neither party treated the device as an aberration reserved for emergencies. Both parties, in periods of majority control, used closed rules for measures where the majority leadership judged that amendment would unravel a negotiated text or consume time the schedule could not spare. Minority members protested individual closed rules in debate, and a minority that later became a majority inherited the same device and applied the device to its own program. The closed rule was a standing instrument, sustained across changes in party control, because successive majorities found the instrument useful for governing a chamber of 435 members.

Special rules fell into families that practitioners distinguished by the degree of amendment the rule allowed. An open rule allowed any amendment that complied with the standing rules of the House, including the germaneness requirement and the restrictions that House Rule XXI and other provisions placed on appropriations and revenue content, subject to the time structure the rule established. Under an open rule, a member who had prepared a germane amendment and who could gain recognition could offer the amendment, and the House or the Committee of the Whole could accept or reject the proposal as a separate question. A structured rule, also called a modified closed rule in some accounts of House practice, allowed only amendments specified in the rule or in the report accompanying the rule, often identified by sponsor, subject, and printed text, with debate time assigned to each amendment in advance. A closed rule allowed no amendments beyond those the rule itself contained or made in order through its own text.

The distribution among those families in a completed Congress showed how completely the curated end of the spectrum had come to dominate major floor consideration. Don Wolfensberger, a former staff director of the Rules Committee, compiled Rules Committee data for the 114th Congress, which sat from 2015 to 2016, and recorded 82 structured rules, 65 closed rules, and only 8 open rules in that Congress. Closed rules and structured rules together accounted for about 95 percent of the special rules in that count. The House Rules Committee recorded that no open rule had been used since June 10, 2014, a date that fell before the 114th Congress convened, so the open rule belonged to a practice already receding as that Congress began. Leadership aides, committee staff, and members drafting amendments in that period planned around the expectation that an amendment required advance clearance through the rule, rather than around the expectation that recognition on the floor would suffice. A provision omitted from the base text and denied a place in the rule had almost no path to a floor vote.

Two further devices inside special rules deepened the control that a rule exercised over a measure's fate. A self executing provision in a rule provided that adoption of the rule itself would have a specified legislative effect, most commonly that an amendment described in the rule or in the accompanying report would be considered as adopted, without a separate vote on that effect as an isolated question. Waivers of points of order performed a quieter but equally consequential function. The standing rules gave any member points of order against provisions that violated specified restrictions, including restrictions on unauthorized appropriations, on legislating in an appropriations measure, and on measures that failed to comply with budgetary requirements that the House had imposed on itself. A point of order, if sustained by the chair, could strike a provision or delay consideration. A special rule frequently waived points of order against consideration of the measure, against specified provisions, or against amendments made in order by the rule. The waiver did not amend the standing rules for any other occasion, and after adoption the point of order was gone for that consideration.

Debate on the previous question on a rule carried weight out of proportion to the modest place that step occupied in a classroom diagram, because ordering the previous question ended debate on the rule and brought the House to a vote on adoption without amendment to the rule. The minority's principal opportunity to alter the terms was to defeat the previous question, a result that would allow the member leading the opposition to offer an amendment to the rule. Such a defeat was rare in the period covered by the counts in this section, because a majority that had assembled a rule through its leadership and its Rules Committee ordinarily held together on the procedural vote. A special rule, however, governed only a minority of the measures that received floor action. The Congressional Research Service, in snapshot reports titled How Legislation Is Brought to the House Floor, recorded for the 114th Congress that of bills and joint resolutions receiving initial floor action, 78 percent came up under suspension of the rules, 18 percent came up under a special rule from the Rules Committee, and 5 percent came up by unanimous consent. The percentages described initial action on bills and joint resolutions, not every motion or item of business that occupied the floor.

### How does suspension of the rules change what can pass?

Suspension limits debate to forty minutes, bars floor amendments, and requires two thirds of members voting for passage, so a measure passes only with broad support assembled before consideration, while a measure with intense minority opposition or unsettled text fails or never reaches the suspension calendar.

The motion to suspend the rules and pass a measure, described in House Rule XV, clause 2 in the durable structure of the standing rules, combined several actions into a single motion. Adoption suspended the standing rules that would otherwise block immediate consideration, set aside the ordinary requirements for committee reporting and calendar placement to the extent necessary for the occasion, fixed a short debate, excluded amendments from the floor, and put the question on passage under a two thirds threshold in the same motion. The Speaker's recognition determined which members could offer the motion, and in practice the Speaker recognized members, ordinarily committee leaders or designees, for motions that leadership had scheduled for a suspension period, commonly early in a legislative week. Forty minutes of debate, divided between a proponent and an opponent where an opponent came forward to claim the time, placed a hard limit on the floor examination a suspension measure received. No amendment, no substitute, and no motion to recommit with instructions reshaped the text during that debate. The text that entered the suspension process was the text that left the process, either passed and sent to the Senate or failed to reach two thirds. Managers therefore had a strong incentive to settle objections before the measure was listed and to remove provisions that a sizable bloc opposed.

The two thirds requirement rewrote the coalition arithmetic that governed passage. A measure under a special rule needed a majority of members voting, a quorum being present, and a coalition built to that threshold could afford to lose a large minority. A measure under suspension needed a supermajority of members voting, and a coalition built to that threshold had to include a substantial share of the minority party in most circumstances, because a majority party acting alone rarely supplied two thirds of the votes cast. Drafting reflected the arithmetic. Provisions that mobilized intense opposition from more than a third of the chamber endangered a suspension measure, even where the provisions pleased a majority. Provisions that attracted broad, low intensity agreement, such as a naming, a technical correction, a reauthorization with settled terms, or a local measure whose benefits were widely distributed and whose costs drew no organized resistance, fit the suspension calendar with little adjustment. The procedure rewarded measures whose support was broad and shallow rather than narrow and intense, because intensity on the opposing side counted heavily where a third plus one of members voting could defeat the motion.

The volume of law that originated through that boundary was larger than the modest profile of individual suspension measures suggested. The Congressional Research Service Report R46364, titled Suspension of the Rules: House Practice in the 115th Congress, recorded that 952 measures were initially considered under suspension in the 115th Congress, which sat from 2017 to 2019. From that suspension stream, 316 measures were presented to the President, and 315 bills and one joint resolution from that stream were enacted. The 316 measures presented to the President therefore represented the portion of the suspension stream that cleared both chambers, and the enactment count showed that the suspension route was not a ceremonial side path. Hundreds of statutes in a single Congress began floor life under a procedure that allowed forty minutes of debate and no amendments, because the texts had been negotiated to a breadth of support that made extended floor examination unnecessary in the judgment of the managers who scheduled the measures. Suspension also gave the minority a practical check that a special rule did not supply, because minority votes were mathematically necessary in most cases, and objections communicated through minority review could cause a measure to be withdrawn from a list, revised, or rerouted.

Unanimous consent supplied a faster route for the routine remainder that did not require even the formal structure of a suspension motion. A member, ordinarily a manager acting with leadership clearance, asked unanimous consent that a measure be considered as read, that debate be limited or dispensed with under an arrangement described in the request, or that the House take a specified action without the steps the standing rules would otherwise require. No member objected, and the action occurred. A single objection blocked the request, after which the measure either waited, moved through a different route, or received a revised request that answered the objection. Requests of that kind handled corrections, measures whose passage had been arranged in every detail, and Senate amendments where the House response had been negotiated before the request was made. Voice votes completed the picture. After debate under suspension or under a consent arrangement, the chair put the question by voice, members answered in chorus, and the chair announced the judgment of the House. For many consent matters, the voice result stood, and the absence of a recorded vote did not imply the absence of a decision.

Taken together, the routes described in this section revealed the House as a majoritarian institution whose procedures converted a scheduling decision by leadership into the effective decision on most measures. Majority control of the Rules Committee produced special rules that a majority could adopt, and adoption of a special rule ordinarily predicted passage of the underlying measure in substantially the form the rule protected, because the same coalition that sustained the rule had the votes to sustain the measure and because the rule denied opponents the amendment votes that might have split that coalition. Leadership control of the suspension calendar produced a list of measures pretested for two thirds support, and listing a measure on that calendar ordinarily predicted passage in the House by a wide margin, because a measure that could not reach two thirds had reason to be withdrawn before a public failure. In each route, the consequential sorting occurred before the formal vote, where participants applied the threshold and the amendment terms of the expected route to the text under discussion. The floor gate was a set of doors, each door opened by a different key and each passage governed by different terms, and the choice of door, made by leadership through the Rules Committee, through the suspension calendar, or through consent clearance, was in most cases the decision that the later vote confirmed. Floor votes recorded the decision in a form that the Constitution and the standing rules recognized, and a rule could fail, a suspension motion could fall short, and a consent request could draw an objection, but those possibilities operated within a structure that assigned the initiative to leadership and the threshold to the route selected.

## The Senate's Consent Regime: Unanimous Consent and the Price of Objection

The Senate conducts almost all of its business under unanimous consent agreements, and that single structural fact explains more about the fate of legislation in the chamber than any vote count. The written rules of the Senate grant individual members rights that the House long ago subordinated to majority control: debate carries no general time limit, amendments need not be germane to the pending text in most circumstances, and a wide range of motions remain open to extended discussion. A chamber organized on those terms cannot proceed from item to item by majority command alone, because at nearly every step a determined member holds a procedural right to slow the proceeding down. Consent is the lubricant that lets such a chamber function. When consent is withheld, the chamber becomes an exercise in arithmetic measured not in votes but in hours.

A unanimous consent agreement is a negotiated order for conducting business, proposed on the floor and adopted when no senator objects. Leaders of the two parties negotiate the terms in advance, usually through their floor staffs, and the majority leader or a designee then propounds the request in the chamber. A typical agreement fixes when a measure will be taken up, how long debate will run, which amendments may be offered and in what order, when votes will occur, and whether certain procedural motions will be waived or deemed made. Adoption requires no tally. The presiding officer states the request, asks whether objection is heard, and treats silence as agreement. From that moment the arrangement binds the chamber as an order of the Senate, enforceable by the chair, unless the Senate later alters it by another consent agreement.

The negotiation behind a consent request runs through the party leadership structures because leaders control the commodity that consent protects: floor time. The majority leader holds the right of first recognition from the presiding officer, a precedent that gives the leader priority in offering motions and amendments and in setting the sequence of business. The minority leader holds no equivalent scheduling power, yet holds something nearly as valuable, the capacity to deliver or withhold the cooperation of the minority in the clearance process. Between them, their staffs assemble the terms under which a proposal reaches the floor, trading commitments on amendment opportunities, debate allocations, and voting times. By the time a request reaches the floor, the public propounding is usually a formality that ratifies a bargain already struck in private. The floor moment matters because it is the point at which any member may break the bargain's premise by objecting.

Before a request is propounded, leadership tests the ground through a clearance procedure known as the hotline. Under the hotline process, the majority and minority cloakrooms and leadership staffs circulate a description of the pending request to every Senate office, specifying the measure involved, the action proposed, and a deadline by which objections must be registered. Offices respond with clearance, with questions, or with an objection. Silence within the stated window is treated as acquiescence, a convention that lets the system process a large volume of routine business without requiring affirmative replies from one hundred offices. The process is informal, resting on staff practice and leadership custom rather than on any rule of the chamber, yet its results govern the floor schedule as reliably as a printed calendar governs the House.

The hotline reveals the distinction between support and clearance, a distinction at the heart of Senate practice. Support records a senator's position on the merits of a proposal. Clearance records whether a senator will permit the proposal to proceed without consuming contested floor time. A member may support a measure warmly and refuse clearance over an unrelated dispute, a missing amendment opportunity, or a demand for information from an agency or a committee. Another member may doubt a measure's merits and clear it anyway, judging the question not worth the chamber's hours or preferring a recorded vote to a procedural fight. Leadership reads hotline responses accordingly, as measurements of friction rather than as whip counts. A proposal with broad support and one unresolved objection is, for scheduling purposes, a blocked proposal, while a proposal with lukewarm support and no objection is a candidate for passage before the evening ends. The schedule follows the friction, not the enthusiasm.

### How can one senator hold up a bill?

A senator places a hold by notifying party leadership of an intent to object to a unanimous consent request. The objection does not defeat the measure. It denies leaders the fast route, forces formal floor procedures, and makes the majority spend scarce debate time that other business also needs.

The hold is the instrument through which that intent travels. A hold is an informal notice, delivered to a senator's party leader, that the senator intends to object if a specified consent request is propounded. No rule of the Senate creates the hold, no form records it in the chamber's journal, and no vote sustains or overturns it. Leadership honors holds as a matter of practice because the notice forecasts a real event: an objection on the floor that will occur if the request proceeds. Holds have been placed by members of both parties for reasons ranging from substantive opposition to leverage in negotiations over amendments, nominations, and agency action. The essential character of the device has remained constant across those variations. A hold converts one member's private intention into a scheduling fact that the entire chamber must plan around.

The power of a hold derives from the price of overriding the objection it predicts, and that price is denominated in floor time. Floor time is the Senate's scarcest resource because the chamber transacts all of its contested business in a single forum, in sequence, with a limited number of legislative days in any session. Every hour spent forcing one measure past an objection is an hour withheld from appropriations bills, nominations, treaties, and every other item competing for the same hours. A hold therefore confronts the majority leader with a calculation rather than a barrier. The leader may honor the hold and negotiate, clearing the objection through an amendment, a commitment, a side agreement, or delay that lets tempers and circumstances change. The leader may test the hold by propounding the request anyway, calling the bluff of a member unwilling to object in public. Or the leader may route around the objection through formal procedure, accepting the time cost as the price of the measure. Each response spends a different currency, patience, credibility, or hours, and the choice among them is the daily substance of Senate leadership.

That calculation explains why a measure with ninety-nine supporters can wait for weeks while a measure with no organized opposition moves in an evening. The ninety-nine have supplied votes, and votes settle only the final question of passage. They have not supplied time, because no accumulation of supporters shortens the procedural road when consent is denied and the formal road runs through debatable motions and staged votes. The lone objector, by contrast, need not persuade anyone, assemble a coalition, or win a single procedural vote. The objector merely declines to lend the consent that makes the fast road available, and thereby transfers the cost of proceeding onto the majority's calendar. Meanwhile the unopposed measure, cleared through the hotline with no objection registered, rides a consent agreement that fixes a brief debate or none at all and passes by voice vote at an hour when the floor holds a handful of senators. Under the consent theory, both outcomes follow from the same rule: the Senate moves at the speed of its least willing member unless the majority pays to move faster.

Time agreements are the structured form that consent takes when a measure is important enough to debate but settled enough to schedule. A time agreement allocates debate in fixed blocks, often divided between the managers of the measure or between the parties, enumerates the amendments that may be offered, sets the order in which they will be considered, and specifies when voting begins. Such an agreement may waive points of order, deem certain amendments adopted or tabled upon stated conditions, and provide that the final vote on passage occur at a named hour without further intervening action. Senators accept these constraints because the agreement purchases certainty in return: sponsors gain a guaranteed path to a vote, opponents gain guaranteed opportunities to be heard and to force votes on their amendments, and leaders gain a predictable block on the calendar around which other business can be arranged.

Time agreements also expose the leverage that consent confers on individual members during negotiation. Because an agreement requires the absence of objection, any senator may condition clearance on terms: time to speak, a vote on a named amendment, a commitment that a conference report will carry or exclude particular language, or a delay that accommodates a negotiation elsewhere. Leaders assembling an agreement therefore bargain sequentially with potential objectors, and the final text of the agreement often records the residue of those bargains in its amendment list and its exceptions. Reading a time agreement closely thus reconstructs the bargaining map of a measure more faithfully than the ensuing debate does, since the debate performs decisions that the agreement has already made. Both parties' leaders have built agreements on these terms, and both parties' members have extracted the concessions that consent makes available.

When consent is unavailable and the majority decides that a measure justifies the expense, the Senate falls back on its formal procedures, and the fallback is deliberately costly. The process begins with a motion to proceed to consideration of the measure, a motion that is itself debatable and therefore itself exposed to extended opposition. The majority leader offers the motion, debate follows without the limits a consent agreement would have imposed, and ending that debate requires the chamber's supermajority machinery. Under Rule XXII, cloture on most legislation requires sixty votes in a full Senate, the figure at the center of [the filibuster and cloture threshold](/2022/02/15/filibuster-and-cloture-explained/), and invoking it consumes days rather than hours because the rule interposes waiting periods between the filing of a cloture motion, the vote upon it, and the disposition that follows a successful invocation. The formal path therefore functions as an exception route, available for measures the majority ranks above nearly everything else on its calendar, and impractical as a general method of legislating. Leaders invoke it sparingly, and the sparing use is itself evidence of the consent regime's dominance.

Filling the amendment tree is the companion instrument the majority leader uses to control a measure once the formal path or a contested consent negotiation brings it to the floor. The amendment process in the Senate can be pictured as a structure of branches, a limited set of amendment positions that may be pending at one time, arranged by precedent in a definite order. Because the majority leader enjoys priority of recognition, the leader may offer amendments that occupy each open branch in sequence, including amendments whose substantive content matters less than the space they occupy. With the tree filled, no other senator can offer an amendment until a branch opens, which occurs when an amendment is disposed of or when the leader yields. The practice serves scheduling ends: it prevents surprise amendments from hijacking a negotiated sequence, it protects fragile compromises from unraveling votes, and it gives the leader a valve through which amendments are admitted one at a time on negotiated terms. Leaders of both parties have filled the tree. As a scheduling instrument its meaning is neutral and precise: control of recognition becomes control of the amendment agenda, and control of the amendment agenda becomes control of time.

Between the grand bargains and the exceptional cloture fights, routine Senate passage wears a quieter texture, and quorum calls and voice votes supply most of it. A quorum call begins when a senator suggests the absence of a quorum, prompting the clerk to call the roll of the chamber. In routine practice the call rarely aims to assemble a majority of senators in the room. Instead it idles the floor in a suspended state while negotiations continue in the cloakrooms and corridors, giving leaders time to finish a consent agreement, giving staff time to draft its language, and giving a senator time to reach the floor for a debate segment or a vote. The leader or floor manager then asks unanimous consent to dispense with the quorum call, and the chamber resumes business as though a pause button had been released. Participants understand the interval as the chamber's working margin, the blank space in the schedule where the agreements that structure everything else are actually completed.

Voice votes complete the texture. When a measure cleared by consent reaches its final question, the presiding officer puts the question to the chamber and judges the result from the volume of ayes and noes called out in response. No roll is called, no tally is recorded, and the names of individual senators take no part in the record of the decision. Amendments under a time agreement frequently fall or stand the same way, as do procedural motions, conference reports on uncontroversial measures, and the great mass of resolutions, commemorative items, and minor bills that form the bulk of enacted law by count. The voice vote is thus the audible signature of the consent regime: a decision rendered without counting, in a chamber whose rules make counting expensive, on a question whose answer negotiation had established before the question was ever put.

The contrast with the House clarifies the regime by inversion. In the House, a disciplined majority commands the floor through the Rules Committee and the special rules it reports, which fix debate time and the amendment menu in advance and which the majority adopts by majority vote. The operative question in the House is therefore whether a majority exists and holds together, because a majority that holds can schedule, structure, and pass a measure on a timetable of its choosing. In the Senate, no equivalent instrument exists. A special rule cannot be imposed, debate cannot be limited by simple majority command under the standing rules, and the previous question, the motion by which the House ends debate, has no Senate counterpart. The operative question in the Senate is consequently not whether a majority exists but whether any single senator prices objection above the value of the floor time that proceeding would consume. A majority of senators favoring a measure establishes only that the measure would pass if the Senate reached the question. Consent, or the expensive machinery that substitutes for consent, determines whether the question is reached at all, and when, and after how many other priorities have been displaced to pay for the reaching.

Senators and leaders adapt their strategies to that operative question so thoroughly that the adaptation is easy to mistake for the natural order of legislating. Sponsors draft with clearance in mind, consulting likely objectors before introduction rather than after passage stalls. Leaders sequence the calendar to harvest cleared measures in batches, reserving contested items for the rare stretches when the calendar can bear the cost of the formal path. Objectors calibrate the price of their consent, asking enough to make the objection worth registering and not so much that the majority chooses to pay the procedural price instead and leave the objector with nothing. The classroom diagram of a bill becoming law, with its orderly progression from introduction to committee to floor to enrollment, describes the stations on the route faithfully. The consent regime supplies the missing element the diagram cannot draw: the negotiation that decides which travelers may use the road, and the price, charged in hours, to those who travel it over an objection.

## Two Chambers, One Text: How Differences Are Actually Resolved

Article I, section 7 imposes a condition that sounds simple and operates as a demanding engineering problem. A measure cannot be presented to the President until the House and the Senate have passed it in identical form. Every word, comma placement in enrolled text aside, every number, and every cross reference must match. Two separately elected chambers, organized under separate rules, led by separate majorities in many Congresses, and answerable to different constituencies and calendars, must converge on a single document. Neither chamber can impose that document on the other. Neither chamber can waive the requirement for the other. A vote in one chamber, however large the margin, completes only half the task, and a vote in both chambers completes nothing at all if the two votes approved different language.

The difficulty compounds because passage rarely produces symmetry. A bill that cleared the House after a structured rule, with amendments closed off and debate timed to the minute, arrived in the Senate as a starting point rather than a settlement. Senators amended freely, or a Senate committee reported a substitute that replaced the House text after the enacting clause. The reverse sequence produced the same result in mirror image. By the time both chambers had acted, Congress often held two plausible versions of one policy, each carrying the legitimacy of a floor vote, and neither eligible for enrollment. Resolving differences was therefore not an administrative afterthought. It was the stage at which a measure either acquired the single form the Constitution required or stalled between two forms that each commanded support in exactly one place.

That structural fact explains why resolution deserves attention equal to passage. A majority in one chamber demonstrated that a proposal could survive one set of rules, one committee system, and one political calculation. Bicameral resolution tested whether the same language could be made acceptable to both institutions at once. A provision inserted to secure the final votes for initial passage could become the provision that made final passage impossible. The process of closing those gaps, by formal conference, by amendment exchange, or by one chamber accepting the other version entire, determined the content of the law as surely as any committee markup.

### Are conference committees still how Congress settles differences?

No. Formal conferences settled most major bicameral disagreements in earlier Congresses, but leaders in the Congresses examined in the sources cited here turned increasingly to amendment exchange between the houses, informal negotiation before any formal step, and acceptance of the other chamber text outright when time and votes allowed no safer route.

The classroom account begins with the conference committee, and the account has a solid basis in practice over a long span of congressional history. When the House and the Senate passed different versions of the same measure, the two chambers agreed to meet in conference. Each chamber appointed conferees, usually drawn from the committee or committees that had reported the legislation, with party ratios reflecting the chamber majority. The conferees met, formally and in staff sessions, and negotiated a compromise confined in principle to the matters in disagreement between the two versions. The product was a conference report: a single agreed text, accompanied by a joint explanatory statement describing how the differences had been settled. The report returned to both chambers in identical form. Neither chamber could amend it. Each chamber could accept it or reject it, and acceptance by both produced the identical text that enrollment and presentment required.

The design had evident virtues, and an account of its decline that treats the institution as mere ceremony misses why it endured. Conferees were specialists. Members who had sat through hearings, markups, and floor debate carried a measure to conclusion, and the committee system retained authority at the final stage. The conference report created one public document that embodied the settlement. A reader could examine the agreed language and the explanatory statement in one place and trace which chamber position had prevailed, which had yielded, and which compromise language had replaced both. Accountability had an address. Conferees signed the report, and majorities of the conferees from each chamber had to agree before the report could be filed.

Measured against that design, the contraction in formal conferences was steep. Counts compiled from House and Senate records, in an analysis by the Center for Congressional and Presidential Studies at American University, showed conferences falling from 38 in the 106th Congress, covering 1999 to 2000, to 9 in the 110th Congress, covering 2007 to 2008. The figures count formal conferences, and precision about the category matters. Informal meetings among members and staff continued, negotiations between the chambers continued, and settlements continued to be reached. What contracted was a specific institution: the appointed, authorized conference that produced a report under the rules governing conferees and conference reports. A decline from 38 to 9 in less than a decade represented not a marginal adjustment in habit but a change in the default machinery of bicameral settlement.

The numbers also require careful reading because a fall in conferences did not mean a fall in legislation resolved between the chambers. Other machinery absorbed the work. The Congressional Research Service, in Report RL34611 on methods of bicameral resolution, found that roughly 11 to 24 percent of public laws enacted in the Congresses from 1992 to 2007 were resolved by amendments between the houses, the route commonly described as ping pong. That share, the report made clear in substance, understated the significance of the route. Significance rose faster than the count. A raw percentage treats a minor enactment and a major authorization as equal units, one law each. When large, contested, expensive measures began traveling the amendment exchange route in greater proportion, the institutional weight of the method grew even in Congresses where its share of total public laws moved within a modest band. Counting laws answered one question. Tracking which laws, and how much policy those laws carried, answered a more consequential one.

Amendment exchange works through messages, and the formal steps are plain. Suppose the Senate passed a measure, the House amended the Senate text or substituted language of its own, and the House returned the measure to the Senate with that amendment. The Senate could concur in the House amendment and end the matter. The Senate could concur with a further amendment, returning the measure to the House and narrowing or reopening the disagreement. The House could insist on its position, disagree to the Senate amendment, amend again, or accept the Senate language outright. Each action traveled between the chambers as a formal message, and each message required floor action in the receiving chamber unless unanimous consent or an agreed procedure disposed of it. The sequence could run for a single exchange on an uncontroversial measure or extend through repeated exchanges on a contested one, with the text growing closer at each pass or hardening at each pass depending on the state of negotiation behind the messages.

The messages, however, were seldom the negotiation itself. In practice, the negotiation happened before a message was sent. Leaders in both chambers, committee chairs and ranking members, and the staff who served them tested language privately, counted votes against drafts that had not been offered, and identified the amendment a receiving chamber could accept without another return trip. A formal message sent without that preparation risked rejection on the floor, and a rejected message consumed time and exposed divisions. Staff drafting played a central part. Legislative counsel rendered agreed concepts into statutory language, committee staff checked the language against programs within a committee jurisdiction, and leadership staff verified that no provision reopened a dispute that another part of the bargain had closed. By the time a chamber voted to concur, much of the vote had in effect been negotiated into predictability.

Informal pre-conference negotiation filled the space that earlier practice assigned to the conference itself. Rather than appoint conferees and convene a formal conference, the principals negotiated as an unstructured group: the Speaker and the Senate majority leader or their designees, the relevant committee leaders from both chambers, and senior staff empowered to trade language within limits set by the principals. Leadership summits of this kind handled entire packages, linking a disputed authorization level in one title to a disputed effective date in another. Because the group had no formal membership, its composition could expand or contract with the subject. Because it produced no conference report, its product emerged as an amendment, a substitute, or an agreement by one chamber to take up the other chamber text.

Discussions of [conference committees and amendment exchange](/2022/03/15/conference-committees-explained/) often treat the choice between the two routes as a preference about venue, but the procedural consequences diverged in ways that shaped outcomes. A formal conference required threshold steps in both chambers, and each step created an opening for delay or objection. In the Senate, merely going to conference could require a series of motions and unanimous consent agreements, and a single objection could block a consent request. Senators who opposed a measure, or who opposed the expected direction of a conference, could contest the appointment of conferees, the motions to proceed to conference, and related requests, consuming floor time before negotiation produced anything at all. Amendment exchange avoided part of that exposure. A leader who held an amendment already messaged from the other chamber could seek consent to dispose of it, or bring it forward under terms negotiated in advance, without first winning a separate procedural campaign for permission to negotiate.

Control of text supplied a second reason leaders preferred the exchange route. A conference, once appointed, possessed a defined measure and a defined scope, but conferees retained room to bargain within that scope, and a majority of conferees from each chamber signed the result. Leadership influence over conferees was real, especially through appointment, yet the institution distributed authority among committee leaders and members with subject jurisdiction. Amendment exchange concentrated authority. The leader who framed the amendment sent between the chambers framed the question the receiving chamber answered. Language arrived as a settled package, drafted under leadership direction, and the receiving chamber faced a choice closer to acceptance or rejection than to open revision, because amending the package again restarted the exchange and risked the bargain. For a majority leadership managing a crowded calendar and narrow margins, that concentration reduced uncertainty. The text that leaders had counted votes against was the text that reached the floor, and surprises introduced by an autonomous negotiating body were correspondingly fewer.

A third reason involved time. Formal conferences proceeded measure by measure, and a conference on a major bill could sit while unrelated disputes among the same principals blocked agreement. Amendment exchange allowed leaders to clear settled items quickly, isolate the provisions that remained contested, and sequence votes around other floor business. The exchange could be timed to a recess deadline, a funding lapse date, or the availability of members whose votes the count required. None of these advantages belonged to one party. Leaders of both parties, in both chambers, across the Congresses covered by the cited counts, reached for the exchange route when control, speed, and procedural economy pointed in the same direction.

The Affordable Care Act illustrated the logic at its most consequential, at the strength the record supports. The measure was finalized without a conference. In March 2010, the House accepted the Senate-passed text, passing that language so that an identical version existed and presentment could proceed. A separate reconciliation measure then adjusted the enacted framework, carrying changes that the two chambers agreed through the reconciliation process rather than reopening the principal text in conference. The sequence avoided the risk that a conference report, returned to the Senate under the conditions then obtaining, would fail or stall, and it avoided any need to alter a single word of the Senate language in the principal measure, because any alteration would have required the Senate to pass the measure again. Whatever judgment history assigns to the policy, the procedure demonstrated the central point about method: when leaders concluded that the formal route endangered the settlement, another route that satisfied the identical text requirement could be assembled from acceptance and a sidecar measure.

Acceptance outright, the simplest resolution of all, deserves separate attention because the amendment exchange statistics can obscure it. In that pattern, a chamber received the other chamber version, judged amendment futile or dangerous, and passed the received text without change. No conference met and no compromise language was drafted, because the receiving chamber treated the cost of reopening the text as higher than the cost of provisions its members would have written differently. Deadline pressure produced many such decisions, and so did vote arithmetic. A coalition assembled for a specific Senate or House text could fracture if the text moved. Acceptance was therefore not necessarily agreement in substance. It was often a calculated judgment that a flawed enacted measure outranked a preferred unenacted one.

Evenhanded assessment requires naming what the decline of conference removed, because the exchange route solved real problems and imposed real costs at the same time. A conference report was a public artifact of a particular kind. It gathered the settlement into a single negotiated document, filed for both chambers, and paired the text with an explanatory statement that assigned meaning to the compromises it contained. Amendment exchange scattered the record. The final language might rest in an amendment adopted in one chamber, explained in floor statements of varying completeness, and illuminated by no single joint document that both chambers had approved as an account of the bargain. Members voting at the end of an exchange sometimes voted on language whose negotiating history lived in leadership offices rather than in a filed statement.

Conferee accountability diminished along with the report. Conferees were named, their signatures appeared on the report, and the requirement that a majority of each chamber delegation sign gave the institution a roster of members answerable for the result. The minority party held conference seats in proportion that guaranteed its presence in the room even when the majority held the votes to prevail there. Informal negotiation had no equivalent roster. A leadership summit could include minority voices or exclude them, could rest on committee expertise or bypass it, and could produce a settlement whose authorship was diffuse by design. Members outside the negotiating circle then confronted the product late, under time pressure, with amendment opportunities limited by the structure of the exchange.

Set against those losses, the case for the exchange route rested on governability rather than on secrecy for its own sake. Conferences had stalled measures, produced reports that one chamber then rejected, and consumed floor time in appointment fights. Leaders who turned to amendment exchange could point to enacted results: identical text achieved, presentment reached, and programs authorized or funded before deadlines that a formal conference might have missed. Both parties made that argument when in the majority and regretted the method when in the minority, a symmetry that suggests the route answered structural pressures rather than the preferences of a single leadership. Narrow margins and a Senate in which almost all business moved by unanimous consent rewarded whoever could minimize procedural openings, and amendment exchange minimized them by design.

The closing stage, once identical text existed by any route, was enrollment, and its formality was deliberate. The passed text was enrolled, printed on parchment standard paper by the originating chamber, in the form in which both chambers had approved it. The Speaker signed the enrolled measure, and the President of the Senate or the President pro tempore signed it, attesting that the document presented was the document passed. The enrolled measure then went to the President for action under Article I, section 7. Enrollment changed nothing in the language and negotiated nothing. Its function was certification: after conference reports, messages, concurrences, and acceptances had done the political work of convergence, enrollment fixed the converged text in a single authoritative copy. The engineering problem that opened the process, two chambers and one text, closed with a signature on the one text itself.

## Vehicles: Why Major Law Rides on Measures That Must Pass

A vehicle is a measure whose passage has become close to unavoidable, and whose text therefore becomes a place to put other text. The word describes a function rather than a category in any rulebook. Only a few measures in any session carry the combination that makes attachment attractive: a date after which inaction carries a visible cost, a default outcome that enough members refuse to accept, and a coalition broad enough that walking away means accepting blame for failure of the whole. A proposal attached to such a measure borrows all three properties. It has to survive inside a package that leadership, committees, the other chamber, and the President have converging reasons to finish.

The deadline is the first element, and it works because it converts delay into a decision. A freestanding measure that never reaches the floor has simply not been acted on, and the distinction between rejection and neglect remains blurry for most observers. A measure tied to a date certain behaves differently. When a fiscal year ends, when an authorization expires, when a borrowing limit is reached, the absence of a law becomes an event with concrete consequences: agencies without appropriations, programs without authority, a government unable to pay obligations already incurred. The deadline does not guarantee agreement on content. It guarantees that disagreement has a price, and that the price rises as the date approaches.

The default consequence is the second element, and it explains why some deadlines bind while others slip without effect. Congress sets many dates that pass quietly, because the underlying authority continues, because an extension is routine, or because no constituency notices the lapse at once. A binding vehicle rests on a default that pivotal actors consider worse than any plausible compromise. For appropriations, the default is a gap in funding. For a debt limit measure, the default involves doubt about payment of obligations the government has already taken on. The severity of the default determines the strength of the vehicle, because a faction willing to accept the default can credibly threaten to block the package.

The coalition is the third element, and it is the least visible in the formal record. A must pass measure is not simply a measure with many supporters. It is a measure whose supporters include actors who cannot easily defect, because the underlying function matters to their own priorities, their own committees, or their own states and districts. Defense authorization draws strength from members who disagree sharply about weapons systems and personnel policy, yet agree that an annual defense policy law should be enacted. Appropriations draw strength from the fact that every member represents a place served by federal agencies. Attachment exploits that breadth. A provision that could command only a narrow majority gains protection from a coalition assembled for entirely different reasons.

Appropriations supply the most regular fleet of vehicles because funding decisions recur on a fixed cycle, and because the twelve annual appropriations bills cover, between them, nearly every corner of the federal government. In an orderly version of the process, each of the twelve moves as a separate law, written by a separate subcommittee, debated on its own terms. In the completed fiscal years recorded in this series, the twelve repeatedly arrived as consolidated packages instead. Floor time is finite, Senate consent is fragile, and each separate bill offers a separate opportunity for delay, amendment fights, and a veto threat. Consolidation reduces twelve passages and twelve presentments into a far smaller number of decisions, each larger and harder to refuse.

Fiscal 2020 supplied a clean illustration. All twelve annual appropriations bills were enacted in two consolidated packages, the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2020, assigned Public Law 116-93, and the Further Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2020, assigned Public Law 116-94, both signed on December 20, 2019. Twelve subcommittee products, covering defense and diplomacy, health and education, energy and agriculture, transportation and the legislative branch itself, became law through two signatures. The structure of [omnibus bills and must-pass lawmaking](/2022/04/15/omnibus-bills-and-riders/) is visible in that pairing: the individual bills existed, their allocations had been negotiated in committee, but enactment traveled by consolidation. A provision inside either package was judged as part of a funding settlement, not in isolation.

Fiscal 2021 extended the same logic one step further. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021, assigned Public Law 116-260 and signed on December 27, 2020, combined all twelve annual appropriations bills with pandemic relief legislation in a single act. Funding the government for a full fiscal year and delivering emergency economic relief were distinct undertakings, with distinct committees, constituencies, and controversies. Enactment joined them anyway, because joining them solved a scheduling and coalition problem that separation could not. Each half strengthened the other as a vehicle. The appropriations half carried the deadline of expiring funding. The relief half carried a constituency far beyond the usual appropriations audience. A member voting against the act had to vote against both.

Continuing resolutions occupy the lower end of the same fleet. A continuing resolution keeps agencies funded for a limited period, generally at a rate tied to a prior year, when the regular appropriations process has not finished by the start of a fiscal year. A continuing resolution is deliberately temporary, but as a vehicle it has distinctive properties. It is negotiated under acute time pressure, often against the immediate prospect of a funding lapse. Its temporary character lowers the cost of including provisions that members would examine more slowly in a permanent law, because an anomaly or an extension of an expiring authority can be defended as a bridge measure. The brevity of the extension also limits the cargo, since a provision needing a durable statutory home gains only weeks or months.

The annual defense authorization demonstrates that authorization measures can acquire must pass status through repetition and institutional commitment. The Senate Committee on Armed Services recorded the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2022, assigned Public Law 117-81 and signed on December 27, 2021, as the 61st consecutive annual defense authorization enactment. Sixty one consecutive enactments describe a practice, not a coincidence. The defense authorization sets policy for military personnel, weapons acquisition, construction, benefits, and the organization of the armed forces, and its annual rhythm gives both armed services committees a predictable legislative season. That predictability makes the measure a vehicle. Proposals for defense policy are written with that measure in mind from the beginning, rather than drafted as freestanding bills in hope of floor time that may never arrive.

Periodic reauthorizations extend the vehicle pattern across longer cycles. Surface transportation law, farm legislation, and aviation law each return to Congress on a multi year rhythm because the underlying statutes were written with expiration dates, formula periods, or program horizons that force a return visit. Farm legislation gathers nutrition programs, commodity supports, conservation, credit, and rural development into one recurring negotiation. Surface transportation reauthorization gathers highway, transit, and safety programs whose funding formulas matter to every state. Aviation reauthorization carries the operating authority and safety framework for a national air system. In each case the scheduled expiration does the same work as the annual appropriations deadline, only more slowly, and provisions that missed one reauthorization are redrafted for the next.

Debt limit measures form an occasional and more volatile class of vehicle. An increase or suspension of the statutory borrowing limit authorizes no program and appropriates no money; it permits the Treasury to finance obligations that earlier laws created. That narrow function might suggest a narrow vote, but the consequence of failure has made the measure difficult to treat as ordinary legislation, and both parties, in different Congresses, have attached conditions, budget provisions, and unrelated priorities to debt limit action. The vehicle's strength and its danger come from the same source. Because the default is so severe, the pressure to finish is intense, yet a faction willing to approach the deadline gains bargaining power out of proportion to its numbers.

What attachment does to a provision's politics can be stated plainly. A measure that could never win a standalone vote becomes nearly impossible to strip from a vehicle the government needs, because the vote to strip it is never a vote on the provision alone. Removing text from a consolidated appropriations act near a funding deadline risks unraveling allocations and interchamber bargains that were struck as a set. Objecting to a single title of a defense authorization means objecting in the shadow of the other titles that organize and direct the armed forces. The provision is protected not by enthusiasm for its merits, but by the cost of reopening the package. Vehicle politics therefore reward placing text where the surrounding measure carries it, and keeping that text inside through final passage.

Riders, the customary name for provisions attached to a vehicle that reach beyond the vehicle's central subject, encounter different procedural terrain in the two chambers, and the asymmetry matters for where cargo can be loaded. In the House, a germaneness rule governs amendments, so an amendment must relate to the subject under consideration, and appropriations measures carry additional restrictions on legislating and on unauthorized appropriations under House rules. In the Senate, no general germaneness requirement applies to amendments on most legislation, so senators may offer amendments on subjects far from the underlying measure, subject to specific exceptions for appropriations measures after cloture, for budget measures under statutory rules, and for measures considered under special agreements. The House tends to receive riders through reported text and special rules, while the Senate can add cargo on the floor itself.

## The Discharge Petition and the Formal Route's Remaining Teeth

Against the vehicle system stands a device designed for the opposite situation: a measure with majority support that cannot reach the floor at all, because a committee will not report it or because leadership will not schedule it. The discharge petition is the House's formal answer to gatekeeping from inside the gate. Under House Rule XV, clause 2, members may sign a petition to discharge a committee from consideration of a measure, and if the petition gathers the required signatures, the measure can be called up over the objection of the committee and the majority leadership that controls the calendar. The device is deliberately difficult, since it requires majority party members, in most realistic cases, to sign against the scheduling decisions of their own leadership.

The history of the rule measures its intended rarity. A precursor discharge rule followed the 1910 revolt against Speaker Joseph Cannon, an episode that weakened the Speaker's control over the Rules Committee and over recognition on the floor. The rule took its essential form in 1931. In 1935, the signature requirement was raised to an absolute majority of the House, 218 signatures. For decades after that change, signatures remained secret until the requirement was reached, a design that protected signers but prevented organizers from proving momentum in public. A rules change in 1993 made signers public as they signed, converting the petition into a visible count that other members could track signature by signature.

The Congressional Research Service count for the long middle period of the rule supplies the statistical frame. From 1931 to 2002, 563 discharge petitions were filed. Of that total, 47 obtained the required signatures. The House voted for discharge 26 times. It passed 19 of the measures involved. Only two became law through the discharge route itself. Each step in that sequence discards most of what survived the step before it. Filing was common enough to be a routine parliamentary gesture across seven decades. Completion was uncommon, a discharge vote was rarer than completion, and enactment attributable to the discharge route itself, as opposed to enactment of a similar measure by another path, was rarest of all.

The record after that long span, with successful petitions separated by decades rather than by sessions, is consistent with that frame, and this series has described it in the same terms in its campaign finance profile. The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act reached the House floor on a discharge petition that reached 218 signatures in January 2002, and that petition stands in this series as only the second successful discharge petition since the 1980s. A successful petition required an unusual alignment: a measure with demonstrated support in both parties, a leadership unwilling to schedule it on acceptable terms, and enough majority party members willing to sign against that leadership in public. Absent any one of those conditions, a petition stalls short of 218.

### Do discharge petitions ever work?

Yes, but rarely through completion alone. Between 1931 and 2002, 563 petitions were filed, 47 reached 218 signatures, and only two measures became law through discharge itself. Petitions succeeded more often as pressure, forcing leadership to schedule action under another procedure before a floor fight occurred.

The Export-Import Bank episode of 2015 showed both the possibility and the pattern. A discharge petition in that fight reached 218 signatures in October 2015, the first completed petition since the campaign finance petition of 2002. Thirteen years separated completion from completion. The underlying dispute concerned reauthorization of a federal export credit agency whose charter had lapsed, and the petition demonstrated that a floor majority for reauthorization existed despite the refusal of the normal scheduling route to produce a vote. The ending, however, belonged to the vehicle system rather than to the discharge route in its pure form. The reauthorization was finally enacted in December 2015 inside the surface transportation act, the FAST Act, assigned Public Law 114-94.

Petitions also work indirectly, in ways the completion count understates, and the CRS record acknowledges that measures with completed or pending petitions were often taken up under other procedures, with some of those measures becoming law. The mechanism is leverage over the calendar. A petition gathering signatures in public is a forecast of a floor defeat for leadership control, delivered in installments. Each new signature is evidence that a majority is assembling outside leadership management, and leadership retains options that disappear once 218 is reached: schedule the measure under a special rule, negotiate changes to the text, or move a leadership version. A petition that ends short of 218 may therefore have accomplished its purpose if the threat of the remaining signatures produced a scheduled vote.

Measured against the vehicle system, the discharge petition is best understood as the formal route's exception that measures the rule. Its difficulty is the point. A device that any determined faction could use at will would transfer control of the floor from elected leadership to whichever group organized first, and the House, which organizes itself by majority, has never accepted that transfer for long. The 218 threshold, set in 1935, keeps the device available for the rare case of a genuine floor majority blocked by gatekeepers, while preserving gatekeeping for the ordinary run of legislation. Most measures become law by consent, by special rule, by suspension, or by vehicle because those routes are cheaper than discharge in signatures, in time, and in political exposure.

The counter reading deserves full weight, because an account that treats vehicles and leadership scheduling as the whole of lawmaking overstates the informality of a process that remains rule bound at every decisive point. The formal process is not irrelevant. Committee expertise shapes text in ways that no leadership negotiation replaces. The armed services committees accumulate knowledge of weapons programs, personnel systems, and military construction across decades of annual authorization work, and the text they report reflects that accumulation in provisions that no floor coalition could draft from scratch under deadline. Appropriations subcommittees perform the same function for agency budgets, program by program. Even when enactment travels by vehicle, the cargo was largely written and refined inside the committee system.

Rules also strike real provisions, and the strikes are part of the record of every Congress. Germaneness requirements in the House exclude amendments that reach beyond a measure's subject. Points of order under budget law and chamber rules remove or endanger provisions that would breach allocations, exceed authorizations, or legislate on an appropriations measure in violation of House Rule XXI. Senate procedure imposes its own constraints, through the treatment of extraneous matter in reconciliation measures under the Byrd rule, through the cloture threshold of three fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn, 60 in a full Senate, under Senate Rule XXII for most legislation, and through the power of a single objection to block a unanimous consent request. Negotiators bargain inside those constraints rather than around them.

Above every route stands the constitutional floor, which binds vehicles, discharge petitions, consent agreements, and leadership settlements identically. Article I, section 1 places all legislative powers in Congress. Article I, section 5 leaves each House to determine its own rules, the source of both the discharge rule and the scheduling power it challenges. Article I, section 7 requires passage by both Houses in identical form and presentment to the President before a bill becomes law, requires revenue bills to originate in the House, and permits enactment over a veto only by two thirds of both Houses. No vehicle avoids bicameral passage in identical text. No petition avoids the Senate. No leadership agreement avoids presentment or the possibility of a veto.

The accurate statement of the relationship between the two systems is therefore one of layers. The formal route is a floor on what must occur, not a description of what usually does. Committees must, in the ordinary case, produce or bless the text. Both chambers must pass the same words. The President must sign, or two thirds of both chambers must override. Those requirements are met in every enactment, and they explain why the process cannot be reduced to leadership will. What the formal diagram omits is the distribution of initiative within those requirements: which committee acts, which measure carries the text, whose consent the Senate requires, which deadline forces the settlement, and which provisions ride inside a package no member would have assembled in exactly that form.

## A Diagnostic for Any Measure: Four Questions That Predict Its Fate

A reader holding a pending measure, a news report about a proposal, or a member's announcement of an introduction can predict the likely fate of that measure with four diagnostic questions, each keyed to a gate described in the preceding sections. Committee calendars, leadership schedules, consent practice, and the contents of large enacted packages are matters of public record, and disciplined attention to those records separates a plausible forecast from a recitation of hopes. Applied in order, the four questions locate the gate at which a measure stands, and the first gate that returns a negative answer usually settles the prediction, because the gates operate in sequence and a measure rarely recovers from failure at an early one.

The first question asks whether a committee chair has scheduled the measure. Referral alone means nothing beyond delivery to a destination, and a referred measure that no chair places on a hearing calendar or a markup agenda remains an introduced text with a number. Scheduling leaves evidence: a hearing notice naming the measure and a markup session at which amendments are offered and a vote to report is taken. Silence leaves evidence of the opposite kind, since a Congress runs on a fixed two year clock and months without committee action consume the only resource no procedure can restore. A measure with a scheduled markup has cleared the gate at which most proposals end. A measure without one has a sponsor, a title, and a press release, and none of those assets counts as progress under the rules of either chamber.

The second question asks whether leadership has granted the measure floor time or a place in a package. In the House, that grant appears as a special rule from the Rules Committee structuring debate, as a place on the suspension calendar for a measure able to command two thirds, or as inclusion in a larger text that leadership has decided to move. In the Senate, the equivalent grant appears as a consent agreement setting the terms of consideration or as a majority leader's willingness to spend days on the cloture process. Floor time is the scarcest commodity in a legislative session, and leaders allocate that commodity to measures that serve a coalition, meet a deadline, or discharge an obligation. 

The third question asks whether the measure can clear the Senate's consent practice or command sixty votes. Almost all Senate business moves by unanimous consent, so the practical test for most measures is the absence of a determined objection rather than the presence of a majority. A single senator's objection, signaled in advance through the informal device of a hold, can force a measure onto the formal track, where cloture under Rule XXII requires three fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn and consumes days of floor time that leadership must take from other business. Measures with intense opposition from even a small number of senators fail the test unless a majority leader judges the prize worth the formal price. Depth of declared opposition therefore matters alongside the number of declared supporters, because under consent practice one committed objector can outweigh dozens of passive supporters.

The fourth question asks whether the measure is attached to a vehicle with a deadline. Attachment changes every calculation that precedes it. A provision folded into a consolidated appropriations act acquires the funding deadline of the fiscal year, a provision inside the annual defense authorization acquires the momentum of an enactment streak that the Senate Committee on Armed Services counted at sixty one consecutive years with the signing of the fiscal 2022 act on December 27, 2021, and a provision inside any expiring authorization acquires a constituency organized around preventing a lapse. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021, signed on December 27, 2020, combined all twelve annual appropriations bills with pandemic relief legislation in a single act, and every provision inside that act became law on the strength of the combination. A freestanding measure must generate its own schedule, its own consent, and its own urgency, against competitors that arrive with all three supplied by a carrier.

Scored together, the four answers sort pending measures into recognizable classes. A measure with a scheduled markup, a leadership grant, a clear consent path, and a place on a deadline vehicle is legislation in transit, and observers who follow [the study guide to US legislation since 1950](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/) will recognize the profile of enacted statutes across many Congresses in that combination. A measure missing two or more elements belongs to the large population of proposals that exist to stake positions, build records, and await a Congress with different gatekeepers. Researchers assembling the record of a measure across sessions can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) so that scheduling evidence, consent history, and vehicle attachments accumulate in one place rather than scattering across disconnected files. Students preparing for examinations in American government can [practice and revise US government and civics material on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) with the diagnostic in hand, testing each textbook example against the four gates instead of memorizing the sequence alone.

Integration of the two accounts resolves the tension between them. The classroom diagram is the floor: every enacted law satisfied bicameral passage in identical form, presentment, and either signature, unvetoed expiration of the presentment period, or a two thirds override in both Houses, and no forecast that ignores those requirements describes a lawful outcome. The diagnostic is the forecast: gate by gate, the four questions estimate whether a given measure will ever reach the floor that the diagram describes, and the historical record, from the 2 percent conversion rate of the 116th Congress to the collapse of formal conferences from thirty eight in the 106th Congress to nine in the 110th, shows how rarely arrival occurs and how consistently the gates predict it. Neither account replaces the other. The floor states what law is. The forecast states what lawmaking does. A citizen who holds both can read a headline about an introduced bill with the discipline that a parliamentarian brings to a calendar, asking first about chairs, schedules, consent, and carriers, and only afterward about the merits that the diagram assumes will decide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does the legislative process actually work?

In practice, the legislative process works through gates rather than through a single automatic sequence. A measure is introduced and referred to a committee, where a chair decides whether the measure receives a hearing or a markup. A measure reported by a committee then requires a scheduling decision by chamber leadership before either chamber debates it. The House usually acts through a special rule from the Rules Committee, through suspension of the rules, or by unanimous consent. The Senate conducts almost all business through unanimous consent agreements. After both chambers pass identical text, differences are resolved, the text is enrolled, and presentment to the President completes the constitutional sequence.

### Q: What share of bills in the legislative process become law?

In the 116th Congress, which ran from 2019 to 2021, members introduced 16,602 bills and 344 public laws were enacted, a conversion of about 2 percent. The two counts do not divide exactly, because a few public laws arrive as joint resolutions rather than bills. The enacted share also included a substantial commemorative element. The Congressional Research Service found that in the five Congresses examined in its report on commemorations, almost 18 percent of all statutes enacted were post office naming acts alone, and commemorative bills enacted in the 113th Congress numbered 46, about 21 percent of that Congress's enactments.

### Q: Where do most bills die in the legislative process?

Most bills die in committee, without a hearing, a markup, or a vote of any kind. Referral assigns a measure to a committee, and the chair of that committee controls whether the measure appears on a hearing calendar or proceeds to a markup at which amendments are considered and a decision to report is taken. A chair opposed to a measure, indifferent to its subject, or occupied with a crowded agenda can end a proposal through inaction alone. Death by inaction leaves no recorded vote for supporters to challenge and no decision for the chamber to reverse, which is why the committee stage accounts for the largest share of attrition in every Congress.

### Q: Are formal negotiations between chambers still used in the legislative process?

Formal conference committees remain available, but recorded use contracted sharply across the Congresses measured in House and Senate records. Conferences fell from 38 in the 106th Congress, which ran from 1999 to 2000, to 9 in the 110th Congress, which ran from 2007 to 2008, according to counts compiled in an analysis by the Center for Congressional and Presidential Studies at American University. Amendment exchange between the Houses filled much of the space left by that decline. The Congressional Research Service estimated that roughly 11 to 24 percent of public laws enacted in the Congresses from 1992 to 2007 were resolved by amendments between the houses, a share that understated the importance of the route for major measures.

### Q: What is a closed rule in the legislative process?

A closed rule is a special rule reported by the House Rules Committee that prohibits amendments to a measure during floor consideration, so the House votes the reported text up or down without alteration. Structured rules permit only designated amendments, while open rules permit any germane amendment. Compilations of Rules Committee data for the 114th Congress recorded 82 structured rules, 65 closed rules, and only 8 open rules, meaning closed and structured rules together accounted for about 95 percent of special rules in that Congress. No open rule had been used after June 10, 2014, according to the House Rules Committee record cited in those compilations.

### Q: What is suspension of the rules in the legislative process?

Suspension of the rules is a House procedure that limits debate, bars floor amendments, and requires a two thirds vote for passage, reserving the route for measures with broad support and limited controversy. Congressional Research Service snapshot reports on House floor practice recorded that in the 114th Congress, of bills and joint resolutions receiving initial House floor action, 78 percent came up under suspension of the rules, 18 percent under a special rule from the Rules Committee, and 5 percent by unanimous consent. In the 115th Congress, 952 measures were initially considered under suspension, and of the suspension originated measures, 316 were presented to the President and 315 bills and one joint resolution were enacted.

### Q: Do must pass vehicles carry most major legislative process outcomes?

Major outcomes regularly travel on measures that must pass, because a deadline or an annual cycle gives the carrier momentum that freestanding bills lack. Fiscal 2020 appropriations supplied a clear case: all twelve annual appropriations bills were enacted in two consolidated packages, both signed on December 20, 2019. Fiscal 2021 appropriations went further, combining all twelve annual appropriations bills with pandemic relief legislation in a single act signed on December 27, 2020. The National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2022, signed on December 27, 2021, was recorded by the Senate Committee on Armed Services as the 61st consecutive annual defense authorization enactment, a record that reflects the drawing power of a reliable vehicle.

### Q: How often does a discharge petition work in the legislative process?

A discharge petition rarely completes the full route to enactment. The Congressional Research Service recorded that from 1931 to 2002, 563 discharge petitions were filed, 47 obtained the required signatures, the House voted for discharge 26 times, the House passed 19 of the measures involved, and only two became law through the discharge route itself. Petitions also worked indirectly, because measures with completed or pending petitions were often taken up under other procedures, and some of those measures became law. A petition requires 218 signatures, an absolute majority of the House, and a 1993 rules change made signers public as signing proceeded.

### Q: What happens to a bill after both chambers pass it?

After both chambers pass a bill in identical form, the text is enrolled on parchment standard paper by the originating chamber. The Speaker and the President of the Senate, or the President pro tempore, sign the enrolled text, and the measure is presented to the President. Article I, section 7 then governs the outcome. The President may sign the measure, allow the presentment period of ten days, Sundays excepted, to expire without a signature in circumstances where the measure becomes law, or return the measure with objections as a veto. A veto sends the measure back to Congress, where two thirds of both Houses can enact the measure over the objection.

### Q: Who decides which bills get a vote in the House?

Scheduling authority in the House rests principally with the Speaker and the majority leadership, exercised through the Rules Committee for measures considered under special rules and through the suspension calendar for measures requiring two thirds support. Committee chairs exercise the earlier decision, since a measure that no chair schedules for a markup never reaches the point at which floor scheduling arises. The discharge petition stands as the formal exception to leadership control, because 218 members can sign a petition to remove a measure from a committee and bring the question of consideration before the House. In practice, that exception has succeeded rarely, and leadership control of the calendar remains the governing condition for almost every measure.

### Q: Do cosponsors help a bill become law?

Cosponsors signal support, and a long cosponsor list can demonstrate that a measure commands sympathy across a chamber, but cosponsorship confers no procedural right. A cosponsored measure receives no hearing, markup, or floor time by virtue of signatures attached to an introduction. The attrition record of the 116th Congress frames the limit of the signal: 16,602 bills were introduced and 344 public laws were enacted, a conversion of about 2 percent, in a Congress where cosponsorship was a routine feature of introduced measures. Gatekeepers decide scheduling, and scheduling decides survival. Cosponsor counts therefore measure declared sympathy rather than legislative momentum, and measures with extensive cosponsorship regularly end in committee alongside measures with none.

### Q: Why does the Senate do so much business by unanimous consent?

The Senate conducts almost all business by unanimous consent because the formal alternative consumes days. Senate rules leave debate largely unlimited in the absence of an agreement, so routine action depends on consent agreements that set debate time, structure amendments, and schedule votes in advance. A single objection can block a consent request, and holds operate as informal notices to leadership that a senator intends to object. When consent fails and the majority proceeds formally, cloture under Rule XXII requires three fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn, sixty votes in a full Senate, for most legislation. Consent practice therefore reflects arithmetic and time pressure rather than courtesy, and objection functions as a source of negotiating leverage.

### Q: Does a bill have to start in the House of Representatives?

Most bills may be introduced in either chamber, and many move as companion measures introduced in the House and the Senate at the same time. One constitutional exception exists. Article I, section 7 provides that bills for raising revenue shall originate in the House of Representatives, while expressly preserving the Senate's power to propose or concur with amendments as on other bills. By long practice, general appropriations bills also begin in the House, although that sequence rests on custom rather than on a constitutional command. Origination rarely decides a measure's fate by itself, because the second chamber may amend a text wholesale; what the starting chamber controls is the vehicle and the first draft, and control of the vehicle is one of the four determinants that decide whether a measure becomes law.

### Q: What happens to a bill when a Congress ends?

A bill that has not completed passage in both chambers and presentment when a Congress ends expires with that Congress. Introduction numbers, committee referrals, reports, and partial floor action carry no force into the succeeding Congress, because each Congress organizes under its own rules, elects its own officers, and constitutes its own committees. A proposal that a sponsor wishes to pursue after expiration requires a fresh introduction, a new bill number, a new referral, and a new passage through every gate, including any gate cleared during the earlier attempt. The two year life of a Congress therefore operates as a deadline on every pending measure, and proposals that miss the deadline return to the starting point without credit for distance traveled.

### Q: Can a bill become law without the President's signature?

A bill can become law without a presidential signature through two constitutional routes. Under Article I, section 7, a presented bill becomes law if the President neither signs nor returns the bill within ten days, Sundays excepted, while Congress remains in a position to receive a return. The same provision creates the exception: if adjournment prevents return of the bill during that period, the bill does not become law, a result known as a pocket veto. The second route follows a veto. After a President returns a bill with objections, two thirds of both Houses can vote to enact the bill over the veto, completing passage without signature and without further presentment.

### Q: Why are companion bills introduced in both chambers?

Companion bills carry the same or closely similar text in the House and the Senate at the same time, so that committee work and floor consideration can proceed in parallel rather than in sequence. Parallel consideration shortens the path to the stage at which the chambers must agree on identical text, because each chamber develops a product on the same framework and negotiators reconcile a narrower range of differences. Companion introduction also tests support in both chambers early, revealing whether a proposal faces a fatal objection in one chamber before sponsors invest a full Congress in the other. The practice reflects the structure of bicameralism: passage in one chamber alone accomplishes nothing, so serious campaigns for enactment organize for both chambers from the start.

### Q: How long does it take for a bill to become law?

No rule fixes a standard duration, and the historical record shows a range from days to repeated Congresses. A measure carried on a deadline vehicle can move from agreement to signature within the compressed calendar of a funding deadline, as the fiscal 2020 appropriations packages demonstrated with enactment in two acts signed on December 20, 2019. A freestanding measure dependent on committee scheduling, floor time in both chambers, and consent in the Senate can consume months without reaching a vote. A measure that expires with a Congress must begin again under a new number. Duration therefore depends less on the length or complexity of a text than on scheduling decisions, consent, and attachment to a carrier with a deadline.

### Q: Why do so many bills pass without a recorded vote?

Many bills pass without a recorded vote because the procedures that carry routine measures are built around consent and supermajority agreement rather than roll call decision. The House considers a large share of measures under suspension of the rules, a route that accounted for 78 percent of bills and joint resolutions receiving initial House floor action in the 114th Congress, and passage under that route rests on a two thirds threshold that discourages the scheduling of contested measures. The Senate conducts almost all business by unanimous consent, under which a measure passes when no senator objects. Voice votes and consent dispositions therefore reflect a sorting process: measures expected to divide a chamber are routed elsewhere or not scheduled, and measures that reach the floor through consent based routes arrive with opposition already measured and found insufficient to block passage.

### Q: What should a student remember about how a bill becomes law for an exam?

A student preparing for an examination should hold two accounts together. The constitutional account supplies the requirements every law satisfies: passage by both chambers in identical form under Article I, presentment to the President, and signature, expiration of the ten day presentment period in qualifying circumstances, or an override by two thirds of both Houses. The practical account supplies the explanation for outcomes: committee chairs control scheduling, leadership controls floor time, Senate action depends on consent or sixty votes for cloture, and major measures travel on vehicles with deadlines. Examination answers that present only the diagram describe the floor of the process. Answers that add the gates explain why 16,602 introductions in the 116th Congress produced 344 public laws.

### Q: How can a reader tell whether a bill has a real chance of becoming law?

A reader can assess a bill's prospects through evidence at four gates. Committee action provides the first test, since a scheduled hearing or markup signals that a chair has committed institutional resources to the measure. Leadership scheduling provides the second test, visible in a special rule, a suspension calendar placement, or inclusion in a larger package. Senate consent provides the third test, measured by the absence of declared objection or by demonstrated capacity to assemble sixty votes for cloture. Attachment to a vehicle with a deadline provides the fourth test, because carriers such as consolidated appropriations acts and the annual defense authorization move on schedules that freestanding measures lack. A bill passing all four tests has entered the small class of measures with a realistic path to enactment.
