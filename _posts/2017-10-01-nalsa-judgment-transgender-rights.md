The NALSA judgment, delivered by the Supreme Court of India on 15 April 2014, answered a question that no Indian statute had ever squarely faced: what is a person's gender in law when the gender they know themselves to be is not the sex recorded against them at birth? The question sounds simple, and for most legal purposes the legal system had never needed to ask it, because the system was built on an assumption it treated as a fact of nature, that every person is either male or female and that the record made at birth settles the matter for life. The petitioners before the Court, the National Legal Services Authority and, alongside it, a petition filed on behalf of members of the community itself, said the assumption was the injury. A person whose documents contradict their identity is not merely embarrassed by the mismatch. Every form that offers two boxes and no third, every recruitment notice that calls only for men and women, every hospital ward organised on the binary, converts the mismatch into exclusion from education, employment, health care and public space. The legal question was therefore never really whether gender identity exists. It was whether the Constitution permits the state to refuse to see it.

![The NALSA judgment on transgender rights, showing the recognition of a third gender and the right to self-identified gender - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-115.webp)

The Court, a bench of two judges, K. S. Radhakrishnan and A. K. Sikri, held that it does not. Justice Radhakrishnan wrote the leading opinion and Justice Sikri a separate concurring opinion, and between them the judgment did four things that this article separates carefully, because public summary habitually runs them together. It declared that hijras and eunuchs, apart from the binary gender, are to be treated as a third gender for the purpose of safeguarding their rights under the Constitution. It upheld every transgender person's right to decide their self-identified gender, as male, as female or as third gender, and held that any insistence on sex reassignment surgery as a condition of that recognition is immoral and illegal. It read the word sex in the constitutional provisions on discrimination and public employment as including gender identity, so that discrimination on that ground is discrimination the Constitution forbids. And it issued a set of directions, nine in number, telling the Centre and the State Governments what to do, among them a direction to treat transgender persons as socially and educationally backward classes and to extend all kinds of reservation in admission to educational institutions and for public appointments. The case is reported as National Legal Services Authority v Union of India, (2014) 5 SCC 438, and also at AIR 2014 SC 1863.

The judgment matters beyond its subject because of what makes it measurable. Most constitutional judgments declare principles and leave the distance between principle and practice to be argued about. This one numbered its directions. Five years after it was delivered, Parliament enacted the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019, a statute that claimed the judgment as its origin. Because the directions are numbered, the comparison between what the Court ordered and what the statute enacted can be made direction by direction, and the gap, where there is a gap, is a matter of record rather than of rhetoric. That comparison is the analytical core of this article, and it produces the article's central finding. The statute implemented the recognition and dropped the remedy. The two directions with the greatest material consequence, self-identification without a medical requirement and reservation as a backward class, are the two the legislation did not carry.

## The petitions and the grievance behind them

The lead petition was filed in 2012 by the National Legal Services Authority, the statutory body constituted under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 to provide free legal services to the weaker sections of society. It was heard with Writ Petition (Civil) No. 604 of 2013, and the community whose rights were in issue was therefore not spoken for only by an institution. The grievance, as the judgment records it at the outset, was that the members of the transgender community sought a legal declaration of their gender identity other than the one assigned to them, male or female, at the time of birth, and contended that non-recognition of that identity violated Articles 14 and 21 of the Constitution. Hijras and eunuchs, who form part of that larger group, claimed legal status as a third gender with all legal and constitutional protection.

The judgment begins by fixing its subject with care, because the vocabulary in this field is routinely misused. Transgender, Justice Radhakrishnan wrote, is generally described as an umbrella term for persons whose gender identity, gender expression or behaviour does not conform to their biological sex. The term takes in persons who do not identify with the sex assigned at birth, including hijras and eunuchs, who do not identify as either male or female. The community, the judgment records, comprises hijras, eunuchs, kothis, aravanis, jogappas and shiv-shakthis, among others, and it has a strong historical presence in the country, in Hindu mythology and other religious texts, where the concept of tritiya prakriti, the third nature, is an integral part of Vedic and Puranic literature. That historical record mattered to the Court for a reason that becomes important when the directions are read, because one of them is restorative. The community was not asking the law to invent a place for it. It was pointing to a place it had occupied and lost.

The loss, as the judgment traces it, was substantially a colonial legal artefact. British administration treated the community's existence as a problem of order, and the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871 carried a part directed at eunuchs, providing for the registration of their names and residences and treating their public presence as an offence-adjacent condition to be recorded by the police. A community that had held recognised, if circumscribed, social roles found itself entered in registers of suspicion. The judgment's opening pages set that history against the constitutional promise, and Justice Radhakrishnan's framing sentence, much quoted since, is that society rarely realises or cares to realise the trauma, agony and pain which the members of the transgender community undergo, and does not appreciate the innate feelings of a community whose mind and body, in the judgment's words, disown their biological sex. He located the failure precisely. It was, he wrote, society's moral failure to contain or embrace different gender identities and expressions, a failure the Constitution did not share, because on the Court's reading the constitutional text had never confined its protections to two genders in the first place.

The evidence of exclusion placed before the Court was structural rather than anecdotal. The judgment records that members of the community are shunned and treated as untouchables in public places, at railway stations and bus stands, in schools, workplaces, malls, theatres and hospitals. The 2011 Census had, for the first time, permitted a return outside the male and female columns, and just under 4.9 lakh persons were recorded in the other category, a figure the judgment and every serious account treat as a floor rather than a count, since a census answer given to an enumerator at the door is a poor instrument for measuring a stigmatised identity. The community's own case, and the material filed in support of it, described the practical mechanics of exclusion: identity documents that could not be obtained in the lived gender, and in many cases could not be obtained at all; admission and employment processes that presupposed the binary; harassment under the provision of the Penal Code that criminalised carnal intercourse against the order of nature, which the judgment noted in its survey of the legal landscape even though the validity of that provision was not the question before this bench. The point the petitioners pressed, and which the Court accepted as the frame for everything that followed, was that these were not separate misfortunes. They were the downstream consequences of a single legal refusal, the refusal to recognise gender identity as a fact about a person that the law must record accurately.

One feature of the litigation deserves emphasis because it shaped the judgment's second half. While the petitions were pending, the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment had constituted an Expert Committee on issues relating to transgender persons, and that committee submitted its report on 27 January 2014, recommending among other things that transgender persons be declared a third gender with the option of identifying as man, woman or transgender independent of surgery or hormones, and that the third gender be marked on official documents. The judgment, in its operative part, directed that the committee's recommendations be examined in the light of the legal declaration the Court had made and implemented within six months. The judgment therefore did not speak into a vacuum. It spoke into a policy process already under way, and it converted the committee's recommendations from advice a ministry could shelve into a programme a court had ordered to be assessed against declared constitutional rights.

The identity of the lead petitioner is itself part of the case's meaning. The National Legal Services Authority is not an advocacy organisation but a creature of statute, constituted under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 to organise legal aid for the weaker sections of society, with its counterparts in every state and district. Its appearance as petitioner signalled that the claim was framed, from the first, as a failure of access to justice: a community that could not obtain documents could not instruct lawyers, file claims or enforce such rights as it formally had, so the institution charged with legal services carried the case that the community's exclusion from paperwork made it hard for the community to carry alone. The Authority's involvement also shaped the remedy. A legal services authority thinks in terms of machinery, camps, certificates and entitlements delivered through district administrations, and the judgment's directions, with their emphasis on documents, centres, schemes and welfare delivery, bear the imprint of a petitioner whose daily work is the machinery of access. The community's own petition, heard with it, supplied what an institution cannot: the testimony of identity from inside, and the named human presence that made the psychological test a conclusion from evidence rather than a theory.

## The record of exclusion the Court acted on

Declarations of right are only as significant as the condition they answer, and the judgment is careful to build the condition before it builds the law. The picture it assembles, from the petitions, the interveners' material and the Expert Committee's work, is of exclusion operating at every stage of a life and through instruments that are individually mundane. It begins in the family and the school: a child whose presentation does not match the record is exposed to ridicule and pressure, leaves education early, and enters adulthood without the certificates on which every formal opportunity depends. The judgment records the community's confinement, in consequence, to a narrow band of livelihoods, the traditional occupations of blessing and performance at births and marriages, begging, and sex work, each of them precarious and two of them exposed to the criminal law's attention through statutes against begging and immoral traffic that are enforced against the visible poor. Employment in the formal economy is foreclosed less by any rule of exclusion than by the documentary threshold: a person who cannot produce identity papers in a consistent name and gender cannot be appointed, enrolled, examined or paid through a bank.

Health is the second theatre, and the judgment's treatment of it explains why two of its nine directions are medical. A community a large part of whose health needs are specific, in sexual health, in hormone therapy, in surgery, in mental health under the weight of stigma, confronted a hospital system organised on the binary at the level of the ward, the form and the toilet, and staffed by professionals whose training had never addressed it. The judgment records fear, shame, gender dysphoria, social pressure, depression and suicidal tendencies among the problems the state was directed to address, a list that reads like a clinical summary because it is drawn from the material the community placed before the Court. The third theatre is the street and the police station. The judgment notes the harassment the community faced, the use of the Penal Code's provision on unnatural offences as an instrument of extortion and abuse against persons whose offence was visibility, and the treatment of the community in public places as untouchables, the word the eighth direction takes up. A reader who holds this record in mind will read the directions differently: they are not a benevolent court's wish list but a schedule of repairs, each one keyed to a documented failure, and their uneven implementation is correspondingly an uneven repair of documented harms.

Two features of this record deserve the researcher's attention. The first is its reliance on the community's own testimony and organisations rather than on official statistics, which for this population barely existed; the census figure the judgment works with is, as noted, a floor built from a single enumerative category. The discipline the series applies to numbers matters here: where a population is defined by an identity that disclosure endangers, every count is an undercount of unknown depth, and the judgment was right to rest its holdings on the structure of exclusion rather than on its measured scale. The second feature is the record's age profile. The historical sections show a community whose marginalisation deepened under colonial law and continued, by administrative inertia, under a Constitution whose text did not authorise it. That is the precise configuration in which a declaration judgment is the apt remedy: the wrong is not a statute to be struck down but a settled practice to be declared unlawful, after which the machinery of the state must be rebuilt to match a text that was never the problem.

## A legal system built on two boxes

To understand what the Court was being asked to change, the starting point is not the Constitution but the ordinary statute book, because the Constitution's general language had coexisted for six decades with a body of law that recognised exactly two genders and organised itself accordingly. The judgment itself made the survey, and its examples are instructive. The Indian Penal Code defines its terms in the binary: its early sections provide that the pronoun he and its derivatives are used of any person, whether male or female, and that the word man denotes a male human being of any age and the word woman a female human being of any age. The laws of marriage, adoption, divorce, inheritance and succession are built on the same foundation, each of them allocating rights and obligations by reference to a husband and a wife, a son and a daughter, a widow and a widower. Welfare legislation is no different; the judgment instanced the rural employment guarantee statute of 2005, whose machinery of job cards, households and entitlements presupposes the same sorting. Service rules prescribe different standards by sex for physical tests, retirement provisions and family benefits. Forms, the operative law of daily life, complete the structure: a form with two boxes is a rule that a third kind of person does not exist for the purpose in hand.

The interpretive problem this created was subtle, and it is the problem any serious account of the case has to expose before the holdings can be understood. There was no single provision whose validity the petitioners could attack, because the exclusion was not located in a provision. It was located in an assumption shared by all of them. A challenge framed as an attack on one statute would have failed for the reason such challenges usually fail, that the statute's classification was rational on its own terms. The petitioners therefore attacked at the level of the assumption itself, arguing that the constitutional provisions on equality, expression and life were written in language that had never enacted the binary, that person and citizen and sex are words of general reach, and that the binary was a habit of administration rather than a command of law. That framing determined the judgment's shape. A court persuaded at the level of the assumption cannot merely strike down; it must declare what the general words mean and direct the administration to conform its machinery to the declaration. Which is what this Court did.

The personal laws repay a closer look, because they are where the binary does its most intimate work and where the judgment's reading has had its longest reach. The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 solemnises a marriage between a bride and a bridegroom and conditions its validity on the parties answering those descriptions. The Special Marriage Act, 1954 speaks of the man and the woman and prescribes different minimum ages by sex. The Christian and Parsi marriage statutes are built the same way, and uncodified personal law allocates the roles of husband and wife, son and daughter, with the same assumption underneath. Succession law distributes by the same map: a son and a daughter take in defined shares under the Hindu Succession Act, a widow and a widower hold defined positions, and every one of those positions is inaccessible to a person the record will not place. Maintenance, adoption and guardianship repeat the pattern. None of these statutes was before the Court, and the judgment amended none of them, but its declaration changed the premise on which each of them operates, because a bride, after April 2014, is a person whose gender the law determines by the psychological test the judgment adopted, and the Madras High Court's marriage decision, examined later in this article, is simply that premise at work in a single statute. The gap between the premise and its application, statute by statute and form by form, is the space in which much of the litigation in this field continues to be fought.

The survey also explains a feature of the judgment that readers sometimes find strange, its attention to history and to foreign law in a case about Indian administrative forms. The binary's claim to authority rested on its appearance of naturalness: male and female presented themselves not as legal categories but as facts prior to law, which law merely recorded. History and comparative law were the solvents of that appearance. History showed a period, within the country's own civilisation, in which a third category was named and placed; comparative law showed legal systems, several of them working within the same common law tradition, that had ceased to treat the birth record as conclusive. Once the binary was visible as a choice rather than a fact, the constitutional question could be asked in its proper form: not whether nature permits a third gender, but whether the Constitution permits the state to refuse recognition to one.

## The arguments the Court heard

The petitioners' case, as the judgment records it, was built in layers. The foundation was the record of exclusion: a community locked out of education, employment, health care and public space, and locked out, the argument ran, not by any law directed at its existence but by the refusal of every legal instrument to record it accurately. Above that stood the constitutional submissions. Article 14 was said to be violated because the community was denied the equal protection of laws that speak of any person. Articles 15 and 16 were said to be violated because sex, properly read, includes gender identity, so that discrimination against the community is discrimination on a prohibited ground. Article 19(1)(a) was engaged because a person prevented from presenting in their identified gender is prevented from expressing the most basic fact about themselves. And Article 21 was said to contain the whole matter, because a life lived under a legal identity that contradicts the self is a denial of dignity and of personal autonomy in their most elementary form.

The Union's position, presented by the Additional Solicitor General, was not a defence of exclusion, and the difference matters to the judgment's tone. The problems of the community were accepted to be a sensitive human question calling for serious attention, and the Court was told of the Expert Committee the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment had constituted to study those problems and recommend measures, with representation to be given to the applicants before the Court so that their views could be heard. The litigation therefore lacked the usual adversarial structure in which a court must choose between a right and a state interest asserted against it. No compelling state interest in non-recognition was ever formulated, because there is none that survives statement: it is impossible to say what legitimate purpose is served by recording a person in a gender they are not. The contest in the case was instead about machinery and remedy, about whether recognition should come from the Court by declaration or from the political process by legislation, and about how far a court may go in directing the second from the bench. The judgment's answer, declaration by the Court and a directed timetable for the executive's programme, with legislation left to follow, is the characteristic product of that unusual posture, and it explains why the judgment reads as an act of constitutional administration as much as an act of adjudication.

The community's own presence in the litigation should not be passed over, because it recurs in the judgment's reasoning. The second petition brought the claimants before the Court not as a statistical class but as persons whose testimony about their own identity the Court treated as the primary evidence of what gender is. That evidentiary choice is the psychological test in embryo. A court that hears a person say who they are, and finds no reason in the record to disbelieve them, has already travelled most of the distance to holding that the person's own identification is the substance of the legal question. The comparative jurisprudence the Court surveyed, from the European Court of Human Rights in Christine Goodwin to the courts of Nepal and Pakistan, which had both recognised a third gender before this judgment, confirmed that other legal systems had made the same journey on the same evidence. The judgment is often described as the Court leading society. On its own record it is better described as the Court believing the petitioners, which is a quieter and more defensible account of what happened.

## Sex in Articles 15 and 16: the reading that carries the judgment

Every holding in the case rests on a prior act of interpretation, and the act is this. Articles 15 and 16 of the Constitution prohibit discrimination on grounds only of, among other things, sex. The question was whether sex in those articles means biological sex, the male and female of the birth record, or whether it extends to gender identity. The Court held that it extends. Both gender and biological attributes, Justice Radhakrishnan wrote, constitute distinct components of sex. Biological characteristics include genitals, chromosomes and secondary sexual features, but gender attributes include a person's self-image, the deep psychological or emotional sense of sexual identity and character. The discrimination on the ground of sex under Articles 15 and 16 therefore includes discrimination on the ground of gender identity, and the expression sex in those articles is not limited to the biological sex of male and female but is intended to include persons who consider themselves to be neither male nor female.

The reading repays slow attention because of how much it carries. Article 14, the general equality clause, speaks of any person, and the Court treated its language as gender neutral, extending to every human being without regard to the binary. Article 15(2), which forbids subjecting any citizen to disability, liability, restriction or condition in regard to access to shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment, wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort, was held to have been denied to transgender persons in practice, which is the doctrinal route by which exclusion from public space became a constitutional wrong rather than a social one. Articles 15(4) and 16(4), the enabling provisions for special provisions and reservation in favour of socially and educationally backward classes, were held to be provisions to which the community was legally entitled and eligible, because, in the judgment's words, transgender persons are such a class and the state is bound to take affirmative action for their advancement so that the injustice done to them over centuries could be remedied. That sentence is the seed of the reservation direction, and it is also the point at which this judgment touches the wider architecture of Indian reservation law, including the ceiling jurisprudence that the series treats in its account of [the Indra Sawhney judgment and the reservation ceiling](/2017/05/15/indra-sawhney-reservation-ceiling/). The reading of sex as a ground that includes gender identity has consequences well outside this field, because every statute and rule that sorts persons by sex, from service rules to the personal laws governing marriage, adoption, divorce, inheritance and succession, sits downstream of it; the judgment itself made the point by observing that the binary notion of gender runs through the Penal Code and the welfare legislation alike, and the same interpretive question has been carried into the series' treatment of [how the law reads sex across the women's rights statutes](/2015/11/01/womens-rights-laws-india-guide/).

### What did the NALSA judgment hold about the word sex in the Constitution?

The Court held that sex in Articles 15 and 16 is not limited to biological sex and includes gender identity, because sex has two components: biological attributes of the body, and gender attributes of self-image and psychological identity. Discrimination against a person for gender identity is therefore discrimination on the ground of sex.

One passage of the leading opinion does quieter work and is often missed. The Court observed that the expressions the Constitution uses in this field are themselves gender neutral: Article 14 speaks of any person, Article 15 of the citizen, Article 16 of citizens, Article 19 of the citizen and Article 21 of the person, and all these expressions, evidently referring to human beings, take within their sweep hijras and transgender persons and are not as such limited to the male or female gender. The observation matters because it reverses the burden of textual argument. The petitioners did not need the Constitution to mention them; the Constitution's silence about the binary is the point, since a text that protects persons without sorting them cannot be read as though the sorting were written into it. Every exclusionary practice therefore had to justify itself as something other than a constitutional command, and none could. That is also the answer to the objection, heard whenever this judgment is discussed, that the Court legislated a new category into a Constitution that does not contain it. The category the Court acted on, the person, is the Constitution's own first word on the subject, and the third gender is a recognition about some persons, not an amendment of the text.

The consequence the Court drew for public employment deserves separate statement, because it is the one most often dropped in summary. The judgment records that transgender persons had been denied rights under Article 16(2) and discriminated against in respect of employment under the state on the ground of sex, and that they are entitled to reservation in the matter of appointment as envisaged under Article 16(4), the state being bound to take affirmative action to give them due representation in public services. Representation, on this reasoning, is not a welfare concession. It is the remedy that the equality code itself supplies for a class that satisfies the constitutional description of backwardness, and the Court treated the community's satisfaction of that description as established by the record of exclusion rather than as a matter requiring a fresh commission of inquiry. Whether that treatment was doctrinally orthodox is the genuine open question in the case, and this article returns to it when it examines the reservation debate. What is not open is that the Court held it.

## Expression, dignity and autonomy: Articles 19(1)(a) and 21

The equality reading established that transgender persons could not be discriminated against. Two further provisions established what they could affirmatively claim. The first is the freedom of speech and expression. Article 19(1)(a), the Court held, includes a person's right to expression of their self-identified gender. Self-identified gender, in the judgment's own sentence, can be expressed through dress, words, action or behaviour or any other form, and no restriction can be placed on a person's personal appearance or choice of dressing, subject only to the restrictions the Constitution itself permits under Article 19(2). The freedom, the Court elaborated, includes the freedom to express a chosen gender identity through varied ways and means, by expression, speech, mannerism and clothing, because gender identity lies at the core of a person's personal identity, gender expression and presentation. The holding was then stated at full width: the values of privacy, self-identity, autonomy and personal integrity are fundamental rights guaranteed to members of the transgender community under Article 19(1)(a), and the state is bound to protect and recognise those rights.

The placement of privacy and autonomy inside the expression clause is one of the judgment's distinctive moves, and a reader comparing it with the later privacy jurisprudence should notice the sequence. In this case, decided in 2014, autonomy was grounded substantially in expression: a person speaks their identity through presentation, and the state may not silence the statement by policing the presentation. Three years later, a nine-judge bench in the Puttaswamy case placed privacy, dignity and autonomy at the centre of Article 21, and the reasoning of this judgment on dignity and personal autonomy was among the materials the later decision drew upon in holding that the Constitution protects the intimate choices by which a person constitutes a self, a line of reasoning this article returns to when it places [the Puttaswamy privacy and Aadhaar judgment](/2019/05/15/puttaswamy-privacy-aadhaar-judgment/) in the sequence of later decisions. The second provision in this case is Article 21 itself. The right to life and personal liberty, the Court held, includes the right to dignity, and recognition of a person's gender identity lies at the heart of the fundamental right to dignity, because gender constitutes the core of a person's sense of being and an integral part of identity. Legal recognition of gender identity is therefore part of the right to dignity and freedom guaranteed under the Constitution, not an administrative courtesy the state may extend or withhold.

On autonomy the judgment was equally specific. Article 21, it held, guarantees the protection of the personal autonomy of an individual, and it adopted the formulation from Anuj Garg v Hotel Association of India, where the Court had held that personal autonomy includes both the negative right not to be subject to interference by others and the positive right of individuals to make decisions about their life, to express themselves and to choose which activities to take part in. Self-determination of gender, the judgment concluded, is an integral part of personal autonomy and self-expression and falls within the realm of personal liberty guaranteed under Article 21. The doctrinal yield of these passages is considerable. If gender identity is a matter of personal autonomy protected against state interference, then a state procedure that conditions recognition on proof the person cannot or does not wish to supply is not a neutral administrative requirement; it is an interference requiring justification. That is the logic that produces the surgery holding, and it is the logic the 2019 Act would later have to answer.

### How does expressing a gender count as freedom of speech and expression?

The Court treated gender presentation as expression because identity is communicated through it. A person's self-identified gender is expressed through dress, words, action, behaviour, mannerism and clothing, and Article 19(1)(a) protects that expression as it protects other speech, subject only to the restrictions permitted by Article 19(2), and the state therefore cannot prohibit, restrict or interfere with it.

## The test of gender: the psyche preferred to the body

A court that declares a right to self-identified gender must answer an institutional question: when identity and record conflict, what determines gender in law? The existing authority the judgment had to confront was the English decision in Corbett v Corbett, which had fixed sex by biological criteria, chromosomes, gonads and genitals, assessed at birth, and had denied recognition to a person who had undergone surgery and lived as a woman. The Indian Court refused to follow it, in terms that leave no ambiguity about the choice being made. The judgment records that Indian law on the whole recognises the paradigm of binary genders of male and female based on biological sex, and continues, in a sentence that does the analytical work of the whole case: we cannot accept the Corbett principle of the biological test, rather we prefer to follow the psyche of the person in determining sex and gender, and prefer the psychological test instead of the biological test.

The preference was not asserted as intuition. The leading opinion surveyed the comparative and international record, including the European Court of Human Rights' decision in Christine Goodwin v United Kingdom, which had held that a state could not refuse legal recognition to a transsexual person in their acquired gender, and the statutory recognition regimes of other jurisdictions, and it drew on the Yogyakarta Principles, the statement of how international human rights law applies in relation to sexual orientation and gender identity, which the Court treated as not inconsistent with the fundamental rights guaranteed by the Constitution and therefore as material an Indian court could act upon. The Directive Principles in Part IV of the Constitution supplied the domestic bridge the judgment needed for its affirmative directions: the constitutional commitment to a social order in which justice, social, economic and political, informs all the institutions of national life, and the direction that the state promote the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections, gave the Court constitutional text on which duties of advancement, and not merely duties of non-interference, could rest. The international instruments, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights among them, were read in the same direction, as the Court has read such instruments where they do not conflict with the constitutional text.

Justice Sikri's concurring opinion covers much of the same ground and then goes further in two respects that have given it an independent afterlife. He traced the position of the community across Indian history in greater depth, and he developed the test question with a frankness about its human difficulty that the leading opinion does not attempt. The determination of gender, in his reasoning, cannot rest on the body, because the experience the law is being asked to recognise is the experience of a person whose psychological sense of gender is at variance with the body, and a test that looks only at the body decides the question by refusing to look at the evidence that matters. A test that asks after the psyche, by contrast, asks the person, and the person's own identification is therefore not merely evidence of gender but, for legal purposes, its substance. Justice Sikri also used the concurrence to underline the difference between the Court's subject and the subject of the sexuality cases: gender identity, who a person is, and sexual orientation, whom a person is drawn to, are distinct, and a judgment about the first decides nothing about the legality of any act. That distinction, as later sections of this article show, is the key to placing this case correctly among the decisions that followed it, with which it is routinely conflated.

It is worth pausing on the bench, because the judgment's authority is part of its story. Two judges decided it. A two-judge bench decision binds later benches of equal or smaller strength and is ordinarily treated as settled law within its field, and this one has been cited with approval by benches of far greater strength, including the nine-judge bench on privacy and the Constitution Benches that followed on related questions. But the directions in the case run against governments, and a direction of a two-judge bench to treat a class as backward for reservation purposes entered a field that a nine-judge bench had mapped in Indra Sawhney, without this bench purporting to reopen that map. The reservation direction's vulnerability, examined below, begins there.

### What is the psychological test for gender that the Court preferred?

The psychological test determines gender by the person's own psychological sense of their gender rather than by biological criteria fixed at birth. The Court rejected the biological test of the English decision in Corbett, under which sex was settled by chromosomes, gonads and genitals, and held that the law should follow the psyche of the person.

## Two opinions and the weight of concurrence

Because the operative declarations sit in the leading opinion, commentary sometimes treats the concurrence as commentary indeed, an essay attached to the decision. That underrates it in two ways. A concurrence that agrees in the result and differs in the route is, for later courts, a map of the alternative foundations on which the result can rest, and Justice Sikri's opinion supplies foundations the leading opinion leaves thinner: the historical record in depth, the international jurisprudence including the views of the United Nations Human Rights Committee in Toonen v Australia on privacy and sexual identity, and the sustained argument that the state's obligation is not exhausted by non-interference but extends to the measures by which a stigmatised community is brought within the ordinary protections of citizenship. When later benches cite the case for a proposition about the positive obligations of the state, it is frequently the concurrence's reasoning that carries the proposition, with the leading opinion supplying its authority.

The two opinions also divide the labour of the subject between them in a way that explains the judgment's durability. Justice Radhakrishnan's opinion is architectural: it builds the constitutional structure, article by article, and issues the operative directions in the spare form of a decree. Justice Sikri's is anthropological: it dwells on who the community is, how it has lived, and what recognition means from inside the experience of transition, including the difficulty of a process the law had previously treated as either invisible or deviant. A reader preparing the case for examination or argument needs both, the decree for what was ordered and the concurrence for why the ordering court thought the order was a matter of justice rather than of concession. The distinction between the opinions is also the answer to a recurring examination confusion: the psychological test is not the concurrence's eccentricity but the Court's shared position, appearing in the leading opinion's rejection of the Corbett biological test and developed in the concurrence, so that it is attributable to the judgment without qualification.

## The comparative company the judgment kept

The judgment's comparative survey was not decoration, and its choices repay attention, because a court choosing a test for gender is choosing among the answers other systems had already given. The oldest answer in the survey is the one rejected: the English common law of Corbett, which made the body at birth conclusive and which the United Kingdom itself abandoned by legislation in the Gender Recognition Act of 2004, under which a person may obtain a gender recognition certificate and be treated in law in the acquired gender. The European Court of Human Rights, in Christine Goodwin v United Kingdom, had held a year before that Act that the United Kingdom's refusal of recognition violated the rights to respect for private life and to marry, reasoning that a person living in an acquired gender could not be left in an intermediate zone by a state that permitted the surgery, recognised its effects for some purposes and denied them for others. The Indian Court's use of Goodwin is pointed: the incoherence the Strasbourg court condemned, a state that medicalises transition and then refuses its legal consequences, is the mirror image of the incoherence this judgment condemned, a state that might demand surgery as proof and thereby make a person's legal existence depend on an operation.

The neighbours are more instructive still. The Supreme Court of Nepal, in Sunil Babu Pant v Government of Nepal in 2007, had directed its government to recognise a third gender in citizenship documents and to study the legal reforms the recognition required, on a record materially identical to the Indian one, a community with an indigenous third gender vocabulary confronting documents that denied it. The Supreme Court of Pakistan, in the proceedings commenced by Muhammad Aslam Khaki in 2009, had directed the protection of hijra citizens, their inclusion in welfare and inheritance, and their registration in the national database. The Indian judgment sits in this regional line and knew it: the subcontinent's courts, confronting communities their own societies had named for centuries, reached recognition before the subcontinent's legislatures did. Australia and New Zealand appear in the survey for the documentary mechanics, the passport and record systems that first admitted a third marker, which mattered because the Indian petitioners' most concrete grievance was documentary. Argentina's gender identity statute of 2012, the first national legislation to rest recognition wholly on self-declaration without medical or judicial gatekeeping, represented in the survey the legislative form of the principle the Court was being asked to declare judicially.

The documentary systems in the survey carried a practical lesson that the Indian administration would spend years absorbing, and it is worth drawing out because it converts the comparative material from authority into instruction. The systems that admitted a third marker, the Australian passport arrangements that admitted an X entry and the New Zealand passport system that did the same, demonstrated that a third category is an ordinary act of records management: a database field widened, a form reprinted, a verification rule adjusted. No institution collapsed, and no fraud of consequence followed, because the marker records what the issuing authority has been told and has verified to its own standard, which is all any identity document ever does. The lesson cuts against the instinct, visible in the Indian certificate debates that followed, to treat the third entry as a profound administrative novelty requiring exceptional proof. The systems in the survey treated it as the correction of a field that had been defined too narrowly, and the Indian judgment's documentary direction, that the third gender be recognised in the state's paperwork, asked for nothing more exotic. That the correction in India proceeded form by form over years, rather than by a single instruction to all record keepers, says more about the federation's administrative habits than about the direction's difficulty.

The point of assembling this company was argumentative. Against the submission, implicit in the state's caution, that recognition was an experiment, the survey demonstrated that recognition was the direction of travel of every system that had examined the question, and that the systems differed only in instrument, some by statute and some by judgment. Against the biological test, the survey demonstrated that no system retaining it had defended it on its merits for a generation; Corbett survived in England until legislation buried it, and its own author court's jurisdiction had disowned its conclusion. The Indian Court's psychological test was therefore not an innovation dressed as interpretation. It was the adoption, with attribution, of the position to which the comparative record had converged, and the judgment's authority in later Indian cases owes something to that convergence, because a bench citing NALSA cites a proposition that arrives with an international record attached.

## The declarations and the nine directions

The operative part of the leading opinion is short enough to be stated completely, and it should be, because summary has been its enemy. The Court first concluded that discrimination on the basis of sexual orientation or gender identity includes any discrimination, exclusion, restriction or preference which has the effect of nullifying or transposing equality by the law or the equal protection of laws guaranteed under the Constitution. It then declared, in the first of its declarations, that hijras and eunuchs, apart from binary gender, be treated as third gender for the purpose of safeguarding their rights under Part III of the Constitution and the laws made by Parliament and the State Legislatures. The second declaration upheld transgender persons' right to decide their self-identified gender, and directed the Centre and the State Governments to grant legal recognition of their gender identity such as male, female or as third gender. The drafting of that sentence matters. The right upheld is the right to decide, and the recognition directed is of the identity decided, in any of the three forms. Nothing in it makes the third gender the community's only lawful destination, a point to which this article returns, because the later debate about the category turns on it.

The remaining declarations are directions to government, and they are conventionally counted, with the two recognition declarations, as nine directions in all. The third directs the Centre and the State Governments to take steps to treat transgender persons as socially and educationally backward classes of citizens and extend all kinds of reservation in cases of admission in educational institutions and for public appointments. The fourth directs the Centre and the State Governments to operate separate HIV sero-surveillance centres, since hijras and transgender persons face several sexual health issues. The fifth directs the Centre and the State Governments to seriously address the problems being faced by the community, such as fear, shame, gender dysphoria, social pressure, depression, suicidal tendencies and social stigma, and it is in this direction that the Court placed its most quoted sentence: any insistence for sex reassignment surgery for declaring one's gender is immoral and illegal. The sixth directs proper measures to provide medical care to transgender persons in the hospitals and also to provide them separate public toilets and other facilities. The seventh directs steps for framing various social welfare schemes for their betterment. The eighth directs steps to create public awareness so that transgender persons will feel that they are also part and parcel of social life and are not treated as untouchables. The ninth directs the Centre and the State Governments to take measures to regain the respect and place in society which the community once enjoyed in the country's cultural and social life. A further operative paragraph dealt with the Expert Committee: its recommendations were to be examined on the basis of the legal declaration made in the judgment and implemented within six months.

Three features of this operative part deserve analytical notice. The first is its addressee. The directions run to the Centre and the State Governments jointly, because the subjects they touch, health, education, welfare, public employment, are divided between the two levels of the federation, and no single government could implement the programme alone. The second is its mixture of kinds. The first two declarations state rights, which courts enforce. The remaining seven direct programmes, which governments execute and courts can supervise only loosely. The judgment therefore contains, inside one operative part, both a bill of rights and a policy agenda, and the implementation record, predictably, is strong where the document declares rights and patchy where it orders programmes. The third feature is the surgery sentence's placement and wording. It is not in the discussion of medical facilities. It is attached to the direction about the community's psychological burdens, and it states a rule about declaration: insistence on surgery for declaring one's gender is what is condemned, as both immoral and illegal. When commentators ask what the judgment held about surgery, the accurate answer is that it held the demand for surgery as a condition of declaring gender to be unlawful, and said so in the operative part of the judgment, not in a passing observation.

## Declarations, directions and the space between them

The remedial architecture of the judgment is worth a section of its own, because it predicts the implementation record with uncomfortable accuracy. Constitutional courts disposing of a case of this kind have three instruments. They may declare the law, which binds everyone and executes itself in the sense that every authority must conform its decisions to the declaration. They may strike down or read down a provision, which executes itself by removal. And they may direct the executive to act, which executes nothing by itself, because a direction is performed by the institution directed, at its pace, through its budget, and a court that has disposed of the case retains no machinery of supervision unless it keeps the matter pending as a continuing mandamus. This judgment used the first instrument for identity and equality, and the third for everything else. It did not keep the petitions pending. The writ petitions were allowed and closed, with the single timed obligation, the six months for examining the Expert Committee's recommendations, left to run without a listed date for reporting compliance.

The consequence is written across the record. The declarations were self-executing and have never since been seriously disobeyed in terms: no authority after April 2014 could lawfully maintain that transgender persons stand outside Articles 14, 15, 16, 19 or 21, and the identity declaration translated, through forms and documents, into administrative fact. The directions were executory and have been performed in the manner of executory things in a federation of many governments: promptly where performance was cheap or already under way, slowly where it required construction or rules, and not at all where it required a redistribution that some government would have to defend. A reader comparing this case with the series' implementation studies of statutes will recognise the pattern, with the difference that here the unimplemented instrument is a judgment rather than an Act. The lesson generalises, and it is the lesson the series thesis draws from this cluster: a legal text changes the world through the institutions charged with executing it, and a text whose executors are directed but not supervised changes the world selectively, in the order of their convenience.

There is a defence of the Court's restraint that should be recorded, because the strongest version of the case for the judgment includes it. A court that retains a case to supervise nine directions across twenty-eight states and the Union assumes a managerial role for which it has neither staff nor budget, and the history of continuing mandamus in Indian public law is a history of early energy and long tail. The bench may also have calculated, correctly, that the declarations were the durable asset: governments change, schemes lapse, but a reading of the Constitution, once delivered and taken up by larger benches, is close to irreversible. On that calculation the judgment traded supervision for permanence, and the trade looks better with each later citation. The reservation direction is the exception that exposes the trade's cost, because it was drafted in the executory mode, as steps to be taken, for the one item that most needed either the declaratory mode or a legislature.

## Nine directions, three outcomes: the findable record

The comparison that follows is the reason this article exists. The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019, assented to on 5 December 2019 and brought into force on 10 January 2020, is the statute Parliament enacted on this subject after the judgment, and [the pillar account of the Transgender Persons Act 2019](/2017/09/15/transgender-persons-act-2019-explained/) owns its provisions in detail. What belongs here is the measurement of that statute against the nine directions, direction by direction. The outcomes fall into three groups. Some directions the statute implemented in terms. Some it modified, carrying the subject into law while changing the judgment's rule on the way. And some it omitted, leaving the directed thing unlegislated, to be pursued, if at all, through schemes, rules and litigation. Advocates and students who keep the two documents in a single working file, the judgment's directions against the Act's sections, can use an [online viewer for Office documents such as Excel, Word and PowerPoint files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) to hold the comparison open side by side.

Reading the outcome column is the article's argument. Of the nine directions, the statute legislated the recognitional and programmatic middle of the judgment and declined its two hardest edges. ## The claim the comparison supports

The series has traced statutes enacted after judgments across many fields and found that the fit is rarely exact. What can be stated here, and stated as a verdict because the numbered directions make it a matter of counting rather than characterisation, is this. The statute implemented the recognition and dropped the remedy. Recognition, in the judgment, had two components: the existence of a third gender in law, and the person's sovereign choice among male, female and third gender, exercisable without medical proof. The Act enacted the first component and qualified the second at the decisive point, because a person recognised as transgender on self-perception who seeks the male or female marker must produce, under Section 7, a certificate from the Medical Superintendent or Chief Medical Officer of the institution where surgery was undergone. The remedy, in the judgment, was reservation: treatment as a socially and educationally backward class with all kinds of reservation in admissions and appointments. The Act contains no reservation provision of any kind. The two directions with the greatest material consequence for a community whose exclusion is economic as much as social are therefore the two the legislation did not carry, one modified against the judgment's express rule and one omitted entire.

That verdict needs its own qualification, and the qualification is structural rather than apologetic. A judgment's directions do not bind a legislature the way they bind the executive. Parliament legislating on a subject after a judgment is not in contempt when its statute differs; it is exercising legislative power, and the statute's conformity with the judgment is tested, if it is tested, against the Constitution and not against the judgment as though the judgment were a statute. The challenges to the 2019 Act that followed its enactment, filed in the constitutional courts and directed principally at the certificate provisions, raised exactly that constitutional question, whether a surgery condition on the male or female marker can coexist with the rights the judgment declared under Articles 14, 19 and 21. That question belongs to the courts and is not resolved by this article. What can be resolved here is the prior, factual question on which the legal one depends, and it has been resolved by the table: the Act and the judgment differ, at identified provisions, in identified ways.

That qualification has a comparative dimension the series is positioned to state. Legislatures responding to judgments choose from a menu the judgment cannot control: they may codify the judgment, codify around it, or codify against the parts of it they dislike while honouring its vocabulary. The 2019 Act is an unusually legible instance of the third course, because it adopted the judgment's definitions and declared objects while reversing its operative rule on the point the judgment had stated in moral as well as legal terms. The pattern is not unique to this field. What is unique here is the availability of the measurement: nine numbered directions, a statute enacted within the same decade, and a table in which every cell can be filled from the two documents alone. Where the fit between a judgment and its sequel statute is argued about in most fields, in this one it can be exhibited, and the exhibition is the strongest evidence for the series' general claim that a statute is not its text alone but the whole record around it, including the judgment it answers and the directions it declines.

It follows that the two documents must be read as a pair in any serious study, and read in the right order. The judgment first, for the rights and the directions in their own words, because the statute's meaning is partly a function of what it answered; the statute second, for the machinery, because machinery is what a person applying for a certificate, an admission or an appointment actually meets. Reading the statute alone produces the common error of attributing its conditions to the Constitution. Reading the judgment alone produces the opposite error of supposing the declared rights to be the administered reality. The pair, read together with the table open, is the complete legal position, and it is materially different from either document taken singly, which is the deepest justification for treating a judgment and its sequel statute in separate articles of one series rather than in a single summary of both.

## The implementation record, direction by direction

The recognition direction was the first to be executed, and its execution began before the statute. Government forms and identity systems acquired a third option: the Election Commission had permitted an others category in electoral rolls from 2009, and after the judgment the third gender or transgender entry spread through identity documents, examination forms and administrative applications at both levels of government. The change is easy to underrate because it is clerical. It is in fact the difference the petitioners had sued for: a person who can state an identity in the state's own paperwork can open the accounts, claim the rations, sit the examinations and register the tenancy from which the undocumented are excluded. The certificate machinery that the 2019 Act and its 2020 Rules then built, application to the District Magistrate and a certificate of identity as the proof of recognition on which other documents follow, is the administrative completion of this direction, and its detail belongs to the statute's own article. The direction on identity was, in substance, implemented.

The legislative path to the Act was neither straight nor quick, and its stages explain the statute's final shape. The Expert Committee reported in January 2014 and the Court ordered its recommendations examined against the judgment within six months. In April 2015 the Rajya Sabha passed the Rights of Transgender Persons Bill, a private member's bill moved by Tiruchi Siva, which embodied self-identification without a medical condition; it was not taken up in the Lok Sabha and lapsed. The government's own bill of 2016 departed from the judgment at several points, was sent to the Standing Committee on Social Justice and Empowerment, and was passed by the Lok Sabha in December 2018 in a revised form that restored some ground and kept the certificate structure; it lapsed with the dissolution of that Lok Sabha. The bill of 2019, enacted that year, retained the architecture the government had settled on: self-perceived identity as the stated right, a District Magistrate's certificate as its proof, and a surgical certificate as the gateway to the male or female marker. Each stage of that path moved the text some distance from the private member's bill, which is to say from the judgment, and the direction of travel was consistent: recognition was never again in dispute, and its conditions were the whole argument.

Education shows the same pattern in a quieter register. The judgment's equality reading entitled the community to admission without discrimination and, through the reservation direction, to affirmative provision; the Act's Section 13 obliges every educational institution funded or recognised by the appropriate government to provide inclusive education and opportunities for sports, recreation and leisure to transgender persons without discrimination on an equal basis with others. Between the direction and the duty sits the machinery of admission itself, and it is there that implementation has concretely occurred: university, scholarship and examination application systems acquired the third category in their forms, following the judgment and the regulatory communications that transmitted it, so that a transgender applicant can at least state an identity the system will record. What the forms cannot deliver is the rest of the educational record the judgment described, the retention of children driven out by ridicule, the sensitisation of institutions, the hostel and toilet on the campus, and on those the record remains one of policy statements and local initiative rather than of a delivered programme, which is the recurring shape of this judgment's programmatic half.

The health, welfare and awareness directions were executed through the scheme route rather than the statutory one, and the record is correspondingly uneven. The 2019 Act itself carried the sero-surveillance direction into Section 15 and legislated healthcare facilities, including provision for sex reassignment surgery, hormonal therapy, counselling and insurance coverage, so that the medical half of the programme has statutory footing. Its welfare half runs through the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment: the national portal through which certificate applications and scheme information flow, and the SMILE scheme, the ministry's umbrella programme for the community, under which Garima Greh shelter homes are supported. Separate public toilets, directed in terms by the Court, were never legislated in the Act; they have been pursued through building bye-laws, scheme guidelines and the practice of public institutions, which is to say through the slowest of the available instruments. A researcher asking which directions were implemented should therefore distinguish three kinds of answer: legislated, as with identity and healthcare; administered, as with welfare and awareness; and aspirational, as with toilets and with the restoration of the community's historic place, which no instrument has seriously attempted to define, let alone deliver.

One obligation in the operative part had a date attached, and its fate is a small parable of the whole. The Expert Committee's recommendations were to be examined against the judgment and implemented within six months. No public compliance accounting under that direction ever became part of the record; the committee's programme was instead absorbed into the ordinary processes of government, its umbrella scheme recommendation emerging in altered form in the ministry's later programming and its legislative recommendations entering the bill process that produced, first, the private member's bill the committee's logic supported and, eventually, the government's Act that departed from it. A timed direction without a reporting mechanism is, in practice, an exhortation with a date, and the six months mattered less as a deadline than as a signal of the judgment's expectation that the political process would move. It did move, over five years and three bills, and the distance between the committee's recommendations and the enacted text is another measure, parallel to the table, of how much of the judgment's programme survived contact with the legislative process. The committee had recommended recognition independent of surgery or hormones, as the judgment required; the Act's Section 7 is the point at which that recommendation, twice endorsed, was set aside.

The documentary execution of the recognition direction can be followed through the forms themselves, and the sequence is the most tangible measure of the judgment's reach. The electoral roll's others category predated the judgment. After it, the third entry spread: the Aadhaar enrolment form's transgender option, the permanent account number application's transgender entry, the passport system's third gender provision, the application forms of the Union Public Service Commission and the staff selection machinery, bank account opening forms and the records of the public distribution system each acquired, at different speeds, the third answer the judgment had declared. Each acquisition was a small administrative act, and together they constitute the direction's real implementation, because a right to identity is in practice a right to complete the paperwork of citizenship in one's own name. The certificate under the 2019 Act then became the master document from which corrections in other records were to follow, which is why the conditions attached to the certificate, and to its revised form, carry a weight out of proportion to their drafting length: they gate not one document but the whole documentary existence the judgment set out to repair.

The 2020 Rules made under the Act adjusted the certificate's conditions in the direction of the judgment at the first stage. An applicant for the certificate of identity as transgender proceeds on an affidavit of self-declaration, without medical examination, which is the judgment's self-identification principle in administrative form. The adjustment stops at the statute's boundary, as subordinate legislation must: the revised certificate recording the holder as male or female remains governed by Section 7 of the Act, with its requirement of surgery and a medical officer's certificate, and no rule could remove a condition the Act itself imposes. The two-stage structure that results, self-declaration for the transgender marker and medical certification for the binary markers, is therefore not an administrative accident but the exact imprint of the statute's modification of the judgment, legible in the daily experience of every applicant who reaches the second stage.

State policy frameworks fill part of the space the statute left. Kerala adopted a state policy for transgender persons in 2015, the first of its kind, committing its departments to identity documentation, education, employment and protection against discrimination, and Karnataka followed with a state policy issued by its Department of Women and Child Development in 2017, with a certification and monitoring mechanism at district level; Tamil Nadu's welfare board for the community predates the judgment itself. These instruments are policy, not law, and their enforceability is correspondingly weak, but they are the level at which the awareness and welfare directions have chiefly been executed, and a state-by-state account of them is the honest form of the implementation record for those directions. On health, the insurance limb of Section 15 acquired administrative substance through the arrangement between the National Health Authority and the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment extending Ayushman Bharat health assurance to transgender certificate holders, an example of a direction implemented neither by the statute that cites it nor by the court that issued it, but by an inter-agency arrangement years later. The pattern across all of this deserves naming, because it is the series' recurring finding in this cluster: a direction is implemented in proportion as some institution finds it administratively convenient, and the directions requiring redistribution or construction, toilets in the one case and reservation in the other, wait longest.

The awareness direction, the eighth, deserves its own sentence in any honest accounting, because it is the direction whose performance is least capable of proof. Governments have conducted sensitisation programmes for officials, police and teachers, the ministry maintains campaigns around the certificate and the portal, and the National Council constituted under Sections 16 and 17 of the Act carries the inclusion agenda into its advisory work. None of this is measurable against the direction's own standard, which was that transgender persons should feel part and parcel of social life and not be treated as untouchables, a standard set in the language of social experience rather than of programme delivery. The direction is best recorded as implemented in form and unmeasured in substance, which is the fate of every direction a court phrases as a change in how a society feels, and recording it so is not a criticism of the Court that issued it. It is the recognition that the ninth direction's object, the restoration of a community's respect and place, lies beyond the competence of both the instruments the judgment commanded and the instruments the statute built, in a territory where law can remove obstacles and punish discrimination but cannot legislate regard.

## The health directions: why a rights case contains a medical programme

Two of the nine directions are medical, and their presence in a constitutional judgment is unusual enough to require explanation. Courts declare rights against hospitals; they do not ordinarily direct the operation of surveillance centres. The explanation lies in the record. The community's encounter with the health system was not incidental to its exclusion but one of its principal sites, and the Expert Committee's material documented it in operational detail: sexual health services that did not exist for the community as a class, a burden of HIV infection concentrated by the very conditions of exclusion, doctors and curricula that had never been required to address the community's needs, and hospitals whose wards, toilets and admission forms made a transgender patient an administrative impossibility before they were a clinical one. A judgment that had confined itself to recognition would have left the site of some of the gravest harms untouched, and this bench chose instead to direct the programme the record described.

The fourth direction, on sero-surveillance, is the most specific in the judgment and the one whose legislative afterlife is most exact. The Court directed the Centre and the State Governments to operate separate HIV sero-surveillance centres, since the community faces several sexual health issues. Section 15(a) of the 2019 Act reproduces the direction almost in its words, requiring the appropriate government to set up separate HIV sero-surveillance centres to conduct sero-surveillance for transgender persons in accordance with the guidelines issued by the National AIDS Control Organisation. Around that clause, Section 15 builds the rest of the medical programme the judgment had sketched in the fifth and sixth directions: medical care facilities including sex reassignment surgery and hormonal therapy, counselling before and after, a health manual on sex reassignment surgery in accordance with the standards of the World Professional Association for Transgender Health, a review of medical curriculum and research, facilitated access to hospitals and healthcare institutions, and insurance coverage for medical expenses. There is an irony the table makes visible and the text should not hide. The statute that reversed the judgment on surgery as a condition of recognition is meticulous in providing for surgery as a facility, funding the operation it made compulsory for one purpose while the judgment had insisted that the operation be available without being compulsory for any purpose. The difference between the two documents on medicine is therefore not provision but compulsion, and it is the same difference, in clinical dress, as the difference on identity itself.

The toilet limb of the sixth direction stands at the opposite pole of the implementation record, and the contrast is instructive. Separate public toilets required no doctrine, no classification and no redistribution between classes; they required construction budgets and building regulation, the most routine instruments of the state. Yet the Act does not mention them, and their provision has proceeded through the general law of building standards and the discretion of facility managers, unevenly and without a reporting line. A direction can fail, the comparison shows, for reasons having nothing to do with controversy: because it belongs to no department's legislative programme, because its beneficiaries lack the organisational weight to audit it, and because a court that has closed the case will never ask. The health directions as a group are thus the judgment in miniature, legislated where an existing programme could absorb them, as the AIDS control machinery absorbed sero-surveillance, and neglected where delivery required an institution to build something new for a constituency it had never served.

## The reservation direction and its discontents

The third direction ordered the Centre and the State Governments to take steps to treat transgender persons as socially and educationally backward classes of citizens and extend all kinds of reservation in cases of admission in educational institutions and for public appointments. It is the direction with the largest economic content and the emptiest record. The 2019 Act contains no reservation provision. No central rule has classified transgender persons as a backward class for the purposes of the Union's admissions or appointments. The question the direction poses has instead been litigated and legislated piecemeal in the States, which is where the honest account of it must be assembled.

The doctrinal objection deserves its strongest statement, because it is made by serious lawyers and not only by opponents of the community. Reservation in the constitutional scheme, as mapped in Indra Sawhney, is organised around classes identified by social and educational backwardness, and that concept was developed through, and remains entangled with, caste: the caste of a group is the starting point of the inquiry into its backwardness, and the lists of backward classes are lists of castes and caste-like communities. A class defined by gender identity cuts across every caste, containing persons of all castes and none, privileged and oppressed by turns, and it is not obvious, the objection runs, how such a class is to be fitted into a machinery of vertical quotas without either displacing the caste inquiry or duplicating it. There is a second-order difficulty of the same kind. If transgender persons are reserved as a class, does a transgender person of a Scheduled Caste hold two entitlements, and against which quota is a seat or a post to be counted? The horizontal reservation device, familiar from the reservation for women, answers that difficulty in principle, because a horizontal quota cuts across the vertical compartments and counts the beneficiary within their own compartment. But the judgment did not specify the device, and the objection concludes that a direction to extend all kinds of reservation, issued without the caste analysis that Indra Sawhney made the ordinary price of a backward class determination, asked governments to perform a classification the doctrine had not equipped them to perform.

The defence of the direction, also at its strongest, answers that the constitutional category is not confined by its history. Article 15(4) speaks of socially and educationally backward classes of citizens, and Article 16(4) of any backward class of citizens not adequately represented in the services; neither text says caste, and the community's social and educational backwardness is not an inference but a documented condition, of exclusion from schools, families and employment over generations, which the judgment's own survey records. The defence adds that the horizontal form dissolves the supposed competition with caste-based quotas, since a transgender candidate selected against the horizontal share occupies a seat within their own vertical compartment and displaces no other class's entitlement. On this view the direction's non-implementation is not doctrinal caution but political choice, and the choice is visible in the fact that the same governments which found the classification too difficult for reservation found no difficulty in certifying identity for every other purpose.

The record in the States shows both readings at work. Tamil Nadu notified transgender persons within its Most Backward Classes category, a vertical solution of the kind the objection contemplates. Kerala made ad hoc provision of seats in higher education. Karnataka, after litigation in Sangama v State of Karnataka in which the organisation Jeeva intervened to press for the horizontal form, amended the Karnataka Civil Services (General Recruitment) Rules in 2021 to insert Rule 9(1D), providing one per cent horizontal reservation for transgender persons in direct recruitment, one per cent within the general category, within the Scheduled Castes, within the Scheduled Tribes and within each category among the Other Backward Classes, making it the first state to give the direction general effect in public employment. The Karnataka route is the analytically interesting one, because it adopted the device the doctrinal debate had converged upon, and because it was produced by litigation under the judgment rather than by the judgment's own machinery. The national position, by contrast, remains the one the table records: a direction unimplemented by the Union, defended and attacked on grounds the Supreme Court has not revisited, and therefore a question this article reports as open, as it is.

The employment limb of the direction has a textual feature that the admissions limb lacks, and it strengthens the defence's hand. Article 16(4) permits reservation for any backward class of citizens which, in the opinion of the state, is not adequately represented in the services under the state. Inadequate representation is a condition the community satisfies on any view of the record: a class that could not obtain the documents required to apply was, at the date of the judgment, represented in the public services at a level indistinguishable from absence, and the few appointments that existed had been won through individual litigation rather than through any systemic provision. The direction's critics therefore cannot rest on the representation condition, and their argument is driven back to the classification question alone, whether a class defined by identity can be a backward class within the clause at all. On that question the judgment's own reasoning is complete in form: it found the social and educational backwardness as fact, treated the class as within the clause's words, and left the mechanics to the governments. A future bench asked to sustain a horizontal provision of the Karnataka kind would find in the judgment every premise it needs, which is one more reason the direction, though unimplemented at the Centre, is better described as dormant than as dead.

The mechanics of the horizontal device are worth stating plainly, because public discussion of this direction routinely confuses the two forms and the confusion distorts the argument. A vertical reservation carves the field of seats or posts into compartments, general and reserved, and a candidate competes within a compartment. A horizontal reservation operates inside every compartment at once: within the general compartment, within the Scheduled Caste compartment, within the Scheduled Tribe compartment and within each backward class compartment, a stated share is held for members of the horizontal class who are selected on merit within that compartment. A transgender candidate of a Scheduled Caste selected against the horizontal share is counted against the Scheduled Caste compartment, not against a separate transgender pool; a transgender candidate of no reserved caste is counted against the general compartment. That is why the horizontal form answers the double entitlement puzzle that troubles the vertical reading of the direction, and why the advocacy in the Karnataka litigation, pressed by the intervenor Jeeva, was for the horizontal form specifically. It is also why the direction's defenders describe its non-implementation as a choice: the device existed, was familiar from the reservation for women, and required no doctrinal invention, only a rule amendment of the kind Karnataka eventually made in 2021. The National Commission for Backward Classes was drawn into the same proceedings, the Karnataka High Court being informed that the Commission had submitted a report recommending reservation for transgender persons, which illustrates the unfinished institutional position: the body charged with advising on backward class lists had spoken in the direction's favour in that proceeding, and no central classification followed.

### Why does the choice between vertical and horizontal reservation matter here?

Because the two forms distribute the cost differently. A vertical reservation creates a new compartment and takes its seats from the whole field, which sets the transgender claim against the existing classes. A horizontal reservation takes a share inside every compartment, so the candidate counts within their own caste category and no class's compartment shrinks for another's.

## The third gender: recognition or segregation

The last of the judgment's debates is internal to the community it recognised, and it should be reported in the terms in which it is conducted rather than resolved from outside. One position holds that the third gender category is the judgment's great gift: it names in law an identity that the binary erased, it matches the self-understanding of hijra and other communities who have never claimed to be women or men, and it gives the state a category it can count, serve and protect. Laxmi Narayan Tripathi, the activist whose petition was heard with the Authority's, defended recognition in these terms, as the legal arrival of a community that had always existed outside the law's field of vision. On this view, a legal order that offers only male and female offers the community a choice between two falsehoods, and the third category is the truthful box.

The other position holds that a separate category can operate as segregation, and that it answers a question many transgender persons were not asking. A trans woman, on this argument, seeks recognition as a woman, not as a member of a third class set apart from women; a trans man seeks recognition as a man. To route all transgender persons through a third gender is to tell them that their identified gender is one they may not have, and to build the refusal into the very instrument of recognition. The judgment, read closely, is not vulnerable to the whole of this criticism, because its second declaration upholds the right to decide the self-identified gender as male, female or third gender, and the third gender declaration is expressly addressed to hijras and eunuchs apart from the binary. The vulnerability lies in administration and in the statute rather than in the declaration: where a form, a certificate or an official treats transgender and third gender as interchangeable, the choice the judgment protected is collapsed into the category it created as one option among three. The 2019 Act's certificate of identity certifies the holder as transgender, and the male or female marker is the revised certificate behind the surgical gateway, so the statutory scheme realises, in structure, the very collapse the second position warns against. That is why the internal debate and the compliance debate, which are usually conducted separately, are the same debate at this point.

The administration of the category shows the same tension at ground level, and the examples are concrete. A school record, a sports competition, a prison housing decision, a hostel allotment, each of these is a place where an institution must convert the abstract category into a placement, and placements made by default, every transgender person to the third column regardless of the gender they claim, reproduce the segregation the second position fears, while placements made by the person's own declaration reproduce the judgment. The variance between institutions, and between states, on this single operational question is wide, and it is rarely the product of policy; it is the product of which official read the judgment and which read only the form. That is a further reason the internal debate cannot be dismissed as a quarrel over labels. The label determines the column, the column determines the ward, the hostel, the queue and the cell, and a community that has experienced institutions as instruments of humiliation reads a new category with the suspicion its history has taught it. The judgment's defenders answer, correctly, that the text protects the choice; the debate persists because the choice is exercised against administrators, one form at a time, and the text's protection is only as general as the forms that embody it.

## The NALSA judgment among the later decisions

No judgment in this field is misread as often as this one, and the misreading is usually a conflation. Readers merge it with the decisions on sexuality that came before and after it, and attribute to it holdings it does not contain. The distinctions are not pedantic; each decision decided a different question. This judgment concerned gender identity, who a person is, and the recognition the law owes to that identity. It did not concern the criminality of any sexual act, and it did not concern marriage.

The sequence begins before it. In 2009 the Delhi High Court in Naz Foundation read down the provision of the Penal Code that criminalised carnal intercourse against the order of nature, holding it unconstitutional in its application to consensual adult acts in private. In December 2013, months before this judgment, a two-judge bench of the Supreme Court in Suresh Kumar Koushal reversed that reading and restored the provision, treating the affected population as a minuscule fraction whose rights could be left to Parliament. The NALSA bench, which sat in the shadow of that restoration, was careful about the boundary. Its subject was gender identity, a matter it treated as distinct from sexual orientation, and it decided nothing about the provision's validity, while recording in its survey of the legal landscape the harassment the community faced under colour of it. The care was rewarded with survival: when the provision returned to the Court, it returned on the sexuality track, not the identity track, and nothing in this judgment stood in the way.

On the identity and autonomy track, the line runs forward. The nine-judge bench in Puttaswamy, in 2017, held privacy to be a fundamental right and treated dignity, autonomy and identity as its constituent values, drawing on this judgment's reasoning that gender identity lies at the core of personal autonomy; the plurality's account of the constitutional self, a self entitled to constitute its own identity, is unintelligible without the step this case had taken three years earlier. In 2018 a Constitution Bench in Navtej Singh Johar read down the Penal Code provision to exclude consensual adult relations, and the opinions treated this judgment as settled ground on the equality of gender identity and on the proposition that discrimination on the ground of sex reaches beyond the biological, while deciding the distinct question of the provision's validity that this bench had left alone. The provision's afterlife belongs to the criminal codes: the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, which replaced the Penal Code, omitted the provision altogether, a legislative silence whose consequences for the residual uses of the old section are traced in the series' comparison of [the IPC and the BNS](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/). In 2023, in Supriyo v Union of India, a Constitution Bench declined to recognise a right to marry for same-sex couples, and the judgments there dealt with this case as authority on identity and discrimination, not on marriage, a subject on which it says nothing at all. The pattern across a decade is consistent: later benches have received this judgment as the foundation of the identity line of doctrine and have been careful, as it was careful, not to make it carry the sexuality and marriage questions it never decided.

The identity line also ran through the High Courts, and one decision illustrates what the judgment made possible at the level of an individual life. In Arun Kumar v Inspector General of Registration, decided in 2019, the Madras High Court held that a marriage between a man and a trans woman, solemnised according to Hindu rites, is a valid marriage entitled to registration, reading the statutory word bride in the Hindu Marriage Act in the light of the judgment's declaration that a person's self-identified gender is their gender in law. The decision is the template working as designed: the declaration supplied the premise, the High Court applied it to a statute the Supreme Court had never mentioned, and a couple acquired a legal status that the binary reading of the same word would have denied them. Registration officers, school boards, prison administrators and examination bodies across the country have worked through the same exercise on their own instruments, citing the judgment as the warrant, which is the unglamorous mechanism by which a declaration becomes a legal culture.

## How the judgment is used in practice

For the advocate, the judgment's working life is in its paragraphs rather than its reputation, and the citations that recur in pleadings are predictable. The declarations are cited from the operative part of the leading opinion, where the nine directions are set out, and the propositions for which the case is authority in argument are four: that sex in Articles 15 and 16 includes gender identity; that self-identification, determined by the psychological test, is the legal basis of recognition; that insistence on surgery as a condition of declaring gender is illegal; and that the community is a socially and educationally backward class entitled to the affirmative action the Constitution enables. Service and admission litigation is where these propositions earn their keep. Recruitment notifications that call only for men and women, physical standards applied to disqualify a transgender candidate without reference to the identified gender, admission processes that offer no third entry, these are the recurring fact patterns, and in case after case, from the Karnataka police recruitment litigation that produced the horizontal reservation rule to the Tamil Nadu litigation in which a transgender candidate for the police services fought successively over the validity of her application, the relaxation of standards and her disqualification on a physical test, the judgment is the first and often the sufficient authority. Prithika Yashini's appointment as a sub-inspector in Tamil Nadu, achieved through that litigation route, is the standard illustration of the judgment operating as a working tool rather than a text for celebration.

For the researcher and the student, the judgment's use is different and makes a demand of accuracy that summary defeats. The case is examined material in constitutional law papers precisely because it tests whether a candidate can separate what a court held from what a court said, and the separations this article has maintained are the ones the examiners reward: the declarations from the directions, the leading opinion from the concurrence, the identity question from the sexuality question, and the judgment from the statute that followed it. A candidate who writes that the Court granted reservation has the direction right and the record wrong; a candidate who writes that the Court required a certificate has the statute's machinery wrongly attributed to the judgment; a candidate who treats the case as a decision about Section 377 has merged two lines of doctrine the benches themselves kept apart. The judgment, handled at the level of its text, is a complete education in how constitutional adjudication works, which is why it survives in the syllabus while more celebrated decisions fade from it.

## What the judgment did not decide

A judicial interpretation study owes its reader the negative space of the case as carefully as its holdings, and this case's negative space is extensive. The judgment did not amend any statute. Every binary provision it surveyed, in the Penal Code's definitions, in the marriage, adoption, divorce, inheritance and succession laws, in service rules and welfare statutes, remained in force in its existing terms the day after the judgment, to be applied, where a conflict arose, in the light of the constitutional reading the judgment had declared. A transgender person's claims under the personal laws, to marry in the identified gender, to adopt, to inherit as a son or as a daughter, were not decided, and they have been litigated since on a provision by provision basis, with the judgment as premise rather than as answer. The judgment did not decide the validity of Section 377 of the Penal Code, for the reasons given in the sequence above. It did not create a crime, a compensation scheme or a commission; the statutory machinery of protection, the offences and penalties and the National Council, are creations of the 2019 Act and belong to its article. It did not fix any percentage for the reservation it directed, name the authority to maintain a list, or specify the horizontal or vertical form, silences that the reservation debate has had to work around ever since.

It also did not do something subtler, which careful readers should register. It did not hold that gender is irrelevant to law, or that the state may not record sex where sex is material, in medicine, in sport, in prison administration or in the criminal law's protections framed for women. Its holding is about identity and its recognition: that the state's record must answer to the person's identity, determined by the psychological test, and that differential treatment resting on the refusal of recognition is discrimination. Questions about the material relevance of sex in particular statutory schemes remain questions about those schemes, to be argued with the judgment as the starting point and not as a universal solvent. Much weak advocacy, and some weak commentary, treats the case as having decided every question in the field. It decided the foundational one, and its discipline in deciding no other is a large part of why the foundation has held.

## What remains unsettled, and the verdict

Three questions remain genuinely open, and the article's honesty requires them to be named as open. The first is the reservation question in its doctrinal form: whether treatment as a socially and educationally backward class can be effected for a class defined by gender identity within the ceiling and classification jurisprudence of Indra Sawhney, and if so by the vertical or the horizontal route, is a question no Supreme Court bench has answered since the direction issued, and the Karnataka litigation shows it being answered administratively in one state while remaining unanswered as doctrine. The second is the constitutional fate of the 2019 Act's certificate scheme, and in particular of the surgical condition in Section 7, which sits in visible tension with the judgment's operative sentence on the subject; the challenges raising it invoke the judgment as constitutional authority, and until a bench decides them, the accurate statement is that the statute governs and its most contested provision is under challenge on the judgment's own ground. A fourth open question is definitional, and it arises from the fit between the judgment's concepts and the statute's text. The judgment's subject was gender identity: persons whose internal sense of gender does not conform to the sex assigned at birth, together with the socio-cultural identities it named. The Act's definition of a transgender person, in Section 2(k), follows that subject and then extends beyond it, sweeping in a person with intersex variations, which are variations of sex characteristics rather than of gender identity, and which many intersex persons and their advocates say belong to a different legal and medical conversation altogether. The definitional detail belongs to the statute's own article; what belongs here is the observation that the judgment supplies the conceptual apparatus by which the extension is criticised, since a framework built on the distinction between the body and the psyche is an awkward home for a category defined by the body, and the challenges and consultations on the Act's definitions have argued the point in exactly the judgment's vocabulary. The third is the precedential question in reverse: how much of the judgment's programme a later bench would reaffirm if the directions themselves, and not merely the declarations, were put in issue, given that they issued from a bench of two into fields administered by many governments. This account is legal information and analysis of a judgment and a statute, and it is not advice on any individual application for recognition, admission or appointment, on which the rules of the particular institution and the discipline of its timelines must be checked in the case at hand.

There is a final measurement to record, and it is the one by which the judgment will be read when the present controversies are history. Constitutional decisions are remembered for the proposition for which later law cannot do without them. The proposition for which this judgment is indispensable is not the third gender, which a statute could have created, nor the directions, most of which a scheme could have announced. It is the reading of sex: the holding that a word the Constitution uses in its equality code carries both the body and the psyche, and that discrimination against a person's gender identity is discrimination on a ground the Constitution names. That reading has been absorbed into the reasoning of the privacy and sexuality benches, it structures the argument in every pending challenge to the 2019 Act, and it will structure questions the present generation of litigants has not yet framed, wherever a statute sorts persons by sex and a person sorted wrongly objects. Judgments that supply the premise for other people's cases outlive judgments that merely win their own, and by that measure this two-judge decision has already outlasted most of the Constitution Bench work of its decade.

The verdict on the judgment itself can be given without hedging, because the record supports it. As a declaration of rights, the judgment did what it said: it read the Constitution as it is written, in provisions whose language is general, and derived from them a recognition that the legal system had withheld by assumption rather than by text. As an instrument of government, it achieved the parts of its programme that needed a form, a centre or a scheme, and failed at the two parts that needed a redistribution, of status in the case of the male and female marker, and of seats and posts in the case of reservation. A judgment cannot appropriate money or amend a service rule, and this one's history is the cleanest demonstration in the series of where judicial power over social policy ends: at the point where the directed change must be legislated to be real. The statute that followed proves the demonstration from the other side, by legislating the recognition the Court had declared and declining the remedy the Court had directed. Readers who hold both documents together hold the whole of the matter, and readers who hold only one of them hold, in the strictest sense, a position the law has already moved beyond or has not yet reached.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the NALSA judgment decide?

The Supreme Court held that hijras and eunuchs, apart from binary gender, are to be treated as a third gender for safeguarding their rights under the Constitution, and that every transgender person has the right to decide their self-identified gender as male, female or third gender. It read sex in Articles 15 and 16 as including gender identity, grounded gender expression in Article 19(1)(a) and dignity and personal autonomy in Article 21, and issued nine directions to the Centre and the State Governments, covering recognition, reservation, health, welfare and public awareness.

### Q: Did NALSA require surgery for gender recognition?

No, and the judgment states the opposite in its operative part. In the fifth of its directions, addressed to the problems of fear, shame, gender dysphoria, social pressure and stigma faced by the community, the Court declared that any insistence for sex reassignment surgery for declaring one's gender is immoral and illegal. Recognition under the judgment rests on the person's own decision of their self-identified gender, reached through the psychological test the Court preferred to the biological test. A medical procedure is neither the proof of gender nor a condition the state may impose before declaring it.

### Q: What directions did the Court give on reservation in NALSA?

One direction dealt with reservation, and it was cast in the widest terms the Court could have chosen. The third direction required the Centre and the State Governments to take steps to treat transgender persons as socially and educationally backward classes of citizens and to extend all kinds of reservation in cases of admission in educational institutions and for public appointments. The direction rested on the judgment's reading of Articles 15(4) and 16(4): the community, the Court held, is a socially and educationally backward class legally entitled and eligible to the benefits those clauses enable, and the state is bound to take affirmative action for its advancement.

### Q: How did NALSA read sex in Articles 15 and 16?

The Court held that sex has two components. Biological characteristics, genitals, chromosomes and secondary sexual features, are one component; gender attributes, a person's self-image and the deep psychological or emotional sense of sexual identity and character, are the other. Discrimination on the ground of sex under Articles 15 and 16 therefore includes discrimination on the ground of gender identity. The expression sex in those articles is not limited to the biological sex of male and female and is intended to include persons who consider themselves to be neither male nor female, which brings the whole equality code to bear on gender identity.

### Q: Which NALSA directions were implemented?

The record divides by kind. The recognition direction was implemented through identity documents and forms and then through the certificate machinery of the 2019 Act and its Rules. The sero-surveillance and healthcare directions were legislated in Section 15 of that Act, and the welfare and awareness directions were carried into Sections 8 and 14 and executed through schemes, the national portal and the SMILE programme. Separate public toilets rest on executive measures rather than statute. The reservation direction was not implemented by the Centre or by the Act at all, and the restoration of the community's historic place remains an aspiration rather than a delivered programme.

### Q: Does the 2019 Act comply with NALSA?

In part, and the parts can be identified exactly because the judgment numbered its directions. The Act implements the recognition of a transgender identity, the sero-surveillance centres, the healthcare facilities and the welfare obligations. It departs from the judgment at two points. Its Section 7 makes a revised certificate recording a person as male or female conditional on surgery certified by a medical officer, against the judgment's declaration that insistence on surgery for declaring gender is immoral and illegal. And it contains no reservation provision, leaving the direction to treat the community as a socially and educationally backward class with all kinds of reservation unimplemented. Compliance is therefore real on the programmatic directions and absent on the two directions of greatest material consequence.

### Q: What is the third gender category?

It is the legal category the judgment declared for persons who are neither male nor female, stated in the first declaration: hijras and eunuchs, apart from binary gender, are to be treated as third gender for the purpose of safeguarding their rights under Part III of the Constitution and the laws made by Parliament and the State Legislatures. The category corresponds to identities the judgment records as historically present in the country, including the tritiya prakriti of Vedic and Puranic literature. It operates in documents, forms and entitlements as a third answer, alongside male and female, to the question of a person's gender.

### Q: How does NALSA relate to later judgments on sexuality and marriage?

It is the foundation of the identity line and an authority on neither of the other two questions. The Constitution Bench in Navtej Singh Johar, which read down the Penal Code provision on consensual adult relations in 2018, treated the judgment as settled ground on gender identity and on discrimination, while deciding the distinct question of that provision's validity. The nine-judge bench in Puttaswamy drew on its reasoning on dignity, autonomy and identity for the privacy right. In Supriyo, the Constitution Bench dealt with the judgment as authority on identity and discrimination and declined, on other grounds, to recognise a right to marry for same-sex couples, a subject the judgment never addressed.

### Q: Who decided the NALSA case, and when was the judgment delivered?

The case, National Legal Services Authority v Union of India, was decided on 15 April 2014 by a bench of two judges of the Supreme Court, Justice K. S. Radhakrishnan and Justice A. K. Sikri. Justice Radhakrishnan wrote the leading opinion, containing the declarations and directions, and Justice Sikri wrote a separate concurring opinion that agreed in the result and developed the reasoning on history and on the test for determining gender. The lead petition, Writ Petition (Civil) No. 400 of 2012, was filed by the National Legal Services Authority and was heard with Writ Petition (Civil) No. 604 of 2013. The case is cited as (2014) 5 SCC 438.

### Q: How does NALSA distinguish gender identity from sexual orientation?

The judgment treats the two as different subjects that public discussion habitually confuses. Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their own gender, of being male, female or of a third gender, and it is about who a person is. Sexual orientation concerns the persons to whom one is drawn, and it is about conduct and attraction rather than identity. The distinction did practical work in the case: the bench was concerned with recognition of identity and decided nothing about the validity of the Penal Code provision on sexual acts, a question that belonged to a different line of cases and was decided on its own terms in Navtej Singh Johar.

### Q: What did Justice Sikri add in his concurring opinion in NALSA?

Justice Sikri agreed with the declarations and directions and wrote separately to deepen two parts of the reasoning. He traced the historical position of the transgender community in India at greater length, and he developed the test by which gender is to be determined, reasoning that since the experience to be recognised is psychological, a test confined to the body decides the question by ignoring the evidence that matters, so that determination must rest on the psychological test and on the person's own identification. He also underlined the distinction between gender identity and sexual orientation, and the responsibility of the state towards a community whose exclusion the record documented in detail.

### Q: How did NALSA use the Yogyakarta Principles?

The Yogyakarta Principles are a statement, drawn up by international human rights experts, of how existing international human rights law applies in relation to sexual orientation and gender identity. The Court treated them as material it could act upon because, as it put it, they are not inconsistent with the various fundamental rights guaranteed under the Indian Constitution. They supported the judgment's central moves: that gender identity is to be defined by the person, that recognition must not be conditioned on medical procedures, and that states bear obligations of protection against discrimination. The judgment read them alongside the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the Directive Principles, as persuasive material pointing the same way as the constitutional text.

### Q: What did NALSA hold about gender expression under Article 19(1)(a)?

The Court held that the freedom of speech and expression includes a person's right to expression of their self-identified gender. Self-identified gender, in the judgment's words, can be expressed through dress, words, action or behaviour or any other form, and no restriction can be placed on a person's personal appearance or choice of dressing beyond the restrictions Article 19(2) permits. The Court placed the values of privacy, self-identity, autonomy and personal integrity within the protection of Article 19(1)(a) for members of the transgender community, and held that the state cannot prohibit, restrict or interfere with a transgender person's expression of a personality that reflects their inherent identity.

### Q: What role did the Expert Committee on transgender issues play in the NALSA story?

The Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment constituted an Expert Committee on issues relating to transgender persons while the petitions were pending, and the committee reported on 27 January 2014, recommending that transgender persons be declared a third gender with the option of identifying as man, woman or transgender independent of surgery or hormones. The Supreme Court, in the operative part of its judgment, directed that the committee's recommendations be examined on the basis of the legal declaration made in the judgment and implemented within six months. The committee therefore supplied the administrative programme, and the judgment converted it from advice into a programme governments were ordered to assess against declared rights.

### Q: What happened to the private member's bill on transgender rights passed by the Rajya Sabha?

The Rights of Transgender Persons Bill, moved in the Rajya Sabha as a private member's bill by Tiruchi Siva, was passed by that House in April 2015. It embodied the judgment's scheme closely, including self-identification of gender without a medical condition. A private member's bill must still pass the other House to become law, and the bill was not taken up in the Lok Sabha; it lapsed. The government's own bills of 2016 and 2019 followed a different architecture, built around a certificate issued by the District Magistrate, and it was that architecture, rather than the private member's text, that Parliament ultimately enacted in the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019.

### Q: Why do some transgender persons object to a separate third gender category?

The objection is that a separate category can operate as segregation rather than recognition. A trans woman, on this argument, seeks recognition as a woman, and a trans man as a man; to route every transgender person through a third category is to withhold the identified gender and to mark the person apart in every document and queue. The contrary position, held by many in the hijra and other communities, is that the third gender names an identity that is neither male nor female and that erasure into the binary would be the greater falsehood. The judgment itself protects choice among male, female and third gender; the objection is directed at practice and at statutes that treat transgender and third gender as interchangeable.

### Q: Can a person choose recognition as male or female under NALSA instead of the third gender?

Yes. The second declaration upholds transgender persons' right to decide their self-identified gender and directs the Centre and the State Governments to grant legal recognition of their gender identity such as male, female or as third gender. The third gender declaration is addressed to hijras and eunuchs apart from the binary; it creates an additional category and does not confine any person to it. Choice among the three is the substance of the right the judgment declares, exercisable on the person's own decision and, on the judgment's operative words, without insistence on surgery as a condition of declaring the chosen gender.

### Q: How have States acted on the NALSA reservation direction?

Divergently, and only in part. Tamil Nadu notified transgender persons within its Most Backward Classes category, fitting the community into the existing vertical lists. Kerala made ad hoc provision of seats in undergraduate and postgraduate courses. Karnataka, following litigation in Sangama v State of Karnataka in which the organisation Jeeva intervened, amended its Civil Services (General Recruitment) Rules in 2021 to provide one per cent horizontal reservation for transgender persons in direct recruitment, applied within the general category, the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and each category among the Other Backward Classes, the first general state measure of its kind in public employment. No equivalent central measure has been made.

### Q: What did NALSA say about the historical position of the transgender community in India?

The judgment records that the community, comprising hijras, eunuchs, kothis, aravanis, jogappas and shiv-shakthis among others, has a strong historical presence in the country, in Hindu mythology and other religious texts, and that the concept of tritiya prakriti, the third nature, is an integral part of Vedic and Puranic literature. It traces the decline of that position to colonial law, including the part of the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871 directed at eunuchs and their registration. The ninth direction is built on this history: the Centre and the State Governments were directed to take measures to regain the respect and place in society which the community once enjoyed in the country's cultural and social life.

### Q: What authority does a two-judge bench decision like NALSA carry for later courts?

A decision of a two-judge bench is binding precedent for benches of equal and smaller strength and states the law unless a larger bench holds otherwise. In this instance the authority has run in the opposite direction from doubt: benches of greater strength, including the nine judges in Puttaswamy and the Constitution Benches in the later sexuality cases, have cited the judgment with approval on identity, dignity and the reading of sex. The point on which its strength is genuinely untested is narrower. Its programmatic directions, and above all the reservation direction, entered territory mapped by larger benches, and no later bench has had to decide how far those directions bind governments that have not carried them out.
