## The Argument a Face Makes

Casting Gatsby is the first act of interpretation any film version performs, and it happens before a single line of Fitzgerald's dialogue is spoken. How each film cast the roles determines what the audience believes about these people from the moment they appear, because a face carries a set of assumptions with it: age, class, hardness, sincerity, whether this person is someone things happen to or someone who makes things happen. Five screen versions across nearly a century have made five distinct sets of choices, and read side by side those choices form a record of how each era decided to understand the novel.

![Casting Gatsby: how each film cast the roles across five screen adaptations - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The usual way of discussing this is to score the performances, which produces opinions rather than arguments. A more useful method treats each casting decision as a proposition about the character and asks whether the novel supports it. Fitzgerald gives unusually precise physical and vocal descriptions of almost everyone in this book, and he gives them for a reason. Tom's arrogance is written into his shoulders. Daisy's power is located in her voice rather than her face. Gatsby's charm is specified as a smile with a mechanism attached. When a film casts against those specifications, it is not making a mistake so much as advancing a counter-reading, and the counter-reading can be evaluated.

The claim this article defends is that the screen versions disagree systematically rather than randomly. Two questions divide them. The first is whether Gatsby is cool or ardent, a man of poised surface or a man of visible hunger. The second is whether Daisy is a victim of her circumstances or a participant in them. Every major casting decision in every version answers one of those two questions, and once you can see the answers, the differences between the films stop looking like variations in quality and start looking like arguments.

## Five Versions, Five Casts

The record needs to be accurate before it can be read, and it is frequently reported wrong.

The first adaptation appeared in 1926, a year after publication, directed by Herbert Brenon. Warner Baxter played Gatsby, Lois Wilson played Daisy, Neil Hamilton played Nick, Hale Hamilton played Tom, and Georgia Hale played Myrtle. The film is lost. No complete print is known to survive, and what remains is a trailer, which means that the earliest screen interpretation of these characters exists now only as a set of names and a fragment of footage. That absence is itself part of the reception history, and it has the odd effect of making the first casting the one nobody can argue about.

The 1949 version, directed by Elliott Nugent, put Alan Ladd in the lead, with Betty Field as Daisy, Macdonald Carey as Nick, Barry Sullivan as Tom, Ruth Hussey as Jordan, Shelley Winters as Myrtle, and Howard Da Silva as George Wilson. Ladd was a star of hard-boiled crime pictures, and the film leans into that association, treating Gatsby as a gangster with a romantic weakness. This is the most consequential single casting decision in the history of these adaptations, because it commits the film to reading the criminality as the character's centre rather than as his background.

The 1974 version, directed by Jack Clayton from a screenplay by Francis Ford Coppola, cast Robert Redford as Gatsby, Mia Farrow as Daisy, Sam Waterston as Nick, Bruce Dern as Tom, Lois Chiles as Jordan, Karen Black as Myrtle, Scott Wilson as George Wilson, and Howard Da Silva, returning to the material twenty-five years later, as Meyer Wolfshiem. Redford was at the height of his career as an emblem of American golden handsomeness, and the film uses that emblem more or less as it stands.

A television version arrived in 2000, directed by Robert Markowitz, with Toby Stephens as Gatsby, Mira Sorvino as Daisy, Paul Rudd as Nick, Martin Donovan as Tom, and Francie Swift as Jordan. It is the least discussed of the versions and it makes some of the most interesting choices, particularly in its Nick.

The 2013 version, directed by Baz Luhrmann, cast Leonardo DiCaprio as Gatsby, Carey Mulligan as Daisy, Tobey Maguire as Nick, Joel Edgerton as Tom, Elizabeth Debicki as Jordan, Isla Fisher as Myrtle, Jason Clarke as George Wilson, and Amitabh Bachchan as Wolfshiem. The full evaluation of that picture appears in [the dedicated analysis of the 2013 Baz Luhrmann Gatsby](/2029/09/10/gatsby-2013-luhrmann-film/), and the ranking of all the versions against one another is worked out in [the survey of every Great Gatsby film adaptation](/2029/09/03/gatsby-film-adaptations-ranked/).

### Who has played Gatsby on screen?

Warner Baxter played him in the lost 1926 silent film, Alan Ladd in 1949, Robert Redford in 1974, Toby Stephens in the 2000 television version, and Leonardo DiCaprio in Baz Luhrmann's 2013 picture. Each casting encodes a different reading of whether the character is primarily a criminal, an icon, or a striver.

## What Fitzgerald Actually Wrote About These Faces

The novel is far more specific about physical presence than most readers remember, and the specificity is doing analytical work in every case.

Gatsby's first full description arrives after Nick has already spent a chapter hearing about him. Fitzgerald calls him an elegant young roughneck, a year or two over thirty, and the compound is doing everything. Elegant and roughneck are not compatible, and the sentence does not resolve them. It leaves a man who has acquired polish that does not quite cover something underneath. That is a casting note. It says the actor must show the seam.

The smile is described with almost mechanical precision. It was one of those rare smiles with a quality of eternal reassurance in it, that you may come across four or five times in life. Fitzgerald then explains its operation: it faced the whole eternal world for an instant, and then concentrated on you with an irresistible prejudice in your favour. It understood you just so far as you wanted to be understood. And then, crucially, precisely at that point it vanished. This is not a description of warmth. It is a description of a technique with a timer on it. The reader is told the charm is real and manufactured at once, and the ideal casting would let an audience feel both.

Daisy is defined by sound rather than sight. Nick registers her low, thrilling voice, the kind of voice that the ear follows up and down, and adds that each speech is an arrangement of notes that will never be played again. Her face is sad and lovely with bright things in it, bright eyes and a bright passionate mouth, but Fitzgerald immediately pivots back to the sound. Gatsby's own diagnosis, delivered in chapter seven, is that her voice is full of money. [The complete character study of Daisy Buchanan](/2023/06/26/daisy-buchanan-character-analysis/) works through what that verdict implies about her culpability.

Tom is written as physical intimidation. He is a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty, with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner, and two shining arrogant eyes had established dominance over his face. Fitzgerald then names the effect: not even the effeminate swank of his riding clothes could hide the enormous power of that body, and later, flatly, a cruel body. The casting note here is unambiguous. Tom must be large and must read as dangerous before he speaks.

Jordan is compact and self-contained, extended full length at her end of the divan, with a wan, charming, discontented face and grey sun-strained eyes. Myrtle is in the middle thirties, faintly stout, and carries her flesh sensuously as some women can, with an immediately perceptible vitality about her. Her husband is a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome, which is a description of a man already half erased.

### Does Fitzgerald describe Gatsby's appearance?

Yes, and precisely. He is an elegant young roughneck, a year or two over thirty, a compound Fitzgerald leaves unresolved. The smile is analyzed as a mechanism that concentrates on you with an irresistible prejudice in your favour and then vanishes, a casting note demanding charm and calculation at once.
## The Cool Gatsby and the Ardent Gatsby

The central division across the versions is temperature. Every Gatsby on screen is either cool or ardent, and the choice determines the shape of the whole film.

The cool Gatsby is Redford's. He is composed, beautiful, and slightly remote, and the film asks the audience to accept that composure as the character. The advantage of this reading is that it takes seriously how much work Gatsby has put into his surface. A man who spent years constructing a persona out of borrowed material has, by definition, gotten good at it, and a Gatsby who is visibly straining has failed at the thing the character has succeeded at. The cool reading also handles the class question elegantly, because Redford's ease is precisely what old money possesses and new money is trying to buy.

The disadvantage is that the cool reading has trouble with the hunger. Fitzgerald's Gatsby is not a poised man. He is a man whose poise is a container for something enormous, and the novel keeps showing the container failing. He knocks a clock off a mantelpiece. He stares at a green light with his arms stretched out. At the reunion his head leans back so far that it rests against the face of a defunct mantelpiece clock, which he then catches with trembling fingers. A performance built on composure has to find room for a man who cannot hold still, and the 1974 picture's persistent reputation for being beautiful and inert is partly a consequence of not finding it.

The ardent Gatsby is DiCaprio's. This version makes the effort visible: the strain of the accent, the anxiety in the reunion scene, the rage that surfaces at the Plaza. It reads the character as a striver who is one bad afternoon away from coming apart, which is what happens. The advantage is that it captures the vitality of the illusion, and it makes the collapse legible because the audience has been watching the seams the whole time. The disadvantage is symmetrical to the cool reading's: if the audience can always see the effort, it becomes hard to believe that a room full of sophisticated people could not.

Alan Ladd's 1949 Gatsby is neither, and that is why the version matters more than its reputation suggests. Ladd plays him as a professional criminal, which means the poise is not aspiration but occupational discipline. This reading has real textual support that the romantic versions tend to suppress. Gatsby's money comes from bootlegging and from a bond operation, Wolfshiem is a fixer with a documented career, and Tom's research at the Plaza is not a slander, it is accurate. A Gatsby played by a crime-picture star forces the audience to hold the criminality in view for the whole picture, and the argument over how much that criminality should weigh in a reading of the character is worked out in [the study of Jay Gatsby as a self-made man reconsidered](/2023/05/15/jay-gatsby-self-made-man/).

Toby Stephens, in the 2000 television version, offers a fourth option that deserves more attention than it gets: a theatrical Gatsby, one whose gentility is performed with an actor's technique rather than a criminal's discipline or a romantic's sincerity. The version has limitations of budget and scale, but the choice is intelligent, because Gatsby is in fact performing, and a stage-trained actor showing the performance is a defensible literalization of Fitzgerald's roughneck compound.

### How do the films' Gatsbys differ?

They divide on temperature. Redford plays him cool and composed, which honors the constructed surface but struggles with the hunger. DiCaprio plays him ardent and visibly straining, which captures the collapse but weakens the illusion. Ladd plays him as a professional criminal, keeping the bootlegging in view where the romantic versions bury it.

## Why Daisy Is the Hardest Role in the Novel

Every version has more trouble with Daisy than with Gatsby, and the difficulty is structural rather than a failure of casting.

The problem starts with the fact that her central attribute cannot be filmed. Fitzgerald locates her power in her voice and then declines to reproduce what it sounds like. He gives metaphors instead: an arrangement of notes that will never be played again, a singing compulsion, a whispered promise. The most famous verdict, that her voice is full of money, is Gatsby's own and Nick immediately endorses it. A film has to supply an actual voice, and any actual voice is a decision that closes off the possibility Fitzgerald keeps open. The novel's Daisy is partly a hypothesis the reader constructs; the film's Daisy is a woman with a specific timbre.

The second difficulty is that Daisy is presented almost entirely through the perceptions of men who want something from her. Nick is her cousin and is charmed. Gatsby has organized his life around her. Tom owns her socially. The reader never gets an unmediated view. When she says she hopes her daughter will be a fool, that a beautiful little fool is the best thing a girl can be in this world, it is the closest the novel comes to letting her state a position, and even that arrives secondhand in a performance Nick suspects of insincerity. A film must show her doing things in a way the novel refuses to.

The third difficulty is moral. If Daisy is a victim, the reader can love the novel as a tragedy of a man destroyed by a system. If Daisy is complicit, the novel becomes something harder: a story in which the woman at the centre makes a choice, lets another woman die for it, and retreats behind her money. Fitzgerald wrote both possibilities and never resolved them. Casting has to choose, because an actor must play a person with intentions.

Mia Farrow's Daisy is fragile, girlish, and slightly unmoored, which makes the tragedy Gatsby's alone and makes her closer to an object than an agent. Carey Mulligan's is wounded and self-aware, a woman who understands her own trap and is not strong enough to leave it, which distributes the moral weight more evenly and produces a sadder, more difficult film. Mira Sorvino plays her warmer and more adult, with less of the little-girl register that both the more famous versions use in different measures. None of them is wrong. Each is a defensible reading of a character the novel deliberately underdetermines.

### Why is Daisy so difficult to cast?

Because her defining attribute is a voice Fitzgerald describes only in metaphor, and any real voice forecloses what the prose leaves open. She is also filtered entirely through men who want something from her, and the novel never settles whether she is a victim or a participant. An actor must choose; the book does not.

## Nick, and the Casting Nobody Argues About

Nick Carraway is the most consequential casting in any Gatsby film and the one least discussed, because he is the least glamorous role and because audiences forget that the entire novel is his account.

The whole book is a retrospective narrative delivered by a man who tells you in his first page that he is inclined to reserve all judgements, and then judges continuously. He calls Gatsby a man who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn and then exempts him from the scorn. He describes himself as one of the few honest people that I have ever known while giving an account that omits, softens, and rearranges. That gap between what Nick claims and what Nick does is the novel's engine.

A film cannot easily reproduce it. Voiceover puts Nick's words in the audience's ear but strips the irony, because a voice speaking directly to you sounds sincere by default. The casting therefore has to carry the unreliability physically, through a performance that shows a man managing his own story.

Sam Waterston's Nick is watchful and decent, which produces a trustworthy narrator and therefore a less interesting novel. Tobey Maguire's is framed by a device that has him writing the account from a sanatorium, which at least acknowledges that the narration is a construction, though it locates the unreliability in illness rather than in character. Paul Rudd's, in the 2000 version, is the most quietly successful, playing Nick as a man with a slightly evasive relationship to his own opinions.

The honest conclusion is that no screen version has solved Nick, and this is the deepest reason the novel gives filmmakers trouble. The difficulty is examined at length in [the article on why The Great Gatsby resists adaptation](/2029/12/10/why-gatsby-resists-adaptation/), and [the comparison of the novel against its film adaptations](/2028/11/27/gatsby-novel-vs-films/) tests every version on the same criteria.
## Tom, Jordan, Myrtle, and the Wilsons

The supporting casts reveal each version's reading as clearly as the leads do, and sometimes more honestly, because there is less star pressure distorting the choice.

Tom is the character the novel describes most physically and the one films most often soften. Fitzgerald establishes him as a body before he is a personality: the enormous power under the riding clothes, the eyes that had established dominance over his face, the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward, and eventually the flat verdict about a cruel body. Bruce Dern plays him as brittle and petulant, a man whose menace is social rather than physical, which fits a reading in which Tom's power comes entirely from money. Joel Edgerton plays him closer to the page, as a large man who could hurt someone and occasionally does, which restores the physical threat the novel insists on. The difference matters because Tom breaks Myrtle's nose with an open hand, and a Tom who cannot plausibly do that changes the moral geometry of the book.

Jordan is consistently the best-served character in these films, probably because her defining qualities are visual and behavioural rather than interior. Fitzgerald gives her posture, self-sufficiency, a wan, charming, discontented face, and grey sun-strained eyes, and he tells us plainly that she was incurably dishonest and that she instinctively avoided clever, shrewd men. That is a role an actor can build. Elizabeth Debicki's height and detachment in the 2013 version make the self-sufficiency architectural, and Lois Chiles supplies the athletic coolness in 1974. Neither version has much trouble with her.

Myrtle is harder, because she is the one character the novel treats with something close to contempt and something close to pity in the same paragraph. She has an immediately perceptible vitality about her and no facet or gleam of beauty, and she is faintly stout and carries her flesh sensuously. Casting has to deliver appetite without caricature. Shelley Winters in 1949 and Karen Black in 1974 both find the vitality; Isla Fisher in 2013 pushes further toward the desperation. All three read her as a woman reaching for a life she can see and cannot enter, which is correct.

George Wilson is the character whose casting most often goes wrong through neglect. Fitzgerald writes him as a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome, and that last clause matters enormously. Wilson is not ugly. He is drained. He was once a man Myrtle chose, and the marriage's failure is not that he is repulsive but that he has been emptied out by the garage and the ash. A film that casts him as merely pathetic loses the reason Myrtle's disappointment is a tragedy rather than a mistake. Scott Wilson in 1974 and Jason Clarke in 2013 both find the faintly handsome part, which is why the shooting lands in those versions.

### How have the films cast Tom Buchanan?

Fitzgerald writes Tom as physical threat: enormous power under the riding clothes, arrogant eyes that dominate his face, and finally a cruel body. Bruce Dern plays him brittle and socially menacing, which locates his power in money. Joel Edgerton plays him closer to the page, restoring the bodily danger the novel requires.

## The Wolfshiem Problem

Meyer Wolfshiem is the novel's most uncomfortable creation and the casting history around him cannot be discussed honestly without saying so.

Fitzgerald introduces him with a description that is straightforwardly antisemitic caricature, calling him a small, flat-nosed Jew and lingering on the nose, the nostril hair, and the tiny eyes, and he later has Wolfshiem speak in a comic dialect and wear human molars as cufflinks. The character is also a criminal fixer whose function in the plot is to be the source of Gatsby's money and the man who will not come to the funeral. The caricature and the plot function are welded together, and every adaptation has to decide what to do about that weld.

The available strategies have all been tried. The 1974 film cast Howard Da Silva, who had played George Wilson in the 1949 version, and played the role broadly. The 2013 film cast Amitabh Bachchan, a decision that changes the character's ethnic coding entirely and thereby detaches the criminality from the caricature. Whatever one thinks of the result, the choice is legible as a response to a genuine problem in the source rather than as indifference to it.

There is no neutral option here. Casting the role as written reproduces a slur. Casting against it removes something Fitzgerald put in the book and which a reader has to reckon with. Cutting the character removes the mechanism that connects Gatsby's fortune to organized crime, which is structurally load-bearing. The adaptations' collective struggle with Wolfshiem is a useful reminder that fidelity is not automatically a virtue, and that some things in a canonical novel are worth being unfaithful to.

## The Patterns Across a Century

Read chronologically, the casting history tracks American attitudes toward wealth with some precision.

The 1949 version, made in a decade preoccupied with crime and postwar moral accounting, casts a crime star and treats Gatsby's money as the story's centre. The 1974 version, made during a period of nostalgia for a mythologized American past, casts the era's most iconic embodiment of golden handsomeness and treats Gatsby as an emblem rather than a man. The 2013 version, made after a financial collapse, casts an actor associated with charismatic self-destruction and treats the whole world as a bubble waiting to burst.

That progression is not a coincidence and it supports a general claim about this novel: it is elastic enough to absorb whatever a decade is anxious about, and the casting is where the absorption becomes visible. A film's Gatsby tells you what its moment thought about ambition, and a film's Daisy tells you what its moment thought about women who marry money.

A second pattern concerns age. Fitzgerald's characters are startlingly young. Gatsby is a year or two over thirty. Tom is thirty. Nick turns thirty during the novel and treats it as a catastrophe, noting the promise of a decade of loneliness. Daisy is in her early twenties when Gatsby meets her and is roughly twenty-three during the summer of 1922. Adaptations frequently cast older, and the effect is to make the story feel like a mature reckoning rather than what it is, which is a disaster produced by people who are not yet done being young. A cast in its early thirties makes Gatsby's five-year fixation look like arrested development. A cast in its forties makes it look like fate.

### Does the age of the actors matter?

Considerably. Gatsby is a year or two over thirty, Tom is thirty, and Nick turns thirty during the novel and dreads it. Casting older converts a catastrophe caused by young people who cannot stop wanting things into a mature reckoning, which softens the recklessness the book depends on.
## Casting From the Page: What the Text Actually Requires

A useful exercise for a reader is to derive the casting brief from the novel itself, ignoring every film, and then see which versions met it. Fitzgerald supplies enough specification to do this, and the exercise clarifies what is negotiable and what is not.

For Gatsby the brief has four requirements. He must be roughly thirty-two and must read as young, because the novel's pathos depends on a man who is still early enough in life to believe a mistake can be undone. He must be able to project ease and let the audience glimpse the effort behind it in the same shot, because the roughneck compound is unresolved and a performance that resolves it in either direction loses half the character. He must have a smile that operates rather than merely appears, since Fitzgerald analyses the smile as a device that faces the whole eternal world for an instant, concentrates on you with an irresistible prejudice in your favour, and then vanishes precisely at that point. And he must be able to go quiet, because the character's most exposed moments are silences: standing alone on his lawn with his arms stretched out toward the dark water, or waiting in the rain outside Nick's cottage.

For Daisy the brief is mostly vocal and mostly impossible. The voice must do the work Fitzgerald assigns it, which is to make listeners lean in and to sound like money without sounding vulgar. Everything else in the description, the sad and lovely face with bright things in it, the bright eyes and the bright passionate mouth, is subordinate to the sound. The second requirement is harder: the performance must sustain a reading in which the audience cannot tell whether Daisy is helpless or calculating, and must sustain it through the Plaza scene, where she is asked to disown five years of her marriage and cannot, and through the aftermath of the accident, where she says nothing and disappears.

For Nick the brief is the one no production has met. He must sound honest while behaving evasively. He announces on the first page that he is inclined to reserve all judgements, describes himself as one of the few honest people that I have ever known, and then delivers two hundred pages of judgement and omission. The role requires an actor who can make an audience trust him and simultaneously plant the suspicion that the trust is misplaced, and it requires the film to build a structure that supports the doubt rather than flattening it with voiceover.

For Tom the brief is unambiguous and mostly physical. He must be large, must lean forward, and must be capable of the violence he commits. Fitzgerald spends most of Tom's introduction on the body precisely so that the reader never mistakes his power for wit.

For Jordan the brief is posture and containment, and for Myrtle it is appetite without caricature. For George Wilson it is the hardest and most easily missed note, which is a man who was once attractive and has been emptied out, since faintly handsome is not a throwaway phrase but the reason his marriage is a loss rather than a joke.

### What does the novel require from a Gatsby?

Four things: an actor around thirty-two who reads young, one who can project ease while letting the effort show in the same shot, one whose smile operates as a device rather than a mood, and one who can hold silence, since the character's most exposed moments are wordless.

## The Roles the Films Keep Cutting

An underexamined part of the casting record is who does not get cast, because the omissions are as interpretively loaded as the choices.

Owl Eyes is the first casualty in most versions, and losing him costs the novel one of its sharpest jokes and one of its few moments of grace. He appears in the library during the party, drunk and astonished, having discovered that the books are real rather than cardboard, and he treats this as evidence of thoroughness in a way that is both admiring and damning. He then reappears at the funeral, one of the only guests to attend, and delivers the novel's bleakest verdict on the crowd that consumed Gatsby's hospitality. Cutting him removes the book's most economical statement of its own theme, which is that the construction was real and the audience was not.

Henry Gatz, Gatsby's father, is cut or minimized nearly as often, and the loss is comparable. He arrives after the death carrying a photograph of the house and a boyhood schedule his son wrote in the back of a book, and the schedule is the clearest window the novel provides into the machinery of self-invention. Without him, Gatsby's transformation has no visible origin, which makes the character more mythic and less human.

Klipspringer, the boarder who lives at the mansion and plays the piano and then telephones after the funeral about a pair of tennis shoes, is a comic figure whose function is to demonstrate exactly how much the guests took and how little they returned. Adaptations that cut him lose the specificity of the ingratitude and are forced to state it instead.

The pattern in these omissions is consistent. What gets cut is the material that makes the novel's judgement precise rather than atmospheric. A film can convey that Gatsby was used by people who did not care about him through mood and empty rooms. It takes Owl Eyes, Henry Gatz, and Klipspringer to convey exactly how, and casting them costs money and screen time that adaptations usually prefer to spend on parties. The result is that the versions are consistently better at the spectacle of the novel than at its accounting, which is the same imbalance the party sequences produce, and the reason so many readers come away from the films with the surfaces and not the argument.

## The Screen Test Nobody Passes

There is one scene that functions as the real audition for any Gatsby cast, and it is not the reunion or the party. It is the confrontation in the suite at the Plaza in chapter seven, because that scene requires all four principals to be doing something different at once and gives none of them a speech to hide behind.

Tom has to be right and repellent simultaneously. His research into Gatsby's business is accurate, his motive is possessive rather than moral, and the scene collapses if an audience is allowed to enjoy his victory or to dismiss his evidence. Gatsby has to lose control in a way that confirms every suspicion Tom has raised while remaining the person the audience has invested in. Daisy has to be visibly unable to say what Gatsby needs her to say, and the performance has to leave open whether the inability is weakness or unwillingness. Nick has to be present, silent, and complicit, watching a marriage and a fantasy destroy each other while doing nothing, and the scene is also where he registers that he has turned thirty.

Fitzgerald stages all of this in a hot room with the portentous chords of Mendelssohn's wedding march drifting up from a ballroom below, which is the novel's most efficient piece of irony: a wedding underway downstairs while a marriage is dismantled overhead. No adaptation has fully realized the scene, and the failures are diagnostic. Where the Tom is too petty, the scene becomes a bully cornering a romantic and the audience's sympathies never move. Where the Gatsby is too composed, his unravelling has to arrive from nowhere. Where the Daisy is too passive, the ambiguity collapses and she becomes furniture in an argument between men.

Judged by this scene rather than by star wattage, the ranking of the casts changes. The versions that give Tom real weight and Daisy real interiority do better here than the versions with the more famous leads, which is a useful corrective to the habit of evaluating adaptations by their Gatsby alone. The character everyone remembers is not the character the material tests hardest.
## What Casting Reveals About the Novel

The casting record is worth studying because it functions as a hundred-year experiment in which the same text is repeatedly forced into concrete decisions, and the places where the decisions diverge mark the places where Fitzgerald deliberately withheld an answer.

Three such places emerge from the comparison.

The first is Gatsby's sincerity. The novel never tells you whether the charm is genuine. It tells you the smile understood you just so far as you wanted to be understood, which is a sentence describing an effect without adjudicating a cause. Redford's coolness and DiCaprio's ardor are two resolutions of an ambiguity the prose maintains, and the fact that both are defensible proves the ambiguity is real rather than a gap.

The second is Daisy's agency. Every version has to decide whether she knows what she is doing, and no two versions decide the same way. This is the strongest available evidence that Fitzgerald built her as an open question, and it is why arguments about whether Daisy is a villain never terminate. Her most revealing line, the wish that her daughter be a beautiful little fool, can be played as bitter clarity or as performed helplessness, and the novel supplies just enough of Nick's suspicion to license either.

The third is the class of Gatsby's charm. Is he a man who has learned gentility well, or a man whose gentility is visibly a costume? The roughneck compound refuses to say. What the casting history shows is that the answer determines the whole film, because a Gatsby whose performance is transparent produces a story about a fool, and a Gatsby whose performance is convincing produces a story about a society that cannot tell the difference between manners and worth.

There is a fourth revelation, more diffuse but more important. Comparing casts shows how much of this novel exists in Nick's sentences rather than in its events. Strip the prose away and what remains is a fairly ordinary melodrama: a rich woman, a poor man who becomes rich, an affair, a hit and run, a shooting. Every adaptation discovers this and every adaptation compensates with production design, music, or framing devices. The casting cannot fix it, because the missing element is not a person but a voice.

## Writing About Casting in an Essay

Casting is legitimate essay material and it is also one of the easiest ways to write a weak paragraph, so the distinction between the two uses is worth setting out.

The weak use is the review. Reporting that Redford was handsome but distant and that DiCaprio was more emotional, and then declaring one better, produces an opinion about films rather than an argument about a novel. Examiners recognize it immediately as a way of filling space with material that does not require reading the book.

The strong use runs in the opposite direction: the casting becomes evidence for a claim about the text. Begin with a genuine interpretive question, such as whether Gatsby's charm is sincere. Establish the textual basis for the question, which here is the smile passage and its clause about understanding you just so far as you wanted to be understood. Then show that two adaptations resolved the question differently, and argue that the divergence demonstrates the ambiguity is authorial rather than accidental. The film material is doing analytical work in that structure instead of decorating the paragraph.

A second strong use is the counterfactual. Ask what a casting choice costs. If Tom is played as merely petulant, the broken nose becomes inexplicable, so the physical menace Fitzgerald writes into his shoulders is load-bearing rather than decorative. If Wilson is played as pathetic rather than faintly handsome, Myrtle's disappointment becomes a mistake rather than a tragedy. Reasoning from what a change breaks is one of the most reliable ways to demonstrate that a textual detail matters.

The one rule that governs both uses is that the paragraph must end in the novel. A sentence about a performance that does not lead to a sentence about Fitzgerald's prose has spent its space on trivia. ## Verdict

Casting is the most underrated interpretive instrument in the adaptation history of this novel, and reading the versions against one another produces a clearer picture of Fitzgerald's design than any single film does.

The strongest single conclusion is that the screen versions disagree in an orderly way. They divide on whether Gatsby is cool or ardent, on whether his criminality is his centre or his background, and on whether Daisy chooses or is chosen. Those are the three questions the novel leaves genuinely open, which is why a century of professional interpreters with money at stake have failed to converge on answers. The disagreement is not evidence that some casts got it wrong. It is evidence that the book is built to sustain more than one reading of its central figures.

If a single verdict is required on the casts themselves, it is this: the versions have collectively solved Jordan, mostly solved Myrtle and Tom, divided productively on Gatsby and Daisy, and entirely failed on Nick. The failure on Nick is the important one, because he is the novel. Everything else is a story he is telling, and no casting director can hire a man who tells you he reserves judgement in a tone that lets you hear him judging.

The practical takeaway for a reader is to treat every cast as a hypothesis and to check it against the descriptions Fitzgerald wrote. He specified more than most novelists do, and he specified it because the surfaces in this book are the argument. A face that reads as trustworthy, a voice that sounds like money, a body that could break something: these are not costume notes. They are the terms in which the novel makes its case about class, and a casting choice that overrides one of them is disagreeing with Fitzgerald, which is allowed, and worth noticing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did each film cast the central roles?

Five screen versions have made the attempt. The lost 1926 silent film cast Warner Baxter and Lois Wilson as Gatsby and Daisy, with Neil Hamilton as Nick. The 1949 version cast Alan Ladd and Betty Field, with Macdonald Carey as Nick, Barry Sullivan as Tom, and Howard Da Silva as George Wilson. The 1974 version cast Robert Redford and Mia Farrow, with Sam Waterston as Nick, Bruce Dern as Tom, Lois Chiles as Jordan, and Karen Black as Myrtle. The 2000 television version cast Toby Stephens and Mira Sorvino, with Paul Rudd as Nick. The 2013 version cast Leonardo DiCaprio and Carey Mulligan, with Tobey Maguire as Nick, Joel Edgerton as Tom, Elizabeth Debicki as Jordan, and Isla Fisher as Myrtle.

### Q: How do the films' Gatsbys differ?

They divide on temperature and on criminality. Robert Redford plays him cool, composed, and slightly remote, which honors how successfully the character has built his surface but struggles to convey the hunger underneath. Leonardo DiCaprio plays him ardent, with the strain visible in the accent and the anxiety in the reunion, which makes the collapse legible but weakens the illusion the other characters are supposed to fall for. Alan Ladd, cast out of hard-boiled crime pictures, plays him as a professional criminal whose poise is occupational discipline rather than aspiration, keeping the bootlegging in view. Toby Stephens plays him as a theatrical performer of gentility, which literalizes Fitzgerald's compound description of an elegant young roughneck.

### Q: Why is Daisy difficult to cast?

Because her defining attribute is unfilmable. Fitzgerald locates her power in her low, thrilling voice, describes it only through metaphor as an arrangement of notes that will never be played again, and endorses Gatsby's verdict that her voice is full of money. Any real voice forecloses what the prose deliberately leaves open. She is also presented entirely through men who want something from her, so the reader never gets an unmediated view of her intentions. And the novel refuses to settle whether she is a victim of her position or a participant in it, which an actor cannot replicate, since a performance requires a person with motives. Mia Farrow plays fragility, Carey Mulligan plays wounded self-awareness, and both are defensible.

### Q: What does each casting reveal about the era's reading?

The chronology tracks American anxieties about money with some precision. The 1949 version, made in a decade preoccupied with crime and postwar moral accounting, cast a crime star and treated Gatsby's racket as the story's centre. The 1974 version, made during a wave of nostalgia for a mythologized American past, cast the period's most iconic embodiment of golden handsomeness and treated Gatsby as an emblem rather than a man. The 2013 version, made after a financial collapse, cast an actor associated with charismatic self-destruction and treated the whole world as a bubble. The novel is elastic enough to absorb whatever a decade fears, and casting is where the absorption becomes visible.

### Q: Is casting incidental?

No. It is the first interpretive act any adaptation performs, and it happens before dialogue. A face carries assumptions about age, class, hardness, and agency, and audiences absorb those assumptions immediately and revise them slowly. Fitzgerald understood this, which is why his physical descriptions are unusually specific and analytical: Tom's arrogance is written into his shoulders, Gatsby's charm is described as a mechanism with a timer, Wilson is not ugly but drained. A casting choice either honors those specifications or advances a counter-reading, and either way it commits the film to a position on questions the prose leaves open. Treating casting as incidental means missing the largest interpretive decision in the production.

### Q: How does casting argue an interpretation before a line is spoken?

Through the assumptions a body imports. When Alan Ladd walks on, an audience trained on crime pictures already believes this man has done something illegal, so the film does not need to argue for Gatsby's criminality and can spend its running time on the romance instead. When Robert Redford walks on, an audience already believes this man belongs at the top of the social order, so the film's central irony, that he does not belong there, has to be argued against the face. Casting therefore sets the audience's default and determines what the film must spend effort overturning. It is closer to a thesis statement than to a production detail.

### Q: Who played Gatsby in the 1926 silent film?

Warner Baxter played Gatsby in the 1926 adaptation directed by Herbert Brenon, opposite Lois Wilson as Daisy and Neil Hamilton as Nick. The film appeared just a year after the novel's publication, which makes it the only screen version made while Fitzgerald was alive and while the world it depicts was still current rather than historical. It is now a lost film. No complete print is known to survive, and what remains is a trailer, so the earliest cinematic interpretation of these characters exists only as a cast list and a fragment. That loss is itself part of the novel's reception history, and it means the first casting decisions in this line are permanently beyond evaluation.

### Q: Who played Gatsby in the 1949 version?

Alan Ladd, in the version directed by Elliott Nugent, with Betty Field as Daisy, Macdonald Carey as Nick, Barry Sullivan as Tom, Ruth Hussey as Jordan, Shelley Winters as Myrtle, and Howard Da Silva as George Wilson. Ladd's established persona came from hard-boiled crime pictures, and the film uses that association as its interpretive foundation, presenting Gatsby as a criminal with a romantic vulnerability rather than a romantic with a criminal past. It is the most consequential casting decision in this entire history, because it forces the audience to hold the bootlegging in view for the whole picture, where the more famous versions let the money become atmospheric.

### Q: Was Robert Redford right for the role?

He was right for one reading and wrong for another, and the debate has never resolved because both readings are in the book. Redford's ease and beauty capture how successfully Gatsby has constructed his surface, and they handle the class question elegantly, since ease is exactly what old money possesses and new money is trying to purchase. What the performance struggles with is the hunger. Fitzgerald's Gatsby cannot hold still: he stretches his arms toward the dark water, he knocks a defunct mantelpiece clock with his head at the reunion and catches it with trembling fingers. A performance built on composure has to find room for a man visibly coming apart, and the 1974 film's reputation for being beautiful and inert is partly the consequence of not finding it.

### Q: Was Leonardo DiCaprio a good Gatsby?

He solved the problem Redford left open and created the mirror-image one. DiCaprio makes the effort visible: the strain in the acquired accent, the near-panic in the reunion scene, the eruption at the Plaza when Tom presses him. That reading captures the colossal vitality of the illusion and makes the collapse legible, because the audience has been watching the seams. The cost is that a Gatsby whose performance is transparent to the viewer becomes hard to reconcile with a room full of sophisticated people who cannot see through him. The novel needs both, since the charm must work on the characters and fail for the reader, and no single performance has yet held both at once.

### Q: Who has played Nick Carraway on screen?

Neil Hamilton in 1926, Macdonald Carey in 1949, Sam Waterston in 1974, Paul Rudd in the 2000 television version, and Tobey Maguire in 2013. Waterston plays him watchful and decent, which produces a trustworthy narrator and therefore a less interesting book. Maguire is framed by a device that has him writing the account from a sanatorium, which at least acknowledges the narration is a construction, though it locates the unreliability in illness rather than character. Rudd is the quietly most successful, playing a man with an evasive relationship to his own opinions. No version has solved him, because the gap between Nick claiming to reserve all judgements and Nick judging continuously lives in prose rather than in behaviour.

### Q: Who has played Tom Buchanan on screen?

Hale Hamilton in 1926, Barry Sullivan in 1949, Bruce Dern in 1974, Martin Donovan in 2000, and Joel Edgerton in 2013. The split runs between social menace and physical menace. Dern plays Tom as brittle and petulant, a man whose power is entirely a function of money, which is a coherent reading but a partial one. Edgerton plays closer to the page, as a large man who could hurt someone, which restores what Fitzgerald insists on: the enormous power of that body under the riding clothes, the arrogant eyes that had established dominance over his face, and eventually the flat phrase about a cruel body. The distinction matters because Tom breaks Myrtle's nose with his open hand.

### Q: Which actress came closest to the Daisy of the book?

The question has no settled answer because the book's Daisy is underdetermined by design, but the case for Carey Mulligan is the strongest on textual grounds. Fitzgerald gives Daisy one moment of apparent self-knowledge, the wish that her daughter grow up a beautiful little fool, described as the best thing a girl can be in this world, and he immediately has Nick suspect the sincerity of the delivery. A performance that shows a woman who understands her own trap and is not strong enough to leave it accommodates both halves of that moment. Mia Farrow's fragility resolves the ambiguity toward victimhood and makes the tragedy Gatsby's alone, which is a cleaner film and a smaller novel.

### Q: How have the films cast Jordan Baker?

Consistently well, and the reason is that Jordan's defining qualities are external. Fitzgerald gives her posture and self-possession rather than interiority: she is extended full length at her end of the divan, she has a wan, charming, discontented face and grey sun-strained eyes, she instinctively avoided clever, shrewd men, and Nick concludes flatly that she was incurably dishonest. Those are all playable. Lois Chiles supplies the athletic coolness in 1974 and Elizabeth Debicki's height and detachment in 2013 make the self-sufficiency almost architectural. Jordan is the one major character on whom the adaptations essentially agree, which suggests the novel is unambiguous about her in a way it is not about the others.

### Q: How have the films cast Myrtle and George Wilson?

Myrtle has generally been well served and George frequently neglected. Fitzgerald gives Myrtle an immediately perceptible vitality and no facet or gleam of beauty, and notes that she carries her flesh sensuously as some women can, so the casting has to deliver appetite without caricature. Shelley Winters, Karen Black, and Isla Fisher all find the vitality, with Fisher pushing hardest toward desperation. George is the more common failure. Fitzgerald calls him a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome, and that last clause is essential. He is not repulsive, he is drained, and Myrtle once chose him. A Wilson played as merely pathetic turns her disappointment into a mistake rather than a tragedy.

### Q: Why was Wolfshiem's casting controversial?

Because Fitzgerald wrote the character as antisemitic caricature and welded that caricature to a load-bearing plot function. The introduction calls him a small, flat-nosed Jew and dwells on the nose, the nostril hair, and the tiny eyes; he speaks in comic dialect and wears human molars as cufflinks; and he is simultaneously the source of Gatsby's money and the man who will not attend the funeral. Every adaptation must choose among reproducing the caricature, casting against it, or cutting the character and losing the link between Gatsby's fortune and organized crime. The 2013 version cast Amitabh Bachchan, which detaches the criminality from the ethnic coding. There is no neutral option, and the difficulty is in the source.

### Q: Does the age of the actors matter for these roles?

Considerably, and adaptations routinely get it wrong by casting older. Fitzgerald's characters are young. Gatsby is described as an elegant young roughneck, a year or two over thirty. Tom is a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty. Nick turns thirty during the novel and treats it as a disaster, seeing before him the portentous, menacing road of a new decade and a decade of loneliness with a thinning list of single men to know. Daisy is around twenty-three. When the cast ages up, a catastrophe produced by people who have not finished being young turns into a mature reckoning, and Gatsby's five-year fixation stops looking like arrested development and starts looking like destiny.

### Q: Which casting choices changed the story most?

Three stand out. Alan Ladd's crime-picture persona in 1949 relocated the story's centre from romance to racket, making the film a study of a criminal's sentimental weakness. Robert Redford's iconic handsomeness in 1974 made Gatsby appear to belong to the class he is excluded from, which forced the film to argue its central irony against its own leading man. Amitabh Bachchan's casting as Wolfshiem in 2013 severed the character's criminality from Fitzgerald's antisemitic caricature, changing what the audience is asked to accept. Each of these decisions altered the film's argument rather than its texture, which is the test for whether a casting choice is interpretively significant or merely a matter of taste.

### Q: How do I use casting in a comparative essay?

Make the casting evidence for a claim about the text rather than the subject of the paragraph. Start with a real interpretive question, such as whether Gatsby's charm is sincere, and establish its textual basis, which here is the smile that understood you just so far as you wanted to be understood. Then show that two adaptations resolved the question in opposite directions and argue that the divergence proves the ambiguity is authorial rather than accidental. A second reliable structure is the counterfactual: ask what a casting choice breaks. A merely petulant Tom makes the broken nose inexplicable, which demonstrates that the physical menace is load-bearing. Every such paragraph must close on Fitzgerald's prose, not on the performance.
