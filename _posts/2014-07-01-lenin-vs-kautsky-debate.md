Karl Kautsky was, for roughly a quarter of a century, the most authoritative living interpreter of Marxism, and Vladimir Lenin was among his most devoted readers. That single fact rearranges the whole quarrel this article examines. The two men are usually introduced as lifelong antagonists, a revolutionary and a renegade facing each other across a fixed divide, and the familiar portraits leave the impression that the enmity of their final decade merely expressed a difference that had always been there. The documentary record points the other way. Lenin learned his Marxism substantially from the older man's books, cited those books as the standard of orthodoxy in dispute after dispute, defended their author against rivals inside the movement, and treated an endorsement from Stuttgart as a prize worth having. The rupture, when it arrived, arrived suddenly, over conduct in a crisis rather than over doctrine patiently diverging, and its bitterness cannot be understood apart from the intimacy it destroyed.

![Lenin vs Kautsky, teacher and pupil divided over the dictatorship of the proletariat and democracy - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

What followed the rupture was the most consequential theoretical exchange inside Marxism in the twentieth century. In two pamphlets written within months of each other, and in a longer sequel from the older man, the former teacher and the former pupil argued out the questions that every subsequent revolutionary movement has had to face in some form: whether a class can rule through democratic institutions or only through a party acting in its name, whether a socialist minority may govern a population that has not chosen socialism, whether parliaments are a road to power or a waiting room that neutralises those who enter it, and what a government that suppresses political pluralism will predictably become. Because both writers argued from inside the same tradition, quoting the same canonical texts and claiming the same inheritance, the exchange has a clarity that arguments across traditions rarely achieve. Each man knew exactly which premises he shared with his opponent, and each aimed his fire at the precise point where their roads divided.

This article grades that exchange by a method its subject matter demands. Rather than crowning a winner of the whole quarrel, it separates the questions the two men were actually arguing about and assesses each side on each question in turn: the character of the Russian revolution, the meaning of democracy under proletarian rule, the fate of the parliamentary road, and what each predicted about the road the other proposed. The result is named early so that neither camp can quote what follows selectively. The split verdict holds that Kautsky's prediction about the consequences of one party rule was substantially borne out by the record, while his own strategy for reaching socialism was vindicated nowhere, and that the exchange therefore establishes what will not work without establishing what will. Grading a famous quarrel question by question is slower work than awarding it to a side, but it is the only grading the evidence supports.

A note on sources and discipline belongs at the front. Both men wrote as partisans in a live struggle, and both wrote about a regime that held state power, destroyed opponents, and was defended and indicted in the same pamphlets that contain the theory. The account below keeps the theory and the state record in separate accounts, as the fairness of the grading requires. Where the record of the Soviet regime enters, it enters as documented history attributed to the historians who established it, neither softened into apologetics nor sharpened into a cudgel. Kautsky's case against the Bolsheviks is presented at the strength he gave it, because it was argued from inside Marxism and deserves to be met there. Lenin's case against Kautsky is presented at its strength too, including the uncomfortable fact that the older man's own road, followed for decades by the largest socialist party in the world, ended in votes for war credits and in failure at every decisive moment. The reader who finishes this article should be able to state both cases, name the point where each was strongest, and explain why a split verdict is a finding rather than a compromise.

## Kautsky the teacher and the making of an orthodox pupil

To grasp what was lost in the rupture, begin with the standing the older man held in the movement before it. Kautsky edited the theoretical journal of German social democracy for decades, advised its leadership, drafted the programme commentary that taught a generation what the party's own founding document meant, and wrote the popular expositions through which workers and students across Europe first encountered Marx's economics in systematic form. Contemporaries called him the pope of Marxism, and the nickname, half admiring and half ironic, captured something real: disputes about what Marxism required were carried to his desk the way questions of doctrine are carried to an authority whose rulings settle them. He was not an original thinker on Marx's scale, and he did not claim to be. His gift was systematisation. He took a body of work that was vast, unfinished, and scattered across journalism, manuscripts, and correspondence, and he rendered it as a coherent doctrine that could be taught, defended, and applied to the questions of the day.

The young Lenin encountered that system as a student encounters a master. Exiled, imprisoned, and then exiled again, he read the German literature voraciously, and the name that recurs through his early polemics as the standard against which deviation is measured is the older man's. When Russian socialists quarrelled over whether consciousness could arise spontaneously from the workers' movement or had to be brought to it from outside, the authority Lenin invoked for the second position was Kautsky, whose formulation he quoted at length and treated as decisive. The irony has often been noticed: the sentence most frequently credited to Lenin as the founding claim of Leninism about the limits of spontaneous working class consciousness was one he borrowed, proudly and with attribution, from the man he would later brand a renegade. Borrowing of that kind was not incidental to the relationship. It was the relationship. The pupil's orthodoxy was the teacher's doctrine, learned thoroughly and applied with a rigour the teacher sometimes found alarming.

Their disagreements before the war were real but minor, and they ran in the direction of the pupil pressing the teacher to be more himself. Lenin worried periodically that the German party was growing comfortable, that its parliamentary fraction was acquiring interests of its own, that its theorist was too patient with compromisers. Yet these were the complaints of a disciple who feared the master was softening, not the complaints of a rival building an alternative doctrine. On the great contested questions of the prewar movement, the two stood together. Both rejected the revisionism that proposed to dissolve the revolutionary goal into a sequence of reforms. Both defended the party programme against attempts to update it into respectability. Both treated Bernstein's challenge as the danger to be defeated, and the defeat, when it was administered in party congresses and in print, was administered substantially with arguments Kautsky had forged. A reader who knows only the pamphlets of their enmity will misread the entire prewar period by projecting that enmity backwards onto years in which it did not exist.

### What did Lenin and Kautsky disagree about?

At the start, almost nothing of substance. For years the younger man treated the older as the standard of Marxist orthodoxy and quoted him as settling authority. They divided first over socialist conduct when war came, and afterwards over whether proletarian rule means a class governing through democratic forms or a party wielding state power in the class's name.

The depth of the pupil's admiration is worth a paragraph of evidence, because later legend on both sides has reason to obscure it. Lenin's private correspondence shows him following the German theoretical journal issue by issue, recommending its articles to comrades, and measuring Russian disputes against its judgements. He translated and circulated the teacher's work. He sought the older man's opinion on Russian controversies and was gratified when it matched his own. When the two finally met and talked at length, the encounter was recorded by the pupil in terms of respect verging on deference. None of this makes the pupil insincere in his later fury; it explains its temperature. Apostasy, in the eyes of a believer, is a worse offence than unbelief, and the word renegade only makes sense as an accusation against someone who once held the true doctrine and abandoned it. The pamphlet that made the word famous is incomprehensible unless the reader first understands that its author had spent twenty years treating its target as the doctrine's guardian.

The teacher's side of the relationship had its own asymmetry. Kautsky followed Russian affairs with interest, wrote about the Russian revolution of the middle of the prewar decade with sympathy for its most militant wing, and regarded the young Russian as a promising representative of a movement he took seriously. But Russia, for the German theorist, was a province of the movement, instructive and sometimes inspiring, not its centre. The centre was Germany, where the largest, best organised, most electorally successful socialist party in the world was accumulating the strength that his strategy required. That difference in vantage point mattered later. The pupil came from a country where the movement was illegal, hunted, and repeatedly driven underground, and where the state offered no parliamentary road worth the name. The teacher's whole strategic outlook was formed inside a movement that could publish newspapers, hold congresses, win millions of votes, and still be excluded from government. Each man's theory of the road to power carried the imprint of the road he had actually walked.

### How did each man's circumstances shape his theory of power?

Circumstance taught each man his strategy before doctrine justified it. The pupil learned politics under a police state, where legality was a favour the regime could withdraw, and concluded that power is taken in moments of rupture. The teacher learned it inside a tolerated mass movement and concluded that power is accumulated until refusal becomes impossible.

The pupil's formative experiences were conspiracy, exile, and a state that answered organisation with the police. From those experiences grew a lasting conviction that the decisive moments of politics are moments of rupture, that institutions built by a hostile state will be used against the movement at the crisis, and that a party which cannot act illegally, decisively, and against the law when the law is the enemy's weapon will be destroyed by one that can. The teacher's formative experience was the opposite curriculum. He had watched a party survive a dozen years of anti-socialist legislation by organisation and legality, emerge stronger, and then grow for two more decades inside a constitutional order that was hostile but navigable. Patience, in his biography, had been vindicated by results. Accumulation worked; he had the membership figures and the vote totals to prove it. When the two men later argued about whether power could be taken by a determined minority or only assembled by a patient majority, they were generalising from the two most opposite political educations European socialism offered, and neither could fully credit the other's experience as evidence.

One further circumstance deserves emphasis because it determined what each man could see in the other. The pupil read the teacher's books as scripture under conditions where books were contraband and study was an act of resistance; the words carried the charge of forbidden knowledge. The teacher wrote those books at a desk, on a schedule, for a movement with publishing houses. The later quarrel replayed that asymmetry in reverse. When the pupil seized power and wrote his indictment of the teacher from the Kremlin, he wrote as a head of government conducting a war, and the teacher answered as a private theorist in Vienna and Berlin, armed with nothing but the doctrine they had once shared. The exchange is partly a dialogue of situations: the man of power explaining why power required what the man of doctrine insisted doctrine forbade.

## The shared orthodoxy and the fight against revisionism

The alliance between the two men was not merely personal warmth between a famous theorist and a distant admirer. It was a working partnership inside a movement that defined itself against a heresy, and the heresy had a name and an author. Eduard Bernstein, once an intimate of Engels, proposed in a series of articles and then in a book that the movement should abandon its revolutionary goal, acknowledge that capitalism was adapting rather than collapsing, and become openly what he said it already was in practice: a party of democratic reform. The proposal struck at the foundation both men stood on. If Bernstein was right, the party programme was a fiction, the doctrine was a relic, and the teacher's life work of systematisation had systematised an error. The campaign against revisionism was therefore existential for Kautsky in a way later readers underestimate, and Lenin, fighting parallel battles against Russian variants of the same temptation, threw his weight behind the German campaign with enthusiasm.

Examine what the shared orthodoxy actually contained, because the later dispute is often misdescribed as a contest between that orthodoxy and its rejection. The creed both men defended held that capitalism generated its own gravediggers by concentrating production and swelling the ranks of wage labour; that the working class, organised and educated by its party, would become the majority force in society; that political power would pass to that class through a transformation whose exact form no one could schedule; and that the resulting order would socialise the means of production under democratic control. Kautsky's commentary on the party's Erfurt Programme, which he drafted in its theoretical part, remained for decades the standard exposition of this creed, translated everywhere the movement existed. Lenin treated the commentary as a model of how doctrine should be taught. The point cannot be pressed too hard: the programme whose interpretation made the teacher famous was a revolutionary programme, and its interpreter spent the prime of his life insisting that socialism meant a transformation of property and power, not a softening of capitalism's edges.

This history corrects the most durable misreading of the whole dispute, the picture of Kautsky as a lifelong gradualist who was always, in essence, a reformer waiting to be exposed. For most of his career he occupied the position his movement called the revolutionary centre: against the right, which wanted to drop the goal; against impatient currents on the left, which wanted to force the pace; and for a strategy that combined intransigent doctrine with organisational patience. He polemicised against Bernstein from the left. He defended the mass strike as a weapon when conservatives in his own party wanted it disowned. As late as the eve of the war he published a book on the road to power that spoke of an approaching era of revolutions and predicted that the coming period would shake the foundations of the existing order. Lenin read that book with delight and said so. The teacher of those years was not a man the pupil had any reason to despise, and the pupil did not despise him. The renegade of the later pamphlet had to be manufactured out of a revolutionary by events, and the manufacturing process is the story of the war.

A second correction follows from the first. Because the later quarrel was conducted in the vocabulary of loyalty and betrayal, readers inherit a cast of characters: the consistent revolutionary and the backslider. The reality is less theatrical and more instructive. Both men changed under the pressure of the same catastrophe, in opposite directions, and each experienced the other's change as a fall from a shared position that both had genuinely held. Kautsky believed to the end of his life that he had kept the doctrine and that his former pupil had abandoned it for a voluntarist gamble dressed in Marxist phrases. Lenin believed with equal sincerity that the doctrine had always pointed toward the seizure of power by a resolute party and that the teacher had lost his nerve at the moment the doctrine demanded action. A grading of their dispute that begins by accepting either man's account of his own consistency has surrendered before the evidence is heard. The article therefore treats consistency itself as one of the contested questions, and asks of each position not whether its author claimed the inheritance but what the position predicted and what it delivered.

### Why did the pupil defend the teacher against the revisionists?

Because the attack on the teacher's orthodoxy was simultaneously an attack on the foundations of the pupil's own politics. Bernstein's proposal, if accepted, dissolved the goal that justified the sacrifices of an illegal movement in Russia, where activists risked prison and exile for a transformation that revisionism pronounced unnecessary.

A Russian revolutionary who conceded that capitalism could reform itself into decency had no answer to the comrade who asked why anyone should endure Siberia for a programme of municipal improvements. Defending the German orthodoxy was therefore self defence conducted on a larger stage. There was also a plainer intellectual reason: the pupil believed the teacher was right. He had tested the doctrine against Russian conditions, found it illuminating, and built his faction's identity around fidelity to it. In the factional struggles of Russian social democracy, to be the party of Kautsky's orthodoxy was to claim the centre of the world movement against both liquidators who wanted to dissolve the illegal party and recallists who wanted to abandon legal work entirely. The teacher's name functioned in Russian disputes as a certificate of seriousness, and the pupil spent that certificate freely.

The certificate mattered abroad as well as at home. The Russian movement was divided, poor, and quarrelsome, and its factions competed for recognition from the International's leading parties. A word of approval from the German centre could decide an exile's standing, open the columns of journals, and unlock solidarity funds. Lenin understood this economy of prestige perfectly and worked within it without cynicism, since his admiration was genuine, but also without innocence, since he knew exactly what an endorsement was worth. The relationship thus joined sincere discipleship to the ordinary politics of a world movement, and it is a mistake to purify it in either direction. When the break came, both elements turned to bitterness together: the pupil felt betrayed in his faith and outraged in his judgement of men, while the teacher felt his authority over doctrine challenged by the one reader he could not dismiss as an ignoramus. Each had too much invested in the other for the quarrel to stay polite.

There is a further, quieter strand in the alliance that the pamphlet war buried and that the historian must excavate: the two men agreed about the party. The teacher's doctrine of the party was not the loose electoral machine of later social democracy. He conceived the party as the organised bearer of socialist consciousness, the institution that fused the workers' movement with socialist theory and held the long term goal in trust against the short term pressures of daily struggle. His famous formulation about consciousness being introduced into the movement from outside its spontaneous economic life was the theoretical charter of that conception, and it was precisely this charter that the pupil adopted as his own in his most famous early book. On the theory of the party, the division that later writers draw between a democratic Kautsky and an authoritarian Lenin projects a later argument onto a period when the relevant text was written by the supposed democrat and brandished by the supposed authoritarian as his warrant. The genuine divergence on the party came later, and it concerned not whether the party should lead but what the party might legitimately do with state power once it held power alone.

## The strategy of attrition and the party that accumulated strength

Every strategic doctrine is an answer to a situation, and the situation the German party faced explains the strategy its theorist elaborated. The party operated inside an empire that permitted elections to a parliament without permitting that parliament to govern. It could win votes by the million and seats by the hundred while ministers answered to the crown, the army answered to the general staff, and the franchise in the largest state was rigged by wealth. Under those conditions the party grew into a state within the state: newspapers, publishing houses, trade unions allied to it, insurance funds, cycling clubs, choral societies, a whole civilisation of organised working class life. Its theorist drew the conclusion his vocabulary announced. Borrowing terms from military science, he distinguished a strategy of overthrow, appropriate to an era when small forces could storm a weak order, from a strategy of attrition, appropriate to a strong modern state: the patient accumulation of forces, the winning of majorities, the avoidance of premature confrontation, and the use of every legal opening until the correlation of strength made the transformation irresistible. The distinction, which he drew from the military historian Hans Delbrück's contrast between strategies of annihilation and exhaustion, became the strategic common sense of the Second International.

Attrition is easy to caricature and was caricatured by its enemies, including, in the end, by the former pupil. Stated at its strongest, the strategy rested on three claims. First, modern states were too strong, too well armed, and too deeply rooted in administration to be overthrown by insurrection except under conditions of collapse that no party could manufacture at will. Second, the working class was growing in numbers, organisation, and cultural weight with every decade of capitalist development, so time fought on the movement's side if the movement survived to collect its inheritance. Third, premature confrontation was the one error that could destroy the accumulated strength at a stroke, by inviting repression against a party that had staked everything on a single throw. The strategist of attrition therefore prized discipline, legality where legality protected accumulation, and doctrinal intransigence as the guarantee that patience would not decay into mere habit. He was explicit that the goal remained a revolution in the social order. What he postponed was not the goal but the date, and he postponed it on the argument that history, properly read, was delivering the preconditions on schedule.

The pupil absorbed this strategy along with the rest of the doctrine, and for years his writings breathe its air. He too warned against premature action, mocked putschist impatience, and treated the accumulation of organised strength as the movement's daily task. His quarrels with the teacher in this period were about tempo and tone, about whether the German centre was allowing tactical caution to blur doctrinal edges. But there were hairline cracks that the war would split open. The Russian had always insisted, against an objection he associated with the teacher's more cautious moods, that the party must prepare for illegality and insurrection as real possibilities even while working legally. He had watched a revolution erupt in his own country in the middle of the prewar decade, seen soviets arise outside every party's plan, and drawn the lesson that history does not always wait for majorities to be assembled in good order. The teacher, observing the same Russian events, drew a different lesson: that a revolution in a backward country would be compelled to borrow the forms of the advanced countries and could not leap over the stages of development. Both lessons were available in the shared doctrine. The war decided which lesson each man would stake his life on.

### What is the strategy of attrition in the teacher's own terms?

In his own terms, attrition was the strategy of a movement too weak to storm a strong state and strong enough to outgrow it. The party would win members, votes, and institutions year by year, refuse battles it must lose, and convert its accumulated weight into power when the old order's crisis finally arrived.

Attrition names a way of winning against a strong opponent without storming his position. The party gathers members, votes, unions, newspapers, and skills year after year, refuses engagements it cannot win, erodes the opponent's legitimacy by its visible growth, and holds its doctrine intact so that accumulated strength, when the crisis arrives, converts into a transformation of society rather than a larger share of office.

That definition deserves a slow reading, because every element of it later became a charge in the indictment. Gathering strength year after year became, in the pupil's later telling, an excuse for never using it. Refusing unwinnable engagements became a habit of refusal itself. Eroding legitimacy became parliamentary cretinism, the delusion that votes were power. Holding doctrine intact became, most painfully, the teacher's own accusation reversed: the pupil charged that the doctrine had been preserved as a museum piece precisely by never being applied. A fair grading must hold both readings in view. The strategy's logic was sound against the opponent it was designed for, a stable empire that rewarded accumulation and punished adventurism. Its fatal exposure was to a change in the opponent's behaviour: a general war, which the strategy had treated as a catastrophe to be prevented by international solidarity, and for which, once prevention failed, the strategy supplied no instructions at all. A doctrine of patience presupposes a future in which patience can continue. In the first week of the war that future was cancelled, and the party of attrition discovered that it had accumulated everything except a plan for the day accumulation stopped working.

The teacher knew the exposure existed and had written about the danger of war for years, denouncing armaments, colonial rivalry, and the diplomacy of the great powers as the standing threats to his whole construction. His theory of imperialism, which treated the arms race as a policy choice of finance capital rather than an iron necessity, even held out the possibility that the capitalist states might learn to cooperate and spare themselves the catastrophe. The pupil seized on that possibility, named ultra-imperialism, as evidence of evasion: a doctrine that comforted the movement with the thought that the worst might not happen. Their dispute over imperialism, treated in the companion guide to the imperialism text at [lenin imperialism readers guide](/2014/04/15/lenin-imperialism-readers-guide/), belongs to the prehistory of the rupture and shows its pattern in miniature. On paper, the difference was an analytical disagreement about the tendencies of capital. Underneath, the difference was temperamental and strategic: one man building a theory that made patience rational, the other building a theory that made rupture inevitable. When the guns fired, both theories claimed vindication, and only one of their authors was positioned to act on his.

## The war and the collapse of the shared world

The Second International rested on a promise its member parties had repeated at congress after congress: that the workers of the belligerent countries would refuse to shoot each other, and that a general strike or some comparable act of solidarity would greet any declaration of war. The promise was the moral foundation of the whole edifice the teacher served. When the crisis came in the summer of the war's first year, the foundation gave way within days. The German parliamentary fraction voted the credits that financed the Kaiser's armies. Sister parties across Europe rallied to their own governments. The International did not die in a single dramatic vote; it dissolved as each national section discovered that its members, its newspapers, its funds, and its legal existence were national assets that a government at war could confiscate, and that its voters, in the first fever of mobilisation, wanted defence of the fatherland more than they wanted solidarity with foreign workers. Historians of the International, among them Georges Haupt, have documented the speed of the collapse and the stunned disbelief of the leaders who had built their lives on the promise. The teacher's party, the largest and proudest section, led the capitulation it had sworn to prevent.

For the pupil, in exile in Switzerland, the news arrived as a personal catastrophe layered on a political one. Reports describe his initial refusal to believe the German party's newspaper announcing the vote; the report, he insisted, must be a forgery planted by the German general staff. The anecdote survives because it captures the psychology of the rupture better than any manifesto. A forgery was more thinkable than the fact. The man whose orthodoxy had organised the pupil's intellectual life for twenty years presided, as the party's leading theorist, over the abandonment of the International's most solemn commitment, and then set about explaining why the abandonment had been necessary or at least excusable. From that month the pupil's letters and articles treat the teacher's name as a synonym for betrayal. The word renegade entered his vocabulary for the older man early in the war and never left it. What had been the certificate of orthodoxy became, in the pupil's new dispensation, the brand of the apostate, and the International's surviving militants were invited to choose which of the two authorities they would follow.

The teacher's conduct in those months deserves a precise account, because the pupil's version of it, a simple tale of cowardice and corruption, became the received account in one tradition, while a sanitised version, in which the theorist merely erred, became the received account in the other. The documented record, as historians of the German party have reconstructed it, shows a man trying to hold a disintegrating centre. He proposed formulas that would let the party abstain rather than endorse; he was outvoted by the fraction's majority and by a leadership determined on national defence. He then did the thing that destroyed him in the pupil's eyes: he stayed, he lent his authority to unity, and he constructed theoretical justifications for a party that had chosen its nation over its International. His wartime writings searched for a middle position between the government's war and the antiwar left, defending the party's conduct as a tragic necessity and attacking the small minority, around Luxemburg and Liebknecht, that refused it. Each justification was, in form, an exercise of his old systematising gift. Each was, in substance, the doctrine of attrition applied to a situation it had never contemplated: accumulate unity, avoid the split, wait for the fever to pass. The strategy of patience, meeting the one crisis that could not be waited out, produced a theorist rationalising the indefensible in the cadences of orthodoxy.

### Why did the war credits vote matter more than any doctrinal dispute?

Because it converted an abstract disagreement about strategy into a demonstrated fact about institutions. A party built to accumulate strength inside the nation state had, at the decisive test, behaved as an institution of that state, and its theorist had supplied the reasons afterwards.

No pamphlet about parliamentarism could prove what that single vote proved about where accumulated strength actually lived and whose purposes it would serve when the pressure came.

The antiwar opposition that gathered at Zimmerwald and in the smaller conferences that followed was tiny, fractious, and easily caricatured by the governments it opposed. The pupil threw himself into it with the energy of a man whose whole world picture had been confirmed by disaster. His wartime writings rebuilt his politics on the ruins of the old International: imperialism as the economic explanation of the war, the split in the movement as a cleansing rather than a loss, the demand for a new International purged of the old leaderships, and the readiness to turn an imperialist war into civil war as the slogan that separated revolutionaries from patriots. In those writings the teacher appears again and again as the chief theoretical obstacle, the authority whose prestige reconciled workers to the betrayal. The pupil's polemic had a precise strategic logic. The old man's name still certified orthodoxy for millions; until that certification was revoked in the eyes of the antiwar minority, no new International could be built. The ferocity of the later renegade pamphlet was prepared in these wartime articles, where the demolition of a reputation was understood, correctly, as a precondition of organising its former believers.

Yet it would falsify the story to treat the wartime pupil as already the author of the seizure of power, reading the teacher's fall as his own opportunity in a simple sense. Between the collapse of the old International and the October insurrection lie years of development in which the pupil changed his own mind on fundamentals: on the possibility of a revolution in a backward country leaping beyond the textbook sequence, on the soviets as a form of state, on the alliance with the peasantry, on the national question. His intellectual biography, traced in the companion study at [lenin intellectual biography](/2014/03/01/lenin-intellectual-biography/), shows a thinker in furious motion, discarding positions he had defended for years when events refuted them. The teacher, by contrast, changed hardly at all, and therein lay both his dignity and his doom as a controversialist. He defended in the war's last year almost exactly the doctrine he had taught in its first decade. The pupil could therefore portray him, with surface plausibility, as a mummy guarding a tomb. What the portrayal omitted was that the tomb contained the doctrine they had shared, and that the pupil's new positions, however creative, required the burial of some of his own most cherished earlier certainties. Neither man entered the pamphlet war of the revolution with clean hands in the matter of consistency. The difference was that only one of them admitted, even privately, to having changed.

The revolution in Russia converted their quarrel from a dispute among exiles and editors into an argument about a state. When the pupil's party took power in the capital, dissolved the old order's institutions, and began to rule through the soviets in the name of the working class, the teacher faced a decision that no theorist of the Second International had expected to face in his lifetime: a party claiming his doctrine had seized a great state, and it was doing so by methods his doctrine, as he read it, forbade. His response, worked out in a pamphlet written in the revolution's first year, is the hinge of this whole article. He chose to judge the new regime by the doctrine rather than revise the doctrine to bless the regime. That choice, whatever its consequences for his influence, was an act of intellectual honour, and the article grades his arguments as arguments, not as the reflexes of a man who had lost. The pupil's response, written from the seat of power in the intervals of a civil war, chose the opposite priority: the regime's survival first, and the doctrine reinterpreted as the regime required. Between those two priorities the whole dispute is contained, and a reader who keeps them in view will understand both pamphlets better than a reader who asks only which author had the better logic.

## The pamphlets of the dictatorship answering each other

The teacher struck first in print. His pamphlet on the dictatorship of the proletariat, published in Vienna in the year the Russian civil war began, undertook to rescue a phrase of Marx from the use the Bolsheviks were making of it. The phrase itself receives its full treatment in the companion article on the [dictatorship of the proletariat](/2013/01/01/dictatorship-of-the-proletariat/), and only its role in this quarrel is followed here. Kautsky's argument proceeded in steps. Marx, he held, had used the expression to name a condition, the political rule of the working class as a class, not a form of government and certainly not the rule of a single party over the class and over everyone else. A class rules through institutions in which it can express and correct its will, which means, for a class comprising the majority of society, through universal suffrage, a free press, and parliaments answerable to electors. A party that abolishes those institutions in the class's name has not established the rule of the class; it has established its own rule and forbidden the class to dismiss it. The argument was not imported from liberalism. It was built from the tradition's own premises, and its author took care to ground it in Marx's writings on the Paris Commune and on the political emancipation of the working class.

From that premise the pamphlet drew its indictment of the Russian experiment point by point. The dispersal of the Constituent Assembly, in which the Bolsheviks had won only a minority of the vote, was treated as the founding act that revealed the regime's character: a minority that could not win a majority had chosen to rule without one. The suppression of opposition newspapers, the exclusion of rival socialist parties from the soviets, and the creation of a political police were presented not as emergency excesses but as the logical equipment of minority rule, since a government that cannot persuade must compel, and compulsion requires machinery. Economic policy received the same treatment. Expropriation decreed from above, in a country whose industry was collapsing, would produce not socialism but a new bureaucracy administering scarcity, while the attempt to organise production by command would disorganise it further. The pamphlet closed with a prediction stated in the measured tone of its author's old textbooks, and it is the sentence on which this article's verdict ultimately rests: a regime founded on the suppression of democracy would not wither into freedom but harden into a despotism resting on the very apparatus built to defend the revolution.

The pupil's reply, written within weeks and published under a title that joined the older man's name to the word renegade, is among the most ferocious polemics in the Marxist canon. Its method is quotation and confrontation. Passage after passage of the teacher's pamphlet is set beside passages from the teacher's own earlier writings and from Marx and Engels, and the reader is invited to watch the renegade refute himself. On the central question the reply denies the premise that democracy is a single thing equally available to all classes. Democracy under capitalism, it argues, is democracy for the wealthy, hedged by property qualifications in fact where not in law, by ownership of the press, by the bureaucracy and the standing army; the democracy of the soviets, which excludes the exploiters and organises the exploited, is democracy for the immense majority and therefore a million times more democratic than any parliament. The dictatorship of the proletariat is defined in the reply as power based directly on force and unrestricted by laws, and the definition is offered without apology, as the honest name for what every ruling class does and only the socialist ruling class admits. Around this core the reply arranges its subsidiary charges: that the teacher had falsified Marx by converting a condition into a parliamentary procedure, that his arithmetic of majorities ignored the civil war the old order had launched, and that his solicitude for the Constituent Assembly mourned an institution the revolution had outgrown.

### How did the renegade pamphlet work as a piece of persuasion?

It worked by biography as much as by argument. By binding the teacher's name to the word renegade in its very title, the pamphlet instructed its readers to interpret every argument that followed as the rationalisation of a fall, and it supplied the fall with a history: the years of accumulation, the wartime justifications, the fear of the masses the author claimed to detect beneath the democratic scruples. Readers who accepted the biography no longer needed to answer the prediction, because a prediction uttered by an apostate could be discounted as the wish of a man who needed the revolution to fail. The technique was devastatingly effective inside the movement, and it is the chief reason the teacher's case went unheard for generations in the tradition that won. A grading of the dispute must therefore do deliberately what the pamphlet was designed to prevent: separate the author's standing from the argument's content, and test the prediction against the record as though an unknown writer had made it. Tested that way, as the sections below will show, the prediction does not need its author's reputation to survive. It needs only the chronology of the regime it described.

The teacher answered the answer. His longer sequel, devoted to terrorism and communism, widened the indictment from the regime's political forms to its methods of rule, comparing Bolshevik practice with the Jacobin terror and arguing that a socialism built by terror would reproduce terror as its normal instrument. He also returned to the theoretical charge, insisting that the pupil's redefinition of democracy emptied the word of the content that made it worth defending: if the exclusion of opponents from political life counted as a higher democracy whenever the excluders claimed to represent the majority, then no despotism in history lacked a democratic self description. The exchange then froze into the positions from which later generations inherited it. The pupil's party founded a new International on the repudiation of the teacher; the teacher became the honoured theorist of a rival International that commanded no armies; and the pamphlets settled into the libraries as documents partisans quoted rather than arguments contemporaries weighed. Recovering them as arguments, live and answerable to evidence, is the work the remaining sections undertake, with the help of an instrument designed to keep the questions apart.

## The dispute grid as an instrument for grading

Polemic rewards conflation. A reader carried along by the renegade pamphlet's momentum readily slides from thinking its target wrong about the Constituent Assembly to thinking him wrong about everything, just as a reader of the teacher's pamphlet slides from the prediction's accuracy to a verdict on the whole Bolshevik enterprise. The dispute grid exists to arrest that slide. It sets out the five matters the two men actually contested, states each position in the form its holder would recognise, and reserves a final column for what the record shows, so that the assessment of each row can proceed without borrowing strength from the row beside it. The grid is printed here, before the detailed grading, because the argument of the article follows its rows. Readers preparing essays will find that the grid doubles as an essay architecture: take any row, expound the two positions from the pamphlets, test them against the record column, and a self contained section exists. Readers pressed for time can extract the whole dispute from the table alone and return for the reasoning when the hour allows. For keeping the pamphlet arguments, the grid rows, and the verdict aligned during revision, a structured study aid such as the [Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) companion lets the five rows be stored as five retrievable entries rather than as one undifferentiated quarrel.

| Issue | Kautsky's position | Lenin's position | What the record shows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Theory of the party | The party organises and educates the class, which rules through democratic institutions | The party may take and hold power directly in the class's name | A single party held power and progressively fused with the state |
| Assessment of the war | National defence by the great parties was a tragic necessity to be managed | The war was imperialist and the old International's collapse a cleansing | The International dissolved and its sections backed their own governments |
| Meaning of the dictatorship of the proletariat | The rule of a class through democratic forms, not a form of government | Power based directly on force and unrestricted by laws | The regime ruled through a party apparatus, with soviet forms subordinated to it |
| Role of parliamentary institutions | Indispensable arenas in which the class expresses and corrects its will | Instruments of bourgeois rule, usable tactically but never the road itself | The Constituent Assembly was dispersed and no sovereign parliament replaced it |
| Strategy for taking power | Attrition: accumulate forces and majorities, avoid premature confrontation | Overthrow: identify the revolutionary situation and seize the moment | Power was seized by a minority party; the accumulated German majority never took power |

Reading down the final column teaches the article's central lesson before the prose argues it. Some rows settled clearly in one man's favour, some settled against both, and one row, the assessment of the war, settled as a fact about institutions that both men interpreted to suit themselves. The grid thus disciplines the verdict in advance: any conclusion that awards every row to a single side contradicts the table's own evidence, and the split verdict, when it is formally stated near the end, will surprise only readers who skipped the instrument. Notice also what the grid omits. It has no row for sincerity, none for personal courage, none for literary power, because the dispute's historical weight does not rest on those qualities, abundant as they were on both sides. It omits, finally, any row asking which man was the truer Marxist, a question the participants considered decisive and the historian must decline, since the canon they shared was capacious enough to furnish both pamphlets with proof texts, and an inheritance dispute cannot be settled by counting quotations.

### How does the grid change the way the quarrel should be read?

It changes the quarrel from a duel into an audit. A duel produces a corpse and a survivor, and the reader's sympathy follows the survivor. An audit produces findings, some favourable and some unfavourable to each party, and obliges the reader to hold them simultaneously.

That obligation is the grid's real service, because the actual history refuses the duel's tidy outcome: the man whose prediction failed least about Russia never converted his correctness into a workable politics, and the man whose politics conquered a state bequeathed a regime that illustrated his opponent's warning. Reading pamphlet against pamphlet without an instrument, most readers emerge as partisans of whichever they read second. Reading with the grid, a student can specify the exact row on which a sympathy rests, and can say, for instance, that the teacher wins the row on the meaning of the disputed phrase and loses the row on strategy, without feeling that the two findings contradict each other. Precision of that kind is what distinguishes an examined opinion from an inherited one, and the dispute, which generated more inherited opinion than almost any other in the Marxist tradition, repays the effort disproportionately.

One row of the grid conceals a subtlety that the detailed sections must bring out: the row on the party. Superficially it records a disagreement, and so it was by the time of the pamphlets. But the grid's first column, read against the prewar record surveyed earlier, shows how recent the disagreement was. The teacher's early doctrine of the party as bearer of consciousness had equipped the pupil's faction; the pupil's later doctrine of the party as direct holder of state power equipped a regime. Between those two doctrines lies the single most important theoretical question the dispute poses for later readers: at what point does an organisation that leads a class become an organisation that replaces it, and by what test could contemporaries have known the point had been passed? The teacher's pamphlet proposed a test: whether the class retains institutions through which it can dismiss its leaders. The pupil's reply proposed a different test: whether the organisation's class composition and programme remain proletarian, whatever the institutional forms. The record column of the grid reports what happened to the first test's institutions. The second test, being internal to the ruling organisation, could never be failed by its own administrators, and that asymmetry, the presence of an external check on one side and its absence on the other, is itself a finding the grid makes visible.

## Question one: the character of the Russian revolution

The first question for grading is what kind of event the Russian revolution was, since each man's strategy presupposed an answer. The teacher's answer followed from his stage theory of history. Russia, in his analysis, remained a country of peasants, small production, and a young, numerically modest industrial working class; its revolution was therefore bourgeois in its objective tasks, clearing away the autocracy and the remnants of feudal landholding so that capitalist development and, with it, the growth of the proletariat could proceed. A socialist party that attempted to leap directly to socialism in such a country would be attempting to decree away the material preconditions of its own goal, and the attempt would deform both the party and the goal. On this reading the Bolshevik seizure of power was a category error armed with a state: the forms of a socialist revolution imposed on a society whose content could not fill them.

The pupil's answer began from the same facts and inverted the conclusion. Precisely because the Russian bourgeoisie was weak, entangled with the autocracy and with foreign capital, the bourgeois tasks of the revolution would not be performed by the bourgeoisie; they would fall to an alliance of the proletariat and the peasantry, and a revolution that began by performing those tasks would grow over, under the pressure of war and economic collapse, into a struggle for power by the working class itself. The world war completed the argument: the chain of imperialism, to use his image, breaks at its weakest link, and the revolution in a backward country was to be the opening of a general European revolution whose advanced contingents would supply what Russia lacked. His seizure of power was thus never, in his own theory, the wager that Russia alone could build socialism by decree. It was a wager on contagion, placed in the expectation that the German working class above all would follow, and the teacher, whose party commanded that class, was in the pupil's eyes the gaoler whose key refused to turn.

Grading this question requires separating its components, for the men were arguing about three distinguishable matters at once. On the diagnosis of the old order's collapse, the pupil's reading proved the more penetrating: the autocracy did fall, the liberal bourgeoisie did fail to take and hold power, and the soviets did become the real arena of sovereignty in the revolutionary year, much as his spring theses had claimed. On the immediate sequel, honours divide: the revolution did spread beyond Russia's borders in the war's aftermath, with soviets proclaimed in Bavaria and a soviet republic in Hungary, yet each rising was crushed, and the European revolution on which the wager depended did not arrive. On the long construction, the teacher's stage logic collected its debt. A regime committed to building socialism in a peasant country was driven, across the following decades, into improvisations its founders had not theorised: retreat to market mechanisms under the New Economic Policy, then a forced industrialisation conducted by command, at a cost in famine and coercion that historians such as Robert Conquest and, with different emphases, Sheila Fitzpatrick have documented in grim detail. The pupil had predicted a bridge built by Europe. The teacher had predicted that a bridge attempted without Europe would be built by compulsion. On the character of the revolution, therefore, the verdict again splits: the pupil read its outbreak better, the teacher read its predicament better, and the distinction between outbreak and predicament is one the grid exists to preserve.

The strongest statement of the pupil's case on this question deserves its own paragraph, stated as his defenders state it. Had the Bolsheviks declined power, the argument runs, power would not have passed to a democratic coalition practising the teacher's patience. It would have passed, as the Kornilov affair foreshadowed, to a military reaction that would have drowned the revolution in blood and restored the old order in a harsher form. The choice in the revolutionary autumn was not between the pupil's road and the teacher's road; it was between the pupil's road and the counter revolution, because the teacher's road required a Germany that did not exist and a Russian majority party of his type that had failed to materialise. This argument has real force, and the grading accepts it in part: it establishes that the seizure of power may have been the least catastrophic option available at that moment. What it cannot establish is the point the dispute actually turned on, which concerns what the seizure would build over time. A drowning man may be right to seize a spar. The teacher's question was what the spar would become once the swimmer, clinging to it, declared it a ship and shot anyone who proposed swimming for shore. Emergency justifies the act of rescue; it does not repeal the laws of buoyancy, and the teacher's pamphlet was an essay on buoyancy addressed to men celebrating the rescue.

### What did the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly settle?

It settled the question of where sovereignty was to reside, and it settled it in the first months, before civil war could be pleaded as the sole explanation. The Assembly, elected by universal suffrage in the most democratic vote Russia had held, returned a majority for parties other than the pupil's.

It met for a single day, refused to subordinate itself to the soviet government, and was sent home by an armed guard whose commander announced that the guard was tired. The teacher treated the episode as the regime's birth certificate: a government that disperses the only assembly the whole people elected has declared that its mandate comes from itself. The pupil's side replied that the electoral lists predated the revolution's realignments, that the Assembly represented a Russia that had ceased to exist, and that the soviets, rooted in workplaces and barracks, embodied a living democracy beside which the Assembly's parchment mandate was a corpse. The reply's factual elements were not invented; the lists were outdated and the soviets were alive with participation in that season. Yet the teacher's deeper point survived the reply, for it concerned precedent rather than that single night. No mechanism was ever afterwards created by which the population, as distinct from the ruling party's own organisations, could withdraw the mandate the Assembly had been denied the chance to exercise. The dispersal settled sovereignty in practice. The argument about whether the settlement was justified is the second question, and it is to democracy itself that the grading now turns.

## Question two: democracy and the rule of the working class

The second question is the theoretical heart of the dispute, and the section of the exchange most worth reading entire. It can be stated as a disagreement about the grammar of a single phrase. When Marx wrote of the dictatorship of the proletariat, did he name a way of governing, comparable to monarchy or republic, or a social condition, the state of affairs in which the working class holds political supremacy, achievable under several forms of government and destroyed by none of them in particular? The teacher argued the second reading with a scholar's patience. Marx, he pointed out, had praised the Paris Commune for its elected and recallable officials and had treated universal suffrage as the working class's indispensable instrument. A condition of class rule, on this reading, is perfectly compatible with, indeed normally requires, the fullest democratic forms: a class that constitutes society's majority has nothing to fear from counting heads, and everything to fear from a machinery that counts them on its behalf. The pupil argued the first reading with a soldier's bluntness. Dictatorship names rule based on force and not on law; every state is the dictatorship of a class; the candour of the socialist version lies in admitting what the bourgeois version conceals. Between a condition and a form of government, between counting heads and dispensing with the count, the whole practical dispute unfolds.

The teacher's democratic objection is persistently misreported as a liberal objection, imported from outside the movement by a man who had absorbed the values of the society he opposed. The pamphlet refutes that report on its face. Its argument is internal at every joint. Socialism, for the teacher, is the self emancipation of the working class, and self emancipation is not a metaphor: a class that cannot govern itself through its own chosen and removable representatives has not emancipated itself, whatever is done in its name. Democracy is not a garnish on the socialist project but the form of the class's own activity, the school in which it learns to rule and the mechanism by which its errors are corrected without catastrophe. A minority that undertakes to emancipate the class by ruling over it faces a contradiction that no sincerity can dissolve: it must either educate the class into a majority that can dismiss it, which its monopoly of press and organisation prevents, or perpetuate its tutelage, which its mission forbids. The teacher pressed the practical corollary. Production organised by a bureaucracy answerable only to itself would lack both the information that markets and a free press supply and the correction that independent unions and parties provide; economic democracy and political democracy, in his analysis, stand or fall together.

The pupil's counter argument also deserves statement at full strength, since caricature serves it no better. Bourgeois democracy, he held, is a form calibrated to a society of owners: the voter is formally equal and materially dependent, the press is free for those who can buy presses, parliament debates within limits set by the permanent machinery of army, police, and bureaucracy that no election dissolves. To demand that the working class, in the act of expropriating the owners, confine itself to forms designed by the owners is to demand that it fight with its hands tied by its opponent's rulebook. The soviet form, he argued, was not the negation of democracy but its extension to the point of production: delegates elected in workplaces, recallable by their electors, fusing executive and legislative functions as the Commune had done, and excluding only the exploiting minority whose exclusion every democratic revolution in history had practised in some degree. That the expropriators of the February revolution's promises should complain of exclusion struck him as the pamphlet's richest irony. On the theory of the state the reply added its systematic claim: the old machinery could not be taken over and wielded for new purposes but had to be broken, and the interval between the breaking and the withering away of all coercion was precisely the dictatorship the teacher found so alarming in name and so familiar in bourgeois practice.

Grading this exchange by its arguments rather than its outcomes, honours are closer than either tradition admits. The teacher's philological case about Marx's usage was strong, and most subsequent scholarship on the phrase has moved in his direction: Marx used the expression rarely, loosely, and never as the title of a party regime. The pupil's sociological case about the limits of formal democracy under conditions of concentrated property was also strong, has been absorbed far beyond his own tradition, and permanently changed how democratic forms are analysed. Where the grading must divide them is on the institutional question that both pamphlets finally address: what happens to a government that suppresses the means by which it might be corrected? Here argument gives way to prediction, and prediction is checkable. The teacher predicted that the suppression of pluralism, decreed as an emergency, would become the regime's permanent constitution, because no holder of unchecked power schedules its own correction. The pupil predicted that coercion directed against a minority of exploiters would wither as classes themselves withered, leaving an administration of things in which coercion had no object. The record of the regime, as documented by historians across the interpretive spectrum, from the dispersal of rival socialist parties and the banning of factions inside the ruling party itself to the construction of a security apparatus that outlived every emergency invoked to justify it, follows the first prediction's script in episode after episode. On the question of democracy and class rule, the verdict therefore runs: the pupil won the argument about bourgeois democracy's limits, and the teacher won the argument about what replaces those limits when nothing is permitted to replace them.

### Why did the faction ban matter to this question?

The ban mattered because it abolished the last lawful channel through which the ruling party could correct itself. Platforms and organised minorities had kept leadership answerable to debate; after the prohibition, disagreement survived only as private doubt or as conspiracy, and the secretariat that defined unity acquired the means to punish it.

The prohibition of organised factions within the ruling party, adopted at the congress that coincided with the crushing of the Kronstadt rising, closed the last channel through which the class's supposed ruling party could correct its leadership from inside. Opposition platforms within the party had been, until that measure, a real feature of Bolshevik life; debates were furious and public, and the pupil himself had more than once been outvoted by his own central committee. After the ban, disagreement could survive only as private grumbling or as conspiracy, and the apparatus that enforced unity acquired the means to define it. Historians of the regime, among them Moshe Lewin in his study of the pupil's final struggle against the bureaucracy, have treated the measure as a hinge: conceived as an emergency expedient for a besieged party, it supplied the legal form in which a later leadership annihilated the party's inner life altogether. The episode matters to the grading because it confirms the teacher's mechanism rather than merely his conclusion. He had argued that suppression, once accepted as an instrument against opponents outside the class, would migrate inward, since the boundary between the class's enemies and the government's critics is drawn by the government. The sailors of Kronstadt, celebrated as revolutionary heroes a few years earlier, were reclassified as counter revolutionaries in the week they demanded soviet elections. A definition of the enemy that can absorb Kronstadt can absorb anything, and a theory of democracy that contains no institution capable of resisting such reclassification has, as the teacher warned, dispensed with the count altogether.

Beneath the institutional argument lies a philosophical one that the pamphlets conduct half openly, and a careful grading should surface it. For the teacher, democracy is partly a means and partly the thing itself: the self government of associated producers is not the road to the goal but a large part of the goal's content, so that a road which abolishes self government cannot arrive at it, whatever else it delivers. For the pupil, democracy is an instrument valued by its results for the class that wields it: forms are good insofar as they advance the transformation and expendable insofar as they obstruct it, so that a road is vindicated by its destination. The two positions generate different responses to the same evidence. Confronted with the regime's hardening, the teacher's framework pronounces the project falsified in its means; the pupil's framework, at least as his successors operated it, pronounces the hardening a detour imposed by encirclement. The dispute about Russia was therefore also a dispute about what kind of failure would count as failure, and it explains why the exchange never converged even as the facts accumulated. Each side's criterion of success was internal to its own theory. The grading offered in this article applies the one criterion both men professed in their shared years: that the emancipation of the working class must be the work of the working class itself. By that criterion, quoted from the rules of the First International and honoured by the teacher's pamphlet more faithfully than by the regime the pupil founded, the second question's heaviest weight falls on the teacher's side of the scale.

## Question three: the fate of the parliamentary road

The third question concerns parliaments, and it is the question on which the teacher's position suffered the most complete practical refutation, though not at the pupil's hands. The teacher never worshipped parliament. His doctrine assigned it a precise role: the arena in which the class counted its strength, educated itself in public affairs, won reforms that improved its capacity to struggle, and would, at the transformation, supply the legitimate form through which a majority class assumed power. The strategy of attrition required parliament the way a siege requires a circumvallation: not as the breach itself but as the work that makes the breach possible and governs what follows it. Against this the pupil's writings marshalled a lifetime of contempt for what he called parliamentary cretinism, the illusion that the talking shop was the real seat of power, while conceding, and practising with notable skill, the tactical use of elections and parliamentary tribunes for agitation. His theoretical claim was narrower than his rhetoric: not that parliaments were useless, but that the decisive transfer of power would never be transacted inside one, because the possessing classes, facing a parliamentary majority resolved on expropriation, would appeal from the chamber to the barracks.

History then conducted an experiment neither man designed. In the teacher's own country, in the aftermath of defeat and revolution, his party at last obtained the parliamentary supremacy his strategy had pursued for half a century, and found itself governing a republic it had not planned, in coalition with the machinery of the old order it had not broken. The record of that government, its reliance on the old officer corps, its suppression of the revolutionary left by military force in the weeks when Luxemburg and Liebknecht were murdered, and its eventual bequest of a republic too weak to withstand the catastrophe that consumed it a decade and a half later, constitutes the heaviest single count against the strategy of attrition ever assembled. It was assembled not by the pupil's party but by events, and the assessment of it in the companion comparison of the traditions at [marxism vs social democracy](/2019/11/15/marxism-vs-social-democracy/) traces how completely it discredited the road in the eyes of the generation that inherited both traditions. The pupil's prediction on this row of the grid, that a parliamentary majority for socialism would be answered by force, was never tested in its pure form in Germany, since the majority, when it came, declined to attempt socialism. His deeper prediction, that the chamber would not be the place where power changed hands, was confirmed by the manner of the republic's birth and death alike.

Justice to the teacher requires the other half of the row. The parliamentary road he theorised was never actually travelled, in Germany or anywhere else, by a party that combined his three conditions: a working class majority won to a socialist programme, a party resolved to use that majority for transformation, and a crisis of the old order deep enough to disable its veto. What was travelled, in Germany and later elsewhere, was a shortened version in which the majority was won to a party that had quietly exchanged the transformation for office. The teacher lived to lament the exchange and to insist, against his own successors, that the doctrine had not been tried and found wanting but found inconvenient and left untried. The defence is not worthless. Strategies are not falsified by the infidelity of their executants in the same way they are falsified by their own logic. Yet the grading must record the weakness the defence exposes: a strategy that requires half a century of discipline from an organisation it simultaneously embeds in the institutions of the existing order courts exactly the infidelity it later pleads. The pupil's wartime diagnosis of the German party, that its accumulated strength had become an interest in itself, an endowment to be protected rather than a weapon to be used, explains the truncation from inside the movement's own sociology. Attrition built a fortress; the fortress acquired a garrison; the garrison declined to sally. On the third question the verdict divides as follows: the pupil was right about what parliaments would not do, the teacher was right that nothing else in his century reliably did it either, and the row closes with both roads barricaded, one by the enemy's force and the other by its own side's prudence.

### Did any country vindicate the strategy of attrition?

No country vindicated it in its author's intended sense, and the honest answer must distinguish that sense from looser ones. Parties descended from the teacher's tradition governed much of Western Europe in the decades after the second great war, and they built welfare states, extended social rights, and civilised capitalism in ways their electors valued.

But the transformation of property relations that the strategy existed to deliver receded as the governing proceeded, until the parties themselves struck it from their programmes. A strategy whose terminal goal is formally abandoned by its own executants, after a century in which its preparatory stages succeeded beyond its author's dreams, stands refuted in a peculiarly complete way: not by defeat but by graduation into something else. The pupil's road, by contrast, delivered its executants to power and held them there for generations, at the price the teacher's pamphlet itemised. Posterity is left with a transformation that never happened by accumulation and a transformation that happened by seizure and then consumed its stated purpose. The grid's strategy row is therefore the saddest in the table, not because one side lost it, but because the century tested both entries and returned a negative finding on each.

## Question four: what each predicted about the other's road

The fourth question is the one on which grading can be most nearly scientific, because predictions, unlike interpretations, either come true or do not. Each man published, before the outcomes were known, a forecast of where the other's road would lead. Set the two forecasts side by side and the dispute resolves into its most instructive form, since the century obligingly ran both experiments to completion.

The teacher's forecast of the pupil's road was specific, and its specificity is what entitles it to be called a prediction rather than a complaint. A minority party holding power against the majority's will, he wrote, must rule by coercion; coercion requires an apparatus; the apparatus recruits administrators whose interest becomes the apparatus; opposition, denied legal existence, reappears as conspiracy and is met with wider coercion; the ruling party, unable to permit the class it represents to organise independently, substitutes itself for the class in every institution; and the result, whatever its flags and anthems, is a despotism of officials over a population excluded from political life, administering an economy whose failures cannot be corrected because correction requires the free criticism the despotism forbids. He extended the forecast to the regime's self understanding: such a government, he argued, would mistake its bureaucratic command for socialism and educate its subjects to repeat the mistake, so that the word socialism itself would be compromised for generations among the workers of other countries who watched the experiment. Each element of that forecast can be checked against the documented record, and the companion assessment in the [case against leninism](/2014/06/15/case-against-leninism/) assembles the record in the form the teacher's prediction implies. The suppression of rival socialist parties came first, within the revolution's initial year. The fusion of party and state followed, then the faction ban, then the elevation of the security police into an estate of the realm, then the succession in which the apparatus chose the leader who promised it the fullest licence, and finally the terror turned inward upon the old guard of the revolution itself. Historians dispute the weighting of causes, the roles of war, backwardness, and encirclement, and no honest grading treats the forecast as a complete explanation of the regime's trajectory. But a prediction is graded against its rivals as much as against perfection, and no rival forecast from within the dispute, including the pupil's own, described the direction of travel with comparable accuracy.

The pupil's forecast of the teacher's road was equally specific and has been equally neglected, because its fulfilment lacked a single dramatic scene. A party practising attrition, he argued across his wartime and postwar writings, would not remain a revolutionary party in waiting; it would become a party of the existing order's left margin, its parliamentarians absorbed into the state's routines, its theorists retained to bless the absorption, its ultimate goal preserved as liturgy for congresses and abandoned in every crisis where the order demanded loyalty. At the supreme test the party would choose the nation, as it had in the war's first week; faced with revolution it would choose order, as its German leaders did in the republic's first winter; and its doctrine, guarded by the teacher as a pure flame, would end as the property of men who cited it to forbid the actions it was written to inspire. That forecast, too, can be checked. The German party's trajectory from the war credits through the republic's founding bargains to its helpless legalism before the republic's destroyer realises the pupil's script with a fidelity that would be uncanny if the mechanism he named, institutional self preservation, were not so ordinary. Where the forecast erred was in its corollary: the pupil expected the masses of the teacher's party to desert it for the revolutionary alternative once its betrayal stood revealed. Some did. Most did not, and the working class of Germany remained divided between the two traditions into the catastrophe that destroyed both. The pupil predicted the teacher's party with precision and its followers hardly at all, a reminder that forecasts about institutions are more reliable than forecasts about the people inside them.

### Which prediction carries the greater weight?

The teacher's, and the grading should say why without flinching. Both forecasts were substantially fulfilled, but they were not symmetrical in kind. The pupil forecast the corruption of a party that never held revolutionary power; the teacher forecast the character of a regime that did, and his forecast concerned the fate of the very cause both men served.

A failed strategy wastes a movement's decades. A successful seizure that builds the predicted despotism wastes the cause's good name across a century and arms every opponent of socialism with a standing exhibit. There is a second asymmetry. The pupil's forecast of the teacher's road was, in part, a weapon in a recruitment struggle, intended to detach the teacher's followers; its fulfilment served the forecaster's organisation. The teacher's forecast was uttered with nothing to gain: it cost him his standing in the movement he had led, was vindicated in his lifetime only in ways that could give him no pleasure, and defended a class of institutions whose German custodians despised him nearly as much as the pupil did. Predictions made against interest deserve a premium in any audit, and this one was made against every interest its author had. The fourth question therefore closes decisively for the teacher, and it is the weight of this row, more than any other, that the final verdict must register.

Registered, however, is not the same as rewarded, and the distinction leads to the dispute's hardest passage. A prediction is not a programme. The teacher foresaw the destination of the pupil's road with a clarity history has ratified, and he had no road of his own that arrived anywhere. His forecast functions in the record like the warning of a lighthouse keeper whose own ship also sank, on a different reef, in the same storm. The wider argument about the roads not taken, whether gradual transformation toward socialism is possible at all and what it would require, belongs to the companion treatment of the [reform or revolution debate](/2013/02/01/reform-or-revolution-debate/), where the teacher's position is assessed among its rivals rather than against his pupil alone. Within this dispute, the fourth question's finding stands naked: on the single most consequential empirical question the two men contested, what one party rule would produce, the renegade was right and the founder was wrong, and every honest history of the century must build that finding into its foundations even when, especially when, its sympathies run the other way.

## The strongest objection from the pupil's side, stated fairly

No grading is complete until the strongest objection to its own verdict has been stated at full strength and answered without evasion. The objection, in the form the pupil's serious defenders press it, runs as follows. The teacher's correctness about Bolshevik consequences is a cheap correctness, purchased by abstention. His strategy had been tested for decades by the strongest socialist party on earth and it failed at every moment of decision: it failed to prevent the war it existed to prevent, failed to make a revolution when revolution arrived in its own country, failed to defend the republic it had midwifed, and failed finally to offer its followers any resistance worth the name when the republic's murderer came to power by the parliamentary road itself. Against that record, the pupil at least acted, broke the chain of the old order at its weakest link, and built a state that industrialised a backward country and, a generation later, broke the back of the invasion that the teacher's heirs had failed to keep from power. A verdict that hands the argument to the man of the failed abstention, the objection concludes, mistakes the ethics of prophecy for the ethics of politics, in which the worst sin is not error but irrelevance.

Parts of this objection must simply be conceded, and the verdict below concedes them in its very structure. The teacher's road did fail at the moments enumerated, and a grading that pretended the failures were accidents of personnel rather than expressions of the strategy's inner weakness would be apologetics. It is also true that prophecy is not politics, and that the teacher, offered the pupil's choices in the revolutionary autumn, had no third option in his portfolio that the situation would have sustained. Where the objection overreaches is in its final move, the conversion of the pupil's superior efficacy into a vindication of his theory. Efficacy and correctness are different properties, and the dispute's questions were questions of theory tested by events. The pupil's theory maintained that a party holding power in the class's name, through institutions from which the class could not dismiss it, would nonetheless rule as the class's organ and wither toward freedom. Events refuted the theory in the person of its own regime. That his rival's theory also failed, differently, does not repair the refutation; it compounds the tragedy, which is precisely the shape of the split verdict. The objection, fully absorbed, therefore does not overturn the grading. It explains why the grading must be split, since a century that refutes both parties to a dispute has not thereby declared the dispute unimportant. It has declared it unfinished.

One more consideration belongs to the objection's honest treatment, because it cuts against the teacher in a way his partisans rarely acknowledge. His forecast of the pupil's road derived part of its accuracy from a general law he applied impartially: unchecked power corrupts the project it serves. But the same law, applied to his own tradition, predicts the oligarchic decay of a great party embedded in a hostile state, the very decay the pupil forecast. The teacher never applied his law homeward with the same rigour. He treated the German party's bureaucratisation as a regrettable tendency to be corrected by better doctrine, while treating the Russian party's bureaucratisation as the necessary outcome of its doctrine. The asymmetry does not falsify his Russian forecast, which events confirmed on its own terms. It does mark the limit of his self knowledge, and it suggests that each man possessed a theory of institutional corruption that worked only as a weapon against the other. The reader who generalises both half theories into a single law, that organisations insulated from dismissal by their own constituents will serve themselves in the end, extracts from the dispute a lesson neither disputant could state whole, and that lesson may be the most durable thing the exchange has to teach later movements of every kind.

## Luxemburg between the two poles

Rosa Luxemburg stands in this dispute as its most valuable witness precisely because she belonged to neither camp. Before the war she had fought the teacher's centre from the left, accusing it of allowing tactical patience to harden into a refusal of action, and her writings on the mass strike supplied the movement's sharpest internal critique of attrition while attrition still reigned. She had also, years earlier, warned against features of the pupil's organisational doctrine, fearing an ultra centralism that would substitute the committee's initiative for the class's own. When the Russian revolution came, she greeted it from a prison cell with unqualified joy, defended the Bolsheviks against the teacher's criticisms on the question of the revolution's right to exist, and then, in notes published only after her murder, delivered the most famous sentence ever written about the regime's democratic deficit: that freedom reserved for the supporters of the government is no freedom at all, because freedom is always the freedom of those who think differently. The sentence, under fifteen words in its usual rendering and endlessly quoted since, carries its authority from its author's credentials. No renegade wrote it. A revolutionary whom the teacher's party's government had failed to protect from the assassins wrote it, about a regime she had defended against the teacher, in the last months of her life.

Her position in the grading is therefore not a compromise between the two men's but an independent finding that happens to ratify parts of each. With the pupil, against the teacher, she held that the revolution was justified, that the Bolsheviks had earned imperishable credit for daring it, and that the teacher's ledger of the regime's sins omitted the civil war, the blockade, and the German revolution's failure to relieve the siege. With the teacher, against the pupil, she held that the suppression of political freedom was not a regrettable blemish on the revolution but a mortal danger to it, because a public life extinguished at the start would not revive at the convenience of the extinguisher, and the soviets themselves would atrophy into shells once the life around them died. Her notes predict, in effect, the teacher's mechanism operating inside the revolution she loved. She was murdered by the forces the teacher's strategy had unleashed to defend order, weeks after the armistice, and her double location, killed by one side's failure and vindicated by the other's record, makes her the dispute's embodied refutation of every simple verdict. Any grading that cannot accommodate her testimony is grading a smaller dispute than the one that occurred.

## The documentary record and the historians

The pamphlets were written in the heat of events their authors could observe only partially, and a modern grading has resources neither man possessed: the opened archives of the regime, the memoir literature of participants on every side, and a century of scholarship that has quarrelled over the evidence with a freedom the original disputants would have envied. What has that scholarship done to the dispute? In broad terms it has hardened the factual floor beneath the teacher's prediction while softening the moral simplicity with which both traditions once told the story. The sequence of suppressions is no longer seriously disputed as a sequence: the dispersal of the Assembly, the exclusion and then the criminalisation of rival socialist parties, the Red Terror decreed in the civil war's emergency and institutionalised beyond it, the crushing of Kronstadt, the faction ban, the subordination of the trade unions and the soviets to the party apparatus, and the eventual succession struggle resolved by the apparatus in favour of the candidate of the apparatus. Richard Pipes and Robert Conquest, from one interpretive pole, and Moshe Lewin, Stephen Cohen, and Sheila Fitzpatrick, from positions far more sympathetic to the revolution's social aims, differ profoundly over causation and over what might have been. They do not differ over the fact that by the end of the pupil's life the regime's political pluralism, including pluralism inside its own party, had been extinguished, nor over the fact that the pupil himself, in his final writings, recognised a bureaucratic deformation he struggled to name and could not reverse.

That final struggle of the pupil's deserves careful statement, because it is the point where the record touches the dispute most intimately. In his last dictated articles and in the testament his widow preserved, the founder of the regime warned against the concentration of unlimited authority in the hands of his successor as general secretary, fretted over the swelling of an administrative apparatus he described as borrowed from the old regime and only lightly repainted, and proposed remedies, enlarging committees, reorganising inspectorates, that historians across the spectrum have judged unequal to the machinery already built. Lewin's account of these months gives the episode its proper pathos: the author of the renegade pamphlet discovering, from inside his own creation, the bureaucratic logic his renegade had predicted from outside, and reaching for institutional antidotes his own theory had taught him to despise as formalism. The scene does not vindicate every line of the teacher's pamphlet, and the teacher, had he witnessed it, would have been wrong to savour it. It does something more important for the grading. It demonstrates that the prediction's mechanism was not an artefact of the civil war's emergency, since the founder himself confronted the mechanism in the war's aftermath and found his instruments too weak for it. Emergencies explain why doors are opened. They do not explain why no one afterwards can close them, and the explanation the teacher offered in advance, that an apparatus staffed by its own beneficiaries will defend its jurisdiction against any founder's afterthought, remains the most economical account of the founder's defeat.

Scholarship has been kinder to the pupil on the dispute's other flank. The study of the German party, in works by historians such as Carl Schorske and in later reassessments of the teacher himself by scholars recovering him from his pupil's caricature, has confirmed the structural reading of attrition's failure: a party whose daily life was woven into the fabric of the existing order acquired too much to lose, and its theorist's late writings show him perceiving the trap without finding the exit. The recovered teacher of this scholarship is a more formidable figure than the renegade of legend, a thinker whose analyses of imperialism, of the state, and of democracy's relation to socialism repay reading on their merits. Yet recovery has its limits, and the same scholarship records the melancholy of his final decades: the honoured elder of a movement in exile, revising his history of socialism while the continent his strategy was designed to conquer fell to the movements his strategy had failed to stop. The archive, in short, has graded the dispute much as this article grades it, and the convergence is worth noting without exaggeration. Historians working from documents neither disputant saw have arrived, by independent routes, at findings that distribute the honours and the failures in much the pattern the split verdict describes. Convergence of that kind is not proof, but it is the kind of corroboration a verdict hopes for.

### What evidence would have changed the grading?

Specifying the evidence that would reverse a verdict is the discipline that separates a finding from a loyalty, so the article states it plainly. Had the Soviet regime, after its civil war, restored legal existence to the rival socialist parties, permitted contested elections to the soviets, and allowed factions within its own party to organise openly, the teacher's central prediction would stand refuted and the pupil's account of emergency coercion as a temporary shell would stand vindicated. Had the German party, at any of its moments of supremacy, used its majority to begin the transformation its programme promised and been defeated only by superior force, the pupil's diagnosis of attrition as institutional self preservation would stand weakened and the teacher's road would appear untried rather than unviable. Neither body of evidence exists. The regime moved in the opposite direction at every relaxation point, and the party, at its supreme moment, chose the alliance with the old order's sword over the risks of its own programme. A grading is only as honest as its willingness to imagine its own refutation, and this verdict survives the exercise because the refuting evidence is not merely absent but contrary.

There remains the question of the two men's reputations, which the grading has deliberately kept out of the scales and which a closing word can now address. The pupil's reputation in the century after his death was carried by states; the teacher's was buried by them, including by the states that claimed the pupil's mantle and by the parties that claimed the teacher's and preferred not to remember what he had predicted about their sister regime. Reputation distributed by states is reputation distributed by power, and it inverts the grading at almost every point, since the teacher's eclipse was total precisely where his prediction was most accurate. The recovery of his work by scholars, and the slow re reading of the exchange in the terms this article has used, belongs to the period after those states weakened or fell. None of that makes him a prophet to be worshipped in the pupil's vacated shrine. It makes the exchange what it always was beneath the legend: two serious theorists, formed in the same school, divided by a war, arguing about power with the whole of a shared canon at their backs, and leaving behind a record from which a careful reader can still learn how organisations behave when they cannot be dismissed.

## The split verdict stated and defended

The findings of the four questions can now be gathered into the verdict the article promised at its opening, and gathered in a form compact enough to be quoted whole. On the character of the Russian revolution, the pupil read the outbreak more acutely and the teacher read the predicament more truly. On democracy and the rule of the working class, the pupil's critique of formal democracy under concentrated property stands as a permanent contribution, while the teacher's insistence that a class rules only where it can dismiss its rulers states the condition the pupil's regime abolished and could not replace. On the parliamentary road, the pupil foresaw that power would not change hands in a chamber, and the teacher's own party proved him right by the use it made of its majority. On prediction, the teacher's forecast of one party rule's consequences was substantially borne out in mechanism and in outcome, and the pupil's forecast of the teacher's party was likewise borne out, at a lower order of consequence. The sum of the rows is the split verdict: one side won the argument about consequences, neither side won the argument about how a socialist transformation could actually be achieved, and the exchange as a whole establishes what will not work without establishing what will.

Three misunderstandings of that verdict should be forestalled. It is not a verdict of equivalence. The rows are weighted, and the heaviest row, the prediction about a regime that held power over hundreds of millions and shaped the century's imagination of socialism, lies on the teacher's side; a reader who treats the split as a draw has not attended to the stakes of the questions. Nor is the verdict a counsel of despair about the questions themselves. To learn that two roads, the seizure of power by an undismissable party and the accumulation of a majority by an unadventurous one, both fail to reach their stated destination is a large and usable piece of knowledge, the kind on which better questions are built. Nor, finally, is the verdict a retrospective luxury available only to those who know the ending. Every element of it was stated at the time, by the participants or by witnesses like Luxemburg working from the same evidence, and the historian's advantage over the disputants lies not in superior wisdom but in the brute fact of the record. The reader who absorbs the verdict should therefore resist the comfortable thought that the errors were obvious and the alternatives clear. They were clear to a handful of contemporaries at ruinous personal cost, and the cost is part of the lesson.

### How does the verdict distribute its grades between the roads?

It distributes them unevenly and by question, never as a single crown. The pupil's road is graded down for delivering an apparatus instead of the class rule it promised; the teacher's road is graded down for never delivering power to anyone; and the heaviest single grade, on prediction, is entered in the teacher's favour.

By refusing to grade them as a pair. Each road is measured against its own stated destination: the pupil's road promised the rule of the working class and the withering of coercion, and delivered the rule of a party apparatus and the hypertrophy of coercion; the teacher's road promised a majority class assuming power through its accumulated strength, and delivered a party that used supremacy to preserve the order it existed to transform. Judged against their own promises, both roads failed, and the failure of one cannot be entered as a success for the other. That is the whole of the split verdict, and its austerity is its claim on the reader: it offers no surviving champion to follow, only two sets of instructions, tested to destruction, about the behaviour of parties and states. Movements that come after inherit the questions without the answers, which is an uncomfortable bequest and, the article submits, an accurate one.

The verdict also carries an implication for how the dispute should be taught, and the persona sections below develop it for their several audiences. Taught as a morality play, with the parts pre assigned, the exchange trains students to inherit allegiances. Taught as the grid presents it, the exchange trains the skill the disputants themselves conspicuously possessed and their followers conspicuously lost: the skill of stating an opponent's case in a form the opponent would accept, and of specifying the evidence that would change one's own mind. The teacher, whatever his failures as a strategist, never stopped being able to state the pupil's case; his pamphlets quote it fairly before they answer it. The pupil, in his reply, could state the teacher's case only as a symptom. There is a lesson in that asymmetry too, subtler than any row of the grid, about what the possession of power does to the quality of argument. The man answering from a study could afford to understand his enemy. The man answering from a besieged government could not, and the pamphlet that made his reputation as a controversialist is, sentence by sentence, the record of a great mind declining the labour of understanding, because understanding had become a luxury his situation taxed beyond bearing. Later readers, taxed by nothing but their own attention, have no such excuse.

## The war of the Internationals

The pamphlet war acquired institutions. Within two years of the revolution the pupil's party had founded a new International whose conditions of admission read like the renegade pamphlet converted into a checklist: applicant parties were required to break with their own reformists and centrists, to remove them from all responsible posts, to combine legal with illegal work, and to accept the authority of an executive seated in the victor's capital. The teacher's name headed the list of the excommunicated, since the centrists the conditions targeted were, in the new vocabulary, his disciples. On the other side, the surviving parties of the old International regrouped, with a short lived intermediate formation in which the teacher played a leading part attempting to hold a centre between the two camps. The map of world socialism for the rest of the century was drawn in those founding acts: a communist movement defined by its allegiance to the pupil's state and its repudiation of the teacher, and a social democratic movement defined by its repudiation of the pupil's state and its embarrassment at the teacher, whose predictions about that state it privately believed and publicly soft pedalled.

Understanding the conditions of admission illuminates the dispute's transformation from an argument into a boundary. Arguments can be revisited; boundaries must be policed. A young militant joining either International in the years after the war did not encounter the exchange of pamphlets as a debate to be weighed but as a frontier to be located on, and the texts were issued in editions whose introductions instructed the reader in the verdict before the evidence began. The teacher's pamphlet circulated in the communist world, where it circulated at all, as a specimen of renegacy; the pupil's reply circulated in the social democratic world chiefly as a specimen of the brutality it opposed. Each camp thus preserved the text that incriminated the other and neglected the text that incriminated itself. The documentary recovery described in the preceding section had to wait for the weakening of both camps, and the long delay is itself evidence for the dispute's central theme. Organisations insulated from correction preserve their founding verdicts the way the regimes of the grid preserve their founding suppressions: not because the verdicts remain persuasive, but because reopening them would indict the custodians.

The personal ends of the two men measure the distance between their roads more starkly than any text. The pupil died in the seventh year of his regime, after a series of strokes, dictating warnings against the successor his apparatus was preparing, his testament suppressed by the men it warned against. The teacher outlived him by fourteen years, dying in exile in Amsterdam as the continent moved toward the second great war his failures had not prevented, honoured by a shrinking circle, his books still in print, his strategy in ruins, his prediction being enacted daily by the state his pupil had founded and by the successor he had not lived to see at its height. Neither deathbed offers a moral. Together they offer a measurement: the man of action was consumed by his instrument within a decade, and the man of doctrine survived his doctrine's collapse by decades, and survival, in both cases, was the only victory on offer. The dispute's afterlife in the two Internationals repeated the pattern at organisational scale, each tradition preserving the half of the inheritance it could use and interring the half that judged it.

### Why did the conditions of admission name the teacher's followers?

Because the new International was built by secession, and secession requires a criterion that sorts the committed from the hesitant in parties the founders did not control. Naming the teacher's tendency as the obstacle performed that sorting with maximum clarity: his was the position a wavering party would adopt if it wished to applaud the Russian revolution without accepting its discipline, and the founders had decided that applause without discipline was the one commodity they would not import. The condition thus told every applicant exactly which of its own members had to be expelled as the price of entry. That the price was paid, in split after split across Europe, shows how far the authority of the pupil's victory extended. That the teacher's tendency survived the excommunication, in parties that outlasted the new International itself, shows the limit of verdicts enforced by boundary rather than sustained by argument.

The intermediate formation the teacher helped to lead, the union of parties that sought a path between the two Internationals, deserves a sentence of fairness in a story that has mostly mocked it as the institutional form of indecision. Its existence testified that thousands of socialists who rejected the pupil's terms also rejected their own leaders' complacency, and its collapse, squeezed between the two armed camps of doctrine, demonstrated the structural truth the dispute had already established in print: the centre in a polarised argument is not a position but a passage, and parties that occupy it are in transit to one pole or the other. The teacher's personal trajectory in those years, from arbiter of a world movement to elder of a shrinking centre to exile, recapitulates the passage. He did not move; the ground did, and a strategy premised on the ground holding still had no answer to its motion. His pupil's strategy, premised on the ground's motion, had an answer for everything except the morning after victory, when the ground stopped moving and the apparatus built for motion began, inexorably, to govern.

## The afterlife of the quarrel in the two traditions

Each tradition spent the following century managing its founder's defeat at the other's hands. The pupil's tradition managed the teacher's vindicated prediction by reclassifying it: the hardenings of the regime were attributed to the encirclement, the backwardness, the civil war's inheritance, and eventually to the personal deformations of the successor, so that the theory the pupil had bequeathed could be acquitted by convicting the circumstances and the heir. The management was not wholly dishonest, since circumstances and heirs are real causes, and the best historians of that tradition, Isaac Deutscher pre eminent among them, wrote its sorrows with an honesty that approached the teacher's conclusions by a longer road. But the reclassification had a telltale property: it could explain any outcome, including outcomes opposite to those it was devised for, and a theory that explains everything its regime does has quietly exchanged the status of a prediction for the status of a liturgy. The teacher's pamphlet had warned of exactly this exchange, in the passage where he argued that a doctrine held by an undismissable party becomes whatever the party needs it to be.

The teacher's tradition managed its founder's defeat by amnesia of a complementary kind. His parties, governing within capitalism and increasingly proud of the governance, struck his revolutionary goal from their programmes and his name from their pantheons, keeping his democratic objection to the pupil's regime as a convenient stick while burying the doctrine from which the objection drew its force. The objection, severed from the doctrine, decayed into a general preference for democracy that any liberal could sign, and the teacher's specific claim, that democracy is the form of the working class's self rule and perishes when the class is ruled in its name, lost its address. When dissidents inside the pupil's own world later revived that claim, in the workers' oppositions and Charter movements and self management experiments of the century's second half, they typically arrived at it without knowing its author's name, or knowing the name only as the pupil's pamphlet had branded it. There is no cleaner illustration of the renegade pamphlet's success as persuasion: it governed the reading even of those who were living its target's vindication.

A grading that aspires to usefulness must finally say what the dispute offers readers who belong to neither tradition, since most readers of this article belong to neither. It offers, first, a pair of case studies in institutional behaviour whose laws operate far beyond the socialist movement: the party that cannot be dismissed becomes its own constituency, and the party that will not risk its assets becomes their custodian. Organisations of every allegiance, including those devoted to causes the disputants would have despised, exhibit both pathologies, and the pamphlets remain among the sharpest anatomies of each ever written by a participant. It offers, second, a discipline for reading political prophecy: ask of every forecast what would refute it, who profits if it is believed, and whether its author paid for making it. By those tests the teacher's pamphlet scores unusually high and the pupil's polemical method unusually low, whatever one's sympathies. It offers, third, a warning about vocabulary. The words democracy, dictatorship, renegade, and party did more work in this dispute than any army the disputants commanded, and each word arrived at the quarrel pre loaded by its user's theory. The reader who learns to unload such words, to ask what institution would count as democracy's presence and what event would count as its absence, has acquired the skill the whole exchange exists to teach.

## Reading the two men now without inheriting the quarrel

Approach the texts in the order the quarrel produced them, and the exchange becomes legible as an exchange rather than as two monuments. Begin with the teacher's earlier writings on the party and the road to power, not because they are the dispute's subject but because they are its premise: a reader who has not seen the doctrine whole cannot tell what the pupil learned from it or what the teacher believed he was defending. Then read the two dictatorship pamphlets as a pair, the teacher's first and the pupil's reply second, resisting the reply's instruction to read its target as a fallen man. Read the teacher's longer sequel last among the principals, and Luxemburg's notes on the Russian revolution beside it, since her testimony corrects both principals from a position neither could occupy. The scholarly literature can then be entered through the historians named in these sections, whose disagreements with one another are as instructive as their findings. A reader who follows that order will finish with a rarer possession than an opinion about who won: a map of the argument's moving parts, each labelled with the evidence that bears on it.

Two habits of reading should be discarded at the threshold. The first is quotation mining, the practice, industrialised by both traditions, of extracting the sentence that damns the other side and discarding the surrounding argument; the dispute's texts are short enough to read whole, and their arguments live in their structures rather than in their sentences. The second is the moralisation of strategy, the habit of treating attrition as cowardice and overthrow as courage, or the reverse, as though the disputants' choices revealed their characters rather than their situations. The teacher was not a timid man; he had defied his own party's right wing for decades and ended in exile rather than accommodation. The pupil was not a reckless man; his career is a study in patience, preparation, and the refusal of premature action, until the single season in which he judged the moment ripe. Each man's strategy expressed his formation more than his temperament, and readers who grasp that will find the dispute speaking to their own moment, in which organisations of every kind must still decide what to accumulate, what to risk, and what they will do on the morning after they win.

### Is the teacher's own prose worth the effort of reading?

Yes, with a qualification about which prose. His narrative and analytical writings, the programme commentary, the study of the forerunners of socialism, the wartime and postwar pamphlets at the centre of this article, are models of lucid exposition, built to be understood by the self educated readers of his movement, and they reward attention with a clarity few theorists match. His more systematic treatises can be dry in the manner of the textbook tradition he helped to found, and readers pressed for time should begin with the polemics, where the doctrine is under pressure and therefore most visible. The qualification matters because his reputation as a dullard was part of his opponent's construction: a renegade is easier to bury if he bored his readers first. The record shows a writer whose books taught a class to think about its own condition, and the survival of his central prediction suggests that the thinking, whatever its stylistic register, made contact with the real machinery of power.

## The economics beneath the quarrel: ultra-imperialism and the weakest link

Beneath the dispute about parties and parliaments ran a dispute about capitalism itself, and no account of the quarrel is complete without it, because each man's strategy was the political expression of his economics. The teacher's mature economic writings treated imperialism as a policy rather than a destiny. Finance capital, in his analysis, drove the annexations, armaments, and colonial rivalries of the age, but the same concentration that produced those evils also produced their possible antidote: the great capitals, exhausted by the costs of rivalry, might learn to exploit the world jointly, in a phase he named ultra-imperialism, in which competition among states subsided into cartelised cooperation. The political corollary was immense. If the age of catastrophic wars could end by capital's own maturation, then the movement's task remained the accumulation of strength within a peaceable, developing order, and the strategy of attrition retained its premise, a future. The pupil read the same facts and drew the opposite corollary. Imperialism in his theory was not a policy that capital could renounce but the structural form of capitalism in its monopoly stage, uneven in development by its nature, so that war among the great powers was not an accident of diplomacy but the periodic redivision of an already divided world. On that reading the future attrition required was cancelled in advance, the chain of world capitalism would snap at its weakest link rather than ripen uniformly, and the movement's task was preparation for rupture.

Grade this economic sub dispute with the same care as the political one, because it exhibits the pattern of the whole in miniature. The teacher's mechanism was not foolish: the century did eventually produce long periods in which the great capitalist powers cooperated through shared institutions rather than fighting, and a reader of his ultra-imperialism pages can find there an uncanny sketch of a world of integrated finance policed by agreement among its centres. The pupil's counter mechanism was vindicated first and bloodily: the uneven development he theorised did produce the war, the war did break the chain in backward Russia rather than advanced Germany, and the second great war repeated the lesson before the cooperative phase arrived. Sequence matters in grading predictions, and the pupil's economics read the sequence's first half, the half both men lived through, with greater accuracy. Yet the teacher's error had a characteristic shape worth naming. He treated a possibility internal to his system as a tendency, because his strategy needed it to be one, and the wish fathered the analysis. The pupil's corresponding error was symmetrical: he treated the cooperative phase as impossible in principle, because his strategy needed rupture to be permanent, and his successors were left without a theory when the rupture failed to arrive on schedule and the capitalist centres learned, for generations, to compose their quarrels. Each man's economics, in short, was his politics wearing the dress of science, and the dispute teaches its readers to interrogate any political economy, including the ones they favour, for the strategy it is quietly underwriting.

The weakest link doctrine deserves separate attention because it was the theoretical bridge between the pupil's economics and his seizure of power, and the teacher attacked precisely that bridge. If revolutions occur not where capitalism is most developed but where its chain is most strained, then the revolution's social content cannot be read off from the country's level of development, and the teacher's stage theory loses its veto over backward country revolutions. The pupil's bridge carried that traffic brilliantly for the outbreak: it explained why the revolution came in Petrograd rather than Berlin, and why a party that understood the strain could act on it. What the bridge could not carry was the construction traffic in the other direction: the materials for socialism, an educated and organised working class constituting the social majority, a productive apparatus capable of social administration, could not be imported across it quickly enough, and the ally whose industry was supposed to supply them, the German working class, declined the role. The teacher's veto, theoretically refuted for the seizure, reasserted itself over the sequel with the force of material fact. Here the split pattern of the whole dispute appears in its purest form: a doctrine vindicated as an account of how the old order falls, and silent or wrong as an account of what can be built on the site, against a doctrine that forbade the fall and was obeyed by history in everything except the falling.

## Violence, terror, and the ethics of means

The teacher's sequel pamphlet placed the methods of the new regime at the centre of the argument, and any honest treatment of the dispute must follow him there, reporting the record as the historians have established it and keeping the voices of condemnation and defence distinct from the evidence. The facts, in their spare form, are these. Within weeks of taking power the regime created a political police with powers of summary judgement. In the civil war's first autumn, after attempts on the lives of its leaders, it proclaimed a Red Terror as official policy, and hostages were shot in batches announced in the press. The terror's toll is contested in its figures, and this article, following its rule on contested numbers, asserts none; historians of the period agree on the order of the phenomenon, a campaign of exemplary killing numbering its victims at minimum in the many thousands, directed not only at armed enemies but at categories, former officers, former officials, speculators, hostages taken for the acts of others. The regime's defenders, beginning with the pupil himself, answered with the White terror that preceded and accompanied it, with the civil war's invasion and blockade, and with the argument that a revolution which declines to terrorise will be terrorised out of existence. The teacher's answer rejected the arithmetic of reprisal at its root. Terror, he argued, is not a quantity of violence to be balanced against the enemy's quantity. It is a mode of rule that selects its own personnel, corrupts its own information, and becomes the normal instrument of a government that has abolished every other means of resolving conflict. A regime may win a civil war by terror and discover that it has trained itself, in the winning, to govern by nothing else.

The pupil's theoretical defence of terror was of a piece with his definition of dictatorship, and it has a candour his critics should acknowledge before they refute it. Every state, he held, rests on force; the liberal horror of revolutionary force is selective, indulgent toward the standing violence of property, conscription, and empire; and a class war does not cease to be a war because one side fights it with manifestos. The teacher's rejoinder granted the premise and attacked the inference. That all states rest on force does not entail that all uses of force are equivalent in their consequences for the user, and a Marxism that cannot distinguish the force that defends a majority's institutions from the terror that substitutes for a majority has surrendered its analytical soul to a pun. The grading of this exchange follows the now familiar pattern. On the exposure of liberal selectivity, the pupil's point stands and has permanently armed critics of respectable violence. On the trajectory of terror as an institution, the teacher's mechanism, terror recruiting its specialists, abolishing the feedback that might restrain it, and migrating from the regime's enemies to its own ranks, describes the subsequent record, through the faction ban's enforcement to the great purges that consumed the pupil's own comrades, with a fidelity that no rival account from within the dispute approaches. The purges are the dispute's final, ghastly exhibit: the apparatus the teacher described in the civil war's second year, turning, in its maturity, upon the generation that built it, while the teacher, in exile, watched his pamphlet's logic complete itself in the show trials of men he had debated.

### What separates emergency coercion from terror as a system?

The teacher's writings supply the test, and it remains the clearest available. Emergency coercion is specific in its targets, answerable to an authority outside itself, and dated: it expires with the emergency and its agents return to civil life. Terror as a system is categorical in its targets, answerable only upward within its own apparatus, and undated: its agents become a profession and its categories expand to fill the profession's needs. Apply the test to the record and the regime crosses the line early, in the hostage decrees and the police's summary powers, years before the civil war's outcome was secure and decades before the purges. The test's value lies in its independence from sympathy: a reader who believes the revolution was justified in its entirety can still apply it, and must still record where the line was crossed. That is the kind of instrument the dispute at its best produced, and producing such instruments, usable against one's own side, may be the highest service a polemic can perform.

There is a literary dimension to the means question that historians note and graders should not ignore. The pupil wrote about terror the way he wrote about everything, as an instrument to be assessed by results, and his prose on the subject has the chill of a staff memorandum. The teacher wrote about it as a corruption of the socialist idea itself, and his prose warms with a moral passion his systematic works rarely permit themselves. Posterity has tended to trust the warmer prose on this subject and the colder on others, and the distribution of trust is not irrational: the man who experiences terror as a technique will reliably underestimate its institutional appetite, while the man who experiences it as a fall from the idea will reliably overestimate the idea's power to restrain fallible men. Both styles of error are on display across the dispute as a whole, and the reader who learns to correct for an author's characteristic error, discounting the technician's optimism about his instruments and the moralist's optimism about his principles, possesses a hermeneutic that travels well beyond this quarrel.

## The German revolution as the dispute's second laboratory

Russia tested the pupil's road; Germany, in the same years, tested the teacher's, and a grading that examines only the first laboratory rigs the experiment. The German sequence rewards close narration. A naval mutiny and a collapsing front brought down the empire in a matter of days, workers' and soldiers' councils sprang up on the Russian model, and the teacher's party found itself, by the logic of its accumulated strength, holding the government of a revolution it had not made. Its leaders made, in the revolution's first weeks, the choices that defined their tradition's fate. They allied with the old officer corps to preserve order, scheduled elections to a national assembly on the fastest possible timetable, channelled the councils toward their own dissolution, and, when the radical left rose in the capital, licensed the irregular forces that crushed it and murdered its leaders. Each choice was defended in the teacher's vocabulary: avoid premature confrontation, secure the majority, let the assembly decide. Each choice also served, whatever its intention, to preserve intact the judiciary, the bureaucracy, the officer corps, and the industrial ownership of the old order, so that the republic the assembly founded was staffed at every level by men who despised it. The teacher, to his credit and his sorrow, criticised his own party's course from the left in those months, joined the independent party that had split over the war, and found himself once again between the camps: too revolutionary for the government his strategy had produced, too cautious for the revolution his doctrine had prophesied.

Apply the grid to the German laboratory and the rows illuminate one another. The strategy row records that attrition's accumulated strength, at the moment of transfer, was spent on stabilisation rather than transformation, exactly as the pupil's forecast of institutional self preservation predicted. The democracy row records a finding in the teacher's favour of an unexpected kind: the assembly elections he had championed were held, were free, and did express the population's will, which ran against the revolutionary left and for an orderly republic; the German majority, offered the choice the Russian majority was denied, declined the soviet road. That finding complicates the pupil's theory at its foundation, since it suggests that the Constituent Assembly in Russia, had it been permitted to govern, might have presided over a comparable consolidation rather than over socialist transformation, and that the choice the pupil faced was not between his road and the teacher's but between his road and an order in which his party would be a tolerated minority. The complication does not acquit the dispersal; a majority's right to decline a road is the substance of the teacher's democratic objection, and declining is what majorities are for. It does acquit the grading of simplicity. The German laboratory shows the teacher's road producing the republic that reaction later strangled; the Russian laboratory shows the pupil's road producing the apparatus that strangled the revolution. A century that required one clean lesson from the two experiments will find none, which is why the laboratories must be taught together or not honestly at all.

The murders of Luxemburg and Liebknecht stand at the junction of the two laboratories, and the dispute's grading cannot pass them in silence. They were killed by soldiers operating under the authority of a government of the teacher's party, in a crackdown that government licensed and whose perpetrators it largely shielded. The teacher had spent years polemicising against Luxemburg's left; he had also shared with her the old movement's world, and her death at the hands of his own tradition's order keeping was the personal form of his strategy's catastrophe. No statement of his from that winter suggests he mistook the event for anything but a horror. Yet the structural point stands beyond anyone's horror: a party practising attrition, handed power by collapse, required an instrument of order, found the old order's instrument ready to hand, and discovered that the instrument set its own fee, payable in the blood of the revolution's left and in the republic's eventual defencelessness before the same instrument's political masters. The pupil's pamphlets could not have designed a grimmer confirmation of his wartime sociology. Nor could the teacher's defenders design a sadder one for his: the majority the party had accumulated, whose will the assembly expressed, did not rise to defend either the murdered leaders or, in the end, the republic itself. Accumulation had assembled numbers. It had not assembled the disposition to act, and the distinction between those two achievements is the epitaph of the strategy of attrition.

## The peasantry and the countryside neither man theorised well

A shared blind spot deserves a section, because the grading of a dispute should record what both parties missed as carefully as what divided them. Both men were theorists of an industrial working class in a world that remained overwhelmingly peasant, and each handled the countryside as an auxiliary to a drama staged in the cities. The teacher's agrarian writings, among his most careful, concluded that small peasant production was doomed by economic development and that the party should neither hasten nor mourn its passing, a position of analytical patience that offered the peasantry nothing to join. The pupil, to his lasting advantage in the struggle for power, treated the peasantry as an ally to be armed with a programme, land to those who till it, and rode that alliance through the revolution's first year; but his theory regarded the ally as a petty bourgeois force whose interests would collide with the workers' state once the landlords were gone, and the collision duly arrived, in the grain requisitions, the peasant risings of the civil war's end, and the strategic retreat of the New Economic Policy that the pupil, to his credit, forced through against his own party's instincts. The teacher, watching from exile, treated the retreat as a confession that the stage theory had been right all along. It was at least equally a confession that neither man's theory of the countryside could govern a peasant country, and the forced collectivisation that followed the pupil's death, with its catastrophic famine documented by historians including Conquest and, in the Ukrainian case, Anne Applebaum, was the attempt of the apparatus to solve by terror a question that theory had left open.

The blind spot matters to the verdict because it qualifies both men's authority on the dispute's central questions. The teacher's democracy was, in the Russia that actually existed, the democracy of a peasant majority whose expressed will, in the Assembly elections, favoured parties neither disputant led; his pamphlet invoked that majority against the pupil without ever explaining how its will could be squared with the socialist transformation both men wanted, since the majority's programme was land and peace, not socialisation. The pupil's dictatorship, conversely, claimed the mandate of a class that constituted a small minority of the population and ruled the peasant majority by a mixture of alliance, requisition, and concession that his theory described as temporary and his regime experienced as permanent. Each man's democratic arithmetic worked only in the country of the other's problem: the teacher's majority existed in Germany, where it declined the transformation, and the pupil's revolutionary class existed as a majority nowhere. The dispute's deepest irony is geographical. Two strategies, each plausible in the setting that formed it, were universalised by their authors into laws of history, and the century punished the universalisation impartially.

### Why does the alliance with the peasantry complicate both cases?

Because it exposes the arithmetic each man preferred to leave in shadow. The teacher's case against the pupil rests on the majority the Bolsheviks lacked, yet that majority, the peasantry, wanted private land and an end to requisitions, a programme incompatible with the socialism in whose name the teacher invoked it.

The pupil's case rests on the proletarian character of his state, yet his state survived its first years by concessions to peasant production that his theory classified as retreat, and ruled the countryside thereafter by instruments his theory classified as temporary. Both men were, in the countryside, improvising on a stage their theories had furnished for a different play, and the grading that notices the improvisation will trust both men's universal claims a little less, which is the correct adjustment.

## The theory of the transition and the state that refused to wither

Between the wartime collapse and the October seizure the pupil wrote his most systematic book, an unfinished treatise on the state, and its argument forms the theoretical arch under which the whole dispute travels. Recovering Marx and Engels against their social democratic editors, the treatise insisted that the state is not a neutral arbiter standing above society but a machine of class coercion; that the bourgeois machine, its bureaucracy and standing army, cannot be taken over intact but must be broken; that the Commune had shown the form of its replacement, officials elected, recallable, and paid at workmen's wages, armed militia in place of a standing force; and that this replacement state, the dictatorship of the proletariat, was already a state in dissolution, since the majority suppressing a minority of exploiters needs no elaborate machinery and can administer society through the armed people themselves until coercion, its object gone, withers away. The treatise broke off as its author hurried back to the revolution, with a famous postscript remarking that it was more pleasant to make the revolution than to write about it. The teacher read the published fragment as the revolution's philosophical charter and answered it in the pamphlets, and the exchange over the state is, for theorists, the dispute's most concentrated essence: a state designed to dissolve, confronted by a critic who predicted it would congeal.

The teacher's answer to the theory of the withering state proceeded from an observation about personnel. A state is not a blueprint; it is the people who staff it, and the people available to staff a new state in a ruined country are officials trained by the old one, specialists the revolution cannot dismiss, and party cadres whose qualification is loyalty. Set such people to administer scarcity, give them the power to allocate housing, rations, postings, and exemptions, and the blueprint's provisions about recall and wages will be interpreted by the very interests they were drafted to restrain. The critic did not deny that the Commune's forms were admirable. He denied that forms govern the men who operate them when the men hold a monopoly of organised force and the forms' enforcers are the men themselves. Withering, he argued, is not a tendency of states but a hope projected onto them, and no state in recorded history had yet volunteered the demonstration. The pupil's treatise had answered the objection in advance, in its way: the majority's own armed organisation leaves no separate caste to congeal. The regime's practice supplied the empirical footnote within three years, as a standing army with restored ranks and a political police with its own budget replaced the militia sketches of the treatise, and the treatise's author, in his final writings, confronted the apparatus his blueprint had failed to prevent with the bewilderment Lewin's biography records.

Grade the exchange and the familiar distribution reappears with a theoretical refinement worth stating. As an account of the bourgeois state, the pupil's treatise permanently changed the terms of analysis; no serious theory of the state since has been able to treat the machine as a neutral arbiter without answering him. As an account of the post revolutionary state, the treatise's central mechanism was asserted rather than demonstrated, and the teacher's personnel objection identified exactly the joint at which assertion substituted for mechanism: the treatise never explains who dissolves the dissolvers, who recalls the recallers when the recallers control the agenda, the press, and the guard. The history of political theory offers few cleaner instances of a critic locating a theory's load bearing gap. It also offers few sadder instances of the location being ignored, since the tradition that canonised the treatise canonised it as a manual of seizure, reading its chapters on breaking the old machine as instructions and its chapters on the new machine's dissolution as decoration. The teacher read the treatise whole. His reward was to be read, by that tradition, not at all.

### What did the Commune mean to each man?

To the pupil the Commune was a design: proof that a working class government could dispense with the parliamentary separation of powers, govern through delegates under constant recall, and begin the state's dissolution in the act of founding it. He studied its decrees the way an engineer studies a prototype, noting where the design had failed for want of nerve, above all in its reluctance to march on its enemies and seize the bank. To the teacher the Commune was a demonstration of the opposite lesson: a heroic municipal government, elected by universal suffrage and scrupulously democratic in its forms, whose fate proved what happened to revolutions that remained islands in a hostile country. Both men claimed Marx's blessing on the Commune as their warrant, and Marx's own account, rapturous about its forms and silent about a party's monopoly, furnished each with the passages he needed, which is why the article's discipline refuses the question of the truer inheritance and grades the predictions the inheritance was used to make.

A word should be said about the treatise's unfinished state, because it shaped the dispute's documentary balance. The pupil never wrote the promised second part, in which the experience of the Russian revolutions was to be assessed against the theory, and the assessment the teacher wrote in his pamphlets therefore stands as the only full length evaluation of the transition theory by a contemporary of the first rank. The pupil's silence was circumstantial rather than evasive; governments and civil wars consume the hours that treatises require. But the consequence for the record is real. The theory of the withering state was never revised by its author in light of the state he actually built, apart from the fragments of alarm in his final months, and its defence fell to successors whose interest lay in harmonising blueprint and building rather than in measuring the distance between them. The teacher's pamphlets, written against the blueprint while the building rose, preserve the measurements taken at the time. Whatever a reader concludes about the verdict, the measurements are primary evidence, and their author earned the right to take them by the simplest of qualifications: he was watching the building, not the drawing, when nearly everyone else in his tradition was still admiring the drawing or denouncing it unread.

## Style, rhetoric, and the manufacture of a renegade

A dispute conducted in print is also a contest of styles, and the styles of this one did political work that outlasted the arguments. The teacher wrote in the manner he had perfected across forty years of textbooks: periodic sentences, marshalled evidence, a tone of magisterial patience that assumed the reader's intelligence and the argument's sufficiency. The manner carried authority in the world that formed it, the world of party schools and theoretical journals, where reputations were made by exposition. The pupil wrote in the manner of a man addressing a meeting in a garrison town: short blows, repeated epithets, rhetorical questions answered by the asking, long quotations from the opponent hung like evidence at a trial, and a vocabulary of contempt, renegade, lackey, philistine, sycophant, that assigned the opponent his place in a moral bestiary before the argument began. The second manner was built for the world that replaced the first, a world of mass leaflets, civil war, and movements recruiting under fire, and its victory in the dispute owed as much to distribution as to logic. The pamphlet that named the renegade was printed by a state, translated by an International, and distributed by parties on five continents. The pamphlet it answered was printed by its author's dwindling circle and banned in the state the reply governed. Arguments do not distribute themselves, and the grading of a printed dispute must record, as a fact about the dispute and not about its merits, that one side commanded the means of intellectual production the theory itself had taught both men to notice.

The epithet renegade deserves forensic attention, since it became the dispute's most durable export. A renegade is not an opponent; an opponent may be answered, while a renegade is to be shunned, and the word performs three operations at once. It converts a change of position into a fall from grace, so that the accused's earlier agreement with the accuser becomes evidence against him rather than a puzzle for both. It relocates the argument from propositions to biography, inviting the reader to explain the accused's views by his fears, his comforts, and his ambitions, questions on which the accuser's pamphlet is generous with speculation. And it forecloses the accused's reply in advance, since anything the renegade says in his defence is what a renegade would say. The pupil wielded the word with a craftsman's awareness of its operations; his pamphlet is, among other things, a masterclass in the rhetoric of excommunication, studied since by movements that have never read its economics. Fairness requires the symmetrical observation that the teacher had his own softer instruments of the same kind: the magisterial patience that treats the opponent as a pupil who has not done the reading, the appeal to the canon as to a settled estate of which the writer is executor. Authority, too, is a rhetoric, and the teacher's authority had policed the movement's boundaries for decades before the pupil's excommunication displaced it. The dispute is, at this level, a war between two technologies of intellectual government, the school and the tribunal, and readers who see only the tribunal's violence miss how thoroughly the school had governed what could be thought in the old International, and at what cost to the independent minds, Luxemburg's among them, that it kept at its margins.

### Why did the pupil choose a pamphlet instead of a book?

Because a pamphlet is a weapon with a date on it. The reply was written to intervene in a live struggle for the allegiance of the European movement at the moment the new International was being assembled, and its brevity, its quotable violence, and its title, which could be repeated by readers who never opened it, were designed for that intervention. A systematic book would have arrived after the allegiances had settled and would have been shelved with the treatises. The choice reveals the author's hierarchy of purposes with unusual clarity: the founding of an organisation outranked the refutation of an argument, and where the two conflicted, as they did whenever a fair statement of the teacher's case might have complicated recruitment, the refutation was cheerfully sacrificed. The teacher, writing his pamphlets for a readership rather than a recruitment drive, made the opposite choice at every corresponding point, which is why his texts grade better as arguments and worse as events. History reads arguments. It is made by events. The dispute's two authors, each in his own way, understood the trade and chose opposite sides of it, and the reader who would grade them must decide, question by question, whether the grading is of the arguments or of the events, and must not let the events grade the arguments by stealth.

Posterity's handling of the two styles completes the lesson. The pupil's manner became the house style of a world movement, and its costs compounded: generations of polemicists learned to answer arguments by assigning their authors to categories, and the habit survived the causes it served, migrating into the polemics of movements that despise the pupil's memory while reproducing his method sentence for sentence. The teacher's manner, patience as an intellectual ethic, survived mainly in exile from both traditions, in the scholarly literature that eventually re read the dispute and in the practice of readers who discovered that the boring pamphlet had been the accurate one. There is a final irony in the styles' fates that the grading should not leave unrecorded. The pupil's reply sought to bury the teacher's pamphlet beneath an epithet, and for two generations it succeeded; yet the epithet is now the chief reason the pamphlet is read, since the curious arrive asking what could have provoked such fury, and stay to find a forecast of the world the fury built. Rhetoric that powerful creates the audience for its own refutation, given time. The time required was most of a century, and the teacher did not live to meet his new readers, but the meeting occurs in every seminar where the two pamphlets are at last read as a pair.

## Why the quarrel keeps returning

Settled disputes stay in their century. This one emigrates. Whenever a movement that promises deep transformation approaches power, in whatever country and under whatever banner, the two pamphlets' questions reappear wearing local dress: whether the movement's leaders can be dismissed by its followers once they govern, whether its emergency powers carry expiry dates, whether its parliamentary wing is accumulating strength or being accumulated by the state, whether its theorists are describing the world or underwriting a strategy. The questions emigrate because they were never really about Russia or Germany. They are about the behaviour of organisations that claim to act for people who cannot supervise them daily, and that behaviour does not respect the boundaries of ideology or era. Parties of the right and centre furnish their own illustrations, as do trade unions, liberation movements, insurgencies that become governments, and governments that become dynasties. The disputants would have resisted many of the applications; both believed their categories belonged to the struggle between labour and capital. But a mechanism, once correctly described, belongs to whoever can use it, and the teacher's mechanism, insulation from dismissal breeds self serving rule, and the pupil's mechanism, institutions absorb the movements that enter them unprepared, are correctly described mechanisms. Their joint afterlife in organisational sociology, in the literature on oligarchy that Michels founded from inside the teacher's own party, and in the study of revolutions that fail or congeal, testifies that the quarrel identified something real about power itself, beneath the vocabulary of its moment.

The return of the quarrel in scholarship has its own rhythm worth recording. In the decades when the pupil's state stood at its most formidable, the dispute was read chiefly as a prologue to that state's apologetics or to its indictment, and the teacher figured as a footnote to a verdict already reached. As the state weakened and then vanished, the footnote grew: editions of the teacher's works multiplied, monographs reconstructed his system, and the dictatorship pamphlet entered syllabuses it had been barred from for generations, often taught now beside the pupil's reply in the pairing this article recommends. The pupil's reputation travelled the opposite road, from state doctrine to historical problem, and his writings began to receive the treatment reserved for classics: read whole, in context, with the failures annotated rather than denied. Neither reputation's journey should be mistaken for the grading of the arguments, which the record settled, in the main, long before the reputations finished moving. A reader meeting the dispute for the first time inherits the benefit of the delay: the texts can be approached without a camp's permission, and the questions can be asked in the form their authors asked them, as open questions whose answers the evidence had not yet supplied. Recovering that openness is the final task of an honest exposition, and it is harder than it sounds, because every reader knows how the story ends and must labour to unknow it while the pamphlets are open.

### What should a reader unknow before opening the pamphlets?

Chiefly, the sequel. Read the teacher's forecast as though the regime's future were unwritten, and its quality becomes visible: the specificity of the mechanism, the absence of hedging, the willingness to be refuted by a liberalisation that never came. Read the pupil's reply as though its author were an exile pamphleteer rather than a head of state, and its genuine insights, the sociology of bourgeois democracy above all, separate themselves from the authority that power later lent them. Then restore the sequel and grade. The exercise is artificial and indispensable, because hindsight corrupts judgement in both directions at once, making the vindicated forecaster seem inevitable and the defeated strategist seem a fool, when the evidence shows a forecaster who gambled his standing on a mechanism and a strategist whose method had governed the strongest movement of its age. Readers who perform the exercise on this dispute acquire a transferable suspicion of their own hindsight, and will grade the prophets of their own moment, the confident forecasters of every camp, by the teacher's standard: specificity, mechanism, and a stated condition of refutation.

There is also a human residue that grading cannot process and should not pretend to. The two men never met after the rupture and never reconciled. The pupil, in the inventory of his final illnesses, did not revisit the quarrel in any surviving note of regret; the teacher, in a long exile's retrospect, treated his former pupil's career as the great catastrophe of their shared cause and his own early influence on the younger man as a fact he could neither deny nor forgive himself for examining too closely. Between the lines of the teacher's late histories, readers have detected the special bitterness of a man grading his own life's examination and finding the decisive question one he had set for others. The pupil's silence and the teacher's bitterness are not evidence in any row of the grid. They are the cost accounting of the dispute in the currency of two lives, and an exposition that omits them has explained the quarrel without quite conveying why it still hurts to read. The article closes its grading here, with the accounts rendered and the residue acknowledged, and turns to the questions readers most frequently bring to the dispute, answered in the terms the grading has established.

## The councils themselves: the soviet form in practice

Both pamphlets argued about the soviets in the abstract, as a form, and the dispute cannot be fully graded until the form is examined in its working life, since the pupil's democratic claim rested on the councils' vitality and the teacher's scepticism rested on their predictable capture. The councils of the revolutionary year were, by the testimony of participants across the spectrum, genuinely alive: factory committees and soldiers' committees electing and recalling delegates in continuous session, political argument of extraordinary intensity conducted by men and women who had never before been permitted any. The pupil's celebration of the form drew on that reality, and a grading that treats his account as mere propaganda falsifies the record. The councils embodied, for a season, exactly the direct democracy his treatise promised, and their existence explains the moral energy his movement commanded among workers far beyond Russia who had never seen such institutions and hungered for them.

The form's subsequent career is the teacher's evidence. As the civil war proceeded, the councils' elections were managed, then ritualised; rival parties were expelled from them by decree and arrest rather than by vote; the executive committees accumulated the powers the plenary sessions surrendered; and the party's cells inside each institution guaranteed that no council would use its formal sovereignty against the government that spoke in its name. Historians of the revolution, among them Alexander Rabinowitch in his studies of the Bolsheviks in power, have traced the process month by month in the capital's own institutions, and the pattern they document is the teacher's mechanism operating at the level of the form itself: an institution whose agenda, information, and candidacies are controlled by an undismissable organisation retains the shape of self government while its substance migrates to the controllers. The soviets did not die. They persisted for the regime's entire life, meeting, voting, and applauding on schedule, and their persistence in form is the sharpest illustration available of the teacher's central distinction, between the forms of class rule and its reality, since the forms survived intact precisely by ceasing to threaten anyone.

### Could the councils have remained sovereign under any leadership?

The question is the dispute's most instructive counterfactual, and it should be answered with the modesty counterfactuals require. A government fighting a civil war for its life, administering a collapsing economy in a country of scattered literacy and ruined transport, would have found genuinely sovereign councils, with their shifting majorities and rival parties, an almost ungovernable instrument, and the wartime centralisation that hollowed them had drivers no leadership could simply have declined. Yet the hollowing was also chosen at points where choice existed: the exclusion of rival socialists from the councils preceded the worst emergencies, the subordination of the factory committees to state appointed management was a policy debated and imposed against the committees' resistance, and the refusal, after the war, to restore contested elections was a decision renewed annually by men who found the arrangement convenient. The honest finding holds both halves. Necessity built the cage's frame; convenience hung its door. The teacher's pamphlet, which attributed everything to the logic of minority rule, overstated the logic and understated the war. The pupil's defenders, who attribute everything to the war, must explain why the cage outlasted the emergency by the length of a long human life. Neither attribution alone survives the chronology, and the grid's row on the disputed phrase should be graded with the chronology, not with either pamphlet, as the standard.

One consequence of the councils' fate deserves a final paragraph, because it shaped the dispute's transmission to later generations more than any text. Workers and intellectuals in other countries who encountered the soviet idea encountered it, after the first years, exclusively through the pupil's tradition, which presented the form as it existed on paper and forbade inquiry into its working life. The teacher's tradition, which might have supplied the inquiry, preferred to forget the councils altogether and to defend a parliamentarism the councils had been invented to surpass. Between the two custodians, the actual experiment, a form of direct democracy that flowered for a season and was then preserved as a shell for three generations, vanished from the political imagination it might have instructed. Later movements that reinvented council forms, in Turin workshops, in Hungarian factories, in Polish shipyards, did so largely ignorant that they were re running an experiment whose results were already in, and their reinventions reproduced both the flowering and, where they met undismissable parties, the shell. The dispute's two principals, who argued about the councils more searchingly than anyone of their time, thus failed jointly at the task of bequeathing the argument: the winner suppressed its sequel and the loser suppressed its subject. A modern exposition can repair that failure only by doing what this section has attempted, which is to treat the form's whole life, flowering and shell alike, as the evidence on which the pamphlets' claims must finally be graded.

## The contemporaries who graded them first

The grading offered in this article is not the first. Contemporaries inside Russian social democracy delivered assessments of both men that later scholarship has repeatedly consulted, and the most penetrating came from Julius Martov, the Menshevik leader whose life crossed the pupil's at every stage. Martov had shared the pupil's exile, co founded the movement's first newspaper with him, and parted from him over the very question of the party's character that the grid's first row records. Watching the revolution from inside it, first as a tolerated critic and then as a dying exile, Martov made the argument that completes the dispute's triangle. Against the pupil he held that the revolution's degeneration was not imposed by circumstance alone but was inscribed in the method of a party that had decided to carry a backward country to socialism by command, and that the terror was the method's signature rather than its accident. Against the teacher he held, with equal firmness, that the Western parties' horror at Russian methods was hypocrisy so long as those parties excused the violence of their own states and their own order, and he refused to the end of his life to join the chorus that treated the Russian experiment as a crime rather than as a tragedy of the whole movement, his own wing included. Martov's judgements carry a weight in the grading that neither principal's can, because he paid for his independence with everything a politician can lose and gained nothing from either verdict he delivered.

Other contemporary witnesses distributed themselves along the dispute's seams in instructive ways. The anarchist tradition, which had predicted the statist outcome of any Marxist seizure of power a generation before the teacher took up the theme, watched its forecast fulfilled and recorded the fulfilment with a bitterness unmixed with surprise; the teacher's pamphlet, from that quarter, was received as a late conversion to a truth others had died asserting against his own orthodoxy. The liberal and conservative worlds received the exchange with the selective attention of outsiders who needed only its anti Bolshevik half, and the teacher found himself celebrated in quarters whose celebration embarrassed his argument, since his case was an indictment of the pupil's regime in the name of a socialism his new admirers had no intention of permitting anywhere. The pupil's tradition, meanwhile, policed the dispute's memory with the instruments described earlier, and the teacher's name survived inside it chiefly as an epithet detached from any text, so that generations of militants learned to hiss a name whose bearer they could not have summarised. That the two traditions' custodianship jointly prevented the exchange from being read whole for half a century is the dispute's final institutional lesson, and it is the reason an exposition written at a distance, with the archives open and the camps dissolved, can perform a grading that contemporaries of genius, Martov and Luxemburg honourably excepted, mostly could not.

## The exchange completed: the sequel and its answerer

The pamphlet war had a final round that a complete account must register, because it tested the teacher's argument against a defender of the regime more systematic than circumstance allowed the pupil to be. The teacher's sequel on terrorism and communism, published in the war's aftermath, widened his case from the regime's political forms to the genealogy of its methods, arguing that a revolution which adopts terror as its instrument re enacts the Jacobin sequence, in which the machinery of salvation devours the revolutionaries in order of their seniority. The answer came not from the pupil, consumed by government, but from his deputy in the civil war, who published a defence of terror under the same title and met the teacher's argument on its own ground. Terror, in that defence, was not an embarrassment to be minimised but the revolution's acknowledged weapon, justified by the enemy's ruthlessness and by the stakes of a struggle in which the losing side's leaders would be shot without pamphlets being written about it. The exchange between the teacher and the pupil's deputy is in some ways the dispute's purest form, since the deputy conceded the facts the teacher alleged and contested only their meaning. Read together, the two books stage the argument this article has graded in its starkest version: one side holding that the means become the regime, the other holding that in a war of extermination the refusal of means is merely a choice of which side's terror prevails. The subsequent record, in which the deputy's own life ended at the hands of the apparatus he had defended and the teacher's Jacobin sequence completed itself in the trials of the old guard, graded that final round with a severity neither author could have wished on the other, and it closes the documentary exchange. After it, the dispute belonged to the traditions, the archives, and the readers.

## The Austrian footnote: municipal power and its limits

The teacher spent his final productive years in the orbit of the Austrian movement, and that movement conducted attrition's most attractive partial experiment, which fairness requires the grading to notice. In the republic's capital, a socialist municipality elected and re elected by working class majorities built housing estates, clinics, schools, and cultural institutions of a quality that drew pilgrims from across the world, governing one great city as a demonstration of what the strategy's accumulated strength could deliver in office. The experiment's admirers, then and since, have cited it as the parliamentary road's standing reply to the pupil's contempt. Its fate supplies the grading's more melancholy footnote. The municipal achievement rested on a national balance its builders did not control, and when the republic polarised into armed camps, the capital's fortresses of housing became literal fortresses, shelled by the artillery of a government the socialists' national strategy had failed to keep from power. The Vienna experiment thus confirmed both disputants in a single parable: the teacher's followers proved that accumulated municipal power could transform daily life, and the pupil's forecast proved that such power, unbacked by a settlement of the state's coercive machinery, held its gains on the enemy's sufferance. The teacher lived to see the shelling. No verdict this article has rendered cost its subject more than that confirmation cost him, and the grading records it as the point where the split verdict ceases to be an analytical construction and becomes, simply, the shape of the man's life.

## For students and exam candidates

Build the essay on the dispute grid and the exam becomes an exercise in structure rather than recall. The strongest answers open by destroying the premise of lifelong enmity: state the pupilage, date the rupture to the war, and announce the split verdict as the thesis the essay will defend. Each body paragraph then takes one row of the grid, states both positions from the pamphlets in the form their authors would accept, and tests them against one piece of record evidence, the dispersal of the Assembly, the faction ban, the German republic's founding bargains. Conclusions that crown a winner read as weaker than conclusions that weight the rows, because the weighting demonstrates the skill examiners reward under names like evaluation and judgement. Guard against three characteristic errors. Do not present the teacher as a liberal democrat; his objection was Marxist in its premises and loses its force when its premises are removed. Do not present the pupil's definition of dictatorship as a slip; it was deliberate and argued. Do not let the word renegade pass without noting that it is a verdict disguised as a description, and that noting it is itself a mark of analytical distance.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach the exchange as a paired text and the seminar teaches itself. Assign the two dictatorship pamphlets for the same week, with one instruction issued in advance: every contribution must begin by stating the opposing pamphlet's strongest point to the satisfaction of a student assigned to defend it. The exercise reproduces, in miniature, the labour the pupil's reply declined and the teacher's pamphlet performed, and students feel the asymmetry in their own preparation before they are told it exists. A second session can be built on the prediction audit: distribute the teacher's forecast stripped of its author's name and date, and ask the room to grade it against a chronology of the regime before revealing its provenance. The reveal reliably reorders the room's assumptions about authority and evidence. Keep two guardrails on discussion. The state record, the terror, the famine years, the camps, belongs in the room as documented history, neither euphemised for the revolution's sake nor brandished to end discussion; and the contemporary applications students will inevitably reach for should be returned to the texts until the texts have been mastered, since the dispute's gift to later argument is a vocabulary and a discipline, not a set of transferable slogans.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier in this dispute has moved from verdict to mechanism, and the productive questions concern transmission. How did the teacher's forecast, excommunicated in one tradition and embarrassed in the other, nonetheless supply the conceptual skeleton for later analyses of the regime written from inside its own world? Which of the pupil's wartime innovations survived because they solved organisational problems, and which survived because the victorious state canonised them? The opened archives have settled the sequence of suppressions; they have not settled the causal weights among war, backwardness, ideology, and institutional logic, and the most valuable current work, whether on the German party's integration into its state or on the Soviet apparatus's self recruitment, treats those weights as variables to be estimated rather than as articles to be confessed. Journalists covering movements that promise transformation will find the exchange a durable checklist: ask any claimant party how its leaders can be dismissed, what happens to its internal critics after victory, and which of its emergency powers carries a date of expiry. The disputants between them wrote the checklist's rationale, one by proposing the questions and the other by demonstrating, in government, why they cannot be waived.

What remains open, after the grading and the afterlife and the reading, is the dispute's originating problem, which neither road solved and which no subsequent century has solved on their behalf: how a movement committed to a transformation deeper than a change of government can acquire the power to enact it without becoming, in the acquisition or the exercise, something its own founders would have opposed. The teacher's life warns that the refusal of power's risks can deliver a movement bound hand and foot to the order it opposed. The pupil's life warns that the acceptance of those risks can deliver a state that devours the movement's purpose and then the movement. Between the warnings stands no synthesis, only the questions stated with a precision that lesser disputes never achieve, and the record of two formidable intelligences who shared a school, a canon, and a hope, and who ended by writing each other's most damaging biographies. The split verdict is this article's answer to their quarrel. The quarrel itself, read whole, is their bequest to anyone who still believes the questions matter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did Lenin and Kautsky disagree about?

For most of their association, remarkably little. The younger man built his early politics on the elder's exposition of Marxism and invoked that exposition as the standard of correctness in his own movement's disputes. The division opened over conduct during the war, when the German party backed its government's war effort and its leading theorist defended the decision. It then hardened into a disagreement about the nature of proletarian rule after the Russian seizure of power: whether the working class governs through institutions it controls and can change, as the elder maintained, or whether a disciplined party may wield state power on the class's behalf without those institutions, as the younger maintained and practised.

### Q: Was Lenin originally a follower of Kautsky?

Yes, in a strong and documented sense. He studied the German theorist's writings as the authoritative exposition of the doctrine, recommended them to comrades, translated and circulated them, and leaned on them in his own polemics. His most famous early argument, that socialist consciousness reaches the working class from outside its spontaneous economic struggle, was quoted directly from the elder man's formulation and presented as conclusive authority. He defended the elder against revisionist critics, sought his approval in Russian factional disputes, and measured deviation by distance from his positions. The discipleship lasted roughly two decades and ended only with the war, which is why the later accusation of renegacy carried such personal violence: it was levelled by a pupil at the teacher who had formed him.

### Q: Why did Lenin call Kautsky a renegade?

Because the word performed work that ordinary criticism could not. In the younger man's wartime judgement, the elder had abandoned the International's solemn commitments at the moment of testing, voting with his party for the Kaiser's war credits and then constructing theoretical justifications for the capitulation. Renegade named that sequence as apostasy rather than error: the guardian of the doctrine had deserted it, and his subsequent criticism of the Russian revolution could therefore be read as the rationalisation of a deserter rather than as an argument requiring an answer. The epithet became the title of the famous reply pamphlet and organised the new International's boundary, since parties seeking admission were required to break with the elder man's tendency. Its rhetorical success long outlasted its fairness as biography.

### Q: What is the strategy of attrition?

It is the grand strategy the elder man elaborated for a socialist movement confronting a strong modern state, borrowing the term from military historians who contrasted wearing an opponent down with destroying him in a single battle. The party accumulates members, votes, unions, newspapers, and administrative skill over years, refuses engagements it cannot win, and erodes the old order's legitimacy by its visible, disciplined growth, until the relation of forces permits a transformation carried by a majority. The strategy prized doctrinal firmness precisely because patience without a goal decays into routine. Its hidden premise was a future stable enough for accumulation to continue, and its catastrophic exposure arrived when general war cancelled that premise in a single week.

### Q: Did Kautsky predict what would happen in Russia?

Substantially, yes, and the prediction was specific enough to be graded. Writing in the revolution's first year, he argued that a minority party holding power against the majority's will would be driven to rule by coercion, that coercion would require a permanent apparatus of police and administrators, that the apparatus would substitute itself for the class in whose name it ruled, and that the suppression of political freedom, adopted as an emergency measure, would harden into the regime's constitution. The documented record, from the dispersal of the elected Assembly and the banning of rival socialist parties to the prohibition of factions inside the ruling party and the founder's own late alarm at the bureaucracy, follows that sequence closely. Debate continues over causes, but the forecast's direction and mechanism were borne out.

### Q: Did Kautsky's own strategy work?

Nowhere, in the sense its author intended. The German party that practised attrition became the largest socialist party in the world, won its majority position in the republic founded after the empire's collapse, and used that position to stabilise the existing order rather than transform it, allying with the old officer corps and suppressing the revolutionary left. Its theorist's goal, a majority class carrying through a socialist transformation, receded as the party's organisational success grew, and successor parties eventually struck the goal from their programmes altogether. The strategy was not defeated in a single confrontation; it was dissolved by its own executants' investment in the institutions it had built, which is the failure the younger man had forecast for it. A road can fail by arriving, when arrival changes the travellers.

### Q: Who was right about democracy and socialism?

On the component questions, each was right about the other's position and wrong about his own remedy. The younger man's critique of formal democracy under concentrated private property, its formally equal voters and materially unequal means of persuasion, permanently changed how democratic institutions are analysed. The elder man's counter claim, that a class rules only where it possesses institutions through which it can dismiss its rulers, and that a party which abolishes those institutions rules in the class's place whatever it professes, was confirmed by the regime founded on the younger man's theory. If the question demands a single name, the elder's warning concerned the graver stakes, since it addressed what a victorious revolution would become. But the complete answer is the article's split verdict: a correct critique of the old democracy joined to a failed construction of the new one, against a correct defence of democracy's necessity joined to a strategy that never delivered it.

### Q: What did Luxemburg think of both positions?

She is the dispute's most valuable witness because she belonged to neither camp. She had attacked the elder man's strategic patience from the left for years, arguing that it disarmed the movement it claimed to prepare, and she had criticised features of the younger man's organisational doctrine well before the revolution. When the revolution came she defended it passionately against the elder man's criticisms, crediting the Bolsheviks with an imperishable daring, while warning in the same breath that their suppression of political freedom would strangle the revolution's own life, since freedom reserved for a government's supporters is no freedom at all. Her notes, published after her murder by forces acting under a government of the elder man's party, thus ratify each man's criticism of the other. She was killed by one side's failure after vindicating, against the other side, the revolution he had made.

### Q: Is Kautsky worth reading now?

Yes, selectively and without the permission of either tradition that buried him. His dictatorship pamphlet remains the clearest contemporaneous statement, from inside Marxism, of the case against one party rule, and its central prediction gives it a standing few polemics earn. His programme commentary and historical writings model a lucidity of exposition that students of the tradition still learn from, and his analyses of imperialism and of the agrarian question show the systematic intelligence his pupil once treated as orthodoxy's standard. He is best read paired with his opponent rather than alone: the reply pamphlet exposes the evasions in his wartime record, just as his pamphlet exposes the gap in the reply's theory of the state. Read together, the two men still constitute the sharpest seminar their tradition ever held on the relation between democracy and power.

### Q: What caused the collapse of the Second International?

The collapse was structural before it was moral, which is why it was so complete. The International's parties had grown into national institutions, with newspapers, funds, union alliances, and legal existences that a government at war could confiscate, and with electorates whose patriotism, in the crisis, outweighed their internationalism. Resolutions against war, solemnly passed at congress after congress, carried no enforcement mechanism and no agreed plan of action for the day the mobilisation orders arrived. When that day came in the summer of the war's outbreak, each major party, led by the German party the elder man served, voted its own government the means to fight, and the International dissolved into its national sections within days. Leaders who had built their lives on the promise of solidarity, the elder among them, rationalised the failure afterwards. The younger man built his subsequent career on the refusal to forgive it.

### Q: How does the dispute grid separate the personal quarrel from the political one?

The grid works by replacing the question of who betrayed whom with five questions that can each be answered from evidence: the theory of the party, the assessment of the war, the meaning of the disputed phrase about proletarian rule, the role of parliaments, and the strategy for taking power. Personal material, the discipleship, the rupture, the epithets, explains the quarrel's temperature but settles none of those five rows, and the grid's final column, what the record shows, grades each row independently. The separation matters because the personal story is so strong that it smuggles verdicts: a reader who accepts the renegade biography has decided the political questions without examining them. The grid refuses the smuggling. It allows the biography its full explanatory place in the narrative while insisting that predictions be graded against outcomes and strategies against their own stated goals, whoever uttered them.

### Q: What should a teacher separate in Kautsky's case against the Bolsheviks?

Three elements that polemic on both sides habitually fuses. The first is the philological element, the claim about what Marx meant by the disputed phrase, which can be assessed against Marx's texts and on which later scholarship has largely moved toward the elder man's reading. The second is the institutional element, the prediction that suppressing pluralism would become permanent and self perpetuating, which is assessed against the regime's documented chronology rather than against anyone's intentions. The third is the strategic element, the elder man's implied claim that his own road offered the alternative, which must be assessed separately against the German record and which fails there. Students who separate the three discover that a case can be textually sound, predictively accurate, and strategically empty at the same time, and that discovery, more than any verdict on the two men, is the lesson the paired pamphlets exist to teach.

### Q: Why did the renegade pamphlet matter beyond its argument?

Because it founded a boundary and a style, and both outlived the occasion. As a boundary instrument, the pamphlet defined the new International's test of loyalty: movements were sorted by their willingness to repudiate the elder man and his tendency, and the sorting split the world socialist movement into two hostile traditions for the rest of the century. As a style, it modelled a rhetoric of excommunication, in which an opponent's arguments are answered by assigning him to a category of the fallen, that later polemicists in many movements have reproduced whether or not they shared its author's politics. Its argument about the class content of democracy also mattered, permanently complicating any innocent account of parliamentary forms. But its deepest effect may be the unintended one: by branding the elder man's pamphlet as the work of a renegade, it guaranteed that curious readers would eventually seek out the forbidden text and grade its prediction for themselves.

### Q: How does the split verdict grade the two roads to power?

It grades each road against its own stated destination rather than against its rival, and it weights the grades by the stakes of the questions. The seizure road promised the rule of the working class and the withering of coercion, and it delivered the rule of a party apparatus and the growth of coercion; the accumulation road promised a majority class assuming power to transform society, and it delivered a party that used supremacy to preserve the order it existed to transform. On prediction, the heaviest question, the elder man's forecast of his rival's road was borne out in mechanism and outcome, while the younger man's forecast of the elder's road was also borne out at a lower order of consequence. The verdict is therefore split but not equal: one side won the argument about consequences, neither side won the argument about how a transformation can be achieved, and the exchange establishes what will not work without establishing what will.

### Q: What did each side predict about the other's road?

Each published a forecast, before the outcomes were known, of where the other's strategy would end, and the century ran both experiments. The elder man forecast that minority rule in the working class's name would require permanent coercion, build an apparatus of officials and police, substitute that apparatus for the class, and harden emergency suppression into a constitution; the regime's chronology, through the faction ban to the founder's own late warnings about the bureaucracy, fulfilled the forecast's mechanism in sequence. The younger man forecast that the accumulation strategy would absorb its party into the existing order, converting accumulated strength into an endowment to be protected, until the party chose nation over International in war and order over transformation in revolution; the German party's conduct across the war, the republic's founding, and its aftermath fulfilled that forecast as well. Two accurate prophecies, aimed at each other, are the dispute's strangest monument: each man saw the other's destination clearly and his own hardly at all.
