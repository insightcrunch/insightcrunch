## Readjustment Promised and Attainment Measured

The long title of the Servicemen's Readjustment Act, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 22, 1944 according to the Department of Veterans Affairs, stated the purpose in a single phrase: federal aid for the readjustment in civilian life of returning veterans of the Second World War. Readjustment was a deliberately practical word. The statute did not present education as an ornament or as a reward detached from economic life. Education and training sat inside a larger apparatus of transition that the Veterans Administration described as including loan guaranty for homes, farms, or businesses, unemployment pay, and assistance in finding work. The premise, stated plainly across the Roosevelt administration statements preserved by the Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library and Museum, was that service had interrupted civilian lives, that many service members had forgone schooling or vocational training in order to serve, and that a return to civilian life without support risked both personal hardship and a disorderly postwar labor market. The education title therefore carried two burdens at once. For the individual veteran, a benefit was expected to restore lost time and lost opportunity. For the national economy, enrollment was expected to pace reentry into employment rather than force millions of discharged service members into competition for jobs in a single compressed period.

![GI Bill education outcomes and the evidence across four generations of veterans - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Measured against that stated aim, the short answer developed across the remainder of the article is mixed in a specific and documentable way. The readjustment aim was met in aggregate: enrollment absorbed returning veterans on a vast scale, attainment rose for the cohorts the statutes reached, and later Congresses repeated the basic design for successive generations of service. The Veterans Readjustment Assistance Act for Korean War veterans, the Veterans Readjustment Benefits Act associated with the Vietnam era, the Montgomery GI Bill for the post Vietnam force, and the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act each restated the same core proposition, that paid education eases a transition that military service itself had made harder. Yet aggregate success coexisted with unequal access to the colleges, training places, and administrative approvals through which the promise had to pass. The text of each statute spoke in general eligibility terms. The delivery passed through segregated colleges, underfunded institutions, local offices, admissions decisions, and family resources that differed sharply among veterans with identical legal entitlement. That tension organizes the evidence in the sections that follow, beginning with educational attainment, the outcome most directly tied to the education title itself.

The baseline against which attainment must be judged was a system of higher education that reached only a small minority of the population. The Department of Veterans Affairs, in its official history of the original statute, stated the contrast without qualification: before the Second World War, college and homeownership were, for the most part, unreachable dreams for the average American. That characterization matches the statistical record compiled by the United States Census Bureau. According to Census Bureau figures reported in accounts of the period, fewer than five percent of adults aged twenty five or older had completed four years of college in 1940. A degree was therefore not a normal stage in the transition from school to work. Secondary school itself had expanded, and a high school diploma had become a common credential among younger cohorts, but the step beyond secondary school remained exceptional. Enrollment on the scale later associated with mass higher education did not exist. Colleges and universities served a comparatively narrow population, and attendance depended heavily on family capacity to pay fees, to meet living costs, and to forgo earnings during years when a young adult might otherwise have contributed wages to a household.

Cost operated as a filter even where formal admissions requirements could be met. The education benefit in the 1944 statute, as the Brookings Institution summarized the provision, offered five hundred dollars per year toward education or technical training, alongside a living stipend, and that sum was calibrated to a world in which five hundred dollars covered a substantial share of annual charges at many institutions. For a family without savings, the absence of such support left tuition, books, lodging, and forgone wages to be carried privately and simultaneously. Scholarships existed, and public institutions charged less than private institutions, but neither channel amounted to a general entitlement. A student from a prosperous family could treat college as a planned expense. A student from a farm family, a factory household, or a family dependent on seasonal labor faced a different calculation, in which enrollment competed directly with rent and food. The barrier was therefore not a single price. The barrier was a bundle of direct charges, living costs, lost income, and risk, falling on households with very unequal reserves.

Social position narrowed the prewar college population further. Attendance drew disproportionately from families able to sustain a dependent student into early adulthood, from communities with strong secondary schools, and from networks in which college attendance was an expected path rather than an experiment. Women attended college in substantial numbers at women's colleges and coeducational institutions, yet professional and graduate pathways open to women graduates remained restricted by hiring practice and by institutional custom. Black Americans faced a harder structure again. In the segregated South, Black students were barred from the public flagship universities of the states where most Black Americans lived, and the separate institutions available to Black students operated with markedly smaller resources. Sarah Turner and John Bound, in research published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, described the southern Black colleges confronting the postwar period as a limited set of institutions, many of them small, within a segregated system of higher education. A legal right to tuition support, once created, would have to operate inside that institutional landscape. The landscape did not distribute opportunity evenly, and no clause in the 1944 statute rebuilt the colleges themselves.

The war interrupted education as well as employment, and the interruption complicates any simple reading of postwar enrollment. Large numbers of young men left secondary school classrooms, college lecture halls, apprenticeships, and first jobs for military service. Demobilization returned those men older, often married, and frequently with family obligations that made a return to full time study difficult without income support. A surge in enrollment after discharge could therefore reflect several distinct processes mixed together. Part of the surge represented schooling merely delayed, as men resumed plans the war had suspended. Part represented schooling induced, as men entered programs that family resources would never have financed without a federal benefit. Part represented substitution, as training replaced an immediate search for work in a labor market whose capacity to absorb discharged service members remained uncertain in 1944 and 1945. Separating delay from inducement is the central analytical problem in the attainment literature, and the problem explains why raw enrollment totals, however impressive, cannot by themselves establish what the statutes caused.

The usage totals nevertheless establish the scale of participation beyond dispute. The Department of Veterans Affairs reported that, in the peak year identified by the agency as 1947, veterans accounted for forty nine percent of college admissions. The same agency reported that by the end of the original program on July 25, 1956, a date given in the agency history, 7.8 million of the 16 million veterans of the Second World War had participated in an education or training program. The Congressional Research Service, in its primer on veterans educational assistance programs, presented a fuller breakdown drawn from program records. According to the Congressional Research Service table, the total veteran population numbered 15,440,000, the total trained numbered 7,800,000, college trainees numbered 2,230,000, other school trainees numbered 3,480,000, on the job trainees numbered 1,400,000, and on the farm trainees numbered 690,000. The rounded figures repeated in most official histories, 7.8 million users of education or training benefits and about 2.2 million college attendees, rest on that accounting. Participation on that scale transformed campuses in material terms. Colleges expanded classes, hired instructors, converted temporary buildings, and admitted married students with children in numbers that prewar admissions practice had not contemplated.

The composition of usage matters alongside the totals. College attendance accounted for a minority of benefit use, because the majority of trainees enrolled in other schools, in on the job placements, or in farm training, as the Congressional Research Service breakdown records. A reading that equates the original statute with university attendance alone therefore misstates the program that veterans actually used. For many veterans, readjustment meant a trade credential, a business course, a high school completion program, or supervised training inside employment, rather than a bachelor's degree. Attainment research concentrates on college because college completion is measurable in census records across long spans of life and because degree completion carries large consequences for occupation and earnings. That concentration is an analytical choice, not a description of the whole program. Findings about college attainment speak to the most academically selective slice of benefit use, and silent gaps remain around the quality and payoff of the far larger volume of sub baccalaureate training. The first finding of the article concerns the measurable slice, and the limits of that slice deserve emphasis before the estimates enter the argument.

### Did the education benefits raise college attainment?

Yes, according to the estimates of John Bound and Sarah Turner and of Marcus Stanley. Service combined with benefits raised college completion and years of college for eligible cohorts, with the clearest gains among white veterans and among veterans from families in the upper half of the socioeconomic distribution.

The difficulty lies in the comparison. Veterans were not a random sample of the male population. Military screening selected for health and literacy. Volunteers differed from men who remained in civilian life. Combat exposure, injury, and years removed from classrooms pushed attainment downward, while maturity, discipline, and federal support pushed attainment upward. A naive contrast between veterans and nonveterans therefore bundles the effect of benefits together with the effect of selection into service and the effect of service itself. John Bound and Sarah Turner addressed that problem in "Going to War and Going to College," published in the Journal of Labor Economics in 2002, by shifting the comparison from individuals to cohorts. The abstract of the Bound and Turner study, as presented by the University of Chicago Press, states that the estimation strategy focuses on between cohort differences in military service and uses census data to compare the collegiate attainment of veterans and nonveterans. Birth cohorts reaching military age at different points in the war entered service at sharply different rates, because the demands of the armed forces and the operation of the draft fell unevenly across adjacent years of birth. Those differences in service rates, driven by the timing of the war rather than by the educational ambitions of individual men, supplied the variation from which an effect could be estimated.

Explained without technical vocabulary, the logic runs as follows. Men born in one year might have faced a high probability of service because the war reached peak manpower demand as that cohort came of age, while men born a few years earlier or later faced a lower probability for reasons of timing alone. If educational attainment rose and fell across those same cohorts in step with service exposure, and if no other force moved in the identical pattern across the identical birth years, then the correspondence supports a causal reading of the combined package of service and benefits. The method does not observe a veteran deciding to enroll. The method observes populations whose exposure to the package differed for reasons external to personal preference, and measures whether completed schooling differed accordingly. Bound and Turner also drew on the structure of wartime manpower policy, including the treatment of younger men whose schooling had been interrupted most directly, to distinguish men whose education the war had merely postponed from men whose education the benefit plausibly created. The conclusion stated in the abstract of the 2002 study is direct: the net effect of military service and educational benefits was substantial gains in the collegiate attainment of veterans of the Second World War.

Quantification sharpens that conclusion. Estimates attributed to Bound and Turner, as summarized in later research by Joshua Angrist and Stacy Chen on schooling and the Vietnam era benefit, placed the effect of Second World War service on college completion by white men at around five to six percentage points, with effects on years of college ranging from 0.23 years to 0.28 years. A gain of five to six percentage points in completion is large relative to the prewar baseline recorded by the Census Bureau, in which fewer than five percent of adults aged twenty five or older had completed four years of college in 1940. The years of college estimate matters as well, because attainment gains did not arrive only as completed degrees. Additional semesters without a degree represented real increments of education, affecting placement, occupation, and later training, even where a credential never followed. The Bound and Turner design estimates the combined effect of service and benefits rather than the isolated effect of a tuition payment, and that limitation belongs inside the finding rather than outside the finding. Military service removed years and imposed harms that benefits then had to offset. A positive net effect means the package overcame the disruption of service itself, a stronger result than a simple association between benefit receipt and graduation would provide.

Marcus Stanley approached the same question from the next statute in the sequence, in "College Education and the Midcentury GI Bills," published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2003. The abstract of the Stanley study describes the midcentury statutes as the largest direct scholarship program for higher education in American history, a description that captures the fiscal and institutional scale involved. The research design exploited a sharp eligibility boundary in the Korean War statute. Men who commenced service before the cutoff date given in the Stanley research design, January 31, 1955 as summarized in the National Bureau of Economic Research working paper literature building on the Stanley approach, qualified for the Korean War benefit, while men who commenced service after that date fell outside the program. Men on either side of an administrative date of that kind resemble each other closely in background and ambition, because a birthday or an enlistment date separated by weeks carries no plausible connection to academic capacity. A discontinuity in attainment at precisely that boundary therefore points toward the benefit as the cause, in the same way that cohort variation pointed toward the combined package in the Bound and Turner design. Stanley then used elasticities estimated from the Korean War program, together with comparisons between older and younger veterans, to bound the likely effect of the earlier and larger Second World War program.

The headline estimate from Stanley is expressed at the level of a generation rather than an individual. According to the abstract of the 2003 study, the combination of the Korean War and Second World War statutes probably increased total postsecondary attainment among all men born between 1921 and 1933 by about fifteen percent to twenty percent, with smaller effects for surrounding cohorts. Estimates attributed to Stanley in the summary by Angrist and Chen placed the effect of Korean era benefit eligibility on college completion on the order of five to six percentage points, consistent in magnitude with the Bound and Turner estimates for the earlier war. Two independent designs, one built on cohort variation in wartime service and one built on a statutory cutoff date, converge on a similar scale of effect. Convergence of that kind strengthens the attainment finding beyond the strength of either design standing alone. The same abstract records the distributional qualification that gives the finding its edge: according to Stanley, the impacts of both programs on college attainment were apparently concentrated among veterans from families in the upper half of the distribution of socioeconomic status. Men with stronger family resources, better secondary preparation, and greater familiarity with college were positioned to convert a tuition benefit into completed enrollment at higher rates than men whose need was greatest.

Race and region impose a parallel qualification, developed by Sarah Turner and John Bound in "Closing the Gap or Widening the Divide," released as National Bureau of Economic Research Working Paper Number 9044 and published in the Journal of Economic History in 2003. The National Bureau of Economic Research summary of that research records that more than one million Black men had served in the military during the Second World War and that those men shared eligibility for educational benefits that included tuition payments and a stipend for up to four years of college or other training, the duration and components stated in the summary. Eligibility on identical statutory terms did not produce identical attainment. The summary states the central result in uncompromising language: for Black veterans more likely to be limited to the South in collegiate choices, the benefit exacerbated rather than narrowed the economic and educational differences between Black Americans and white Americans. Outside the South, where Black veterans could reach a wider range of institutions, the research found positive effects on attainment. Inside the segregated South, restricted seats at underfunded Black institutions capped the value of a benefit whose text imposed no such cap.

Finding One therefore carries both halves of the record at once. Attainment rose, substantially and measurably, for the cohorts the midcentury statutes reached, and the rise survives demanding research designs that account for selection into service. Attainment gains concentrated among veterans already advantaged by family resources, and, for Black veterans confined to southern institutions, the research of Turner and Bound found no closing of the gap between Black and white attainment at all. The claim of the article can be stated in four words. Universal text, uneven delivery. The eligibility language of the statutes applied in general terms to veterans who met service requirements, while the colleges, offices, and local conditions that delivered the benefit filtered that general promise through unequal institutions, so identical entitlement produced markedly different educational results depending on race, region, and family position.

## Finding Two: Who Gained, and What the Schooling Paid

The second finding concerns distribution. The midcentury bills raised attainment on average, and an average can conceal as much as an average reveals. A benefit offered on equal statutory terms to every eligible veteran did not produce equal gains for every eligible veteran. The research record shows a consistent pattern: the veterans best positioned to convert a subsidy into a completed credential gained the most, and the veterans facing the deepest barriers before service gained the least. Money removed a real obstacle. Money did not, by itself, supply a high school education that had ended early, preparation for college level work, a college willing to admit the applicant, or a family budget able to absorb years of reduced earnings. The equalizing story told about the bills therefore requires a qualification, and the qualification rests on named research rather than on impression.

The sharpest statement of that qualification comes from Marcus Stanley, whose study "College Education and the Midcentury GI Bills" appeared in The Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2003, at pages 671 to 708. Stanley exploited a sharp cutoff in eligibility for the Korean War GI Bill. Men who began service before the cutoff date qualified for benefits, and men who began service after that date did not, and the two groups were otherwise closely comparable. That design allowed Stanley to separate the effect of benefit eligibility itself from the many differences between men who chose college and men who did not. Elasticities estimated from the Korean era program could then be used to bound the likely effects of the larger and earlier World War II program.

Stanley placed the combined effect of the two bills at the generational scale reported in Finding One. A gain of that size, spread across an entire generation of men, represents a large expansion of college attainment. The same study, however, reported the distributional result that qualifies the expansion: the impacts of both programs on college attainment were apparently concentrated among veterans from families in the upper half of the distribution of socioeconomic status.

### Did the Korean War GI Bill widen access to college for disadvantaged veterans?

Marcus Stanley found that Korean War GI Bill eligibility raised college completion, but the gains fell mainly on veterans from families in the upper half of socioeconomic status, because money alone did not supply the schooling, preparation, or college places that disadvantaged veterans lacked before service began at all.

That result deserves careful reading, because the result does not show that the benefit failed, or that disadvantaged veterans received nothing. The result shows where the marginal effect of a tuition subsidy was largest. A veteran from a family in the upper half of the socioeconomic distribution was more likely to have completed high school, more likely to have attended schools that prepared students for college admission, and more likely to have family resources able to supplement a benefit that covered tuition and a modest living allowance without covering every cost of attendance. For such a veteran, price was plausibly the binding constraint, and removing the price constraint released enrollment and completion that preparation had already made possible. For a veteran whose schooling had ended before a diploma, price was only one constraint among several, and a subsidy addressed only that one. The benefit was equal as a matter of statute. The starting positions of the men claiming the benefit were not equal, and the outcomes reflected the starting positions.

A parallel qualification emerged from research on race and region. Sarah Turner and John Bound, in "Closing the Gap or Widening the Divide," published in The Journal of Economic History in 2003 at pages 145 to 177 and first issued as National Bureau of Economic Research Working Paper No. 9044 in 2002, examined whether the World War II bill had different effects for Black and white veterans. Turner and Bound found a substantial and positive effect on educational attainment for white men and for Black men born outside the South. For Black veterans likely to be limited to the South in collegiate choices, the same study found little effect on collegiate outcomes, against a background of segregated colleges and limited capacity at historically Black institutions in the South. The digest concluded that the bill exacerbated rather than narrowed educational and economic differences between Black and white men among men from the South.

The attainment estimates for the World War II program itself come principally from John Bound and Sarah Turner, "Going to War and Going to College," published in the Journal of Labor Economics in 2002. Bound and Turner confronted a hard identification problem: men who served differed from men who did not serve, because men who failed physical or mental screening were excused from service, and simple comparisons between veterans and nonveterans therefore distorted the effect of service and benefits. A between cohort strategy, using variation in the veteran share across birth cohorts and eligibility for service before the end of the war in the Pacific as an instrument, produced more credible estimates. As Angrist and Chen summarized those estimates in 2011, the instrumental variables results for white men matched the scale reported in Finding One. Those figures combine military service with the availability of benefits, a combination that the research design could not fully pull apart.

Benefit design differed across the midcentury programs in ways that shaped who could use each program. Under the 1944 law, tuition payments went to institutions. Under the Korean War program established in 1952, the Congressional Research Service primer on the pre 2008 bills records a single allowance paid to the veteran, set at up to 110 dollars monthly for a single veteran, with higher amounts for veterans with dependents. The veteran paid tuition, fees, books, and living costs out of that allowance, leaving a student at an expensive institution, or a student with family obligations, exposed to any gap between the allowance and actual costs.

The Vietnam era program illustrates the importance of benefit level as well as benefit design. As originally enacted in 1966, the Post Korean Conflict GI Bill, the program generally known as the Vietnam era GI Bill, paid an initial monthly benefit of 100 dollars, according to the Congressional Research Service primer on veterans educational assistance programs. A Congressional Research Service analysis of college prices and federal student aid put that 1966 amount at the equivalent of 603 dollars monthly in constant 2005 dollars, and put the 1952 Korean era benefit at 965 dollars monthly on the same basis. Rates rose in steps after enactment, reaching 175 dollars monthly in the 1970 to 1971 school year and 220 dollars monthly in the 1972 to 1973 school year in the Veterans Administration table reproduced in a government study of the period, and Angrist and Chen calculated that by the 1970s the full time stipend was almost identical in real value to the World War II package. Generosity therefore depended heavily on the year of discharge: a veteran leaving service in 1967 faced a materially weaker benefit than a veteran leaving service in 1974.

Take up evidence for the Vietnam era program points in two directions at once. Early use was low, and the benefit increases of the late 1960s and early 1970s were responses in part to that low use and to complaints that the allowance left a full time student with little or nothing for subsistence after tuition and books, complaints recorded in the government study cited above. Eventual use, however, was not low by the standards of the earlier programs. Angrist and Chen, drawing on the 2001 Survey of Veterans, reported that among whites, 44 percent of World War II veterans and 42 percent of Korean era veterans used benefits for education and training, while the usage rate for Vietnam era veterans was 50 percent. Angrist and Chen also reported that 63 percent of Vietnam era beneficiaries used benefits for college courses, against 56 percent for Korean era beneficiaries and 53 percent for World War II beneficiaries. Lower take up, where the phrase fits the Vietnam record, describes the early years of a weak benefit, rather than the lifetime usage rate of the cohort as a whole.

Completion told a harder story than enrollment. J. Michael Horan, in a 1992 paper for the Education Resources Information Center summarizing research that included a study of 1,006 Vietnam veterans, reported that financial constraints were the primary reason veterans in that study gave for deciding not to attend college on the bill or for discontinuing enrollment before graduation, and that only 47 percent of the veterans in that study who attended college completed the educational goals set by those veterans. The same summary placed the relative benefit for Vietnam veterans at about 50 percent less than the benefit received by World War II veterans, a comparison that reflects the position before or during the early increases rather than the improved real value Angrist and Chen calculated for the 1970s.

The earnings evidence begins with a study that estimated the return to the schooling itself. Joshua Angrist, in "The Effect of Veterans Benefits on Education and Earnings," published in Industrial and Labor Relations Review in 1993 at pages 637 to 652, used data from the 1987 Survey of Veterans to estimate the effect of veterans benefits on schooling completed after entry into military service and on subsequent earnings. A research brief published by the Institute for Veterans and Military Families at Syracuse University summarizes the central estimates: benefits were estimated to increase schooling by roughly 1.4 years, implying annual earnings approximately 6 percent higher than expected in the absence of the benefits. The same summary reports that the premium accrued primarily to benefit users who attended college or graduate school, which aligns the earnings return with degree level study rather than with benefit use in general. Angrist's 1993 design relied on comparisons among veterans rather than on random assignment, and the estimates carry the caution appropriate to that design.

Set against that result stands the best known earnings finding in the entire literature, and the finding runs the other way. Joshua Angrist, in "Lifetime Earnings and the Vietnam Era Draft Lottery: Evidence from Social Security Administrative Records," published in the American Economic Review in 1990, used the randomly assigned risk of induction generated by the draft lottery to construct estimates of the effect of veteran status on civilian earnings that were not biased by the kinds of men most likely to serve. Social Security administrative records showed that in the early 1980s, long after service in Vietnam had ended, the earnings of white veterans were approximately 15 percent less than the earnings of comparable nonveterans, as the published abstract of the 1990 study states. Angrist attributed the loss primarily to lost labor market experience: years spent in uniform were years not spent accumulating civilian experience, seniority, and employer specific skills, and civilian earnings profiles reward experience steeply in the first years after school. The draft lottery estimates measured the effect of service, not the effect of the benefit alone, because lottery numbers assigned the risk of service and the benefit arrived as one consequence of service among several. Read together, Angrist 1990 and Angrist 1993 describe two forces pulling in opposite directions on the same men: service subtracted experience and earnings, and benefit financed schooling added schooling and earnings.

A follow up study traced both forces across a longer horizon. Joshua Angrist and Stacey Chen, in "Schooling and the Vietnam Era GI Bill: Evidence from the Draft Lottery," published in the American Economic Journal: Applied Economics in 2011 at pages 96 to 118, used data from the 2000 census and draft lottery instruments to estimate long term effects of Vietnam era service. Angrist and Chen found marked schooling gains for veterans and argued that the gains reflected use of the Vietnam era GI Bill rather than draft avoidance behavior. The magnitude of the schooling effect was similar to the magnitudes reported for the World War II and Korean era programs by Bound and Turner and by Stanley. Draft lottery estimates of the earnings effect of Vietnam era service in 2000, when the cohorts were middle aged, were close to zero. Angrist and Chen reconciled the schooling and earnings results through the shape of the age earnings profile: the return to experience flattens in middle age, so the cost of lost early experience faded, and the estimated economic return to the GI Bill schooling increment was modest and smaller than the corresponding ordinary least squares estimate. Earnings gains from schooling and earnings losses from lost experience roughly offset one another, producing a net earnings impact of about zero in 2000.

The midcentury earnings picture also carries a selection warning. Joshua Angrist and Alan Krueger, in research published in 1994 and summarized in later reviews of the literature, found that the relatively high earnings of World War II veterans were explained by selection into service rather than by a causal effect of service, because screening had admitted men with characteristics associated with higher civilian earnings. Lottery based and cohort based designs consistently produced smaller earnings effects than raw comparisons suggested, and in the Vietnam case produced a substantial early penalty. The schooling effect survived those stricter designs. The earnings effect depended on the period measured and on the balance between added education and lost experience.

The Montgomery GI Bill changed the mechanism once more, and the enrollment evidence for that program shows a contributory design at work. Where earlier bills conferred eligibility through service alone, [the Montgomery GI Bill of 1984](/2019/03/15/montgomery-gi-bill-1984-guide/) required a financial contribution from the service member: the Department of Veterans Affairs eligibility rules for the active duty program specify a pay reduction of 100 dollars a month for the first 12 months of service, a total contribution of 1,200 dollars, for the main category of entrants who first entered active duty after June 30, 1985. Enrollment was effectively automatic unless a recruit declined in writing, and a decision to disenroll was irrevocable, as Department of Defense program instructions describe. The contribution created a sunk cost that gave every enrolled member a financial reason to use the benefit after service, while the irrevocable opt out meant that a recruit who declined at entry lost the benefit permanently regardless of later intentions. The Montgomery population of eligible veterans was therefore partly self selected at the point of enlistment, which complicates any comparison of usage rates across the programs.

Enrollment in the Montgomery program rose sharply as the all volunteer force matured. A Department of Defense news report on testimony by Army Lieutenant General Samuel E. Ebbesen recorded enrollment rates of 95 percent of recruits in fiscal 1995, up from 50 percent in fiscal 1985. Enrollment and usage are different measures. Enrollment measured a decision made at entry, under automatic enrollment and a payroll deduction, while usage measured a later decision to claim benefits for actual study, often years after discharge. High enrollment demonstrated the reach of the recruiting offer without demonstrating that a comparable share of veterans began, or completed, a program of study.

The most systematic evidence on Montgomery era usage and retention comes from Curtis J. Simon, Sebastian Negrusa, and John T. Warner, whose study "Educational Benefits and Military Service: An Analysis of Enlistment, Reenlistment, and Veterans' Benefit Usage" appeared in Economic Inquiry in 2010 at pages 1008 to 1031, using data from 1990 to 2005. A research brief published by the Institute for Veterans and Military Families summarizes the headline usage result: an increase of 10,000 dollars in education benefits was estimated to increase the probability of Montgomery GI Bill usage by about 5 percentage points, while the duration of benefit usage was estimated to be insensitive to benefit levels. Higher benefits also led to higher separation from service, through a direct incentive to leave and use the benefit and through the attraction of more college oriented youth into service in the first place, and the study found higher education benefits associated with higher separation from the Army and the Air Force, but not from the other services.

The same literature records a long erosion in the real value of the Montgomery benefit during the first years of the program. The real value of the benefit, unchanged in nominal terms between 1985 and 1992, declined at about a 7 percent annual rate in real terms over that period, according to Simon, Negrusa, and Warner as summarized in a case study of the bill by Amanda Goff in Policy Perspectives. Congress approved a 20 percent benefit increase in 1992, the same case study records. Goff also reported usage rates across all services between 49 percent and 51 percent of eligible veterans. A usage rate near one half, sustained across a contributory program with automatic enrollment and a sunk contribution, suggests that the obstacles measured in the earlier literature, preparation, family obligations, employment pressure, and institutional fit, operated under the Montgomery program as well.

Taken together, the studies assembled in Finding Two support a measured verdict on who gained and on what the gain was worth. Stanley demonstrated that the midcentury bills raised attainment substantially and that the college gains concentrated among veterans from families in the upper half of the socioeconomic distribution. Turner and Bound demonstrated that a race neutral statute produced little collegiate gain for Black veterans confined by segregation to southern institutions. Angrist estimated in 1993 that benefit financed schooling added roughly 1.4 years of schooling and about 6 percent to annual earnings for the veterans studied, and Angrist estimated in 1990 that Vietnam era service itself carried an earnings penalty of about 15 percent for white veterans in the early 1980s. Angrist and Chen found in 2011 that the penalty had faded to about zero by 2000 as the schooling return offset the fading cost of lost experience. Simon, Negrusa, and Warner found that Montgomery era usage responded to benefit generosity, while duration of use did not. The bills worked, and the bills worked unevenly, and both halves of that sentence rest on evidence.

## Finding Three: The Regional Divide in Educational Outcomes

Finding Three concerns distribution rather than average effect. A national average for veterans education benefits can describe a large rise in college attendance while concealing a sharply different result for Black veterans in the South and Black veterans outside the South. Sarah Turner and John Bound placed that regional contrast at the center of the evidence on the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944. Turner and Bound found that World War II service and the availability of GI Bill benefits substantially increased educational attainment for white men and for Black men born outside the South. For Black veterans whose educational choices were likely to be limited to the South, Turner and Bound found little effect on collegiate outcomes. The same federal benefit therefore coincided with expanded attainment in one regional setting and with a widening educational difference between Black and white men in another. That distinction governs every racial comparison in this section. A claim about Black veterans in the South cannot be transferred to Black veterans outside the South, and a claim about gains outside the South cannot be used to soften the southern result.

The distinction between eligibility and attainment explains why the finding matters. Eligibility was a legal status earned through military service. Attainment required a sequence of further events: an application had to be processed, a suitable institution had to admit the veteran, a seat and housing had to exist, coursework had to be completed, and the credential had to be recognized in a labor market. A breakdown at any point could leave the legal entitlement intact while producing no additional schooling. Turner and Bound measured collegiate outcomes, not the moral promise contained in the statute and not the number of veterans who possessed a discharge certificate. The regional finding on race emerged at the conversion stage, where a federal entitlement met state law, institutional admissions rules, college capacity, and local administration. Outside the South, Black veterans converted eligibility into substantially greater attainment, according to Turner and Bound. In the South, Black veterans faced a segregated conversion process, and Turner and Bound found little measurable collegiate gain.

Turner and Bound published "Closing the Gap or Widening the Divide: The Effects of the GI Bill and World War II on the Educational Outcomes of Black Americans" in The Journal of Economic History in 2003, at pages 145 to 177 of volume 63. The title posed the two interpretations that later public discussion often treated as alternatives for the nation as a whole. Turner and Bound instead made the answer depend on place. The National Bureau of Economic Research summary of the Turner and Bound research stated that more than 1 million Black men had served in the military during World War II and that those men shared eligibility for educational benefits, including tuition payments and a stipend for up to four years of college or other training. Against that shared eligibility, Turner and Bound compared educational outcomes by race and region. Black men born outside the South experienced a substantial and positive effect, in the language of the Turner and Bound abstract. Black men likely to be limited to the South in collegiate choices did not experience a comparable effect.

The southern result was relative as well as absolute. Turner and Bound concluded that the GI Bill had little effect on collegiate outcomes for Black veterans likely to be limited to the South, resulting in an exacerbation of educational differences between Black and white men from southern states. White southern men could use the benefit at white southern institutions that excluded Black applicants. Black southern veterans were restricted to a separate and much smaller institutional sector. The gap could therefore widen even without a decline in Black southern attainment from an earlier level. A benefit that raised white attainment while leaving Black attainment nearly unchanged increased the distance between the two groups. Outside the South, the pattern differed: Turner and Bound found substantial positive effects for Black men born outside the South as well as for white men. The regional evidence rejects a single national sentence about racial effects, whether that sentence emphasizes expansion or exclusion alone.

### Why did the same benefit produce different results by region?

Turner and Bound found that the benefit required an accessible college seat to convert eligibility into attainment. Outside the South, Black veterans could reach a wider range of institutions. In the South, segregation closed white colleges and limited Black colleges lacked capacity, so eligibility alone produced little measurable collegiate gain.

The mechanism began with statutory neutrality. The National Bureau of Economic Research summary of Turner and Bound described the educational support in the GI Bill as notably race neutral in its statutory terms. Eligibility turned on service and discharge status rather than on a racial classification written into the benefit formula. That neutrality had real significance, because Black veterans outside the South used the benefit to achieve substantial educational gains, as Turner and Bound documented. Statutory neutrality did not, however, guarantee an available institution. The statute supplied tuition support and a stipend, but colleges controlled admission, states maintained segregated public systems in the South, and historically Black institutions operated with severely constrained physical and financial capacity. In the South, the absence of a racial classification in the federal text coexisted with racial classifications throughout the educational system that a Black veteran had to enter. Outside the South, those state imposed educational barriers were not identical, and Turner and Bound found a substantially different attainment result for Black veterans.

Administration formed the second part of the mechanism. A federal benefit administered through state and local structures passes through offices that interpret procedures, counsel applicants, approve programs, and interact with institutions. Ira Katznelson, in When Affirmative Action Was White, published in 2005, argued that the powerful southern wing of the Democratic Party helped shape New Deal and Fair Deal era programs so that administration remained in local hands. Katznelson treated that design as central to the racial consequences of the GI Bill. Local administration inside a segregated southern system did not need to announce a separate federal rule for Black veterans in order to produce unequal conversion of benefits. Counseling could point a Black southern veteran toward vocational training rather than a degree program, institutional approval could favor familiar segregated channels, and delay could consume the period in which study was practical. Outside the South, administration also operated through institutions with racial restrictions, a qualification that prevents an idealized regional contrast. The Turner and Bound result nevertheless remained regional: Black veterans outside the South achieved substantial gains, while Black veterans likely to be limited to southern institutions achieved little measurable collegiate gain.

College supply formed the third part of the mechanism, and the supply constraint was severe in the South. Segregation barred Black southern veterans from white colleges and universities in the South, regardless of federal tuition support. The available alternatives were historically Black colleges and universities, institutions that served Black students under chronic underfunding and that faced an intense postwar surge in applications. Turner and Bound identified relatively limited opportunities at historically Black institutions as a defining feature of the southern setting. A tuition payment cannot create a classroom seat, a laboratory place, a dormitory bed, a faculty line, or an accredited degree program on demand. When demand exceeded capacity, qualified Black southern veterans could be wait listed or turned away, and the federal benefit attached to admission could not be used at an excluded white institution nearby. Outside the South, Black veterans had access to a wider institutional range, although access varied and discrimination persisted in admissions and housing. Turner and Bound found that the wider range outside the South was sufficient to register substantial attainment gains for Black veterans, unlike the constrained southern market.

Prior schooling and expected returns added further friction, and Turner and Bound framed those channels at the outset of the research. The Turner and Bound abstract stated that effects could differ by race because of differential returns to education and differences in opportunities at colleges and universities, with men in the South facing explicitly segregated colleges. Segregated primary and secondary schooling in the South left many Black southern veterans with weaker formal preparation for college admission than white southern veterans, a product of unequal public investment rather than a measure of ability. Occupational segregation also affected the payoff to a degree, because employers could restrict the professional and managerial positions open to Black graduates. Those factors did not replace the institutional explanation, and Turner and Bound placed segregated college opportunity at the center of the regional contrast. Outside the South, Black veterans faced racial barriers as well, but Turner and Bound found substantial gains there, which indicates that the southern combination of explicit college segregation and limited Black college capacity had a distinctive effect.

Geography in the Turner and Bound research requires precise reading. The decisive category was not a simple label attached to every Black veteran living at a particular address after discharge. Turner and Bound distinguished Black men born outside the South from Black veterans likely to be limited to the South in educational choices. Place of birth served as an indicator of the educational market that a veteran was likely to confront. A Black veteran born outside the South ordinarily faced northern or western institutions rather than a legally segregated southern college system. The finding therefore concerns probable educational opportunity, not an assertion that no Black southern veteran attended college or that every Black veteran outside the South succeeded. Turner and Bound identified aggregate patterns: substantial gains for Black veterans outside the South and little measurable gain for Black veterans constrained by the southern system.

Time intensified the capacity problem in the South. Veterans returned in a concentrated postwar wave, and colleges faced simultaneous pressure from civilian students and from veterans of all races. White southern institutions could expand within a larger and better financed sector, while Black southern colleges had less physical plant and less access to public and private capital. Turner and Bound did not reduce the southern finding to any single administrative encounter. The aggregate result followed from a system in which segregation limited the set of permissible institutions and capacity limited admission to the institutions that remained. Outside the South, capacity pressure also existed during the postwar surge, but Black veterans there could distribute applications across a broader set of colleges, and Turner and Bound measured substantial gains for that group.

Ira Katznelson presented the broadest exclusion account. In When Affirmative Action Was White: An Untold History of Racial Inequality in Twentieth Century America, published in 2005, Katznelson argued that key programs from the New Deal and Fair Deal era were created in a deeply discriminatory manner and that the gap between Black and white Americans widened despite postwar prosperity. Katznelson examined Social Security, labor law, housing policy, and the GI Bill within a common political account centered on southern congressional power and decentralized administration. Applied to education, the Katznelson account directs attention to the rules governing access rather than to the generosity printed in the statute. A benefit structured through local institutions in a segregated society could privilege white veterans without naming race in the federal eligibility clause. For Black veterans in the South, the Turner and Bound evidence aligns closely with the Katznelson account: little collegiate gain for Black veterans, substantial opportunity for white veterans, and a wider educational gap within the southern states. For Black veterans outside the South, however, Turner and Bound found substantial gains, so the Katznelson account requires a regional qualification when education is the outcome under examination.

Suzanne Mettler presented a contrasting account centered on incorporation. In Soldiers to Citizens: The GI Bill and the Making of the Greatest Generation, published in 2005, Mettler drew on interviews and surveys with hundreds of World War II veterans and argued that beneficiaries experienced the GI Bill provisions as inclusive, fair, and effective in expanding social opportunity. Mettler found that veterans who used education and training benefits became more active in civic organizations and politics, and the publisher summary of Mettler research reported that education and training users joined more civic organizations and participated in more political activities than veterans who did not use those provisions. Mettler argued that treatment as first class citizens communicated that government concerned people in the position of veterans, and beneficiaries responded with greater civic participation. That incorporation evidence complicates an account of universal exclusion, especially for Black veterans outside the South, whose educational gains Turner and Bound documented. Mettler evidence does not erase the southern education result, because civic participation after benefit use and collegiate attainment among Black veterans likely to be limited to the South are different outcomes measured on different populations.

The regional evidence supplies the resolution between the Katznelson and Mettler positions by narrowing the question. Katznelson asked how federal programs interacted with a racial order and southern political power across several policy domains. Mettler asked how beneficiaries experienced a program and how benefit use shaped citizenship and participation. Turner and Bound asked a narrower empirical question about collegiate attainment by race and region after World War II. On that narrower question, neither a claim of uniform widening nor a claim of uniform inclusion survives without qualification. In the South, Turner and Bound found that educational differences between Black and white men were exacerbated. Outside the South, Turner and Bound found substantial positive effects for Black men. A widening account fits the southern education evidence, while an inclusion account fits a substantial part of the nonsouthern education evidence and the civic incorporation that Mettler documented. Region and outcome together determine which account describes the evidence.

Outcome definition also prevents a false contradiction between Katznelson and Mettler. A Black veteran outside the South could gain education, join civic organizations, and regard the benefit as fair, consistent with the evidence and interpretation presented by Mettler, while a Black veteran in the South could hold identical eligibility and gain little college attainment, consistent with Turner and Bound. A white southern veteran could convert the same statute into a degree and a stronger labor market position, widening the southern racial gap that Katznelson emphasized. Each statement can hold because the populations and outcomes differ. The error lies in converting one statement into a national description of racial effects. Turner and Bound force every version of the argument to specify whether the subject is Black veterans in the segregated southern college market or Black veterans outside that market. Once that specification is made, the apparent choice between closing the gap and widening the divide becomes a regional finding rather than a slogan.

The scope of Turner and Bound also sets limits on generalization. The research concerned collegiate outcomes for men connected to World War II service, because the veteran population and the census comparisons in the research centered on men. The finding does not by itself measure vocational training quality, graduate education, benefits for women veterans, Korean War era benefits, or the Post 9/11 GI Bill. Nor does the finding establish that every later veterans benefit reproduced the southern pattern from the 1940s. Legal segregation in public higher education, the capacity of historically Black institutions, federal enforcement, and college finance changed across later decades. The durable analytical lesson from Turner and Bound concerns mechanism rather than automatic repetition: identical eligibility can yield different attainment when applicants face different institutional supply and administration. For Black veterans outside the South after World War II, Turner and Bound documented substantial conversion of eligibility into attainment. For Black veterans likely to be limited to the South, constrained supply inside segregation blocked that conversion.

Home loan benefits followed a related administrative pattern, although the education finding in Turner and Bound did not depend on housing evidence. The GI Bill guaranteed loans through private lenders rather than supplying mortgages directly through a federal lending office, so bank decisions and racially restrictive housing markets shaped access to the guarantee. Katznelson cited an Ebony magazine survey reporting that, in 1947, only 2 of more than 3,200 VA guaranteed home loans in 13 Mississippi cities went to Black borrowers. Katznelson also reported that, in New York and the northern New Jersey suburbs, fewer than 100 of the 67,000 mortgages insured by the GI Bill supported home purchases by nonwhites. Those figures show that housing barriers operated in the South and outside the South, a pattern distinct from the regional education finding. A later account of [segregation and its consequences for fair housing](/2016/02/01/fair-housing-act-segregation-impact/) belongs to a longer housing history in which postwar mortgage access formed one major episode.

The housing context matters to wealth because a subsidized mortgage could convert income into home equity, while rent paid under exclusion from mortgage markets produced no comparable asset. Katznelson argued that unequal access to postwar homeownership widened an already large racial economic gap, and education and housing could reinforce each other when a degree raised earnings and a mortgage converted earnings into property. The regional qualification remains essential even in that broader account. Turner and Bound documented little collegiate gain for Black veterans in the South and substantial gain for Black veterans outside the South, while Katznelson housing figures documented severe mortgage exclusion in Mississippi and in the New York and northern New Jersey suburbs. Housing exclusion outside the South therefore cannot be used to deny the nonsouthern educational gains that Turner and Bound measured, and nonsouthern educational gains cannot be used to minimize mortgage exclusion outside the South. Each domain requires separate evidence, separate geography, and separate measurement.

Measurement of impact changes once distribution receives that level of attention. A program evaluation that counts total degrees among veterans can register success while missing the southern racial result identified by Turner and Bound. A distributional evaluation asks which veterans gained, where those veterans could study, which institutions possessed seats, and whether a comparison group gained faster during the same period. In the South, white veterans gained access to a segregated institutional sector closed to Black veterans, so relative Black progress failed even under a generous federal subsidy. Outside the South, Black veterans gained substantially, according to Turner and Bound, so relative and absolute interpretations must again be separated by region. The central fact remains the interaction between a race neutral federal formula and unequal local conditions. Turner and Bound showed that the interaction produced substantial gains for Black veterans outside the South and little measurable collegiate gain for Black veterans likely to be limited to the South, with a wider Black and white educational divide among southern men as the consequence.

## Finding Four: Completion Under the Modern Program

Completion posed a measurement problem long before completion posed a verdict. The Department of Veterans Affairs recorded benefit payments with precision, because payment was the administrative act, but payment records alone could not answer whether a veteran who drew benefits in a given term ever earned a certificate or a degree. A paid term might lead to graduation, transfer, a pause, or departure without a credential, and the payment file treated each path alike. For the Post-9/11 GI Bill, that gap mattered from the first cohorts onward, because public argument often moved directly from spending totals to assumptions about waste or success. The records-matching projects examined in this section changed the evidentiary basis of that argument. By linking benefit records held by the Department of Veterans Affairs to enrollment and completion records held by the National Student Clearinghouse, researchers could follow cohorts across institutions and interruptions and count credentials wherever a reporting institution conferred a credential. Readers tracing the benefit structure behind the cohorts can consult [a detailed guide to the Post-9/11 GI Bill of 2008](/2019/03/01/post-911-gi-bill-2008-guide/) for the eligibility tiers and payment rules that framed enrollment choices.

The first of the two projects, the Million Records Project, was published by Student Veterans of America in 2014 in partnership with the Department of Veterans Affairs and the National Student Clearinghouse. The project drew a random sample of Montgomery GI Bill and Post-9/11 GI Bill beneficiary records for veterans who used benefits between 2002 and 2010, and the partners matched that sample against Clearinghouse enrollment and completion data. Student Veterans of America reported that the full sample contained 859,297 records, and that 70,382 records, or 8.2 percent as reported by Student Veterans of America, had a first enrollment date on or after January 1, 2011 and were excluded from outcome analysis because insufficient time had passed for a credential to be earned. The analyzed group therefore numbered 788,915 veterans, a group that Student Veterans of America described as representing approximately 22 percent of the student veteran population that received GI Bill benefits during the period covered, with the comparison population defined as all benefit recipients between 2002 and 2010. The Million Records Project was therefore not a pure Post-9/11 GI Bill study, because Montgomery GI Bill users formed part of the sample, and any rate from the project describes the combined population unless a subgroup result is stated separately.

Within that combined and time-limited frame, the Million Records Project found that 407,483 veterans in the analyzed group of 788,915 had attained a postsecondary credential ranging from a vocational certificate to a doctorate, for an overall completion rate of 51.7 percent reported by Student Veterans of America. The comparison placed beside that rate determines what the rate means. William Hubbard of Student Veterans of America, as quoted in reporting by Diverse: Issues in Higher Education, placed the 51.7 percent veteran completion rate against 56 percent for traditional students and 43 percent for nontraditional peers, with both comparison figures drawn from other research cited in that reporting. On that comparison, benefit users finished at a rate modestly below the rate for traditional students, defined in the comparison as students following the conventional path from secondary school to college, and clearly above the rate for nontraditional peers, defined as students whose enrollment was shaped by age, work, or delayed entry. The honest reading is therefore neither triumph nor failure in isolation, because the majority rate sat between the two comparison rates supplied at release.

The distribution of completions added texture that a single rate concealed. Reporting by Diverse: Issues in Higher Education on the Million Records Project stated that about 72 percent of the student veterans who graduated did so from public institutions, compared with about 15.5 percent who attended private institutions and about 13 percent who attended for-profit institutions in the same completer population reported by Student Veterans of America. Inside Higher Ed, summarizing the report, added that completion rates were higher at public and private nonprofit institutions than at for-profit institutions, with sector serving as the basis of comparison and with a caution that Clearinghouse coverage for the for-profit sector was less complete than coverage for other sectors.

Time to degree complicated any simple judgment based on completion alone. The Million Records Project, as reported by Student Veterans of America, found an average time to completion of 5.1 years for an initial associate degree, with a median of four years and a most frequent value of two years, and an average of 6.3 years for an initial bachelor degree, with a median of five years and a most frequent value of four years, each figure describing the group of completers analyzed for time to completion rather than all benefit users. A release from Student Veterans of America summarized the pattern in rounder terms, stating that a majority of completers finished a bachelor degree within four to six years and an associate degree within four years. The gap between mean and median in the project figures pointed to a skewed distribution, in which a minority of very long paths pulled the average upward while the typical completer finished faster than the average suggested. Student Veterans of America also supplied a concrete mechanism for interrupted paths. Wayne Robinson, president and chief executive officer of Student Veterans of America, stated at the release of the Million Records Project that a single deployment could interrupt a student veteran education for at least nine to 13 months, after which veterans returned to the classroom and completed, a statement that treated deployment interruption followed by return as part of the population experience rather than as an exception.

Prior learning sat inside the time figures in ways that aggregate reporting only partly revealed. Of the 407,483 veterans with a completion in the Million Records Project, Student Veterans of America reported that 79.5 percent, or 323,798 veterans, earned a certificate or degree after enrolling in Department of Veterans Affairs benefits, while 40.8 percent, or 166,073 veterans in the same completer group, had completed at least one postsecondary program before using any GI Bill benefits, with the overlap between the two groups explained in part by veterans who held an earlier credential and then earned another credential. Student Veterans of America further reported that approximately two thirds, or 64.6 percent, of the 166,073 veterans with a prior completion had first earned an associate degree or a lower level credential, and that nearly half, or 49.6 percent, of that same prior completion group went on to use GI Bill benefits to earn another postsecondary degree. Student Veterans of America also disclosed a measurement caution, noting that the Department of Veterans Affairs used an artificial cutoff date to classify whether a degree came before or after benefit enrollment, a practice that may have overstated completions after benefit enrollment and understated completions before benefit use, in the wording of the report.

### Did veterans using the modern GI Bill finish at rates comparable to other students?

Yes, within the limits of each comparison. The Million Records Project found completion close to the rate for traditional students and above the rate for other nontraditional students, and NVEST found completion slightly above the rate for students overall, with persistence raising the combined success rate further.

The second records-matching project narrowed the lens to the modern program alone. The National Veteran Education Success Tracker, known as NVEST, was published by Student Veterans of America in 2017 as a partnership among Student Veterans of America, the National Student Clearinghouse, and the Veterans Benefits Administration of the Department of Veterans Affairs, as described in the NVEST report. The NVEST subset consisted of 853,111 Post-9/11 GI Bill veteran beneficiaries who used benefits between August 1, 2009 and December 31, 2013, with dependents who used transferred benefits excluded by a Department of Veterans Affairs filter described in the NVEST report, so that the subset contained veterans rather than spouses or children. The Clearinghouse matched enrollment records for 822,327 of the 853,111 beneficiaries, a coverage rate of 96.4 percent reported by the NVEST Project, and the match drew on Clearinghouse data current through September 2015, with outcomes observed through that match window. The remaining 30,784 beneficiaries without a Clearinghouse enrollment record, as counted by NVEST, most likely attended a program outside Clearinghouse reporting or used benefits for on the job training or apprenticeships, explanations offered by the NVEST authors rather than treated as evidence of noncompletion.

Against that denominator, NVEST reported a postsecondary completion rate of 53.6 percent, based on 440,441 completion records found among the 822,327 beneficiaries with enrollment records in the NVEST subset. The Hechinger Report, in coverage of NVEST, placed the 53.6 percent veteran completion figure beside 52.9 percent for students overall, with students overall serving as the comparison group in that coverage and with the veteran figure described as seven tenths of a percentage point higher. A summary prepared by the Institute for Veterans and Military Families at Syracuse University offered a second comparison frame, placing a student veteran completion rate of 54 percent, the rounded NVEST rate as presented in that summary, against 53 percent for the national student population and 39 percent for adult learners who first enrolled at age 25 or older, with each comparison group named in that summary. NVEST then added a category that a completion rate alone omitted. The NVEST report counted 18.0 percent of the subset as enrolled in an academic term between January 1, 2015 and September 1, 2015 without a prior completion recorded, and combined completion and continued enrollment into a success rate of 71.6 percent for the NVEST subset, with success defined by the NVEST authors as completion or enrollment in the term preceding the data match. NVEST also reported an attrition rate of 28.4 percent for the same subset, divided into 19.8 percent who completed the last term and did not re-enroll and 8.6 percent who left during a term, with the full subset as denominator.

Credential production gave the completion rate a concrete scale. After NVEST filtered out completions earned before Post-9/11 GI Bill use, the NVEST report counted 347,564 student veterans who completed a postsecondary certificate or degree after using Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits, and 87,018 of that group, or 25.0 percent as reported by NVEST, earned multiple certificates or degrees, producing a total of 453,508 postsecondary certificates or degrees that the Post-9/11 GI Bill at least in part helped to fund, in the count presented by the NVEST report. Bachelor degrees accounted for 162,567 of the credentials earned after benefit use in the NVEST count, followed by 100,760 associate degrees and 67,928 master degrees, with each count drawn from the NVEST tally of credentials earned after benefit use. The NVEST report also stated that 63.4 percent of the completed degrees in that tally stood at the bachelor degree level or higher, with completed degrees after benefit use serving as the denominator.

Enrollment intensity explained much of the distance between veteran time to degree and conventional expectations built around uninterrupted full time study. NVEST measured pace by counting academic terms rather than calendar years, because a calendar measure counted breaks and leaves as enrolled time and thereby penalized veterans for periods outside the classroom, as the NVEST authors explained. Under the term method described by NVEST, four full time academic terms formed the normative benchmark for an associate degree and eight academic terms formed the normative benchmark for a bachelor degree, with both benchmarks stated by the NVEST authors as full time semester equivalents. The NVEST distribution for an initial associate degree peaked at six academic terms, against the four term normative benchmark stated by NVEST, and the distribution for an initial bachelor degree peaked between nine and 11 academic terms, against the eight term normative benchmark stated by NVEST for semester based programs. Part time enrollment supplied the most direct explanation for the extra terms. NVEST reported that only 12.6 percent of the initial degree group analyzed for enrollment intensity, a group of 391,910 student veterans in the NVEST analysis, held full time enrollment status for every academic term, while 55.4 percent of the same analyzed group held an enrollment intensity score of 0.75 or higher, with the score defined by NVEST as average enrollment status across terms on a scale in which full time enrollment equaled one. The average enrollment intensity for the analyzed group was 0.74 as reported by NVEST, with a median of 0.77 and a most frequent score of 1.00 in the same NVEST analysis, a pattern in which the typical veteran studied at or near full time intensity across a career while a substantial minority mixed full time, three quarter time, half time, and less than half time terms.

Withdrawal and return formed a second pace pattern that distinguished veteran enrollment from a simple dropout narrative. NVEST reported that 54.2 percent of the entire NVEST subset had at least one term that ended in withdrawal before the term finished, and the NVEST authors reported small effect sizes for differences by duty status and by branch of service in that analysis. Completion among the withdrawal group contradicted an assumption that a withdrawn term predicted departure. NVEST reported that 52.3 percent of student veterans with at least one withdrawal before the end of a term completed a postsecondary certificate or degree, compared with 54.4 percent of student veterans with no withdrawals in the same NVEST comparison, a gap of about two percentage points between the two groups as tabulated by NVEST.

Military training credit and other prior credit formed the least visible part of the completion story, because the matched datasets captured credentials and enrollment far better than credit awarded for learning outside a classroom. The mechanism for such credit existed independently of the two projects. Guidance from the Department of Veterans Affairs explained that a veteran requests a military transcript through the branch of service, that the transcript records credit recommendations from the American Council on Education for military occupational specialty training, advanced individual training, military jobs, and completed courses, and that each school decides how many of the recommended credits to accept, with the decision rule stated in Department of Veterans Affairs guidance rather than in either records-matching project. That institutional discretion produced variation that neither project could summarize as a national rate, since a recommendation accepted as degree credit at one institution might be accepted only as elective credit or refused at another. NVEST confronted the data limit directly. The NVEST authors observed that the early portion of the associate degree distribution might reflect credit awarded for military training reducing the number of terms required, and then stated that schools did not report credit awarded for experiential learning, including military training, to the Clearinghouse or to the Department of Veterans Affairs, so the hypothesis could not be explored further with the NVEST dataset, in the methodological account given in the NVEST report. The Million Records Project evidence on credentials earned before benefit use, set out earlier in this section, supplied the closest available proxy, because prior certificates and degrees represented prior learning that institutions and the benefit system had already recognized in credential form.

Earnings evidence for modern completers remained thinner than completion evidence through June 2019, and the honest treatment of earnings begins with that asymmetry. Neither the Million Records Project nor NVEST linked benefit records to wage records, so neither project measured the earnings of Post-9/11 GI Bill completers directly. NVEST instead treated completion as a foundation for economic return and cited general education earnings research as the bridge. The NVEST report cited College Board research by Sandy Baum, Jennifer Ma, and Kathleen Payea from 2013, which reported that in 2011 a worker with a bachelor degree earned a median of 21,100 dollars more per year than a worker with only a high school diploma, with the comparison group defined as workers whose highest attainment was a high school diploma in the College Board figures for 2011. That premium described workers generally, not veterans in particular. A Department of Veterans Affairs summary published through VA News in January 2018 offered a veteran framed figure, reporting that veterans with a bachelor degree earned an average of 17,000 dollars more per year and that veterans with advanced degrees earned nearly 30,000 dollars more per year, in a summary that presented Student Veterans of America findings without a matched causal design and without isolating the Post-9/11 GI Bill as the cause of the difference. A separate federal longitudinal source pointed in a consistent direction on attainment. Veterans Education Success, in a November 2018 analysis of the Beginning Postsecondary Students study of the Department of Education, reported that 28 percent of veterans who first enrolled in 2011 to 2012 had earned a certificate or associate degree by 2014, compared with 23 percent of nonveteran peers in the same cohort, and that 20 percent of veterans in that cohort had left without a degree by 2014, compared with 40 percent of nonveteran peers in the same cohort.

Placed side by side, the two records-matching projects support a bounded conclusion about the modern program through the cohorts available by June 2019. Benefit users in the combined Million Records Project population completed at 51.7 percent against 56 percent for traditional students and 43 percent for nontraditional peers in the comparisons supplied at release, and Post-9/11 GI Bill users in NVEST completed at 53.6 percent against 52.9 percent for students overall in the comparison presented by The Hechinger Report, with each rate inseparable from the comparison group, denominator, and observation window that produced the rate. Longer elapsed paths reflected deployment interruptions of nine to 13 months described by Student Veterans of America, mixed intensity enrollment documented in NVEST, withdrawn terms followed by return and completion in a majority of the withdrawal group tabulated by NVEST, and family and employment obligations in a population in which more than half of the subset, as reported by NVEST, had passed age 22 at initial enrollment. Completion under the modern program, measured against students with similar age, work, and enrollment patterns rather than against an idealized uninterrupted student, appeared broadly comparable, and the credential counts and the general earnings premium attached to completed degrees in the College Board research cited by NVEST gave that comparability material weight without converting a descriptive rate into a causal claim about the benefit alone.

## Finding Five: Where the Money Went

Finding Five concerns destination. The Post-9/11 GI Bill made an eligible veteran the bearer of a portable entitlement, and an institution received payment after the veteran enrolled. Destination therefore recorded choices made inside a market shaped by price, program supply, admissions practice, marketing reach, and federal rules. During the early program years, proprietary institutions received a share of benefit dollars far ahead of the sector share of all college students, and spending for each veteran at proprietary institutions ran at roughly twice the spending for each veteran at public institutions, according to analyses by the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions. A benefit could expand access in aggregate and deliver a large part of the investment to institutions where investigators documented weak completion.

Department of Veterans Affairs figures reported by the Center for Public Integrity described the imbalance in the first year of payments. Between August 2009 and July 2010, public institutions received more than 696 million dollars to educate 203,790 veterans, an average of about 3,418 dollars for each student, according to the Center for Public Integrity account of the Veterans Affairs data. Proprietary institutions collected more than 639 million dollars for 76,746 veterans, an average of about 8,337 dollars for each student, according to the same account. Public institutions educated almost three times as many veterans and received only a modestly larger total.

Later tabulations preserved the same shape as spending expanded. The majority staff of the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions, chaired by Senator Tom Harkin of Iowa, reported that proprietary institutions received about 1.63 billion dollars, or about 37 percent, of the roughly 4.4 billion dollars distributed in the 2010 to 2011 academic year, in figures reported by the Chronicle of Higher Education. The same account reported that proprietary institutions enrolled 25 percent of student veterans. A Senate committee summary of the first year, cited in a Brookings Institution account, gave a closely related division, with veterans at proprietary colleges claiming nearly 36.5 percent of benefits while proprietary colleges enrolled 23.3 percent of beneficiaries.

A follow up analysis released by the Senate committee majority staff in 2014 extended the series. The committee reported that total Post-9/11 GI Bill spending rose from 1.75 billion dollars in 2009 to 2010 to 4.17 billion dollars in 2012 to 2013, while the amount received by proprietary institutions rose from 640 million dollars to 1.7 billion dollars. The committee also reported that the eight largest proprietary recipients received 2.9 billion dollars between 2009 and 2014 and received 23 percent, or 975 million dollars, of all benefits in 2012 to 2013. A small number of large proprietary operators absorbed almost a quarter of a national benefit in a single year.

Enrollment shares moved in the same direction. The 2014 Senate committee analysis reported that veterans attending public institutions fell from 62 percent of Post-9/11 GI Bill users in 2009 to 50 percent in 2013, while veterans enrolling at proprietary institutions rose from 23 percent to 31 percent of total enrollees. Total enrollment at the eight largest proprietary recipients declined during the broader period, according to the committee, and veteran enrollment at those recipients rose by amounts ranging from 61 percent to 657 percent. Growth in the program could explain growth in veteran enrollment across all sectors. Growth alone had more difficulty accounting for a sector gaining veterans while losing students overall.

Price accounted for part of the dollar gap. The 2014 Senate committee analysis reported an average taxpayer cost of 7,972 dollars to send a veteran to a proprietary college for a year, compared with 3,914 dollars at a public college or university. A Government Accountability Office study of 2011 spending reported an average payment of 10,000 dollars for each of the 99,000 student veterans served by proprietary institutions, compared with 5,000 dollars on average for the 174,000 student veterans served by public institutions. The two pairs of figures measured different payment bundles in different years. Read together, the figures supported a firm conclusion. The public paid roughly twice as much for each veteran in the proprietary sector.

The wider federal aid pattern supplied context. The 2012 Senate committee investigation reported that in 2010 the proprietary sector enrolled 10 percent of students, received 25 percent of total taxpayer funded student aid dollars, and accounted for almost 50 percent of student loans in default. That comparison covered federal student aid in general rather than veterans benefits alone. Veterans benefits intensified the pattern because the Post-9/11 benefit was comparatively generous, because eligibility rested on military service rather than financial need, and because a veteran brought a large, reliable federal payment that counted differently from the main federal student aid programs.

Completion evidence gave the allocation pattern its force. The 2012 investigation examined thirty large proprietary education companies and reported that 54 percent of students who enrolled in 2008 to 2009 had left without a degree or certificate by the middle of 2010. Among students seeking associate degrees at the examined institutions, the investigation reported that about 63 percent had left without a degree. The investigation covered students in general at the examined companies rather than veterans alone. A veteran who left without a credential had consumed part of a finite earned benefit and part of the transition period after military service, and a withdrawal did not restore the tuition payment to the Treasury.

The Government Accountability Office added a caution. In a 2013 study of spending patterns, the Government Accountability Office reported that outcome data in the available records were not specific to student veterans, and the study used retention and graduation rates for all students at institutions in each sector. A summary of the study reported a mixed picture, with proprietary institutions in one highly funded group tending to have higher graduation rates than public institutions and also higher loan default rates, alongside higher proportions of low income and minority students. The caution did not erase the Senate findings on withdrawal and defaults at the thirty examined companies. The record supported disproportionate spending and documented risk, and a weaker claim about every veteran at every proprietary institution.

### Why did a veteran enrollment carry extra commercial value for a proprietary college?

A veteran paid through Department of Veterans Affairs benefits counted toward the nonfederal side of the revenue calculation, even though taxpayers supplied the money. Each such enrollment therefore helped a proprietary college preserve access to nine enrollments financed through Title IV aid, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in 2011.

The mechanism sat in a revenue test written for another program. The 90/10 rule, a provision associated with the Higher Education Act and applied to proprietary institutions, barred a proprietary college from deriving more than 90 percent of revenue from federal student aid programs administered by the Department of Education under Title IV, according to statements by Senator Richard Durbin of Illinois and Representative Steve Cohen of Tennessee in support of reform legislation. The other 10 percent had to come from sources outside Title IV. Senator Durbin and Representative Cohen described the rule as a market test, a requirement that an institution attract revenue from payers outside the main federal aid system. Failure risked eligibility for Title IV funds.

Veterans benefits fell outside the defined category. Benefits administered through the Department of Veterans Affairs, and tuition assistance administered through the Department of Defense, did not count toward the 90 percent side, because the governing text counted Title IV programs rather than federal education spending as a whole, according to the Durbin and Cohen reform statements. A dollar of Post-9/11 GI Bill tuition therefore landed on the 10 percent side of the ledger, the side reserved in the design of the rule for non Title IV revenue. A private payment could signal willingness to pay from private resources. A Veterans Affairs payment signaled that Congress had awarded an earned benefit and that a veteran had chosen an approved institution. The accounting made no distinction between the two signals.

Holly Petraeus, assistant director for servicemember affairs at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, reduced the arithmetic to a ratio in 2011. For each servicemember or veteran enrolled at a proprietary college and paying with military education funds, the college could enroll nine other students paying with Title IV money, a formulation summarized by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in September 2011. A college close to the ceiling faced a compliance problem measured across the entire revenue base. One veteran enrollment supplied tuition and supplied headroom under the ceiling for a block of Title IV financed enrollments. The marginal value included preservation of eligibility for the wider student body. No other ordinary student category combined a large federal payment with an accounting position outside the ceiling.

Dependence on that position grew among some large operators. The 2014 Senate committee analysis estimated that at least four large proprietary companies received between 43 percent and 63 percent of the revenue counted on the 10 percent side of the calculation from Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits. Reporting by Inside Higher Education on the earlier Senate findings in 2011 recorded Senator Tom Harkin describing the exclusion of GI Bill money from the limit as a technicality that proprietary colleges had exploited aggressively, and the same reporting noted that many providers would have faced difficulty meeting the standard if GI Bill money and Pentagon tuition assistance had counted toward the limit. The estimates did not prove the motive behind any individual enrollment. The estimates established that the incentive existed and that several large companies built substantial regulatory compliance on the rewarded category.

The counting rule interacted with the benefit design of the 2008 statute. The Post-9/11 GI Bill paid tuition and fees at an approved institution, and the benefit followed the veteran across public, private nonprofit, and proprietary sectors. Sector neutrality formed part of the design. A veteran could select a public university, a community college, a private nonprofit college, a technical program, or a proprietary institution, subject to approval rules and payment caps. A generous and portable benefit enlarged the pool of students carrying substantial federal purchasing power. The counting rule attached a regulatory premium to one part of that pool at one class of institution. Public and nonprofit institutions had no 90/10 calculation to satisfy, and a veteran enrollment at a public institution carried tuition value without a compliance premium.

Recruitment supplied the behavioral link between incentive and distribution. The 2012 Senate committee investigation, based on internal documents from thirty proprietary education companies, reported that the examined companies employed 35,202 recruiters in 2010, compared with 3,512 career services staff and 12,452 support services staff. The investigation reported that the same companies spent 4.2 billion dollars on marketing and recruiting during the period examined, equal to 22.7 percent of revenue. Internal documents described by the committee showed managers creating an atmosphere organized around enrollment quotas, with discipline and termination following missed targets. The committee summarized tactics that misled prospective students about program cost, aid obligations, time to completion, completion rates, job placement rates, credit transfer, and accreditation status.

Military recruitment occupied a specific place inside that apparatus. The 2012 Senate committee investigation reported that internal documents showed some institutions pursuing military benefits into settings serving wounded and medically vulnerable servicemembers, including recruitment at wounded warrior centers and veterans hospitals. Holly Petraeus described related accounts in prepared remarks connected with a 2012 attorneys general announcement on GI Bill lead generators. Petraeus stated that education service officers at bases and National Guard units described pressure from recruiters at certain proprietary institutions, including efforts to secure tables at job fairs, offers to sponsor unit events, and repeated campaigns for access to unit members. Petraeus also reported cases in which recruiters obtained exclusive access to wounded warrior barracks while presenting the contact as educational counseling.

Lead generation extended recruitment beyond campus staff. A settlement announced in June 2012 by twenty state attorneys general concerned a website that used the GI Bill name and presented an official appearance while directing veterans toward a limited set of institutions, most of them proprietary, according to the Department of Veterans Affairs account of the settlement. The settlement required the marketing company operating the site to surrender the domain to the Department of Veterans Affairs and to pay 2.5 million dollars to the states, according to the Alabama Attorney General announcement. A veteran searching for benefit guidance could begin college selection inside a channel built by sellers rather than inside a neutral counseling setting. Recruitment at veteran service events, sponsorship around military units, and official looking websites formed parts of one documented pattern.

Sector representatives answered with an explanation grounded in access, scheduling, and choice. Steve Gunderson, president of the Association of Private Sector Colleges and Universities, described military and veteran students at proprietary institutions as adults with employment, spouses, and children rather than as new school leavers living on a residential campus, in remarks reported by Diverse Education after a congressional hearing. Gunderson argued that proprietary institutions designed tailored courses, many delivered online, that could move a veteran toward employment on a faster schedule, and Gunderson cited deployment policies that allowed a student to withdraw and return as service obligations changed. Gunderson also described advisers evaluating military experience for credit for prior learning, and Gunderson cited a 2010 RAND Corporation survey finding higher satisfaction with academic advising at proprietary colleges.

Open enrollment formed the second part of the sector case. Statements by the Association of Private Sector Colleges and Universities in litigation over gainful employment rules, reported by Inside Higher Education in 2015, described students served by the sector as new traditional students juggling family, work, and school, and the association argued that regulation complicated efforts to supply career focused education to that population. Harris Miller, president of the association during debate over legislation in 2010, argued for expanded choice in online and distance programs and stated support for provisions increasing access for rural and disabled students, according to reporting by Law.com. Bob Cohen, a spokesman for the association, argued in 2011 that the proprietary share of spending had changed little from the prior year while the sector educated more students, in comments reported by the Chronicle of Higher Education. Gunderson dismissed the 2012 investigation as inaccurate.

The sector case identified features that a distribution table alone could not evaluate. Rolling start dates, online delivery, evening scheduling, credit for occupational training, and admissions without selective academic screens could fit a veteran entering college after years away from formal education. Preference for flexible programs could explain part of the demand. Preference could not, by itself, explain recruitment at wounded warrior settings, marketing and recruiting absorbing 22.7 percent of revenue at the companies examined by the Senate committee, or a compliance benefit that no public institution gained from a veteran enrollment. Access and incentive operated together in the documented record, and recognition of access does not refute the incentive.

The intended and unintended parts of the outcome require the same separation. The Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008 expanded eligibility and raised the value of the benefit, and the Chronicle of Higher Education account of the early Senate report recorded spending rising from 1.75 billion dollars in 2009 to 2010 to 4.4 billion dollars in 2010 to 2011 after eligibility expansions in 2008 and 2010. Senator Tom Harkin stated during the early committee work that legislators had not anticipated the proprietary sector pursuing the new funding as aggressively as the sector did, in a remark reported by the Chronicle of Higher Education in September 2011. The design directed the benefit to the veteran and permitted use across sectors. The design did not assign a quarter or a third of the money to proprietary institutions or direct colleges to treat Veterans Affairs revenue as a compliance reserve. The distribution emerged after the portable benefit met a preexisting revenue definition in [the Higher Education Act framework governing federal student aid](/2014/09/15/higher-education-act-1965-guide/) and after institutions built recruitment systems capable of reaching veterans first.

Drafters in 2008 wrote a benefit statute inside a federal aid system that already contained the 90/10 definition, and the interaction was foreseeable once the size of the new benefit became clear. Foreseeability after enactment differs from purpose at enactment, and congressional investigations treated the distribution as a defect to be corrected. Senator Richard Durbin authored a proposal known by the POST Act acronym to include Post-9/11 GI Bill funding inside the federal side of the revenue rule, according to a Senate statement issued with the 2014 committee analysis. Parallel proposals by Senator Durbin, Senator Tom Harkin, and Representative Steve Cohen would have moved the test toward an 85/15 standard and counted veterans and military benefits as federal revenue, according to statements by the sponsors. The proposals named the mechanism rather than the student, and assumed that a federal dollar should count as a federal dollar regardless of the administering agency.

Finding Five therefore qualifies the success story told by aggregate benefit use. Part of the movement of billions of federal dollars into programs chosen by veterans represented access that public institutions had not supplied on the schedule or in the format a veteran sought. Another part represented a transfer shaped by an accounting definition. The Senate committee documented proprietary institutions taking roughly a quarter to more than a third of benefit dollars across early analyses, while the sector enrolled about a tenth of all college students in 2010. The Government Accountability Office documented a payment for each veteran at proprietary institutions at roughly twice the payment at public institutions in 2011, and the Senate investigation documented withdrawal by 54 percent of students at thirty examined companies by the middle of 2010. The money went, in substantial part, where a federal definition made a veteran most valuable as revenue, and that destination forms part of the outcome of the 2008 statute.

## Limits, Disputes, and the Closing Assessment

A fair measure of the GI Bills has to hold two records side by side. One record contains expanded enrollment, completed degrees, occupational training, and earnings gains for large numbers of veterans. The other record contains doors that stayed closed, preparation that schooling before service had failed to supply, dollars that reached institutions with weak results, and costs that a benefit check did not cover. Placing the second record beside the first does not erase the gains described in earlier sections, but placement does change the meaning of those gains. A program can raise attainment on average and distribute opportunity unevenly at the same time. The statutes from 1944 onward deserve assessment on both scales, because the text of each law promised eligibility on the basis of service, while delivery depended on colleges, state agencies, local officials, labor markets, and family resources that service alone did not equalize.

The clearest failure involved race and region under the 1944 statute. The law was race neutral in its statutory terms, as Sarah Turner and John Bound emphasized in a study published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, and more than one million Black men had served during the Second World War, in the figures cited in that study. Eligibility on paper, however, passed through segregated colleges, state approving agencies, and local Veterans Administration offices. In the South, Black applicants were barred from most white institutions, and Turner and Bound counted about one hundred public and private institutions listed for Black students, with more than a quarter of those institutions classified as junior colleges offering no bachelor degree. Historically Black colleges carried the resulting demand with limited buildings, limited faculty lines, and state funding that lagged behind funding for white institutions.

The quantitative record matched the institutional record. Turner and Bound estimated that the combination of wartime service and GI Bill availability raised college attainment for white men by about three tenths of a year of college and increased college completion by about five percentage points. For Black men born outside the South, the same combination raised attainment by about four tenths of a year. For Black men born in the South, the authors found few gains in collegiate attainment. The authors concluded that, for men likely to be limited to the South in collegiate choices, the GI Bill exacerbated rather than narrowed economic and educational differences between Black and white veterans. Ira Katznelson, in When Affirmative Action Was White, published in 2005, placed that outcome inside a wider structure of federal policy administered through state and local hands, where national benefits and local discrimination operated together. David H. Onkst, writing in the Journal of Social History in 1998, documented a related pattern in the Deep South, where Black veterans faced steering toward vocational training and away from college, along with outright denial in some cases. Segregated administration therefore sorted veterans into different kinds of education with different returns, following the racial order that the federal text had declined to disturb.

Preparation formed a second barrier, distinct from admissions policy and just as consequential. A tuition benefit presupposes a student ready to enter the institution charging the tuition. Many veterans left segregated and underfunded schools long before enlistment, and military service did not repair gaps in literacy, mathematics, or secondary credentials. Onkst reported that the average educational attainment of southern Black veterans in the period stood at about the fifth grade level, a figure that captures the distance between a college entitlement and college readiness for a large share of that population. Colleges that required a high school diploma or an equivalency pass applied a standard that appeared neutral and functioned as a filter. Veterans without secondary preparation were directed toward vocational courses, remedial paths that consumed entitlement, or no training at all. Congress later acknowledged the gap in part. A Congressional Research Service primer on veterans education programs described amendments enacted in 1967 that allowed veterans without a high school diploma, or veterans needing additional secondary courses, to receive allowances for those courses without reducing regular entitlement. The amendment also confirmed the original design flaw. A benefit built around postsecondary enrollment reached least reliably the veterans whose prewar schooling had been poorest, with the heaviest weight on Black veterans from southern states, rural veterans, and veterans who had left school early for paid work.

A third limit concerned the destination of the money. Education benefits operate as a subsidy that an eligible student carries to an approved institution, and approval has never guaranteed quality. The postwar program drew opportunistic trade schools that charged high tuition for training of little value, a pattern examined by David Whitman in a Century Foundation history of for-profit college scandal published in 2017 and 2018. Whitman traced cycles in which expanded federal benefits, permissive approval, and aggressive recruiting combined to produce fraud investigations under Presidents Truman and Eisenhower, followed by tighter rules. The pattern returned in altered form under the Post-9/11 GI Bill. The Government Accountability Office reported in 2013 that for-profit institutions received an average Post-9/11 GI Bill payment of about ten thousand dollars for each student veteran served in 2011, compared with about five thousand dollars at public institutions, while public institutions served far more veterans in total. A Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions analysis released in 2014 under Chairman Tom Harkin reported that for-profit colleges received one point seven billion dollars in Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits in the 2012 to 2013 school year, and that eight large for-profit companies received two point nine billion dollars over four years. The same analysis put the average cost of educating a veteran at a for-profit college at seven thousand nine hundred twenty-seven dollars, against three thousand nine hundred fourteen dollars at a public college, in 2012 to 2013. Price combined with the completion record assembled by the Harkin investigation, which found that most associate and bachelor degree students at the for-profit colleges examined left with debt and no diploma, supports a firmer charge. The modern program paid premium prices, at scale, to a sector with poor completion results for many entrants, and entitlement expired whether a credential resulted or not.

Coverage formed a fourth limit, clearest in the Vietnam era benefit. The 1944 design separated tuition payments from a subsistence allowance. The Korean era redesign, retained in 1966, folded support into a single monthly check expected to cover tuition, fees, books, supplies, and living costs at least in part. The Congressional Research Service account of the early Vietnam era program put the initial payment for a single veteran in full-time study at one hundred dollars a month in 1966, rising to two hundred twenty dollars a month by late 1972 after congressional increases. An Educational Testing Service comparison prepared for a Senate committee in 1973 found that, after average tuition, fees, books, and supplies at a four year public institution were deducted, the allowance left insufficient funds for estimated living expenses. The same comparison found that tuition at four year private institutions had risen about fivefold since the Second World War, compared with about two and a half times at public institutions, leaving a veteran at a private institution with a shortfall against educational costs alone. Joshua Angrist and Stacey Chen noted that the Vietnam era stipend in the 1970s, adjusted for inflation, stood close in value to the Second World War package at public institutions. Both findings can stand together. A benefit comparable at a low cost public campus could erode badly against private tuition and against living costs that a single check had to absorb. Erosion by inflation and by institutional price growth therefore operated unevenly, hardest where tuition rose fastest and where a veteran lacked family income to fill the gap. A flat benefit in a market of diverging prices buys a different education in different places, and the difference is a policy result, not an accident of geography.

The limits of delivery lead directly to limits in the evidence, because grand claims about transformation often rest on comparisons that cannot carry the weight placed on those comparisons. Postwar America changed on several tracks at once. Households entered the postwar period with savings accumulated during wartime, when consumer goods had been scarce and employment had been plentiful. Union contracts in major industries converted productivity growth into wage floors, paid vacations, and pensions for large segments of industrial labor. Federal housing policy, through mortgage insurance and veterans loan guarantees, subsidized suburban home purchase and the wealth accumulation attached to ownership. Highways, defense spending, and a long economic expansion supplied jobs for credential holders and for workers without credentials alike. Any account that assigns the entire rise of a broad middle class to education benefits ignores those parallel engines. The benefits plainly contributed, through degrees earned, skills certified, and incomes raised for individual veterans. Contribution and sole causation are different claims, and the historical record supports the first claim with far greater confidence than the second.

### Did the benefits create the middle class?

The honest answer is contribution rather than sole creation. The benefits raised attainment for many veterans, yet postwar mobility also rested on savings accumulated during wartime, union wage structures, housing policy, and rapid economic growth. Attributing the entire expansion to education benefits alone exceeds what cohort evidence can support credibly.

Selection effects complicate the evidence further. Veterans who used education benefits differed from veterans who did not use benefits, and veterans as a group differed from nonveterans, before any classroom effect entered the calculation. Military screening excluded many men with serious health limits or very low tested aptitude. Within the eligible population, the decision to enroll reflected ambition, family encouragement, prior grades, access to information, and freedom from immediate earnings pressure. A veteran able to defer full-time wages for two or four years often started with advantages that a simple comparison credits to the benefit. Suzanne Mettler, in Soldiers to Citizens, published in 2005, reported from survey evidence that veterans who used education and training benefits participated in about fifty percent more civic organizations than veterans who did not use those benefits. Mettler interpreted the gap as an effect of a generous and respectful policy on citizenship. A skeptical reader can accept the association and question the interpretation, since veterans predisposed to join and participate may also have been predisposed to claim benefits and persist in school. Association alone cannot separate the effect of the policy from the characteristics of the veterans who took the policy up.

Usage statistics carry a similar weakness. Administrative counts show how many veterans enrolled, how many dollars flowed, and which institutions received payment. Counts of that kind establish scale with precision, and scale is not impact. A count of benefit users says nothing about how many users would have enrolled without the subsidy, or how earnings would have developed for the same veterans in the absence of the program. The stronger designs in the literature attacked that counterfactual problem directly, through variation that pushed otherwise similar men toward or away from service and benefits. John Bound and Sarah Turner compared attainment across birth cohorts whose exposure to wartime service and benefits differed, producing the estimates for white veterans cited earlier in this section. Turner and Bound exploited regional variation as well, comparing Black veterans born inside and outside the South, where the same federal entitlement met different college systems. For the Korean era, Marcus Stanley used variation in eligibility across cohorts and estimated gains in college completion on the order of five to six percentage points, in the summary given in later work by Angrist and Chen. For the Vietnam era, Angrist and Chen used the draft lottery, a source of variation close to random assignment among men at risk of conscription, and linked lottery outcomes to schooling in the 2000 census. The lottery design found marked schooling gains that the authors attributed to GI Bill use rather than to draft avoidance behavior, paired with earnings effects close to zero in 2000, a result the authors reconciled through a modest return to the subsidized schooling and the flattening of earnings profiles in middle age. Designs of that kind deserve greater weight than raw comparisons between users and nonusers. Designs of that kind also carry boundaries. Cohort comparisons can absorb other changes that moved with the war, from labor demand to college expansion. Lottery estimates describe men whose service hinged on a random number, a population that may not represent volunteers or career personnel. Precision about method therefore narrows the claims that honest assessment can make, without emptying the record of meaning.

Contested readings of the record divide less over whether anything happened than over how much happened, for whom, and at whose expense. The optimist reading, stated most fully by Suzanne Mettler in Soldiers to Citizens, holds that the 1944 benefits ranked among the most successful federal social investments of the twentieth century because generosity, broad eligibility, and respectful administration converted service into education, education into occupational mobility, and mobility into civic participation. Glenn Altschuler and Stuart Blumin, in The GI Bill: A New Deal for Veterans, published in 2009, gave a qualified version of the optimist case, arguing that the bill demolished the contention that higher education belonged mainly to young people from affluent families and helped open an era of greatly expanded access, while also recording that only a small number of Black veterans saw comparable change in prospects. On that reading, the attainment estimates from Bound and Turner and from Turner and Bound confirm a genuine expansion, and the civic evidence from Mettler shows returns that earnings data miss, with magnitudes that compound across millions of lives.

The skeptical reading presses on three points, each attached to a named source or finding. The first point concerns deadweight, the share of subsidy paid for education that would have occurred anyway. Summaries of the Bound and Turner calculation put the share of veteran higher education attributable to the program at about one fifth of the veterans who received higher education under the bill, a figure that leaves a large remainder of subsidized enrollment explained by delayed schooling, rising college demand, and family resources rather than by the benefit alone. The second point concerns capture by institutions. David Whitman documented repeated episodes in which proprietary schools organized recruiting, pricing, and program design around the benefit itself, and the Harkin investigation found aggressive marketing to veterans alongside weak completion in the modern for-profit sector. A working paper by Michael Kofoed and coauthors, reported in 2018, added a pricing test and found that for-profit institutions raised tuition by an average of four hundred thirty-seven dollars in states where the Post-9/11 benefit increased under the 2011 formula change, and cut tuition by an average of one thousand two hundred sixty dollars in states where the benefit fell, while nonprofit tuition showed no comparable movement. The authors reported magnitudes that were relatively small against total tuition. Capture does establish that part of a benefit increase can land in institutional revenue rather than in student purchasing power, a distinction that aggregate spending figures conceal. The third skeptical point concerns earnings. Angrist and Chen found schooling gains from Vietnam era service with earnings effects close to zero in 2000, and the broader literature summarized by those authors found returns to subsidized schooling below the returns suggested by simple comparisons of graduates and nongraduates. On the skeptical reading, the programs redistributed opportunity and conferred dignity and credentials at public cost, while delivering smaller net economic gains than commemoration suggests, and delivering the smallest gains to veterans with the weakest preparation and the fewest acceptable institutions.

The evidence supports a division between settled ground and disputed ground. Settled ground includes the direction of the attainment effect for the Second World War cohort outside the segregated South, the near absence of collegiate gains for southern Black veterans under segregated delivery, the repeated vulnerability of benefit programs to low quality proprietary providers across several eras, and the failure of a flat Vietnam era check to keep pace with private tuition and living costs in the Educational Testing Service comparison of 1973. Disputed ground includes the size of the earnings return across a full working life, the share of postwar middle class expansion properly assigned to education benefits against housing, labor, and macroeconomic causes, the civic effects that Mettler attributed to policy design rather than to selection, and the extent of tuition capture beyond the for-profit sector studied by Kofoed and coauthors. A further boundary deserves plain statement. Evidence for the Post-9/11 GI Bill, as the record stood in 2018, remained thinner than evidence for earlier programs, because completion and earnings follow students over periods longer than the program had then run, and because early analyses relied heavily on payment data and on outcomes for all students at an institution rather than on outcomes for veterans alone. The Government Accountability Office noted in 2013 that success measures in the spending analysis described students generally at the institutions veterans attended, not student veterans specifically. Strong conclusions about the modern program therefore outran the available measurement in a way that strong conclusions about 1944 no longer do.

The closing assessment returns to the claim that organizes the evidence across seven decades of statutes: universal text, uneven delivery. Each major law spoke in general terms to a class defined by service, and each law then passed through institutions that sorted, priced, admitted, excluded, counseled, and recruited. Where public colleges had seats, prices stayed within the benefit, and preparation met admissions standards, the benefit changed lives in measurable ways. Where segregation closed doors, where secondary schooling had failed, where a proprietary recruiter reached a veteran before an honest counselor did, or where tuition outran a flat check, the same entitlement produced debt, delay, expired eligibility, or a credential of doubtful value. Placed inside [the broader history of veterans legislation in the United States](/2019/07/15/us-veterans-legislation-history/), the GI Bills appear less as a single triumph or a single disappointment than as a recurring test of whether a federal promise can survive contact with the institutions charged with honoring the promise. The recurring policy question is therefore not whether the benefit works in the abstract. Evidence from cohort and lottery designs settles that a well delivered benefit raises attainment. The recurring question is where the money lands: in completed degrees and durable skills for veterans across regions, races, and levels of preparation, or in institutional revenue, unmet living costs, and seats that never existed for the veterans holding the entitlement. Judged by that question, the GI Bills stand as a major federal achievement with a documented pattern of unequal execution, and judgment of any successor benefit, on the evidence available through 2018, belongs to the same standard of delivery rather than to the generosity of the statutory text alone.

The record assembled across seven decades of statutes leaves one practical habit for further reading: trace each benefit from statutory text through the institution charged with delivery to the credential, the payment, or the closed door that resulted. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Did the GI Bill increase college attainment?

Yes. John Bound and Sarah Turner, in research published in the Journal of Labor Economics in 2002, estimated that Second World War service combined with education benefits raised college completion for white men by around five to six percentage points and added about 0.23 to 0.28 years of college. Marcus Stanley, in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2003, estimated that the Second World War and Korean War bills together increased total postsecondary attainment among men born between 1921 and 1933 by about 15 to 20 percent. Two different research designs, one based on variation across birth cohorts and one based on a statutory eligibility cutoff, produced estimates of a similar scale, and attainment gains did not arrive only as completed degrees, because additional terms of study also carried occupational value.

### Q: Did Black veterans get GI Bill benefits equally?

Eligibility was equal on paper and attainment was not equal in practice, and the difference turned on region. The statutory terms of the 1944 law were race neutral, and more than one million Black men served during the Second World War and shared eligibility for tuition payments and a stipend. Sarah Turner and John Bound found substantial and positive effects on attainment for Black men born outside the South, where a wider range of institutions admitted Black students. For Black veterans likely to be limited to the South, Turner and Bound found little effect on collegiate outcomes, because segregation closed white institutions and historically Black colleges operated with limited seats and funding. Among southern men, the benefit therefore widened educational differences between Black and white veterans rather than narrowing those differences.

### Q: Did the GI Bill work differently in the South?

Yes, and region explains much of the racial pattern in the evidence. In the South, state law segregated public higher education, white colleges and universities excluded Black applicants, and the separate institutions open to Black students were small, underfunded, and unable to absorb the postwar surge in applications. A tuition benefit attached to admission had little value where no seat existed. Outside the South, Black veterans could apply to a broader set of institutions, and Sarah Turner and John Bound found substantial and positive effects on attainment for Black men born outside the South, alongside substantial gains for white men. Turner and Bound concluded that, for men likely to be limited to the South in collegiate choices, the benefit exacerbated rather than narrowed educational and economic differences between Black and white men.

### Q: Do veterans finish degrees using the GI Bill?

Yes, at rates broadly comparable to similar students in the matched records available by June 2019. The Million Records Project, published by Student Veterans of America in 2014, linked Department of Veterans Affairs benefit records to National Student Clearinghouse records and found that 51.7 percent of the analyzed group of 788,915 veterans had earned a postsecondary credential. The National Veteran Education Success Tracker, published in 2017, narrowed the lens to the Post-9/11 GI Bill and reported a completion rate of 53.6 percent. Completion took longer than conventional timelines suggested, because deployment interrupted study for nine to 13 months in many cases and because many veterans enrolled part time or withdrew for a term and returned.

### Q: Did the GI Bill raise earnings?

The evidence points in two directions, because schooling and service pulled earnings in opposite directions. Joshua Angrist estimated in 1993 that veterans benefits increased schooling by roughly 1.4 years, implying annual earnings approximately six percent higher than expected without the benefits, with the premium concentrated among users who attended college or graduate school. Angrist also estimated in 1990, using the Vietnam era draft lottery, that white veterans earned approximately 15 percent less than comparable nonveterans in the early 1980s, a loss attributed mainly to years of civilian experience forgone during service. Angrist and Stacey Chen found in 2011 that the earnings effect of Vietnam era service stood close to zero by 2000, as the return to added schooling offset the fading cost of lost experience.

### Q: Where does GI Bill money actually go?

A disproportionate share of Post-9/11 GI Bill dollars went to proprietary institutions during the early program years. The majority staff of the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions reported that proprietary institutions received about 1.63 billion dollars, or about 37 percent, of the roughly 4.4 billion dollars distributed in the 2010 to 2011 academic year, while enrolling 25 percent of student veterans. Spending for each veteran at proprietary institutions ran at roughly twice the spending for each veteran at public institutions. Public institutions educated the largest share of veterans throughout the period, and total dollars followed enrollment across all sectors, but the proprietary sector drew a share of money well ahead of the sector share of all college students, which stood at about 10 percent in 2010.

### Q: Did the Korean GI Bill help the same people?

Partly, and the overlap carries a warning. Marcus Stanley used a sharp eligibility cutoff in the Korean War statute, comparing men who began service before January 31, 1955 with men who began service after that date, and estimated an effect of Korean era eligibility on college completion on the order of five to six percentage points, a magnitude consistent with the estimates for the Second World War program. The people helped most, however, resembled the people helped most under the earlier bill. Stanley reported that the impacts of both programs on college attainment were apparently concentrated among veterans from families in the upper half of the distribution of socioeconomic status, because a tuition subsidy released enrollment most readily where preparation, admission, and family resources already stood in place.

### Q: What do economists find about the GI Bill?

Economists find a consistent attainment effect and a mixed earnings effect, reached through designs built to separate the benefit from selection into service. John Bound and Sarah Turner compared birth cohorts whose exposure to wartime service differed for reasons of timing. Marcus Stanley exploited a statutory cutoff date in the Korean War program. Joshua Angrist and Stacey Chen used the Vietnam era draft lottery, a source of variation close to random assignment. Across those designs, subsidized schooling rose by magnitudes on the order of five to six percentage points of college completion. Earnings results depended on the balance between added schooling and lost civilian experience, and distributional results showed gains concentrated among advantaged veterans and, for Black veterans, sharply different results inside and outside the South.

### Q: Did the GI Bill create the American middle class?

Contribution is the defensible claim, and sole creation exceeds the evidence. Education benefits plainly contributed to postwar mobility, through degrees earned, skills certified, and incomes raised for individual veterans, and the attainment research gives that contribution a measurable size. Postwar America, however, changed on several tracks at once. Households entered the postwar period with savings accumulated during wartime, union contracts converted productivity growth into wage floors and pensions, federal housing policy subsidized suburban home purchase and the wealth attached to ownership, and a long economic expansion supplied jobs for credential holders and for workers without credentials alike. An account that assigns the entire rise of a broad middle class to education benefits ignores those parallel engines, and cohort evidence cannot carry a claim of sole causation.

### Q: How many veterans used the original GI Bill for college?

College attendance was a minority share of a very large program. The Department of Veterans Affairs reported that 7.8 million of the 16 million veterans of the Second World War had participated in an education or training program by the end of the original program on July 25, 1956. The Congressional Research Service presented a fuller breakdown from program records: a total veteran population of 15,440,000, total trained of 7,800,000, college trainees of 2,230,000, other school trainees of 3,480,000, on the job trainees of 1,400,000, and on the farm trainees of 690,000. In the peak year identified by the Department of Veterans Affairs, 1947, veterans accounted for 49 percent of college admissions, a share that captures the scale of the campus presence even though most benefit use occurred outside degree programs.

### Q: Did the Vietnam-era GI Bill raise educational attainment?

Yes, on the strongest available design. Joshua Angrist and Stacey Chen, in research published in 2011, used the Vietnam era draft lottery and data from the 2000 census to estimate long term effects of Vietnam era service. Angrist and Chen found marked schooling gains for veterans and argued that the gains reflected use of the Vietnam era GI Bill rather than draft avoidance behavior. The magnitude of the schooling effect was similar to the magnitudes reported for the Second World War and Korean era programs. Take up was substantial as well: drawing on the 2001 Survey of Veterans, Angrist and Chen reported a usage rate of 50 percent for Vietnam era veterans, against 44 percent for Second World War veterans and 42 percent for Korean era veterans among whites.

### Q: What share of Post-9/11 GI Bill money went to for-profit colleges?

About a third or more of the dollars in the early years, against about a quarter of the enrollment. The Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions reported that proprietary institutions received about 1.63 billion dollars, or about 37 percent, of the roughly 4.4 billion dollars distributed in the 2010 to 2011 academic year, while enrolling 25 percent of student veterans. A committee summary of the first year gave a closely related division, with veterans at proprietary colleges claiming nearly 36.5 percent of benefits while proprietary colleges enrolled 23.3 percent of beneficiaries. A follow up analysis in 2014 reported that proprietary institutions received 1.7 billion dollars in 2012 to 2013, and that the eight largest proprietary recipients alone received 23 percent of all benefits in that year.

### Q: Why did the revenue counting rule make veterans valuable to for-profit colleges?

The 90/10 rule barred a proprietary college from deriving more than 90 percent of revenue from federal student aid programs administered under Title IV of the Higher Education Act. The other 10 percent had to come from sources outside Title IV. Benefits administered through the Department of Veterans Affairs fell outside the defined category, so a dollar of Post-9/11 GI Bill tuition landed on the 10 percent side of the ledger. Holly Petraeus of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau stated the arithmetic in 2011: for each servicemember or veteran enrolled and paying with military education funds, a proprietary college could enroll nine other students paying with Title IV money. A veteran enrollment therefore supplied tuition and preserved access to a block of Title IV financed enrollments, a compliance value that no public institution gained from the same enrollment.

### Q: What did the Senate investigation find about recruiting veterans?

The investigation, published by the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions in 2012 and based on internal documents from thirty proprietary education companies, found a recruiting apparatus of unusual scale. The examined companies employed 35,202 recruiters in 2010, compared with 3,512 career services staff and 12,452 support services staff, and spent 4.2 billion dollars on marketing and recruiting, equal to 22.7 percent of revenue. Internal documents showed managers creating an atmosphere organized around enrollment quotas, with discipline and termination following missed targets, and described tactics that misled prospective students about cost, completion rates, and job placement. The investigation also reported that internal documents showed some institutions pursuing military benefits into settings serving wounded and medically vulnerable servicemembers, including recruitment at wounded warrior centers and veterans hospitals.

### Q: Do veterans complete degrees at the same rate as other adult students?

Broadly, yes, and against the closest comparison groups the veteran rates held up well. The Million Records Project reported an overall completion rate of 51.7 percent for benefit users in the combined population analyzed, placed beside 56 percent for traditional students and 43 percent for nontraditional peers in comparisons supplied at release. A summary prepared by the Institute for Veterans and Military Families placed a student veteran completion rate of 54 percent, the rounded National Veteran Education Success Tracker rate, against 53 percent for the national student population and 39 percent for adult learners who first enrolled at age 25 or older. Veterans therefore finished at rates close to students overall and clearly above adult learners, a pattern consistent with the finding that withdrawn terms and part time enrollment lengthened paths without ending study, since 52.3 percent of veterans with at least one withdrawal completed a credential in the NVEST tabulation.

### Q: How did researchers separate the effect of the GI Bill from the postwar boom?

Researchers attacked the counterfactual problem directly, because raw comparisons could not separate a benefit effect from selection, wartime savings, or a booming labor market. Bound and Turner compared attainment across birth cohorts whose exposure to wartime service differed sharply for reasons of timing, so that cohort membership stood in for an assignment that individual ambition did not choose. Stanley exploited a statutory cutoff, comparing men whose service began just before an eligibility date with men whose service began just after that date. Angrist and Chen used draft lottery numbers, which assigned the risk of induction on a basis close to random. Each design compared groups that differed in exposure to service and benefits while resembling each other in background, and each design carried boundaries that narrowed the claims an honest assessment could make.

### Q: Did the Montgomery GI Bill increase college use?

Usage responded to generosity, on the most systematic evidence available. Curtis J. Simon, Sebastian Negrusa, and John T. Warner, in a study published in Economic Inquiry in 2010 using data from 1990 to 2005, estimated that an increase of 10,000 dollars in education benefits increased the probability of Montgomery GI Bill usage by about five percentage points, while the duration of benefit usage was insensitive to benefit levels. Usage across all services stood between 49 percent and 51 percent of eligible veterans in the case study literature. Enrollment and usage measured different decisions: enrollment in the contributory program reached 95 percent of recruits in fiscal 1995 under automatic enrollment and a payroll deduction of 100 dollars a month for 12 months, while usage measured a later decision to claim benefits for actual study, often years after discharge.

### Q: Why did Black veterans in the South gain less from the GI Bill?

Capacity and segregation, operating together, blocked conversion of eligibility into attainment in the South. White colleges and universities in the South excluded Black applicants by law and custom, and the separate institutions open to Black students numbered about one hundred public and private institutions listed for Black students, with more than a quarter classified as junior colleges offering no bachelor degree, in the count given by Turner and Bound. Historically Black colleges carried postwar demand with limited buildings, limited faculty lines, and state funding that lagged behind funding for white institutions. Local administration added steering toward vocational training and away from college, documented by David Onkst in the Deep South. Outside the South, Black veterans reached a wider range of institutions, and Turner and Bound found substantial gains there, which isolates the southern institutional structure as the decisive difference.

### Q: How much did the original GI Bill education benefits cost?

The Congressional Research Service, in a primer on veterans educational assistance programs, reported that the nation spent 14.5 billion dollars to provide education and training to 7.8 million Second World War veterans under the original program, a total expenditure of 1,859 dollars for each beneficiary in the accounting presented in that primer. The usage behind the total was broad rather than narrow: 2,230,000 veterans trained in college programs, 3,480,000 in other schools, 1,400,000 in on the job training, and 690,000 in farm training. The benefit that produced the spending offered 500 dollars a year toward education or technical training, alongside a living stipend, at a time when that sum covered a substantial share of annual charges at many institutions.

### Q: Did the GI Bill narrow racial gaps in education outside the South?

Outside the South, the pattern ran in the opposite direction from the southern result. Turner and Bound estimated that the combination of wartime service and GI Bill availability raised attainment for Black men born outside the South by about four tenths of a year of college, against about three tenths of a year for white men in the estimates summarized in the closing assessment of the evidence. Black veterans outside the South could reach a wider range of institutions, and the measured gains for that group were substantial and positive. Inside the South, by contrast, Turner and Bound found few gains in collegiate attainment for Black men and concluded that the benefit widened differences between Black and white men. Any statement about racial gaps therefore has to name the region, because the evidence supports narrowing pressure outside the South and widening inside the South.
