Romeo and Juliet is a tragedy written inside a fashion. In the middle years of the 1590s, when the play was composed and first staged, England was in the grip of the most intense lyric publishing boom of the century: the sonnet vogue. Between the posthumous printing of Sir Philip Sidney's Astrophil and Stella in 1591 and the cooling of the market near the decade's end, printers issued sonnet sequence after sonnet sequence, until the fourteen-line love poem in linked series had become the era's signature literary object, the form in which an ambitious poet proved seriousness and a reader proved taste. The play does not merely date from that boom. It is built out of the boom's materials. Its Chorus speaks in sonnets, its lovers meet inside a sonnet, its hero opens the action performing the sequence lover's stock postures for an unseen lady, and its heroine closes that performance with a four-word review: "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.109, in G. Blakemore Evans's New Cambridge edition). The verdict argued across this article is plain. The sonnet vogue is not background decoration around the tragedy; it is the tragedy's first language, the fashionable idiom the audience had been trained by a decade of printed sequences to hear, judge, and distrust. Shakespeare rides the fashion for two acts because the fashion is what his first hearers knew best, and then he breaks it, on stage, in full view, so that the breaking itself becomes the play's deepest statement about the distance between performed love and felt love.

That verdict needs the vogue restored in detail, because the cliché account of the play's verse treats its lyricism as a private miracle, Shakespeare inventing intensity in a vacuum. The vacuum never existed. A playgoer of 1595 who had read Samuel Daniel's Delia, Michael Drayton's Ideas Mirrour, Edmund Spenser's Amoretti, Henry Constable's Diana, or any of a dozen rival volumes arrived at the theatre already fluent in the conventions the play would quote, bend, and finally discard: the cruel fair, the suffering servant of love, the oxymoron as a lover's native grammar, the promise that verse defeats time. The sections that follow reconstruct the boom as a publishing event and as an education of the ear, then read the play's sonnets and its fashionable idiom as artefacts of that education, and close by weighing what the play does that no sequence on the bookstalls ever attempted.

## The Sonnet Vogue: What Swept England in the Decade of the Play

The phrase sonnet vogue names a specific and datable phenomenon, not a general Elizabethan fondness for short poems. For most of the sixteenth century the English sonnet was a courtly exercise with a thin print record. Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, and Sir Thomas Wyatt had naturalised the form from Petrarch in the 1530s and 1540s, but their sonnets reached print only in 1557, inside Richard Tottel's Songes and Sonettes, and for three decades afterwards the form slept in miscellanies and manuscripts. What changed in the 1590s was scale and packaging. Poets stopped offering single sonnets and began offering sequences: book-length series of sixty, eighty, a hundred sonnets, framed by a title, a mistress with a classical pseudonym, dedications, commendatory verses, and a loose narrative of suit, refusal, and complaint. The sequence was a product designed for a market, and the market, for roughly eight years, could not get enough of it. Arthur F. Marotti, in his essay "Love Is Not Love: Elizabethan Sonnet Sequences and the Social Order" (ELH, 1982), counts the phenomenon as a genuine cultural event, a sudden convergence of print economics, court ambition, and erotic lyric that made the sequence the decade's defining genre.

### What exactly was the sonnet vogue?

The sonnet vogue was the boom of roughly 1591 to 1597 in which English poets issued linked sonnet sequences in numbers, following the posthumous print success of Sidney's Astrophil and Stella. Dozens of volumes by Daniel, Drayton, Spenser, Constable, Lodge, and Watson made the fourteen-line love sequence the fashionable commodity of the decade, imitated and parodied across the reading public.

The word sonnet itself carried the fashion's history. Derived from the Italian sonetto, a little sound or song, it had long been loose enough to cover almost any short lyric; George Gascoigne, in his Certayne Notes of Instruction of 1575, still had to explain that some poems of fourteen lines were "commonly called sonnets," as if the label were unsettled. By the middle 1590s the label had hardened into a brand. A buyer who picked up a volume titled with a woman's name, Delia, Diana, Phillis, Idea, knew the contents before opening it: fourteen-line units in the English rhyme scheme Surrey had settled, a lover addressing an unattainable lady, a plot that advanced by inches and mostly stood still. Standardisation was the point. The vogue was a fashion system, and like every fashion system it sold recognisability. Each new sequence promised the same exquisite suffering in a slightly new voice, and the sameness was not a failure of invention but the commodity itself.

Print made the difference between a coterie pastime and a vogue. The sonnet had circulated in manuscript among courtiers for decades, copied into commonplace books, passed hand to hand, its value partly in its exclusivity. The 1590s sequences were commodities for strangers. Printers such as Thomas Newman, who issued the first Astrophil in 1591, and publishers who followed with Daniel, Constable, and Drayton, were betting that erotic lyric in series could sell to the Inns of Court men, university wits, gentlewomen readers, and prosperous citizens who formed the new book-buying public. The bet paid often enough to keep the presses busy for years, and the failures are as instructive as the successes: many sequences appeared once and vanished, which is the behaviour of a fashion market, not a steady trade. A fashion market rewards speed, novelty of packaging, and the frisson of a name, and it punishes lateness without mercy.

The vogue also had a geography and a sociology. London produced it, the court coloured it, and the universities and Inns of Court consumed and imitated it. Marotti's account stresses the social work the sequence performed for its writers: the erotic suit addressed to an unattainable lady doubled, in the period's code, as a suit for patronage addressed to unattainable power, and the young men who wrote sequences were often the same young men angling for place. Whether or not every reader decoded the allegory, the sequence carried an aura of ambition and insiderhood. To quote the new sonnets, to imitate them, to mock them knowingly, was to display membership in the culture that mattered. A public theatre audience was not identical with that culture, but it overlapped with it, overheard it, and aspired to it, and a playwright who filled a tragedy with sonnet matter was speaking to the overlap.

The pre-history shows how much had to align before a vogue could happen. Wyatt had translated and adapted Petrarch in the Henrician court, turning the Italian master's ice and fire into a wiry English idiom of suit and suspicion; Surrey had regularised the rhyme scheme into the three quatrains and a couplet that English practice would keep, and had shown, in his sonnets on Wyatt's death and his own desires, that the form could carry public as well as erotic weight. Tottel's 1557 miscellany put both men into print as classics of a finished age, Songes and Sonettes presenting them alongside Nicholas Grimald and a crowd of uncertain hands, and there the matter rested for a generation. The sonnet survived the middle decades of the century as an accomplishment rather than an industry: George Gascoigne printed a handful, courtiers exchanged verses in manuscript, and Thomas Watson's Hekatompathia of 1582, a hundred passions in eighteen-line stanzas, each with a prose headnote citing its Italian or classical source, proved that an appetite for systematic love poetry existed while missing the formula that would feed it, the short sonnet, the single lady, the serial hunger. Watson's book is the vogue's dress rehearsal: learned, erotic, programmatic, and stillborn as a fashion.

What the rehearsal lacked was a martyr and a market arriving together. Through the 1580s the conditions assembled quietly. Sidney himself wrote the age's most ambitious sequence and let it live in manuscript, where its reputation grew by report; his sister Mary, Countess of Pembroke, gathered his papers and his cult into the Wilton circle, making the Sidney name a publishing property before a single sonnet of his was sold; prose romances and pastorals from Greene and Lodge salted their narratives with songs and sonnets, habituating fiction readers to lyric interruption; and the print trade, recovering from the cautious middle years of the reign, was learning to sell small elegant quartos to the Inns of Court and the universities. When the detonation came in 1591 it therefore detonated along prepared lines. The vogue was not a sudden invention but a phase change, a coterie liquid turning to commercial gas in a single season, and the speed of the change explains the play's confidence in addressing it: by the time the tragedy reached the stage, the fashion was old enough to have rules and young enough to have heat.

## The European Weather: Petrarch, Ronsard, and the French Wave

The English boom was a late front in a European weather system, and a page of the map belongs to the continent. Petrarch's Canzoniere, the 366 poems of Laura's poet, had been a print classic since the fifteenth century, surrounded by commentary, canonised by Pietro Bembo as the model of vernacular lyric, and imitated in every court of Europe; the fountainhead itself is a neighbouring study's ground in this series and is registered here at the length the vogue requires, as the reservoir from which all the channels were cut. France cut the deepest channel first. Joachim du Bellay published L'Olive in 1549, the first French sonnet sequence, fifty sonnets swollen to 115 in the next year's edition; Pierre de Ronsard's Amours of 1552 made the sequence the vehicle of a national poetry; and Philippe Desportes, a generation later, polished the form into the courtly currency that English travellers and translators carried home. Kennedy's comparative scholarship insists on the sequence of transmissions: the English sonneteers read Petrarch through French mediation and Italian commentary, and their belatedness was doubled, a fashion imported at second hand and known to be so.

The doubleness explains a feature of the English vogue that the play exploits: its practitioners were connoisseurs of imitation, ranking originals and copies with a fluency their audience shared. When Mercutio grades Romeo's new love against the numbers Petrarch flowed in, the joke is European in its reference and London-fashionable in its timing, a bit of comparative criticism delivered as street banter. The English boom also differed from its models in one decisive respect: compression in time. France had spread its sonnet centuries across fifty years; England compressed an equivalent production into seven, and compression is what made a vogue rather than a tradition, a pile-up of rival sequences close enough in date to be shelved, compared, and mocked together. The play sits inside the pile-up. Its Verona tale had come from Italy through France and two English retellings, a transmission chain the source studies of this series trace link by link; its verse fashion had travelled the same roads a generation later, Italian form through French polish into English print, and the symmetry is more than a curiosity. Story and style arrived by the same route, wearing the same foreign cut, and the audience that received both in the middle 1590s received them as one phenomenon: the continent's love culture, naturalised at speed.

The theorists of the moment saw the device plainly and said so before the boom crested. George Puttenham's Arte of English Poesie, published anonymously in 1589 and addressed to the courtly maker, files the sonnet among the instruments of erotic negotiation and treats the courtier's whole verbal art as a practised dissimulation, the skill of saying one thing in the figure of another. Read beside the sequences, Puttenham explains their doubleness from inside the culture: the lover's protestations were understood by their first readers as figures, performances whose sincerity was beside the point, and the pleasure lay in grading the performance's grace. A decade that theorised its love poetry as managed pretence was not deceived by Romeo's opening manner, and the play's originality is to ask what happens when the pretence is interrupted by the real thing. The vogue's own handbook had announced that the idiom was a costume; the tragedy is the story of two wearers who forget, at the worst possible moments and the best, to keep it at costume distance.

## Sidney, Stella, and the Print Explosion of Sonnet Sequences

Every account of the vogue begins with Sidney, and the beginning needs care, because the sequence of cause and effect is stranger than the usual summary. Sir Philip Sidney composed Astrophil and Stella, his 108 sonnets and eleven songs, in the early 1580s, probably in 1581 and 1582, in the aftermath of his thwarted hopes and his marriage to Frances Walsingham. The poems circulated in manuscript among a courtly readership and acquired a reputation that print had not yet ratified. Sidney's Defence of Poesy praised the lyric; his Arcadia displayed his prose; but the sonnets, the work on which the vogue would be founded, stayed in manuscript through his lifetime. He died of a wound taken at Zutphen in October 1586 and was buried with a hero's honours in St Paul's Cathedral in February 1587. Five years after his death, in 1591, the printer Thomas Newman published Astrophil and Stella in quarto, in an edition Sidney's family had not authorised, prefaced by Thomas Nashe's exuberant address to the reader. A second Newman edition followed within the year, and in 1598 the Countess of Pembroke's circle issued the corrected text in the great folio of Sidney's works. The vogue, in other words, was a posthumous print event built on a manuscript reputation: the decade's defining book was a dead courtier's private papers, packaged for sale.

### Why did the boom wait for Sidney's death?

The boom waited because manuscript circulation kept the sequence a privilege while Sidney lived, and his family's control of his papers loosened after his death, when printers could profit from a reputation that martyrdom had enlarged. Newman's unauthorised Astrophil of 1591 turned coterie fame into a purchasable book, and its reissue proved a market that rival poets rushed to supply.

The cascade that followed can be dated almost year by year, and the dates matter to the play. Henry Constable's Diana was entered and printed in 1592, its sonnets arranged in decades and addressed to a composite ideal lady. Samuel Daniel's Delia appeared in 1592, fifty sonnets and an ode in its first form, the fruit of Daniel's service in the household of the Countess of Pembroke, and the volume most often judged the finest of the minor sequences. Thomas Lodge's Phillis followed in 1593, forty sonnets interleaved with pastoral eclogues. Barnabe Barnes published Parthenophil and Parthenophe in 1593, the most erotically explicit of the sequences and the strangest. Thomas Watson, the veteran who had published his own Hekatompathia, a hundred-passion sequence of eighteen-line poems, back in 1582, had his Tears of Fancy printed posthumously in 1593. The year 1594 brought Michael Drayton's Ideas Mirrour, fifty-one sonnets to Idea, and Giles Fletcher's Licia, and William Percy's Coelia. Edmund Spenser's Amoretti, eighty-nine sonnets, arrived in 1595 with the Epithalamion appended, the sequence of a major poet recounting a successful courtship. Bartholomew Griffin's Fidessa and William Smith's Chloris came in 1596, Richard Linche's Diella the same year. By the time John Davies published his Nosce Teipsum in 1599 with its acrostic sonnets to Elizabeth embedded in Hymns of Astraea, the market had thinned; the fashion had begun to eat itself in parody.

William J. Kennedy, in Authorizing Petrarch (1994), reads this flood comparatively, setting the English sequences against their French and Italian counterparts and stressing how self-conscious the English poets were about the act of imitation itself. The English sonneteers knew they were latecomers to a European form, knew Petrarch stood behind Sidney as Sidney stood behind them, and built that belatedness into their poems as a theme: Daniel apologising for his plainness, Drayton revising his sequence across a lifetime of editions, Spenser bending the form toward marriage. For the theatre historian the salient fact is simpler. In the half-decade before Romeo and Juliet, the reading public absorbed sonnet sequences the way a later public would absorb novels: serially, comparatively, with strong opinions about favourites. The play's first audiences did not need the sonnet explained to them. They had been arguing about sonnets for years.

The numbers deserve a moment, because scale is the argument. Sidney's 108 sonnets set a benchmark of amplitude that the followers matched or padded toward: Daniel's fifty growing in revision toward the fifty-five and beyond of later editions, Drayton's fifty-one expanding and contracting across the 1594, 1599, 1600, 1602, 1605, and 1619 printings of Idea, Spenser's eighty-nine, Constable's seventy-five in the enlarged Diana. Alongside the named sequences ran a penumbra of sonnets in miscellanies, commendatory sonnets prefixed to other men's books, sonnets embedded in prose romances, and single sonnets copied into commonplace books. The form was in the air of the print shops. Even a playgoer who owned no sequence had handled its conventions in prefaces, title pages, and borrowed copies, and had heard its cadences wherever young men showed off their reading.

There is a further wrinkle the cautious historian must keep in view, and the brief for this article rightly insists on it: the vogue's chronology is firm at the level of print and slippery at the level of composition. Sequences were printed in the 1590s that had been drafted earlier; sonnets circulated in manuscript years before any printer touched them; Shakespeare's own sonnets would be described by Francis Meres in 1598 as circulating "among his private friends" while remaining unprinted until 1609. So the boom is best pictured not as a starting pistol in 1591 but as a surfacing: a body of coterie practice rising into print, cresting in the middle decade, and breaking. The play belongs to the crest. It was composed, on the dating most editors accept, somewhere between 1594 and 1596, printed in a first quarto in 1597 and a fuller second quarto in 1599, and everything in its lyric fabric behaves like work written by a man whose ear was full of the sequences and whose audience's ears were fuller still.

The mechanics of the explosion repay attention, because the play's first readers met the vogue as physical books before they met it as ideas. Newman's 1591 Astrophil was a quarto of modest size carrying Nashe's prefatory rapture in place of any author's sanction, and its rapid exhaustion of the edition told every stationer in Paul's Churchyard that erotic sequences could move like newsbooks. The second Newman issue corrected some errors and kept the momentum; the trade had its proof of concept. The authorised monument arrived in 1598, when William Ponsonby issued the folio of Sidney's works under the Countess of Pembroke's supervision, Astrophil and Stella set in a corrected text among the Arcadia and the Defence, the sequence canonised between its author's romance and his criticism. Between the pirate quarto and the family folio, the vogue lived its commercial life: Daniel printed by Simon Waterson, Drayton by James Roberts for Nicholas Ling, Spenser's Amoretti issued with the Epithalamion by William Ponsonby, each volume a small, giftable, inscribable object, priced for the gentleman reader and the gentlewoman's chamber alike.

Around the sequences proper orbited the vogue's connective tissue, and the tissue mattered as much as the organs. Commendatory sonnets prefixed to other men's books turned every new volume of any kind into a minor anthology of the form; the miscellany The Phoenix Nest of 1593 gathered courtly lyrics, Raleigh's among them, into a print garland; sonnets turned up inside prose romances and at the ends of plays; and the satirists' complaints that every fop had a sonnet in his pocket testify to saturation more reliably than any title count. A fashion is real when its artefacts become furniture, and by 1595 the sonnet was furniture: in the pocket, on the desk, in the preface, in the air. The dramatist building sonnets into a tragedy in that year was not importing an exotic device. He was building with the commonest prestige material in the culture, and every hand in the audience knew its weight, its grain, and its price.

Two of the rival volumes repay a closer look, because the play's audience had favourites and the favourites set the standard Romeo's performance would be graded against. Daniel's Delia was the connoisseur's choice: fifty sonnets in 1592, printed with the Countess of Pembroke's patronage behind the poet, in a style of deliberate plainness that Daniel advertised against the era's richer ornaments. The famous sleep sonnet, "Care-charmer Sleep, son of the sable Night" (Delia 45 in the expanded editions), shows the manner at its best, the lover petitioning sleep as death's brother in periods of grave, unshowy music, nothing like Barnes's fireworks or the acrostic games elsewhere in the market. Delia's lady is as silent as doctrine requires, and the sequence's distinction lies in how honourably it bears the doctrine, suffering without shrillness. A hearer formed on Daniel brought to the theatre a palate for restraint inside the code, and could therefore register the exact pitch of Romeo's early excess: the Rosaline speeches are Barnes turned up, not Daniel kept down, all oxymoron and no gravity, the vogue at its most perfumed.

Drayton's Idea was the vogue's long-distance runner and its best evidence of the fashion's grip on a working poet's life. The Ideas Mirrour of 1594 presented fifty-one sonnets to a lady named for the Platonic form itself, Idea, a title that confesses the beloved's function as an occasion for poetry; Drayton then revised, cut, expanded, and reissued the sequence in 1599, 1600, 1602, 1605, and definitively in 1619, tinkering with his youthful suit across a quarter century until the sequence had become a life's work in miniature. The most famous single sonnet of the whole English vogue, "Since there's no help, come let us kiss and part," survives from the 1619 revision, a farewell of such finality that it reads as the vogue's own goodbye to itself, the lover at last consenting to the ending his genre forbade. Kennedy's frame fits Drayton like a glove: here is imitation as a lifetime's negotiation, a poet authorising himself against Petrarch and Sidney by endless return. For the play the lesson of Idea is chronological reach. The vogue was not a season's joke to the men who lived by it; it was a career structure, and Shakespeare's decision to raid it for a tragedy was a judgement passed on a form his rivals were still, decades later, unable to put down.

Between Daniel's restraint and Drayton's persistence, with Spenser's marriage plot and Barnes's extremity marking the range, the market offered the playgoer a complete education in what a sequence could do and a complete exhibition of what it could not. It could not answer, could not marry, could not bury, could not end except by exhaustion or revision. The tragedy that opened with two sonnets and a shared third was, among its other audacities, a demonstration staged for the best-educated lyric audience in English history that every one of those incapacities was a choice of the book form, not a law of love. The demonstration required the education; the education required the boom; and the boom, for one crest of years, had made London the most sonnet-literate city in Europe.

Spenser and Constable mark the vogue's two most instructive extremes, and a hearing trained on both was trained for the play. The Amoretti is the boom's one great exception to the law of refusal: its eighty-nine sonnets conduct a courtship across a liturgical year toward an actual consent, and the volume closes with the Epithalamion, the wedding hymn in which the lover's long petition is answered in ceremony, stanza by stanza, hour by hour. Spenser had bent the imported machine toward the one plot it was built to prevent, marriage, and had done so as a major poet staking the form's prestige on the heresy. The play's audience therefore knew that the sequence could be made to end in a wedding when a poet willed it, and could measure Shakespeare's crueller decision against Spenser's kinder one: the tragedy grants the wedding Spenser grants, in a cell, in haste, in secret, and then confiscates everything the Epithalamion celebrates, turning the marriage hymn's furniture to funeral use in Act 4. The comparison is not decoration. It is the difference between the vogue's boldest success and the drama's refusal to let success stand, and it was available, in 1595, to any reader who had bought both books.

Constable's Diana marks the opposite pole, the vogue at its most artificial and its most revealing. The sonnets, arranged in decades and addressed to a lady who is less a person than a composite of the age's ideals, move through their stations with a jeweller's detachment, and Constable's later life, exile, conversion, and a final turn to sacred sonnets, reads like the vogue's own biography in one man: the form as youthful fashion, then as embarrassment, then as an instrument retooled for devotion. The sacred turn was general by the decade's end, sonnets to angels and meditations on the Passion taking over the machinery the ladies had vacated, and the migration confirms the fashion reading from the far side. A form that can be reloaded with new content overnight is a vehicle, not a vision, and the play treats it exactly so, loading it with a feud, a marriage, and a double funeral before discarding the husk. Between Spenser's wedding and Constable's conversion, the boom displayed every destiny the sonnet could have; the tragedy, watching all of them, chose the destiny none of the sequences dared, and ended the form's story in a tomb.

## How the Sequence Boom Trained an Audience's Ear

A vogue is an education. What the sequence boom taught, beyond the mere shape of fourteen lines, was a whole grammar of expectation: how a lover speaks, how a lady answers (by silence), how time is argued with, how suffering is proved. The typical sequence plot, if plot is not too strong a word, runs on refusal. The lover sees, burns, petitions, is scorned, complains of the lady's cruelty and his own constancy, threatens despair, recovers, petitions again. Across fifty or a hundred sonnets the situation barely moves, and the stillness is doctrinal: the lady's refusal is the engine, because a granted suit would end the book. Petrarch had built the machine in the Canzoniere two centuries before, and the English sequences inherited its logic whole. A reader formed by Delia and Diana therefore brought to any love story a set of trained reflexes. Oxymoron, the yoking of sweet pain and pleasing wound, signalled authentic passion. The blazon, the catalogue of the lady's beauties itemised part by part, signalled poetic competence. The vow that verse would immortalise the beloved signalled the lover's seriousness, and the lady's stony silence signalled her virtue.

### What did sequence readers learn to hear?

Sequence readers learned to hear a code: oxymoron as proof of passion, the lady's silence as proof of virtue, and refusal as the plot's engine. Fifty sonnets of ungranted suit taught them that love in verse suffers and advances nowhere. That training is the competence the play exploits for two acts, then dismantles by letting its lady speak and consent.

The training cut both ways, and the doubleness is the key to the play's design. A sequence-trained hearer could savour the code and simultaneously see it as code, because the vogue had bred its own scepticism. The sequences themselves wink at their conventions: Sidney's Astrophil opens by mocking borrowed ornament, promising to look in his heart and write, and the first sonnet of Astrophil and Stella is a small drama of a poet rejecting other men's devices, "great with child to speak, and helpless in my throes, / Biting my truant pen, beating myself for spite" until his Muse snaps, "Fool, said my Muse to me, look in thy heart and write" (Sonnet 1). The anti-rhetorical pose was itself a rhetorical fashion, and readers knew it. By 1595 the sonnet lover was a stock figure of fun and of pathos alike: the sighing, sonneting gallant, dressing his desire in Italianate conceits, was recognisable on the street and on the stage. Nashe's preface to the 1591 Astrophil already treats the volume as a stage property, joking that the book might serve as a theatre for the tragedians of love to perform in. The vogue, at its height, came with built-in irony, and Shakespeare counted on both layers of his audience's competence: the layer that felt the sonnet's beauty and the layer that heard its falseness.

Marotti's social reading sharpens the point. If the sequence's erotic suit doubled as a suit for favour, then its language of abasement and service was a professional dialect, the idiom of young men performing devotion in hope of reward. Heard that way, sonnet speech in 1595 carried a faint odour of careerism: the lover protesting eternal constancy was also, in the code's second register, a client protesting eternal loyalty. A playwright placing that dialect in a young hero's mouth at the start of a tragedy was not merely decorating him. He was marking him as a performer of a fashionable role, fluent in the era's most prestigious love-talk and, by that fluency, suspect. The audience's trained ear did the judging before the plot supplied any grounds for judgement. Romeo is convicted by his accent.

This is also where the practical work of reading the vogue gets done for a modern student of the play, since the sequences survive scattered across rare editions, modern selections, and quotation inside other men's arguments. Keeping the evidence straight, which volume appeared in which year, which sonnet belongs to Daniel and which to Drayton, which passages of the play answer which conventions, is a dossier problem before it is an interpretation problem, and a private research library kept in a tool such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) lets a reader hold the whole crowded decade in one searchable place while the argument is built. The interpretation that emerges from a well-kept dossier is the one this article defends: the play's relationship to the vogue is neither worship nor satire but something more exact, a controlled experiment run on the audience's own training.

The experiment can be stated as a question the play asks its hearers. If the sequence code defines love as beautiful, unmoving, and unanswered, what happens when a dramatist takes the code at full strength, gives it the best verse of the age, and then lets the lady answer back? Everything the audience had learned from the bookstalls said the answer should be more of the same: more petitions, more oxymorons, more exquisite stasis. The play's answer is a marriage, a murder, a banishment, and a tomb. The vogue's forms are all present in the tragedy, but they are present as the first term of a contrast, and the contrast is where the meaning lives. To measure that contrast precisely, the play's sonnets themselves must be examined as what they materially were for their first hearers: artefacts of the fashion, made objects in the vogue's own medium.

The training had a practical, almost athletic character that print alone does not explain. Sequence reading in the 1590s was participatory. Readers copied favourite sonnets into commonplace books under headings, love, cruelty, absence, time; they answered sonnets with sonnets; Inns of Court men exercised in the form the way they exercised in fencing, as a credential of readiness; and the blazon, the inventory of brow, eye, lip, hand, and breast that the sequences rehearsed to the point of liturgy, trained the eye to read a person as an annotated text. When Lady Capulet commends Paris to her daughter as a volume to be read, a book whose fair cover promises a golden story, the metaphor lands because the house has spent five years reading ladies as volumes and lovers as their commentaries. Competence of that depth is not information; it is reflex, and reflex is what a dramatist can play on at speed, confident that a quatrain's turn will be felt in the body before it is parsed by the mind.

It is worth insisting that the competence crossed the lines the period drew around gender and station, because the play's design depends on the crossing. The Countess of Pembroke edited the text of the vogue's founding book; gentlewomen were addressed as readers in dedications and answered as judges in commendatory verse; the sequences' imagined female readership was a selling point, not an embarrassment. On the stage side, the mixed audience of the public theatres, apprentices beside gentlewomen beside citizens beside courtiers, formed a single hearing instrument of uneven but overlapping training, and Shakespeare's sonnet scenes are engineered for exactly that instrument: the form's outline clear enough for the least tutored hearer to follow a shape completing itself, the workmanship fine enough to reward the connoisseur counting rhymes. The vogue had built the instrument. The play was among the first masterpieces written to be played on it.

## The Play's Sonnets as Artefacts of the Vogue

Three complete sonnets stand inside the play, and their placement is a vogue signature before it is anything else. The Prologue to Act 1 is a sonnet: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (1.0.1-2), unfolding through three quatrains of feud and fatal love to the couplet that hands the audience its task, "What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (1.0.14). The Chorus before Act 2 speaks a second sonnet, turning from exposition to commentary: "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.0.1-2), ending with the observation that "passion lends them power, time means, to meet" (2.0.14). And the lovers' first conversation at the Capulet feast composes a third sonnet between two voices, Romeo opening with "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this" (1.5.93-4) and the two trading quatrains until the form closes and Juliet delivers her verdict on the performance, "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.109). Nearly a century and a half of scholarship has admired these structures; the vogue context specifies what the admiration should attach to. In 1595 a sonnet was not a lyric unit floating free of its moment. It was the decade's most fashionable commodity, and to build a play's thresholds and its central love scene out of sonnets was to build them out of the material every reader in the yard had been buying.

Consider the Chorus sonnets first, because their vogue character is easiest to miss. A modern reader treats the Prologue as a spoiler problem or a fate problem; a sequence-trained reader of the 1590s met it first as a genre signal. The sonnet was the form in which a lover surveyed his situation and found it hopeless and beautiful; the Prologue does exactly that work for the whole city, casting Verona's feud in the lover's posture of exquisite deadlock, "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (1.0.3) balanced against the "star-crossed" pair (1.0.6) in a structure whose very neatness flatters the disaster into art. The second Chorus sonnet then does something no sequence ever did: it narrates a change of object, old desire dying and young affection gaping to inherit, in the tone of a connoisseur reviewing the lover's progress between volumes. The pair of sonnets frames the action as if the tragedy were a sequence being read aloud, two lyrics at the front of the book, after which the spoken drama begins. Readers who want the full anatomy of that opening sonnet, its spoiler structure and its quatrains' division of labour, will find it in the companion study of [the Prologue sonnet that spoils the ending](/2022/03/18/romeo-and-juliet-prologue-sonnet-analysis/), which owns that ground; the point here is the frame's fashionableness. Shakespeare opens his tragedy wearing the vogue's livery.

The shared sonnet at the feast is the vogue's centrepiece and its trap. As an object, it is flawless sequence craft: the pilgrim conceit, drawn from the devotional-erotic stock the sequences had made ubiquitous, sustains an extended metaphor across quatrains traded between speakers, and the rhyme scheme holds to the English pattern with a jeweller's precision. The detailed anatomy of the form, its volta and its distribution of lines between the lovers, is set out in the study of [the sonnet form hidden inside the play](/2024/12/27/sonnet-form-in-romeo-and-juliet/), and need not be re-derived here. What the vogue context adds is the social temperature of the scene. At a feast, before witnesses, a young man deploys the era's most prestigious courtship instrument at concert pitch, and the house, trained by five years of Delia and Diana, can grade the performance as it unfolds: strong opening quatrain, deft extension of the shrine metaphor, a lady who, impossibly, answers in kind. The scene's thrill depends on competence. The audience must know the form well enough to feel both its perfection and its impossibility, a sequence compressed into ninety seconds and answered by the one person in the fiction who was never supposed to speak.

Stanley Wells, writing on the play's style in Shakespeare and Co. (2006) and in his essays on the period's lyricism, insists that the early Romeo is best understood as a deliberate exercise in fashionable imitation: the young Shakespeare trying on the lyric modes of his moment, sonnet, song, and conceited dialogue, with the exuberance of a practitioner showing mastery of every current idiom. The vogue frame supports Wells. The play's sonnets are not accidents of a lyrical temperament; they are demonstrations, priced for a market that knew the goods. Yet demonstration is not endorsement. The same scene that perfects the form begins its undoing, because the form's perfection is what Juliet names when she calls the kiss bookish. The vogue's supreme artefact carries its review inside itself, delivered by the person the vogue's rules silenced. That doubleness, artefact and critique in one object, is the play's signature handling of the fashion, and it governs the hero's idiom as strictly as it governs the set pieces.

There is one more artefact class to register before turning to the idiom: the couplets. The play is saturated with rhymed couplets, closing speeches, sealing scenes, punctuating exits, and the vogue context explains their prestige. The couplet was the sonnet's detachable unit, the form's smallest coin, and a decade of sequence reading had trained audiences to feel a closing couplet as a click of completion, a little verdict. Shakespeare spends that trained feeling everywhere, letting couplet after couplet mimic the sonnet's closure inside a drama whose events refuse all closure. The mechanics of that spending, rhyme by rhyme, are mapped in the study of [rhyme in the play, its couplets and sonnets](/2024/12/20/rhyme-and-couplets-romeo-juliet/); the vogue point is that the coin was current because the sequences had minted it, and the play's economy of false completions is a fashion effect before it is a metaphysical one.

The second Chorus sonnet deserves a closer look than it usually receives, because its imagery is drawn from the vogue's own economics. Old desire lies on its deathbed and young affection gapes to be its heir: the metaphor is inheritance, the transfer of an estate from a dying holder to an eager successor, and it describes not only Romeo's change of object from Rosaline to Juliet but the sequence market's own behaviour, in which each new volume gaped to inherit the readership of the last and the lady's name on the title page changed while the machinery of desire ran on unchanged. The sonnet's remaining quatrains reckon the costs of the transfer in the code's counters, desire's groans traded for new-born gapes, the alms-basket of pity exchanged for an exchanged pair of eyes, and the couplet lands on logistics rather than ecstasy: passion lends power, time lends means, the lovers meet. A sequence could not end a poem on logistics; its world contains no meetings. The Chorus sonnet can, because it serves a drama, and the slight generic impropriety, a sonnet behaving like a stage direction, announces the play's whole method: the fashion's forms, filled with the theatre's facts until the forms confess their limits.

The printed play then joined the market it depicted. The first quarto of 1597 announced on its title page a play often performed with great applause, a bookseller's phrase aimed at the same browsers who bought Delia and Diana in the same shops; the volume itself, a tragedy in a portable quarto, sat on the stall as a cousin of the sequences, its Prologue sonnet facing the buyer from the first leaf like a specimen of the goods within. That the play's sonnets could serve as their own advertisement is the vogue's doing: no other decade would have found a sonnet the natural frontispiece for a tragedy. Shakespeare's lyric frame was therefore also his marketing, and the marketing was honest, since the frame was, in fact, the play's chosen instrument, displayed at the door so that the trained ear could tune itself before the action began.

The feast that hosts the shared sonnet is itself a vogue occasion, and the setting is part of the artefact's meaning. A great house's ball, with its masquers, its music, its ordered dancing and its watching elders, was the social theatre in which the culture's erotic performances were licensed and judged; the sequences imagine such rooms constantly, the lover catching sight of the lady across a presence chamber, a compliment paid in company and treasured in private. Shakespeare stages the imagined room. Romeo enters it masked, in a party of young men carrying the evening's licensed impertinence, and deploys the sonnet the way the room deploys its other set pieces, the dance, the toast, the torch-lit entrance, as a choreographed bid for attention whose steps everyone present can follow. Juliet's answering quatrains break the choreography from within, a partner who will not merely be danced at, and the scene's danger, Tybalt's watching fury at its edge, reminds the house that performances in such rooms have consequences the printed sequence never risks. The vogue's lyric had always pretended to happen somewhere; the play shows the somewhere, with its exits watched and its wine going round.

## Fashion Before Feeling: Romeo's Borrowed Idiom

Romeo enters the play as the vogue's finished product. Before he has a plot he has a posture: sighing, sleepless, shunning daylight, composing his misery in the antitheses the sequences had standardised. His first extended speech is a catalogue of the code's counters, love as "a smoke raised with the fume of sighs," a "choking gall" and "preserving sweet," and the famous oxymoron run, "O brawling love! O loving hate! / O anything of nothing first create! / O heavy lightness, serious vanity" (1.1.176-81 in Evans's numbering). Nothing in the speech is felt in the way the play will later teach feeling to be recognised; everything is quoted from the common stock. The beloved is absent, unnamed for long stretches, functionally a pretext: Rosaline has refused him, as the code requires, and her refusal licenses the performance. The dialogue form underlines the diagnosis. Benvolio interrogates, Romeo riddles, and the scene plays like a sequence being read aloud by a young man who has memorised his part.

The play supplies its own fashion criticism, in the Friar's and Mercutio's voices, and both critics speak from inside the vogue's world. The Friar, hearing the object of the sighs has changed overnight, answers with a couplet of pure sequence scepticism: "Young men's love then lies / Not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3.67-8). Mercutio, once Romeo reappears in spirits, performs the connoisseur's comparison, ranking the new love against the old by the old's literary pedigree: "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in. Laura to his lady was but a kitchen wench" (2.4.38-40). The joke assumes a house that knows Petrarch's numbers as a benchmark of fashionable verse, knows Laura as the prototype of the silent lady, and enjoys hearing a friend appraise love affairs the way the bookstalls appraised sequences, by their style. The machinery Mercutio names, the inherited conceits and their long Italian pedigree, is anatomised in the companion study of [Petrarchan conceits and how the play mocks them](/2025/01/10/petrarchan-conceits-romeo-juliet/); here the point is the social fact: in the play's Verona as in Shakespeare's London, the vogue's idiom is the small change of wit among young men, and fluency in it proves fashion, not depth.

The strongest evidence that the idiom is borrowed is the speed and completeness of its abandonment under pressure. Romeo speaks vogue fluently for Rosaline, who never appears; at the feast the idiom meets an answering voice and starts, within the shared sonnet itself, to strain toward dialogue; in the orchard scene Juliet legislates against the code outright, forbidding oaths by the inconstant moon and demanding plain dealing, and Romeo's verse changes character, shortening, questioning, listening. By the tomb his language has shed the conceits almost entirely for statement and imperative. No sequence permits its lover such a trajectory, because the sequence lover's idiom is his identity and its repetition is the product. The play treats the idiom as a costume: becoming, youthful, expertly worn, and finally outgrown at the price of everything. Kennedy's comparative frame helps specify the costume's cut. In Authorizing Petrarch he shows the English sonneteers negotiating a received Italian system with varying degrees of submission and irony; Romeo's opening manner is submission without irony, the full Petrarchan uniform, and the play's work is to educate him, and the house, out of it.

A caveat of fairness belongs here, because the vogue reading can be over-pressed into a satire reading, and the play resists that reduction. Shakespeare does not mock the idiom from outside, as a rival craftsman might; he writes it beautifully, better than the sequences he raids, and the beauty is load-bearing. If Romeo's Rosaline verse were merely bad, its abandonment would cost nothing and prove nothing. The oxymorons are gorgeous, the shared sonnet is the finest single sonnet of the decade by common consent of anthologists, and the Chorus sonnets lend the squalid feud a ceremonial dignity that the action alone could not supply. The verdict has to hold both truths at once: the play's lyric splendour is the vogue's splendour, mastered to the highest pitch, and the play spends that splendour to expose the fashion's central lie, that love is a solo performance for an audience of connoisseurs. Feeling enters the play exactly where fashion's rules are broken, in answer, interruption, and consent, and the verse knows it before the lovers do.

The borrowed idiom is not the hero's private failing, and the play is careful to show the fashion speaking through the older generation's most respectable voice. Lady Capulet, selling Paris to her daughter, reaches instinctively for the vogue's master metaphor: "Read o'er the volume of young Paris' face, / And find delight writ there with beauty's pen" (1.3.82-3), and the pitch runs on through eyes as marginalia, the beloved as a fair book wanting only a cover, marriage as the clasp that beautifies the volume. It is a blazon translated into a mother's salesmanship, the sequence's machinery of inventory applied to a bridegroom by a woman arranging a match, and it proves that in this Verona the fashionable idiom is simply the language of desire's administration, spoken by parents as fluently as by sighing sons. Romeo's opening manner is therefore not a young man's eccentricity. It is the dialect of his whole society, and his distinction is only that he speaks it with genius.

Juliet begins inside the dialect too, and her starting point is easy to overlook because her later plainness is so famous. In the same scene she answers her mother's pitch with a polished couplet of conditional obedience, "I'll look to like, if looking liking move; / But no more deep will I endart mine eye / Than your consent gives strength to make it fly" (1.3.97-9): rhyme in place, metaphor in place, the dutiful daughter performing the code of looking as the book of Paris has been prescribed to her. The orchard scene's legislation against oaths and forms is thus a second conversion matching Romeo's, hers as well as his: the play educates both its lovers out of the vogue, in parallel, at different speeds, and the symmetry is the strongest internal evidence that the fashion, not either character, is the object of the play's critique. What Verona taught its children to say about love, the tragedy spends five acts unsaying.

Even the cure Benvolio prescribes is taken from the books. The sequence tradition knew one remedy for an unanswering lady: a new object, the infection of a fresh pair of eyes displacing the old humour, and Benvolio's counsel, "Take thou some new infection to thy eye, / And the rank poison of the old will die" (1.2.49-50), is that remedy quoted as friendship's common sense, complete with a wager that the new fire will burn out the old. The comedy of the prescription is that it works, catastrophically well, at the feast; the tragedy inside the comedy is that the cure accepts the vogue's premise, that loves are interchangeable styles of looking, and the play spends its remaining acts refuting the premise at the cost of both patients. Benvolio means to change Romeo's taste. The play changes his ontology: from a viewer of ladies to a husband of one, from the consumer of the fashion to its refutation in person.

## Where the Play Breaks with the Vogue

The break can be located with unusual precision, because the play marks it in the vogue's own units. The sequences require a silent lady; Juliet speaks, first inside the shared sonnet at the feast, where her quatrains answer Romeo's point for point and sharpen his conceit past his own control of it, and then at length in the orchard, where she dismantles the code's procedures one by one: no swearing by the moon, no reliance on the lover's stock vows, names interrogated and discarded as accidents, the whole apparatus of performed courtship set aside in favour of a contract negotiated in plain terms. The sequences require endless delay; the play compresses meeting, marriage, and catastrophe into days, as if the drama were answering the sequence's hundred sonnets of stasis with a single weekend of consequence. The sequences require the lover's voice to monopolise the book; the play distributes speech across fathers, nurses, friars, servants, and a prince, so that the lyric idiom is constantly overheard, mistimed, and interrupted by the social world it pretends to transcend.

### Where does the play stop sounding fashionable?

The play stops sounding fashionable in the orchard scene, where Juliet forbids the code's procedures outright, rejecting oaths by the inconstant moon and demanding plain dealing instead of performed courtship. From that scene the sonnets vanish, couplets and broken exchanges take over, and the verse sheds its sequence polish as the lovers trade the solo form for dialogue and consequence.

The formal record after the orchard confirms the break at the level of construction. No completed sonnet occurs in the play after the Chorus speaks before Act 2; the great set speeches of the later acts choose other instruments, the Friar's moralising periods, Juliet's accelerating soliloquies of dread, the broken exchanges of the tomb. Where the sonnet impulse survives, it survives damaged: aborted quatrains, couplets doing verdict work, lyric flights cut short by messengers, bells, and watchmen. A fashion artefact has a life cycle, and the play enacts the vogue's whole cycle inside five days of stage time: the form arrives as prestige, performs its beauties, exposes its inadequacy, and is discarded by the very characters who wore it best. The sequences on the bookstalls could not perform that enactment, because a sequence must keep faith with its form to the final page; the drama, owing the form nothing, could spend it and walk away.

This is the point at which the anti-cannibalisation line with neighbouring studies must be drawn cleanly, because the play's Petrarchan inheritance and its sonnet mechanics are owned elsewhere in the series and are pointed to, not re-covered, in these pages. The claim here is narrower and, for the vogue, decisive: whatever the conceits' Italian genealogy and whatever the shared sonnet's internal engineering, both were received by the first audience as fashion, the way a cut of sleeve is received, and the play's meanings begin in that reception. A modern reader who has never seen a sequence in a bookshop window must reconstruct the reception deliberately, and the reconstruction changes the temperature of famous scenes. The feast scene warms: it is a triumph of fashion executed under pressure. The orchard scene cools the fashion out of the play: two young people agreeing, in effect, to stop performing the decade's favourite script. The tomb scene lands as the fashion's funeral, all solo voices at last, speaking too late to an audience of the dead.

Productions that grasp the vogue context find a lever in it, and productions that miss it leave the lever unused. A Romeo costumed by his verse, entering as the complete sequence lover, gives the audience a before against which the after can be measured; the transformation into the man of the tomb speeches then reads as growth purchased at catastrophic price, not as a change of costume between acts. Directors who cut the Chorus sonnets as expendable prologue matter are cutting the vogue's frame, the two artefacts that announce the fashion and date the play's lyric world at sight. The frame is expendable only if the fashion is forgotten; restore the fashion, and the frame becomes the thesis statement.

The later acts conduct the break through the vogue's own calendar of forms, inverting each in turn. The aubade, the dawn song of parting lovers, haunts the sequences as a wished-for fiction, since sequence lovers never spend a night to part from; the play grants the night and then sings the aubade in earnest in the chamber scene, lark against nightingale argued in quick exchanges whose lyric sweetness is indistinguishable from terror. The epithalamium, the wedding song Spenser had printed at the back of the Amoretti as the suit's triumphant conclusion, is prepared for Juliet in Act 4 with music, flowers, and a bride-bed, and then sung by events in reverse, the Nurse's discovery turning the bridal chamber into a bier and Capulet's festive verse into lament for a flower deflowered by death. Form by form, the play takes the vogue's ceremonial furniture and shows it in the house of mourning, and the furniture's familiarity is what makes the mourning legible at sight.

The tomb completes the pattern with the vogue's founding condition restored as nightmare. The sequence lover's situation is, at bottom, a man speaking beautifully to a woman who cannot or will not answer; the tomb gives Romeo that situation in its literal form, his long final speech addressed to a Juliet whose silence is no longer the code's coyness but death's fact, or its perfect counterfeit. Everything the fashion had aestheticised, the unanswering lady, the exquisite solo, the lover's eloquence feeding on its own echoes, returns stripped of charm, and the audience that had been trained to savour the solo is made to watch what the solo costs when it is all that is left. Juliet wakes into the same condition reversed, her few lines over the body a sequence of one, spoken to a man past hearing. The vogue's engine, refusal, has become the grave's, and the play's judgement on its decade's favourite form is delivered not in satire but in structure: this is where the solo ends.

The orchard scene's centrepiece can be named in the vogue's own terms as an anti-blazon, and the name clarifies what breaks there. The blazon inventories the beloved part by part, eyes, lips, hand, brow, converting a person into a catalogue of praised components; Juliet, asked in effect to receive the lover's inventory of herself, refuses the premise at its root by interrogating the name that heads the catalogue. "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-4) dissolves the labelled item the inventory depends on, and her instruction to Romeo, "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" (2.2.34), extends the dissolution to the lover's own heading. A sequence cannot survive the manoeuvre, since its entire architecture hangs on the lady's name, Delia, Diana, Idea, printed on the title page as the brand. The play hangs its love scene on the brand's repeal. Where the vogue sells the name, the tragedy discounts it to an accident of sound, and the discount is spoken by the person the vogue had priced highest and consulted least.

## The Playwright Inside the Vogue: Sonnets Among His Private Friends

The vogue context has a personal edge that the source studies of the narrative tradition cannot supply: the dramatist was himself a practitioner of the fashion he staged. In 1593, Richard Field printed Venus and Adonis, the erotic epyllion Shakespeare dedicated to the young Earl of Southampton as the first heir of his invention, and the poem became the decade's steadiest seller in its kind, edition after edition, the author's name made in print as a poet of desire while his plays still circulated anonymously. The Rape of Lucrece followed in 1594 with a warmer dedication and a graver argument. These poems are not sonnet sequences, but they belong to the same print economy and the same erotic-classical shelf as Delia and the Amoretti, and their success means that when Shakespeare built sonnets into a tragedy he wrote as an insider of the lyric market, a competitor of the sonneteers for the same purses, not as a theatrical outsider raiding a foreign art.

The sonnets themselves complete the insider's profile while refusing, characteristically, to settle its dates. Francis Meres, cataloguing the English writers in Palladis Tamia in 1598, praises the playwright's mellifluous and honey-tongued manner and adds the vogue's most tantalising documentary trace: his sugared sonnets circulated among his private friends. The phrase proves three things at once. Shakespeare wrote sonnets in the boom years; they travelled by the manuscript system that had carried Sidney's; and they were withheld from the print market that consumed Daniel and Drayton, whether by the poet's choice, a patron's, or the coterie's sense that manuscript exclusivity was itself a distinction. When the poems finally reached print in 1609, in Thomas Thorpe's quarto with its enigmatic dedication to Mr. W. H., the boom they belonged to had been over for a decade, and the book landed as a monument rather than a fashion item, its reception muted accordingly. The caution from the dating discussion applies with full force here: individual sonnets may belong to years on either side of the play, and no argument in these pages rests on a precise order. What the record establishes is milieu. The man who wrote the feast scene had himself been read, in manuscript, as a sonneteer by the very culture that bought sequences, and the play's handling of the form has the authority of practice behind its parody.

There is a competitive moral in the milieu, and it returns the argument to the user's standing question of how one writer outdoes a field. Shakespeare's response to the vogue was not to publish a better sequence, the contest Daniel and Drayton were fighting edition by edition, but to change the arena: to take the form onto the stage, where it could be answered, interrupted, timed, and broken, feats no printed book could perform. The sequences competed within the fashion's rules; the play competed against the fashion itself, offering the book-buying public a spectacle its books could not contain, a sonnet completed by two voices in ninety seconds and a lady who talks back. Judged as a manoeuvre in the literary marketplace of the middle 1590s, it was the boldest available, and its success is written in the survival table: the sequences are scholars' territory, while the play that raided them fills theatres. The vogue gave Shakespeare his materials and his audience; his genius was to see that the fashion's highest use was its own transcendence, performed nightly.

The stage at large confirms that the vogue was theatrical common property, not one dramatist's quarry. The comedies of the same years are furnished with sonnets as stage objects, love poems produced as papers, read aloud, intercepted, mocked, and forged, the document of desire passing from hand to hand while the house grades its workmanship; the extreme case, a king and three courtiers discovered sonneteering to four ladies in a single plot, belongs to Shakespeare's own comic workshop and shows the fashion ripe for farce in the very years of the tragedy. City comedy's gallants quote the sequences as small talk; the satirists' stages hiss the sighing lover on sight. A tragedy that opened in sonnets therefore entered a theatre already fluent in the form's stage meanings, reverence and ridicule simultaneously available, and the playwright's control of the mixture, reverence for the feast, ridicule reserved for the Rosaline manner and discharged through Mercutio, is a musician's distribution of the house's own reflexes. No dramatist invents an audience's reflexes in a season. The vogue had spent half a decade installing them, in print, in song, in manuscript, and on rival stages, before the tragedy presumed to play the instrument at full power.

## Critics on the Vogue and the Play: Marotti, Kennedy, Wells

Three lines of criticism converge on the vogue context, and they do not agree, which is what makes the convergence useful. Arthur F. Marotti's reading, in "Love Is Not Love" and in his subsequent work on manuscript and print circulation, treats the sonnet sequence as a social instrument: its love language is a coded bid for patronage and place, its ladies shadow patrons, its sighs are job applications in fancy dress. Applied to the play, Marotti's frame makes Romeo's opening manner a professional performance, the dialect of a young man trained to sue for favour in the idiom of desire. William J. Kennedy's Authorizing Petrarch pushes the other way: comparing the English sequences with their Italian and French models, Kennedy finds the sonneteers preoccupied not with covert careerism but with the ethics and rhetoric of imitation itself, with what a poet may legitimately take from a master and how a vernacular earns authority. Applied to the play, Kennedy's frame makes the fashionable idiom a question of literary legitimacy: Romeo speaks borrowed numbers because his culture has not decided whether borrowing is homage or theft, and the play stages that indecision as a love affair. Stanley Wells occupies a third position, practitioner-centred: the young dramatist, fresh from narrative poems steeped in the same erotic-classical atmosphere, is showing the town what he can do in every current lyric mode, and the play's vogue materials are best read as a virtuoso's portfolio that gradually discovers a tragedy inside the display.

The disagreement between Marotti and Kennedy is the genuine article, not a staging of near-identical views, because it concerns what the vogue's language was for. If Marotti is right, sequence speech is instrumental all the way down, and Romeo's fluency marks him as a social climber in fancy dress, so that his growth must consist in escaping the dialect of ambition altogether. If Kennedy is right, sequence speech is a meditation on imitation, and Romeo's fluency marks him as a reader before he is a lover, so that his growth consists in learning to author his utterances rather than recite the tradition's. The adjudication this article reaches, on the play's evidence, is that Kennedy better explains the verse on the page while Marotti better explains the reception in the yard, and that the play exploits the gap between the two. The verse on the page is indeed saturated with imitation-consciousness: the shared sonnet is a duet about its own artifice, and Juliet's "by th' book" is a reader's judgement, Kennedy's point exactly. But the first audience's competence, the trained ear that could grade Romeo's performance at sight and suspect it of careerism's polish, is Marotti's point, and the play's early acts lean on that suspicion for their irony. Wells then explains why the contest between the two readings is so pleasurable: the portfolio is real, the display is genuine, and only a craftsman secure in the fashion could afford to break it in public. The verdict, therefore: read the idiom with Kennedy, hear it with Marotti, and credit Wells for the nerve of the performance.

The editors complete the case. G. Blakemore Evans, in the New Cambridge edition of the play (2003), annotates the sonnet materials and the fashionable allusions with a patience that amounts to an argument: the notes keep returning the reader to the sequences, to Petrarch's numbers, to the conventions of the suit, because without them whole speeches flatten into generic lyricism. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition (2000) makes the complementary point about chronology, placing the play's composition squarely in the crest years of the boom and treating the overlap as a fact any reading must absorb. Between the annotator and the chronologer, the editorial tradition quietly ratifies the vogue reading: the play is a document of the sequence decade, and its verse fashion is a historical fact before it is an aesthetic impression.

Reception history quietly confirms the adjudication, because later centuries kept voting with Kennedy's side of the argument while hearing with Marotti's ears. Anthologists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries extracted the play's sonnets and set speeches as lyric specimens, treating the verse as a portfolio of detachable beauties, Wells's display with the covers torn off; the same centuries' playgoers relished the fashionable lover as a figure, sighing in the approved style, exactly the social performance Marotti anatomises. The vogue reading reunites what the reception split apart: the poems' artifice and the audience's knowingness, the text's imitation-consciousness and the yard's suspicion of polish, are one event, the event of a fashion being performed at its peak by the practitioner best equipped to judge it. An account that keeps both halves is not eclecticism. It is the shape of the evidence.

A second, quieter disagreement runs beneath the first and should be surfaced for completeness, between the editors' habits of annotation. Evans's New Cambridge notes treat the vogue materials as the play's necessary furniture, glossing the sonnet machinery, the Petrarchan counters, and the fashion allusions at a density that assumes a reader who needs the decade rebuilt around the text. Levenson's Oxford apparatus leans harder into chronology and the play's place in the company repertory, rebuilding the decade as a theatrical season rather than a bookshelf. The difference is one of emphasis rather than of fact, and adjudicating it is easy: a play is both a book and a repertory piece, and the vogue reached it by both roads, the reading that formed its maker and the market that formed its hearers. The critic who keeps both roads open keeps the whole phenomenon in view; the vogue was never only print, and never only fashion, but the traffic between the two.

## Dating the Play Inside the Vogue: What Cannot Be Known

The complication the brief demands be faced squarely is uncertainty. No document dates the play's composition to a year. The first quarto of 1597 proves existence by publication; the title page's claim of performance "with great applause" proves a stage life before print; Meres in 1598 lists the play among the tragedies in Palladis Tamia while separately praising Shakespeare's sugared sonnets circulating in manuscript; the Nurse's earthquake recollection has been pressed into service as an allusion to the tremor of 1580 and resisted as arithmetic without force. Editors converge, by style, company history, and the texture of the verse, on a window in the middle of the decade, commonly 1594 to 1596, and the vogue reading inherits that window rather than narrowing it. Honesty requires the inheritance to be labelled: the play's fit with the boom's crest is consistent with the accepted dating and is corroboration of atmosphere, not independent proof of a year.

### Can the vogue date the play?

The vogue cannot date the play to a year, because sequences circulated in manuscript before print, and fashionable idiom outlives the fashion that minted it. What the vogue supplies is a plausibility band: the play's sonnets and sonneteering jokes sit most naturally in the middle 1590s, matching the editors' window without replacing harder evidence of quartos and company records.

The caution matters because the vogue argument is seductive, and seduction is how context hardens into fake precision. It would be easy to write that the play must belong to 1595 because the boom peaked then; it would also be wrong, since the play's own vogue materials could have been assembled by a dramatist whose reading ran ahead of print, as Shakespeare's demonstrably did, and the sonnets Meres praised in 1598 remained unprinted for another eleven years. Context should be handled the way the best source scholarship handles Brooke and Painter elsewhere in this series: as a field of probabilities that disciplines interpretation, not as a lock that closes it. The vogue explains why the play sounds as it does whenever exactly in the window it was written; it does not need a single year to do that work, and claiming one would weaken every other claim built on the vogue here.

What can be asserted without strain is sequence and fit. The play postdates the print detonation of 1591, because its fashion jokes presuppose a public for whom the sequence is common property, and Mercutio's ranking of Petrarch's numbers against a kitchen wench is a joke for the many, not the coterie few. The play predates the vogue's exhaustion, because its irony is affectionate and expert rather than the savage parody the satirists of 1598 and 1599 turned on spent fashions. Within the crest, the play breathes the boom's air at full pressure. That is as far as the evidence honestly goes, and it is far enough: the reading of the verse proposed in this article depends on the crest, not on a calendar square.

One more piece of print evidence belongs to the vogue economy rather than to chronology proper, and it supports the fit from the bookseller's side. The second quarto of 1599 advertised the play on its title page as newly corrected, augmented, and amended, the standard formulae of the revised edition, and the formula had been naturalised in the decade precisely by the sequences: Drayton's Idea reached its readers through successive corrected and enlarged states, Daniel's Delia grew between printings, and buyers had learned to want the perfected text of a fashionable work and to pay again for the improvements. A tragedy marketed like a revised sequence was a tragedy positioned for the vogue's public, and the positioning worked, since the second quarto's fuller text displaced its predecessor as the copy from which the play would descend. The fashion's economics thus shaped not only the play's verse but its transmission: what survives to be read is the augmented edition the vogue's market taught its readers to demand.

The internal evidence editors actually lean on deserves its paragraph, since it shows the vogue and the dating resting on the same habits of attention. Verse tests, the proportions of rhyme, of feminine endings, of enjambment and mid-line speech breaks, place the play in the cluster of works from the middle of the decade, after the early comedies' tighter rhyming and before the great tragedies' loosened line; the play's exceptional rhyme density, its couplets and quatrains saturating the first acts, is at once a fashion datum and a chronological one, the young dramatist still writing near the lyric manner that the histories and later tragedies would thin out. Levenson's Oxford introduction sets the play in 1595 on the combined weight of such tests, the company record, and the vogue-saturated texture itself. Texture is doing real work in that verdict, and it is the same texture this article has been mapping: the dating and the reading stand or fall together, each lending the other its strength, which is precisely how context ought to behave when it is honestly handled.

It forbids the single-year dating that would turn atmosphere into alibi, since the vogue's own documents refuse to sit still in one calendar square. And it forbids the lazy inversion in which restoring the fashion licenses dismissing the verse as mere fashion, since the map's fourth column records not mockery but mastery, a dramatist outplaying the decade at its own game before changing the game. Held within those prohibitions, the vogue argument is as strong as context arguments get: dated, documented, edition-anchored, and answerable at every point to the printed record of the boom and the spoken record of the stage.

## Verdict: The Vogue as the Play's First Language

The sonnet vogue shaped the play's verse the way a native language shapes a speaker: not as a set of borrowings but as the medium in which thought first became sayable. Shakespeare took from the boom its forms, the Chorus sonnets and the shared sonnet; its idiom, the oxymorons and conceits Romeo wears for Rosaline; its economy, the couplet as the coin of closure; and its audience, the trained ear that could grade a lover's performance at sight and suspect polish of insincerity. He repaid the loan by subjecting the fashion to the one test no sequence could survive: he let the lady answer, let the plot move, and let the form break under the weight of events. What remains after the break, the plainer, harder, more beautiful verse of the orchard, the potion scene, and the tomb, is not the negation of the vogue but its graduation. The fashion taught the play to speak; the play, in breaking the fashion, taught the fashion's readers what their favourite form had been unable to say. That is why the verse cannot be read in a vacuum, and why the vogue map, not the vacuum, is where any serious account of the play's lyric splendour has to start.

The ranking of causes follows from the evidence assembled here. Source study explains the plot; the narrative line from Masuccio through Brooke accounts for the feud, the potion, and the tomb. Only the vogue explains the sound: why a tragedy opens in sonnets, why its hero sighs in oxymorons, why its heroine's first triumph is a review of a kiss's workmanship, and why the whole first audience could hear the difference between the fashion performed and the fashion outgrown. A criticism that restores the boom restores the sound, and with the sound restored, the tragedy's famous lyricism stops looking like decoration on a borrowed story and stands revealed as the story's sharpest instrument of thought.

For readers meeting the play before the sequences, the order of study the vogue dictates is the reverse of the usual one. Read a single sequence first, Daniel's Delia for its polish or Spenser's Amoretti for its marriage plot, slowly enough to feel the conventions settle into reflex; then read the first two acts and watch the reflexes fire, the sighs recognised, the blazon heard as inventory, the silent lady awaited; then read on and feel the reflexes fail, one by one, as the lady answers, the plot accelerates, and the forms break. The experience replicates, in miniature, the education and disenchantment the first audience carried into the yard, and it converts the vogue from a fact about the play into an instrument for reading it. No account of the verse that skips the instrument can claim to have heard the play at its original pitch.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch vogue map, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch vogue map is a dated table aligning the sonnet vogue's print events, from Tottel's Miscellany in 1557 to the printing of Shake-speares Sonnets in 1609, with the lesson each event taught the reading public and the trace that lesson left inside the play. The pattern it exposes is cumulative: form, book, brand, range, career, marriage, irony, and saturation were taught in sequence by the presses, and the tragedy absorbed the whole curriculum before breaking with it. The map claims fit and influence through the audience's trained ear, not verbatim borrowing, and so it doubles as a discipline for vogue arguments, separating what print history can prove from what enthusiasm might wish to assert.

### Q: Which came first in print, Astrophil and Stella or the play's first quarto?

Astrophil and Stella came first, by roughly six years. Thomas Newman printed Sidney's sequence in 1591, in an edition the Sidney family had not authorised, and a corrected text followed in the folio of Sidney's works in 1598. The play's first quarto, the short text printed by John Danter and Edward Allde, appeared in 1597, with the fuller second quarto in 1599. The order matters because it establishes the direction of influence: the play was composed into a market the sequence had already created, for readers the sequence had already trained. Shakespeare's own sonnets run in the opposite direction, circulating in manuscript during the boom years and reaching print only in 1609, when the vogue they belonged to had cooled into memory.

### Q: Why did publishers keep issuing sonnet sequences when so many of them sold badly?

Publishers kept issuing sequences because the economics of fashion reward attempts, not averages. A sequence was cheap to produce, short, printable in a small quarto, and capable of outsized returns when it caught: Daniel's Delia and Drayton's Idea went through edition after edition, and the Astrophil printings showed how fast a hot property could move. Each new volume also carried dedications and commendatory verses that cost the publisher nothing and advertised his list to patrons. Against the occasional success, a shelf of single-edition failures, Griffin, Linche, Percy, and their fellows, was an acceptable loss. The pattern matches fashion trades in every century: many entrants, few survivors, and a market kept alive by the hope of being the survivor.

### Q: How many sonnets made a sequence, and how were the volumes arranged?

A sequence typically ran from about fifty sonnets to just over a hundred. Sidney's Astrophil and Stella set the grand scale at 108 sonnets with eleven songs interspersed; Daniel's Delia began at fifty and grew in revision; Drayton's Ideas Mirrour held fifty-one; Spenser's Amoretti reached eighty-nine; Constable's Diana arranged its poems in decades, groups of ten. The arrangement usually followed a loose suit narrative, meeting, petition, scorn, complaint, brief hope, renewed refusal, without the causality of a plotted story, and volumes were fleshed out with songs, odes, sonnets to patrons, and commendatory verses by friends. The architecture taught readers to expect abundance without resolution, a hundred variations on a single unmoving situation, which is precisely the expectation the play's speed violates.

### Q: Who bought sonnet sequences, and what social work did owning one do?

The buyers were the new reading classes of the capital: Inns of Court men, university graduates, courtiers and their households, gentlewomen with leisure and literacy, and prosperous citizens emulating the tastes above them. Owning and quoting the new sequences did social work out of proportion to the books' size. Fluency in Delia or Diana signalled fashion, education, and insiderhood; the volumes made portable courtliness available for the price of a quarto. Arthur F. Marotti's account of the sequences stresses their function as instruments of ambition, their love language doubling as the language of suits for patronage, and the same doubleness clung to their readers: to display the book was to display aspiration. The theatre audience overlapped with this public and overheard the rest, which is why the play could spend fashionable idiom like currency.

### Q: How did sonnet sequences reach readers who never bought the printed books?

Sequences reached non-buyers through manuscript circulation, the era's second distribution system. Sonnets were copied into commonplace books, transcribed by friends, carried in letters, and memorised for quotation, so a fashionable poem could be widely known among people who had never seen its printed volume. Sidney's Astrophil had travelled this way for nearly a decade before Newman printed it, and Shakespeare's sonnets travelled it too, described by Francis Meres in 1598 as circulating among the poet's private friends eleven years before print. Miscellanies and songbooks carried single sonnets further still, detaching them from their sequences. The result was a vogue with two tiers, a print market for owners and a manuscript-and-memory market for everyone else, and the playhouse audience was recruited from both.

### Q: Who was Stella, and why does nobody agree on an answer?

Stella, the lady of Sidney's Astrophil and Stella, is most often identified as Penelope Devereux, daughter of the Earl of Essex, later Lady Rich, whom Sidney had been expected to marry before her marriage to Lord Rich in 1581 gave the sequence its biographical sting. The identification rests on the poems' own hints, the rich wordplay in sonnets that pun on her married name, and the testimony of readers close to the circle. Disagreement persists because the sequence is a crafted fiction as well as a document, because Stella functions as a poetic construct whatever her model, and because no statement by Sidney settles the matter outright. The honest position is probability without certainty: a real woman stands behind the figure, and the poems deliberately make her unreachable as fact.

### Q: Did women write sonnet sequences during the boom?

Almost none reached print during the boom's core years, and the absence is itself evidence about the vogue's sociology. The earliest English sonnet sequence by a woman, Anne Lok's Meditation of a Penitent Sinner, a religious paraphrase sequence printed in 1560, predates the vogue by three decades and stands outside its erotic economy. The great exception, Lady Mary Wroth's Pamphilia to Amphilanthus, arrived in 1621, a generation after the crest, written by Sidney's niece in conscious inheritance of his example. Within the boom itself, women appear as addressees, dedicatees, patrons, and readers, the Countess of Pembroke above all as editor and centre of the Sidney circle, but the printed sequence remained a male performance, which sharpens the play's offence against the form: its lady not only answers, she out-versifies the lover.

### Q: Why is there no sonnet for the wedding, the play's happiest formal occasion?

There is no wedding sonnet because the play reserves its completed sonnets for thresholds of performance, the Chorus at the doors of the acts and the lovers at the door of their meeting, and treats the wedding as the point where performance gives way to act. The marriage scene in Friar Laurence's cell is short, urgent, and weighted with warning, its verse plain beside the feast scene's jewelled form, and the Friar's couplets there do cautionary rather than celebratory work. The distribution is an argument: the vogue's supreme form belongs to courtship as display, and the play withholds it from the sacrament, as if to mark that what the sequences celebrate, the exquisite approach, is exactly what marriage ends. Form follows the fashion's values, and the wedding exposes them by its bareness.

### Q: What did the vogue do to the word sonnet itself?

The vogue narrowed the word sonnet from a loose label for any short lyric into the name of a precise form and a marketable book. Early in the century the Italian sonetto, a little sound, could cover songs and short poems of varying shapes, and George Gascoigne in 1575 still explained the fourteen-line meaning as a convention rather than a definition. After 1591 the word hardened: a sonnet was fourteen lines of pentameter in a recognised rhyme scheme, and a book of sonnets was a sequence with a named lady on the title page. The narrowing is the vogue's linguistic monument. When the Chorus's fourteen lines are now introduced to students as a sonnet without qualification, the definition doing the work is the one the boom standardised for commercial reasons.

### Q: How did the sonneteers themselves describe the fashion they were feeding?

The sonneteers described their fashion with a mixture of pride, apology, and self-mockery that shows how self-conscious the boom was. Sidney's first Astrophil sonnet dramatises a poet rejecting borrowed devices and resolving to look in his heart and write, making originality itself the opening performance. Daniel's Delia preface defends plainness against richer styles, an apology that is also a boast. Nashe's preface to the 1591 Astrophil treats the volume as a public entertainment and jokes about its theatricality. George Puttenham's Arte of English Poesie, printed in 1589 on the boom's threshold, already files the sonnet among courtly devices for erotic persuasion. Read together, the poets' own commentary shows a fashion that knew it was a fashion while it flourished, which licenses the play's knowingness as period accuracy rather than modern irony projected backward.

### Q: Why did the vogue fade before the decade ended?

The vogue faded because fashion markets destroy their own products by abundance. By 1597 the shelves held more sequences than readers could distinguish, the conventions had been repeated into predictability, and the sharpest young writers had moved to fresher instruments, verse satire, epigram, and the erotic epyllion among them. Parody did the rest: the sighing sonneteer became a stock joke, and the satires of Hall and Marston at the decade's end treated sonneteering as a spent affectation. The form itself did not die; it migrated into songs, madrigal books, and devotional verse, and it would return in monument form when Shake-speares Sonnets appeared in 1609. What ended was the boom, the brief delirium in which a new sequence was an event, and the play, composed at the crest, preserves the delirium's temperature exactly.

### Q: How many of the boom's sequences were reprinted, and what does the reprint record show?

The reprint record divides the vogue sharply between a durable few and a disposable many. Daniel's Delia returned in edition after edition across the decade and beyond; Drayton's Idea was revised and reprinted in 1599, 1600, 1602, 1605, and 1619, a lifetime's afterlife; Spenser's Amoretti rode inside his collected works; Sidney's Astrophil passed from the unauthorised quartos into the 1598 folio and stayed in print. Against these, most sequences, Coelia, Fidessa, Diella, Licia among them, appeared once and were not reissued. The record shows a real but narrow market: the vogue was wide in titles and thin in sales per title, a froth of attempts over a small durable core. That shape matters for the play, whose fashion jokes assume recognition of the core and the froth alike.

### Q: Why do the sequences keep promising that verse will outlast brass and marble?

The sequences keep promising verse's victory over time because immortality is the one gift the refused lover can give unilaterally. Denied the lady, the sonneteer converts her into his book's subject and pledges that the book will defeat the decay that takes beauty, monuments, and empires; Spenser's Amoretti 75 stages the bargain openly, the lady objecting that she is mortal and the lover answering that his verses will eternise her virtues, closing with the pledge that their love shall live when death has subdued the world. The promise flatters the lady, advertises the poet, and sells the volume in one gesture. The play inherits the topos and darkens it: its lovers are immortalised, as the Prince's closing words concede, but by a tragedy, not by the lover's art, and the gold statues promised in the final scene are the sequence vow translated into civic metal.

### Q: What role did music play in spreading sonnet culture beyond the page?

Music carried sonnet culture to hearers who never opened a sequence. The decades of the boom were also the decades of the English madrigal and the lute air: Thomas Morley's collections from 1593 onward, John Dowland's First Booke of Songes or Ayres in 1597, and a flood of part-books put short erotic lyrics into domestic performance, sung by the same households that bought Delia and Diana. Sonnets and sonnet-like lyrics moved between page and voice, detachable, singable, and memorable, so the conventions of the suit, its sighs, its cruel fair, its oxymorons, circulated as tunes in the ear of the whole culture. Many sequences themselves include songs among the sonnets, Astrophil's eleven among them. The playhouse stood inside this musical circulation, and the play's lyric set pieces were received by ears trained as much by singing as by reading.

### Q: How soon after meeting does the play let its lovers finish a form the sequences stretch across hundreds of poems?

The lovers finish the form within minutes of meeting, and the compression is the play's boldest comment on the vogue. A sequence spends sixty or a hundred sonnets failing to arrive: the lover petitions across a whole volume and ends where he began, refused and rhyming. Romeo and Juliet meet at a feast, compose a complete sonnet between them in a single exchange, follow it with a second attempt at the form, kiss twice, and are separated only by the Nurse's errands, all inside one scene. What the bookstalls' lovers could not achieve in years of print, the play's lovers achieve before the dancing ends, because the dramatist understood the sequence's stasis as a commercial property of the book form, not a law of desire. On stage, desire moves; the vogue's clock is smashed in the play's first act.

### Q: Which boom sequence is quoted or echoed inside the play itself?

No boom sequence can be proved to be quoted inside the play, and the absence of proof is a finding, not a failure. Verbal parallels between the play's lyric passages and Daniel, Drayton, or Spenser belong to the common stock of Petrarchan idiom, the oxymorons, blazons, and sweet-war counters that every sonneteer drew from the same European reservoir, so they demonstrate shared fashion rather than borrowing. Shakespeare's documented reading for the tragedy is narrative, Brooke and Painter; his lyric absorption ran through the ear and the culture, not through a crib. Editors from G. Blakemore Evans onward accordingly annotate the play's sonnet matter by convention and parallel rather than by source. The vogue influenced the play the way weather influences a harvest: pervasively, measurably, and without a single raindrop that can be invoiced.

### Q: Why do editors print the play's sonnets as poems in anthologies of the vogue?

Editors anthologise the play's sonnets because the extraction works: the Prologue sonnets and the lovers' shared sonnet satisfy the form's rules completely, rhyme, measure, and turn, and they stand comparison with the best single sonnets of Daniel or Drayton on the page. Anthologies of the vogue period therefore lift them out of the drama and set them among the sequences, where they serve as the decade's most famous specimens of the article the boom was selling. The practice has a cost worth naming: severed from the stage, the shared sonnet reads as a solo achievement rather than a duet under pressure, and the Prologue loses the strangeness of a love form made to carry a feud. The vogue map's counsel is to read them both ways, as poems in the anthology and as artefacts in the play, and to notice what changes.

### Q: How did the vogue reach the public playhouse audience, who mostly could not buy the books?

The vogue reached the playhouse through the culture's connective tissue rather than through book ownership. Manuscript copies, commonplace books, songs and madrigals, commendatory verses bound into other men's volumes, university and Inns of Court talk, and the simple prestige of a fashion everyone discussed carried the conventions far beyond the circle of buyers. A groundling who had never held Delia had heard sonnet idiom sung, quoted, mocked, and imitated, and had seen the sighing gallant as a figure of street comedy. The public theatre itself was one of the transmission machines, recycling fashionable matter to thousands at a penny a head. Shakespeare could therefore write for a house of mixed literacy with confidence that the vogue's code would land at every level of the yard, graded differently by each hearer and missed by none.

### Q: How can an essay use the vogue map to argue that fashion, not just feeling, shapes the play's verse?

An essay can use the vogue map by arguing in three dated steps. First, establish the curriculum: from the map's print events, show that by the middle 1590s the reading public had been trained in the sonnet's conventions, its silent lady, its oxymorons, its unmoving suit. Second, map the play's artefacts onto the curriculum: the Chorus sonnets as the fashion's frame, Romeo's Rosaline idiom as its uniform, the shared sonnet as its supreme performance, each cited by act, scene, and line from a named edition. Third, locate the break: Juliet's answering voice, the vanished sonnets after Act 2, the plain verse of the wedding and the tomb. The conclusion writes itself from the table's own pattern: the play's feeling is real precisely because its fashion is documented, dated, and then discarded, and feeling defined against fashion is an argument no account of private inspiration can match.
