The Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act 2023 did something no earlier amendment in this series has done. It was passed, assented to, and brought into force, and its central provision then stopped governing. Parliament rewrote the coverage clause of the forest statute so that the Act would reach only land that a legal notification or a government record identifies as forest. The Supreme Court, hearing a challenge to that rewrite, declined to stay the amendment, declined to strike any part of it down, and instead directed every state and Union territory to go on applying the older and wider judicial definition of forest until an administrative exercise in the states is complete. That exercise is not complete. The result is a statute with two operative definitions: the one Parliament enacted, which is law, and the one the Court directed everyone to use, which is practice. The amendment trail, which usually records how a text drifted across decades, here records a text that arrived and was then held in suspension by the branch of government it was designed to displace.

This article traces the whole trail. It sets out the principal Act as enacted in 1980, the single earlier amendment of 1988, and the judicial expansion of 1996 that functioned for twenty seven years as an amendment without a legislature. It then follows the 2023 Bill through its committee stage and its passage, takes the amending text apart provision by provision, states the government's case and the petitioners' case at the strength their authors gave them, and ends with the procedural position that produces the paradox: a definition that is in force, has not been held invalid, and is not the definition being applied to any parcel of land in the country.

## The statute the amendment inherited

The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 began as an Ordinance promulgated on 25 October 1980 and was replaced within weeks by an Act of Parliament, Act 69 of 1980, which was deemed to have come into force on the Ordinance date. The date matters far beyond antiquarian interest, because the 2023 amendment chose it as one of its two anchor dates, and everything in the later dispute turns on what the law covered from that day forward. The Act was short, and its brevity was the point. Where the Indian Forest Act, 1927 had built an elaborate machinery for constituting reserved and protected forests, the 1980 Act did almost nothing except interpose the Central Government between a state government and any decision to release forest land from forest control.

Its operative provision, Section 2, barred state governments and other authorities from making specified orders except with the prior approval of the Central Government. As first enacted, the bar covered two things: an order directing that a reserved forest, or any portion of it, cease to be reserved, and an order directing that forest land, or any portion of it, be used for a non-forest purpose. An Explanation defined non-forest purpose as the breaking up or clearing of forest land for any purpose other than reafforestation, subject to an exclusion for works relating to conservation, development and management of forests and wildlife. Section 3 allowed the Centre to constitute an advisory committee. Section 4 conferred the rule-making power. The rest was machinery. The statute's radicalism lay in what it did not contain: it set no criteria for approval, created no tribunal, prescribed no compensation, and yet it transferred the effective decision on every significant diversion of forest land in India from state capitals to New Delhi, because forests sat in the Concurrent List and a central statute could condition what states did with land recorded as forest.

Two features of the original design explain nearly everything that followed. The first is that the Act never defined the word forest. Section 2 spoke of reserved forests and of forest land, and the Explanation spoke of breaking up or clearing, but no clause said what counted as forest in the first place. In 1980 that omission caused little difficulty in practice, because the Act was administered against the recorded forest area, the estate of reserved, protected and unclassed forests that state forest departments maintained in their records, and administrators treated the record as the boundary of the Act. The second feature is that the Act attached its control to a status rather than to an activity in the abstract. The trigger was that land was forest land; the control was that its use could not change without central approval. Whoever controlled the meaning of the trigger controlled the reach of the statute, and for sixteen years the meaning was supplied by administrative practice rather than by any text.

Diversion before 1980 had proceeded at a pace the statute was built to stop. Land was released for agriculture, dams, mining and industry by state decision alone, and the recorded figure placed before Parliament when the measure was debated put diversions in the decades before 1980 in the region of several million hectares. The Act's supporters have ever since measured its success by the contrast between that rate and the far smaller annual area diverted under central approval, and the comparison is a fair one as far as it goes. What the design could not answer, because the text did not ask the question, was what should happen to land that was forest in fact but appeared in no record, or appeared in a record that a state had never converted into a notification. That unasked question lay dormant until litigation forced it.

## The first amendment and the judicial amendment

Parliament amended the Act once before 2023, by the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 1988, Act 69 of 1988. The 1988 amendment widened the approval bar in Section 2 in three ways. It extended the bar to the assignment of forest land by way of lease or otherwise to any private person or to any authority, corporation, agency or other organisation not owned, managed or controlled by Government. It extended the bar to the clearing of naturally grown trees on forest land for the purpose of using the land for reafforestation, closing the route by which a natural forest could be felled and replaced with a plantation under a forestry label. And it expanded the Explanation's definition of non-forest purpose to name cultivation of tea, coffee, spices, rubber, palms, oil-bearing plants, horticultural crops and medicinal plants as breaking up or clearing for a non-forest purpose, settling doubts that commercial plantation on forest land might escape the Act by calling itself agriculture of a woody kind. The same amendment gave the statute its only penal teeth by inserting Section 3A, under which whoever contravenes or abets the contravention of Section 2 is punishable with simple imprisonment for a period extending to fifteen days, and Section 3B, which fastens liability for offences by government departments and authorities on the head of the department and the persons in charge, subject to a defence of absence of knowledge or due diligence. The penalty was trivial in amount and almost never the real sanction; the real sanction remained the approval bar itself, which made an unapproved diversion unlawful at the root. For thirty five years after 1988, Parliament left the text alone.

The courts did not. In T. N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v Union of India, by an order dated 12 December 1996 in Writ Petition (Civil) No. 202 of 1995, reported at (1997) 2 SCC 267, the Supreme Court answered the question the 1980 text had never asked. The Court held that the word forest in the Act must be understood according to its dictionary meaning, that the Act applies to all forests so understood irrespective of ownership or classification, and that it applies in any event to all areas recorded as forest in any government record, again irrespective of ownership. State governments were directed to constitute expert committees to identify forests within that meaning, including areas that appeared in no notification. The holding converted a status that administrators had read off departmental records into a fact about the land itself: tree cover answering the ordinary meaning of the word attracted the Act whether or not any file said so. The litigation then continued for decades as a continuing mandamus, with the Court supervising forest diversion, constituting the Central Empowered Committee to advise it, and returning repeatedly to the identification exercise the 1996 order had set in motion, a pattern of continuing supervision that the doctrine cluster of this series examines in the [account of environmental jurisprudence in the Supreme Court](/2014/12/15/environmental-jurisprudence-supreme-court/).

### What is the difference between a recorded forest and a deemed forest?

A recorded forest is land identified as forest by a government record or by notification under the Indian Forest Act, 1927 or another law; its status can be checked against a file. A deemed forest is land no record identifies but that answers the dictionary meaning of forest on the ground. The 1996 order placed both inside the Act.

The depth of the 1996 settlement is the subject of the [pillar treatment of the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980](/2014/11/01/forest-conservation-act-1980-explained/), which owns the Godavarman holding and the clearance machinery built on it. What the amendment trail needs from that history is narrower: by 2023, the operative law of forest coverage in India consisted of a four-section statute from 1980, a modest amendment from 1988, and a judicial definition from 1996 that had been applied by administrators, committees and courts for more than a quarter of a century. Anyone wishing to change the law of coverage had to legislate against the judgment, because the judgment, not the text, was where the coverage rule lived. That is the unusual feature of the 2023 exercise. Most amendments adjust a statutory scheme. This one set out to recapture a definition from a court.

## The federal frame within which the definition operates

The coverage question cannot be understood without the constitutional placement of forests, because the definition's practical bite depends on which level of government keeps the records and which level controls diversion. Forests began in the State List. The Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976 moved forests, together with protection of wild animals and birds, into the Concurrent List as Entries 17A and 17B, and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 was among the first major uses of the new concurrent competence: a central statute that did not administer forests itself but conditioned the states' power to release them. The states retained the forest departments, the settlement records, the working plans and the entire field administration. The Centre retained the approval. That division explains a feature of the 2023 dispute that purely environmental accounts miss. The record on which Section 1A makes coverage depend is a state record, maintained by revenue and forest departments of very uneven quality across the country, while the consequence of the record, exemption from or subjection to central approval, operates at the Centre. A state that keeps its forest records expansively enlarges the estate subject to central control; a state that keeps them narrowly shrinks it, and no provision of the amended Act standardises the record-keeping itself. Rule 16 is the instrument meant to close that gap from below, by obliging every administration to build a consolidated record of a stated content, and the February 2024 and 2025 orders are the Court policing the obligation. The petitioners' federal argument, that the amendment combined with the directions power in Section 3C and the order-making powers scattered through Sections 1A, 2 and the Explanation re-centres a concurrent subject on the Union, and the government's answer, that the Centre has held the approval power since 1980 with the states' acquiescence and that a definition clause changes nothing in that settlement, are both arguments about this 1976 architecture rather than about the 2023 text alone. The trail records them because the next amendment in this field, whichever side proposes it, will be drafted inside the same architecture.

## The pressure for change and the road to the Bill

The government's reasons for reopening the coverage question were stated repeatedly and consistently, in the Statement of Objects and Reasons of the Bill, in the environment ministry's submissions to the parliamentary committee, and in the minister's speeches in both Houses, and they deserve to be taken as a set rather than as a slogan. The first reason was uncertainty of application. After 1996, the ministry's own account ran, the Act came to be applied to revenue forest land, to land recorded as forest in government records, and to areas answering the dictionary meaning, and divergent interpretations multiplied on recorded forest land, private forest land and plantations. Land that had been put to non-forest use such as habitations, institutions and roads with the approval of the competent authority before or outside the clearance system continued to attract the Act in some readings, which produced anomalies that a text-based coverage rule would end. The second reason was the plantation deterrent. If raising trees on private or non-forest land could bring the land within the Act once the canopy answered the dictionary meaning, a rational landowner would not plant, and the ministry argued that this deterrent worked directly against the national goals the amendment's new preamble would recite: an additional carbon sink of 2.5 to 3.0 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent by 2030 under India's Nationally Determined Contribution, net zero emissions by 2070, and an increase of forest and tree cover to one-third of the land area. The third reason was strategic. Infrastructure of national importance near the international borders, the Line of Control and the Line of Actual Control had to pass through the same clearance process as any commercial diversion, and the government held that linear projects concerning national security, and small parcels for security and defence purposes in specified areas, should stand outside the Act altogether within defined limits.

These were not new arguments in 2023. In October 2021 the environment ministry had circulated a consultation paper proposing amendments to the Act on recognisably similar lines, including exemptions directed at border infrastructure and a clarification of the Act's application to plantations and recorded lands. The consultation drew extensive objections from environmental organisations, former officials and opposition parties, and the proposals were not taken forward at that time. The episode matters to the trail because it fixes the ancestry of the 2023 text: the Bill that arrived in the Lok Sabha in March 2023 was the second presentation of a settled governmental position, not a response to any single project or judgment, and its drafters had already heard, and had two years to weigh, the main lines of criticism that would be repeated against the Bill.

The 2021 paper is also instructive about which parts of the governmental position were oldest. Its themes were the themes of the eventual Act: that the Act's application should be clarified by reference to recorded categories of land; that land for projects of strategic and national importance, including in border areas, should not travel the ordinary clearance route; that plantations and trees raised outside forests should not attract the Act; and that the list of activities treated as forestry rather than diversion should be revisited. Between the consultation and the Bill, the government changed the vehicle but not the destination, moving from a consultative float to a Bill with a committee reference, and adding two elements the paper had not carried: the renaming of the Act, and the preamble reciting the climate targets. For the petitioners, the ancestry cut the other way: a reform twice presented, and twice met with the same objections from forest officers, ecologists and state actors, could not present those objections as unforeseen when they were repeated in court. For the trail, the salient fact is the interval. Two years separated the float from the Bill, and in those two years no state completed a consolidated forest record of the kind Rule 16 would later demand, which is the factual soil in which the interim order grew.

One further strand of the pre-history belongs in the record, because it shaped the government's confidence that legislation was the right instrument. The dictionary definition was, from the beginning, a construction placed upon a silent statute by a court in a single order, and constructions of that kind are classically open to legislative revision: a legislature that disagrees with a court's reading of its own Act may amend the Act, and the amended text then governs, provided the amendment itself is within competence and does not offend a constitutional limitation. The government proceeded on exactly that footing, and the Statement of Objects and Reasons said, in substance, that the judgment had extended the Act to recorded forests already put to non-forestry use and to dictionary-meaning areas, producing interpretations the amendment would settle. Whether that footing holds against the particular character of the Godavarman proceedings, a continuing mandamus with identification machinery built over decades, is a question the petitioners reserved for the merits, and it is examined in the separation of powers discussion later in this article.

The criticism, when it came, also arrived as a set. The petitioners and parliamentary critics who opposed the Bill argued that the category the coverage rewrite excluded, forest that exists in fact but not in any record, was precisely the category the 1996 order had been designed to protect, because a record-based test hands the definition to the same record-keeping whose gaps made judicial intervention necessary. They argued that an exemption measured by distance from a border, one hundred kilometres, covers a very large share of the country's most ecologically significant terrain, including most of the north-eastern states and long Himalayan stretches, and that the security rationale for a road does not answer the ecological question of what a road does to a forest. They argued that placing zoos, safaris and eco-tourism outside the meaning of non-forest purpose invited commercial use of forest land under a conservation label. And they argued, through the Forest Rights Act lens developed below, that a statute controlling diversion of forest land could not be rewritten without addressing the rights of the communities who live in and from those forests. Each of these arguments returns later in this article in its courtroom form. In Parliament they were made first, and the parliamentary stage is where the trail next goes, because this Bill, unusually for its session, was sent to a committee.

## The Bill in Parliament: committee scrutiny and a contested name

The Forest (Conservation) Amendment Bill, 2023 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 29 March 2023 by the Minister for Environment, Forest and Climate Change, Bhupender Yadav, and was referred on the same day to a Joint Committee of both Houses. The reference deserves emphasis in a series that has repeatedly recorded major bills passing without committee examination. The committee route for contested legislation is the subject of the [analysis of parliamentary committees and bill scrutiny](/2022/04/01/parliamentary-committees-bill-scrutiny/), and this Bill is one of the instances where the route was taken: a thirty one member joint committee, chaired by Rajendra Agrawal, a Bharatiya Janata Party member of the Lok Sabha, received the Bill, invited evidence, and reported before the monsoon session.

The committee's report, laid before Parliament on 20 July 2023, ran to 201 pages and recorded that the committee had received 1,309 memoranda from experts, state governments, departments, public sector undertakings, ministries and the defence forces, among others. Its operative conclusion was simple: the committee endorsed the Bill without change. Four members filed notes of dissent. The dissenting members objected, among other things, to the narrowing of coverage, to the border and security exemptions, to the treatment of zoos, safaris and eco-tourism, and to the renaming of the Act. Critics of the process, including Congress members who had sought a wider opposition presence in the committee's work, made a further institutional point that the trail should record without endorsing: a committee that receives over thirteen hundred memoranda and recommends no alteration to a single clause has performed consultation without visible effect, and the value of the committee stage as scrutiny, rather than as legitimation, was itself part of the controversy. The government side answered that the ministry had furnished detailed explanations on each objection, that the exemptions for border projects were confined to strategic linear projects identified by the Central Government and would not be available to private entities, and that a committee is not obliged to amend a bill it finds sound.

The renaming drew a controversy distinct from the substance, and the article keeps it distinct because the two objections were made by overlapping but different constituencies on different grounds. The Bill proposed, and the Act enacted, the substitution of Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam for Forest (Conservation) Act in the short title of the principal Act, giving a central statute a Hindi name, conventionally translated as the Forest (Conservation and Augmentation) Act. Members from non-Hindi speaking states objected that a Hindi-only title for a law administered in every state was non-inclusive, that it would be inaccessible or alien in the south and the north-east, and dissenting voices in and around the committee described the chosen terminology as Sanskritik and untenable. The objection was about language and federal courtesy, not about trees: an identically drafted amendment under the old English short title would still have faced every substantive objection, and an objector to the name was not necessarily an objector to the coverage clause. The government's answer, given in debate, was that Indian languages should find a place in the statute book, that the name conveyed conservation together with augmentation or enhancement, which the government presented as the Act's widened purpose, and that translations and usage would make the title familiar. The trail records the outcome without a verdict on the language question, which is a question about the character of the statute book rather than about forest law: the name was enacted, and the amended Act bears it.

The Bill as reported by the joint committee was passed by the Lok Sabha on 26 July 2023 and by the Rajya Sabha on 2 August 2023, in a monsoon session marked by opposition protests on unrelated matters and, in the lower House, a brief debate. It received the assent of the President on 4 August 2023 and was published as the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023, Act 15 of 2023. Its own first section left commencement to notification, and the Central Government, by notification S.O. 4765(E) dated 2 November 2023, appointed 1 December 2023 as the date on which the Amendment Act would come into force. The trail therefore has clean dates: introduced 29 March 2023, reported 20 July 2023, passed by the two Houses on 26 July and 2 August 2023, assented to on 4 August 2023, and in force from 1 December 2023. What those dates commenced is the next subject.

## The opposition beyond the floor of the House

Parliamentary opposition was only one register of the resistance the Bill met, and the trail is incomplete without the others, because two of them supplied the personnel and the arguments of the later litigation. In July 2023, while the joint committee's report was awaited, a group of more than one hundred former civil servants, writing collectively to Parliament in the manner of the Constitutional Conduct Group's interventions on questions of governance, urged that the Bill not be passed, arguing that it would strengthen the tendency to release forest land liberally for non-forest purposes and that its reliance on compensatory afforestation to meet the carbon sink target was unrealistic, since plantations raised on diverted land do not reproduce the carbon, hydrology or biodiversity of the natural forest they replace. Former forest service officers made the professional version of the same argument in representations and in the press: that the recorded forest estate is an undercount of the forest India actually has, that the unrecorded remainder is not a drafting error but the product of settlement history, and that experienced administrators did not need a dictionary to know which land was forest when a diversion file reached them. Several state governments, in their responses within the committee process, raised the federal objection that forests are a Concurrent List subject in which the states carry the administrative burden, and that the amendment's combined effect was to enlarge central discretion over state-administered land. Jairam Ramesh, who as environment minister between 2009 and 2011 had himself administered the Act and its clearance system, became the most prominent political critic of the Bill, arguing in Parliament and in print that the exemptions and the coverage rewrite together exposed the forests of the Himalayas and the north-east, the country's most sensitive catchments, to a clearance-free regime at the moment of their greatest climatic stress. The government answered these interventions as it had answered the consultation responses of 2021, by returning to the text: the exemptions are categorical and limited, the record is broader than the critics allow, the clearance system survives untouched for the recorded estate, and the Act's purpose, recited in the new preamble, is augmentation of cover and not its release. The exchange matters to the trail for a structural reason. When the Act was challenged within weeks of assent, the petition was not a fresh construction by lawyers searching for a case; it was the courtroom continuation, by substantially the same community of former officials and environmental organisations, of an argument that had already been made to a consultation, to a parliamentary committee and to both Houses, and had lost at each stage. The interim order is the first institutional forum in which that argument prevailed in any measure, and it prevailed there only to the extent of a pause.

## The renaming in its statute book moment

The renaming controversy is better understood when it is placed in its legislative season. The monsoon and winter sessions of 2023 were the sessions in which Parliament enacted the three new criminal codes with Hindi short titles, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, replacing the Indian Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure and the Indian Evidence Act. The forest amendment's renaming belonged to the same moment and the same stated impulse: that the statute book should shed names inherited from the colonial codes and carry Indian language titles expressing an Indian purpose. The objections tracked the moment as well. Members from Tamil Nadu, Kerala and other non-Hindi speaking states objected in both debates that Hindi or Sanskritised titles for laws that govern every citizen are not a neutral act of decolonisation but a choice of one region's language as the national legal idiom, that the titles would be opaque to the very forest dwellers, litigants and field officers who must use the forest Act in the south and the north-east, and that the English short titles of the older codes had the virtue of being nobody's mother tongue and therefore everybody's working language. The government's answer in the forest debate ran parallel to its answer in the criminal codes debate: the titles translate transparently, Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam meaning the forest conservation and augmentation law, the operative text and the courts' usage would carry both names for a generation, and the Hindi title conveyed the widened purpose, conservation with augmentation, that the government wished the Act to bear. Two practical observations close the point for the trail. The renamed Act continues to be cited in courts, in the ministry's own circulars and in this series' cluster by its old name as often as by its new one, a bilingual afterlife that the criminal codes share; and the renaming, alone among the amendment's provisions, operates without qualification during the interim suspension, so that the statute whose coverage clause sleeps bears, awake and in use, the new name Parliament gave it. A trail that ignored the renaming would miss the amendment's most visible daily effect.

## Section 1A: the coverage rewrite and its two anchor dates

The Amendment Act has six sections, and four of them do the substantive work. Section 2 inserts a preamble into the principal Act, which had none, reciting the national targets of net zero emission by 2070 and the maintenance or enhancement of forest carbon stocks, the Nationally Determined Contribution target of an additional carbon sink of 2.5 to 3.0 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent by 2030, the envisaged increase in forest and tree cover to one-third of the land area, the tradition of preserving forests and biodiversity, and the improvement of livelihoods for forest dependent communities. A preamble in an amending exercise of this kind is not decoration. It tells a court asked to read the operative clauses what purpose Parliament declared, and in this case the declared purpose points in two directions at once: conservation and carbon, which pull toward protection, and facilitation of economic needs and carbon neutrality as the ministry framed them, which the government read as room for the exemptions. The insertion of a preamble by amendment is an uncommon drafting act, and it was noticed as such in the committee evidence and in commentary on the Bill. Most Indian statutes carry no preamble at all, and the principal Act had functioned for forty three years without one. A preamble added in 2023 cannot explain what Parliament intended in 1980; it can only declare what Parliament in 2023 wished the Act, as amended, to be understood as pursuing, and its recitals were drafted with that declaratory purpose in view. The recitals also perform a quieter service for the amendment's defence. By tying the Act's purpose to the Nationally Determined Contribution sink target and to the one-third cover goal that descends from the National Forest Policy, 1988, the preamble frames the narrowing of coverage and the enlargement of tree cover outside forests as two faces of a single strategy: protect the recorded estate strictly, and grow cover vigorously outside it without the deterrent of capture. The petitioners' reply, in substance, was that a preamble cannot enlarge or cut down operative text, and that the strategy's first face is only as good as the record that defines the estate, which returns the argument, as every argument in this dispute returns, to the records. Section 3 performs the renaming, substituting Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam for Forest (Conservation) Act in Section 1(1) of the principal Act. Section 4 inserts the new Section 1A, and Section 1A is where the amendment lives.

Section 1A(1) provides that the following land shall be covered under the provisions of the Act: clause (a), land that has been declared or notified as a forest in accordance with the provisions of the Indian Forest Act, 1927 or under any other law for the time being in force; and clause (b), land that is not covered under clause (a) but has been recorded in Government record as forest, as on or after the 25th October, 1980. A proviso then disapplies clause (b) to land which has been changed from forest use to use for non-forest purpose on or before 12 December 1996 in pursuance of an order issued by any authority authorised by a State Government or a Union territory Administration in that behalf. An Explanation provides that Government record means a record held by the Revenue Department or the Forest Department of a state government or Union territory administration, or by any authority, local body, community or council recognised by that government or administration.

Read as a coverage test, Section 1A replaces a question of fact with a question of record. Under the 1996 holding, an administrator or a court asked whether land was forest and looked at the land and at any record, either route sufficing. Under Section 1A, the inquiry is documentary from end to end: is there a declaration or notification under the 1927 Act or another law, or is there an entry in a government record, within the Explanation's wide but still documentary meaning, recording the land as forest on or after 25 October 1980? Land that is forest in fact, dense with trees, continuous with a notified forest, habitat to scheduled wildlife, but which no notification covers and no qualifying record names, is outside the Act. That is not an incidental gap in the drafting. It is the amendment's central decision, taken deliberately against the deemed forest category, and both sides of the later litigation described it in the same terms while valuing it oppositely: the government as the removal of an uncertainty that had no statutory basis, the petitioners as the removal of the very protection the 1996 order had been crafted to supply, because unrecorded forest is the forest most easily diverted without anyone outside the district ever learning of it.

### Why is 25 October 1980 the pivot date in the amended Act?

The date is the commencement date of the principal Act itself, deemed in force on 25 October 1980 as successor to an Ordinance of the same date. Section 1A(1)(b) counts a government record only if the land stands recorded as forest on or after that date, so the record window opens when the conservation regime opened.

The second anchor date does subtler work. The proviso's cut-off, 12 December 1996, is the date of the Godavarman order itself. Land recorded as forest that had been changed from forest use to non-forest use on or before that date, under an order of an authority authorised by the state or the Union territory, is taken out of clause (b) altogether. The policy is intelligible: conversions lawfully ordered before the judicial expansion should not be reopened by being swept into a record-based coverage clause, and the ministry's submissions repeatedly pointed to habitations, institutions and roads standing on recorded forest land under competent approvals as the anomalies the proviso cures. The choice of date is nevertheless a legislative acknowledgement with few parallels. Parliament fixed the boundary of its own new coverage rule by reference to the date of the judgment the rule displaces, so that the order being legislated against also supplies the grandfathering line for everything approved before it spoke. Conversions after 12 December 1996 that lacked central approval under the Act as the courts read it gain nothing from the proviso; conversions before it, made under state authority, are regularised by the coverage clause's own terms.

Two further features of Section 1A(1) repay attention because they decided the shape of the sequel. The first is the Explanation's breadth. Government record is not confined to the forest department's working plans or the revenue department's settlement registers. It extends to records held by any authority, local body, community or council recognised by the state or the Union territory, which is why the government could say, in Parliament and later in court, that the clause was not the straitjacket the petitioners described: identification exercises, community records and recognised local records could all qualify. The second feature is what the clause omits. It says nothing about who resolves a dispute over whether a record entry exists or what it means, nothing about the standard by which a record is to be read, and nothing about the status of the expert committee identifications made under the 1996 order, some of which were themselves records of a kind and some of which were not. Those silences are why the fight moved, within months, from the text of Section 1A to the subordinate exercise that would decide what the records in fact contained: the consolidated record required by Rule 16 of the rules made under the amended Act, examined in a later section of this article.

Section 5 of the Amendment Act then rewrites parts of Section 2 of the principal Act, and Section 6 inserts a new Section 3C. Both belong with the exemptions, because their combined effect is to widen the space inside the Act for activities and directions that do not require the approval the Act exists to demand, and they are taken next.

## The exemptions: land outside the Act, and activities outside the bar

Section 1A(2) lists the categories of land that shall not be covered under the provisions of the Act, and precision about each limit matters because much public argument about the amendment ran the categories together. The first category is small and specific: forest land situated alongside a rail line or a public road maintained by the Government, which provides access to a habitation, or to a rail and roadside amenity, up to a maximum size of 0.10 hectare in each case. The second category answers the plantation deterrent directly: trees, tree plantations or reafforestation raised on lands that are not specified in clause (a) or clause (b) of Section 1A(1) stand outside the Act, so that planting on land the Act does not cover cannot pull the land into the Act by the growth of the planting. The third category carries the strategic exemptions, and it has three limbs. Forest land situated within a distance of one hundred kilometres along international borders or the Line of Control or the Line of Actual Control, proposed to be used for construction of a strategic linear project of national importance and concerning national security, is outside the Act. Land up to ten hectares proposed to be used for construction of security related infrastructure is outside the Act. And land proposed to be used for construction of a defence related project, or a camp for paramilitary forces, or public utility projects as may be specified by the Central Government, the extent of which does not exceed five hectares in a Left Wing Extremism affected area as may be notified by the Central Government, is likewise outside the Act. Section 1A(3) then conditions the whole of sub-section (2): the exemption is subject to such terms and conditions, including conditions of planting trees to compensate for felling undertaken on the exempted lands, as the Central Government may by guidelines specify. Exempt, in this scheme, means exempt from the Act's approval bar, not exempt from every condition the Centre chooses to attach by guideline, and the strength of that qualification depends on guidelines whose content the Act does not prescribe.

The second half of the loosening works not on land but on the meaning of non-forest purpose, through Section 5 of the Amendment Act and the Explanation to Section 2 of the principal Act. Section 2 as amended is renumbered as Section 2(1), and its Explanation is rewritten so that non-forest purpose does not include any work relating or ancillary to conservation, development and management of forests and wildlife, such as silvicultural operations including regeneration operations; the establishment of check-posts and infrastructure for front line forest staff; the establishment and maintenance of fire lines; wireless communications; fencing, boundary marks and pillars, bridges and culverts, check dams, waterholes, trenches and pipelines; the establishment of zoo and safaris referred to in the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, owned by the Government or any authority, in forest areas other than protected areas; eco-tourism facilities included in the Forest Working Plan or Wildlife Management Plan or Tiger Conservation Plan or Working Scheme of that area; and any other like purposes which the Central Government may by order specify. Two movements in that list deserve separate notice. Several entries, check-posts, fire lines, wireless communications, fencing and waterholes among them, carried forward the exclusions the 1988 Explanation had already recognised, and their restatement was not controversial. The new entries were silviculture as an expressly protected operation, government-owned zoos and safaris outside protected areas, eco-tourism facilities that find a place in an approved plan, and the closing power for the Centre to add like purposes by order. It is the zoo and safari entry that drew the sharpest criticism, because a safari is a visitor attraction conducted for revenue within a forest, and its placement on the conservation side of the line, in habitat that the [Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972](/2014/10/15/wildlife-protection-act-1972-explained/) protects through its sanctuary and national park regime, struck critics as the conversion of a commercial use into a forestry purpose by definition. The government's answer was that the entry is confined to zoos and safaris owned by the Government or an authority, located in forest areas other than protected areas, and that interpretation of the line between a forestry work and a diversion should not be left to litigation parcel by parcel.

The remaining changes in Section 5 are quieter but not minor. In Section 2(1)(iii), the words describing assignees as not owned, managed or controlled by Government were replaced by a condition that assignment of forest land by way of lease or otherwise to a person or entity outside government is subject to such terms and conditions as the Central Government may by order specify. The approval requirement remains, but the statutory description of the class of assignees who trigger it was recast as a power in the Centre to set terms. A new Section 2(2) empowers the Central Government, by order, to specify the terms and conditions subject to which any survey, such as reconnaissance, prospecting, investigation or exploration including seismic survey, shall not be treated as a non-forest purpose, taking a recurring clearance dispute, whether mineral survey work in a forest itself needs forest clearance, and answering it by delegated order. Section 6 of the Amendment Act inserts Section 3C, under which the Central Government may from time to time issue such directions to any authority under the Central Government, a state government or a Union territory administration, or to any organisation, entity or body recognised by any of them, as may be necessary for the implementation of the Act. A directions power of that width, in a statute whose working depends on state records and state administrations, was read by the amendment's critics as one more centralising instrument, and by its defenders as ordinary implementation machinery. Both readings are available on the text, and the trail simply notes that the power exists, is unqualified by any stated procedure, and had no counterpart in the Act before 2023.

Two drafting features of the rewritten Explanation will decide cases long after the definition dispute is settled, and they repay a drafter's attention. The first is the movement from a closed to an illustrative list. The 1988 Explanation had named the excepted works with the word namely, which in Indian drafting practice marks an exhaustive enumeration: check-posts, fire lines, wireless communications and the listed constructions, and nothing beyond them except other like purposes at the margin. The 2023 Explanation says such as, which marks the list as illustrative of a genus, the genus being work relating or ancillary to conservation, development and management of forests and wildlife, and then adds at its end an express power for the Central Government to specify any other like purposes by order. The practical consequence is that the boundary of non-forest purpose, within the Act's surviving estate, is no longer fixed by the statutory list alone; it can be moved outward by executive order under an illustrative genus, and each movement will be tested, if it is tested, against the genus rather than against a closed text. The second feature is the gateway chosen for eco-tourism. An eco-tourism facility is outside non-forest purpose only if it is included in the Forest Working Plan, Wildlife Management Plan, Tiger Conservation Plan or Working Scheme of the area. Working plans are the decennial management documents of forest divisions, prepared by state forest departments and approved through administrative channels. The gateway therefore makes the quality and currency of plan preparation, documents of very uneven quality across divisions, a jurisdictional fact for the exemption, and it hands the effective decision to the planning process of the same department that manages the forest. Neither feature is an oversight; both are choices about where discretion should live, and the trail's observation is only that the amendment, which presents itself as replacing judicial discretion with textual certainty at the coverage stage, reintroduces administrative discretion, extensively, at the activity stage.

The survey provision carries a similar lesson in miniature. Prospecting and exploration for minerals, including seismic survey, had been a recurring clearance controversy, because survey work is transient and often reversible, yet under a strict reading it involved entry upon and disturbance of forest land for a purpose plainly oriented to eventual extraction. Successive administrative clarifications had treated some survey work indulgently and some strictly, and the uncertainty was genuine. Section 2(2) answers the question by delegation: surveys are not a non-forest purpose if conducted subject to terms and conditions the Central Government specifies by order. The legislature decided the principle, that survey should not of itself trigger the approval bar, and delegated the conditions, which is where the environmental content of the answer will actually be written. A reader tracing this amendment for its technique should mark the pattern, because it recurs across the Act: coverage by record, exemption by category, activity by genus and order, implementation by direction. The 2023 amendment is, in that sense, less a single decision than a redistribution of decision-making, from courts and committees applying a fact-based test toward records, categories and central orders, and it is that redistribution, more than any single exemption, that the pending challenge puts in issue.

## Three definitions of forest and which one applies

The artifact for this article is the comparison the whole dispute reduces to. Three definitions of forest have governed, or been enacted to govern, the Act's coverage: the statutory position as administered before 1996, the judicial dictionary definition of 1996, and the record-based definition inserted in 2023. They differ on what land is covered, what is excluded, who determines coverage, and, in the final row, on the point no other amendment in this series has presented: whether the definition in force is the definition being applied.

| Aspect | Statutory position before 2023 | Judicial dictionary definition, 1996 | Definition inserted in 2023 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What land is covered | Land recorded as forest in the forest department's estate and notified reserved or protected forests; the Act itself supplied no definition of forest | All land answering the dictionary meaning of forest, irrespective of ownership, notification or classification, and all land recorded as forest in any government record | Land declared or notified as forest under the Indian Forest Act, 1927 or any other law, and land recorded as forest in a government record on or after 25 October 1980 |
| What is excluded | Forest in fact that appeared in no record was outside the Act as administered, without any text saying so | Nothing that answered the dictionary meaning was excluded; the exclusion question did not arise | Forest in fact that no notification covers and no qualifying record names; pre December 1996 authorised conversions; the border, security, defence, roadside and plantation categories in Section 1A(2) |
| Who determines coverage | The state forest administration, by its records, subject to the Centre's approval power over diversion | The court-articulated test, applied by state expert committees identifying forests and by administrators and courts parcel by parcel | The government record, within the Explanation's meaning, and the consolidated record prepared under Rule 16 of the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Rules, 2023 |
| Whether it is being applied | Superseded in practice by the 1996 definition from December 1996 onward | Being applied, by interim direction of the Supreme Court, until the Rule 16 consolidated record is complete | In force from 1 December 2023 and not applied, by the same interim direction |

The final row is the payload. A reader who understands why the third column can say in force and not applied in the same breath understands the amendment, the litigation, and the administrative exercise on which both wait.

## The government's case, at the strength its authors gave it

The case for the amendment, as the government made it in the Statement of Objects and Reasons, in the ministry's evidence to the joint committee, and in ministerial speeches, rests on a jurisprudential claim before it rests on any environmental one. The claim is that the coverage of a statute should be found in the statute. The 1980 Act never enacted the dictionary test; a bench articulated it in 1996 as a construction of a silent text, and for twenty seven years the operative definition of forest in India was a judicial artefact administered through committees and clarifications rather than a rule a citizen could read. On this view, Section 1A is not a dilution of a statutory protection but the first legislative statement of the protection's boundary, and a legislature that defines the reach of its own Act is exercising the most ordinary of its functions. The ministry supported the claim with the anomalies the record shows: land recorded as forest that had been under habitations, institutions and roads under competent orders, yet continued to attract clearance demands; divergent state interpretations of recorded forest land, private forest land and plantations; and an approval system in which the preliminary question, is this land inside the Act at all, could consume years before the substantive question of diversion was reached.

The environmental limb of the government's case is the plantation argument, and it is stronger than its critics sometimes allow. India's climate commitments, recited in the amendment's own preamble, require a very large expansion of tree cover outside the recorded forest estate, on farmland, commons and private land, through agroforestry and plantation. A coverage rule that captures land by canopy alone penalises the success of that programme: the farmer or company whose planting matures into something answering the dictionary meaning of forest risks losing the free use of the land, and the rational response is not to plant. Section 1A(2)(b), taking plantations on non-covered land outside the Act, and the record test itself, are the government's instruments for breaking that deterrent, and the ministry argued that no lesser clarification had worked in twenty seven years of guidelines. The security limb is narrower and was put in terms of geography: linear projects of national importance along borders that are also lines of military confrontation cannot wait upon a parcel-by-parcel clearance queue designed for mining leases, and the one hundred kilometre belt, the ten hectare security parcel and the five hectare defence and public utility parcel in Left Wing Extremism affected areas were defended as calibrated limits, with every exemption conditioned by Section 1A(3) on terms the Centre prescribes, including compensatory planting. Finally, the government denied the premise of dilution in terms: the Explanation's meaning of government record is wide, extending to records of authorities, local bodies, communities and councils, and the rules framed under the amended Act require a consolidated record that expressly includes forest-like areas, unclassed forests and community forests, so that, on the government's account, the record the amendment makes decisive was being rebuilt to capture the very categories the petitioners feared losing.

## The petitioners' case, at the strength its authors gave it

The case against the amendment begins where the 1996 order began: with the character of unrecorded forest. India carries extensive forest that no notification ever captured, community forests in the north-east held under customary arrangements, forests transferred to forest departments in the zamindari abolition era and imperfectly recorded, revenue forests, and tracts identified as forest-like by the expert committees the 1996 order itself created. The petitioners, led by Ashok Kumar Sharma, a retired officer of the Indian Forest Service, together with other retired forest and civil service officers and, in a connected petition, the environmental organisation Vanashakti, contended that a record-based coverage rule fails exactly where protection is most needed, because the land outside the record is the land that can be diverted, sold or built upon without the diversion ever presenting itself to the clearance system as a forest case at all. The 1996 test, whatever its administrative untidiness, attached protection to the ecological fact; Section 1A attaches it to the quality of a state's paperwork, and the petitioners argued that paperwork quality varies by state in ways that track administrative capacity rather than ecology.

The exemption critique was geographic and specific. A belt one hundred kilometres deep along the international borders, the Line of Control and the Line of Actual Control takes in the whole or most of several north-eastern states and the Himalayan districts of the north, terrain that includes some of the country's richest remaining forests and some of its most fragile slopes, and within that belt the exemption is not project-specific in the statute: it attaches to a category of project described by importance and security concern, terms the Act does not define. The zoo, safari and eco-tourism entries in the rewritten Explanation were attacked as the definitional conversion of commercial recreation into forestry, and the directions power in Section 3C and the Centre's order-making powers over assignment terms and surveys were attacked as a pattern: a statute in the Concurrent List being steadily re-centred on the Union at each point where a state judgment had previously operated. The forest rights dimension, pressed in argument before the Court by counsel including Prashant Bhushan, is that diversion of forest land engages the rights recognised under the [Forest Rights Act, 2006](/2017/03/15/forest-rights-act-2006-explained/) for forest dwelling communities, and that an amendment redrawing which land counts as forest, made without Gram Sabha consent architecture of any kind, withdraws the terrain on which those rights are claimed before the claims are even examined. The petitioners also carried a number into the litigation, and numbers in this dispute are examined on their own terms in the FAQ to this article: drawing on the Forest Survey of India's State of Forest Report figures for forest cover lying outside the recorded forest area, they put the land exposed by the narrowing at about 1.97 lakh square kilometres. The government disputed the figure's meaning rather than its arithmetic, pointing out through the Forest Survey of India's own published clarifications that forest cover outside the recorded forest area includes horticultural plantations, pulpwood and urban and rural green cover, and maintaining that its consolidated record exercise would in any event bring genuinely forest-like land within the Act. The two sides were therefore not, at bottom, disputing a measurement. They were disputing whether a category defined by a map of tree cover and a category defined by a register of legal status could be treated as the same thing, which is the dispute the Court inherited.

## The numbers dispute, stated with its bases

Because the FAQ to this article carries the estimate question, the body states the numerical dispute once, carefully, with each figure attached to its source and its basis, and with the rival figures kept apart as the sources keep them apart. The Forest Survey of India, in the India State of Forest Report 2021, put the country's recorded forest area at about 7.75 lakh square kilometres, roughly 23.6 per cent of the geographical area, divided among reserved forests, protected forests and unclassed forests, and put the country's forest cover, a remote-sensing measure of tree canopy over land of at least one hectare with canopy density above ten per cent, at 7,13,789 square kilometres. The same report divided that cover between land lying within the recorded forest area, about 5.17 lakh square kilometres, and land lying outside it, about 1.97 lakh square kilometres. The petitioners' headline figure is the second of those numbers. Their argument, placed before the Court and widely reported from the petitions, is that forest cover outside the recorded forest area is protected, where it is protected at all, only because the 1996 definition reaches forest in fact; that Section 1A, running on notification and qualifying record entries, leaves such cover outside the Act; and that about 1.97 lakh square kilometres accordingly stands to lose the Act's protection, a figure some reports of the petitions rendered as 1.99 lakh square kilometres. The two renderings describe the same contention and should not be averaged or combined; this article uses the figure as the petitioners framed it against the Forest Survey's published division, about 1.97 lakh square kilometres of forest cover outside the recorded forest area.

The government's numerical answer operates at two levels. At the first level, the Forest Survey's own leadership publicly cautioned, in a note by its Director General Anoop Singh addressing commentary on the 2021 report, that forest cover outside the recorded forest area is a canopy category and not a legal one: it includes mango orchards, coconut and other horticultural plantations, pulpwood plantations and rural and urban green cover, none of which was ever the subject of the conservation statute's concern, so that equating the whole of the outside-recorded cover with natural forest at risk misdescribes the figure's own basis. At the second level, the ministry's position is that the legally relevant universe is not the canopy map at all but the record as the amended Act rebuilds it: the Explanation's wide meaning of government record, and Rule 16's direction to consolidate into the record the forest-like areas, unclassed forests and community forests identified by expert committees, are designed to bring genuinely forest land within Section 1A whether or not it was historically notified, so that the area ultimately outside the Act is the area the completed record does not identify, a number nobody can state until the exercise ends. A third family of figures circulates in commentary and should be kept distinct from both: estimates of the forest area lying within the one hundred kilometre border belt, which measure the exemption's geography rather than the definition's reach, and which vary widely with the belt's treatment of the Line of Control and the Line of Actual Control and of wholly forest districts in the north-east. This article states no belt figure, because the published versions rest on differing and usually unstated assumptions, and a belt estimate merged with the petitioners' cover estimate would produce a number with no source at all. The discipline matters beyond tidiness. In a dispute where the operative legal event is the completion of a record, the honest numerical statement is the one that names its measurer, its instrument and its category, and on that standard the petitioners' figure is an attributed contention with a published basis, the government's answer is a critique of the category plus a promise about a record still being built, and no figure in the case is a finding of any court.

One conflation in public discussion should be corrected before the numbers are left, because it reverses the categories. Unclassed forests are not unrecorded forests. Unclassed forest is a category inside the recorded forest area itself, the residue of the classification into reserved and protected forests, and the India State of Forest Report 2021 records about 1.21 lakh square kilometres of it within the recorded total of about 7.75 lakh square kilometres, alongside about 4.42 lakh square kilometres of reserved forests and about 2.12 lakh square kilometres of protected forests. Land in the unclassed category stands in the government record as forest land, however imperfect its management classification, and Rule 16 names it expressly among the lands the consolidated record must capture. The petitioners' exposure figure is a different quantity: it is canopy measured by satellite outside the recorded boundary altogether. Commentary that treats the 1.97 lakh square kilometre figure as the area of unclassed forest, or the unclassed figure as the measure of the amendment's exposure, merges a legal classification with a remote sensing category and produces a dispute about a number that neither side asserted. The distinction will matter when the consolidated records are finally placed before the Court, because the Rule 16 exercise operates on the first set of categories while the petitioners' case was built substantially on the second, and the judgment on the merits will have to say how the two maps relate.

## The challenge in court, and the order that suspended the definition

The writ petition Ashok Kumar Sharma, Indian Forest Service (Retired) and Others v Union of India and Another, Writ Petition (Civil) No. 1164 of 2023, with connected petitions, challenged the constitutional validity of the Amendment Act. The Supreme Court issued notice to the Union in October 2023, before the amendment had even commenced. The first significant order came on 30 November 2023, the day before commencement, from a bench of Justices B. R. Gavai and P. S. Narasimha. The Additional Solicitor General, Balbir Singh, stated on instructions that there was no intention to dilute the scope of forest as defined in the Godavarman judgment, and that no precipitative action would be taken by the Union in respect of forest as understood in the dictionary sense until further orders, the guidelines under the new provisions then being in finalisation. The bench recorded the statement. The petitioners had sought a stay of the amendments; no stay was granted then or later, and the distinction between what the petitioners asked for and what the Court eventually did is the hinge of the whole sequel, because the Court protected the position without ever stopping the statute.

The operative order is the interim order of 19 February 2024, passed by a bench of Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud and Justices J. B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra. The bench recited the petitioners' apprehension: that Rule 16 of the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Rules, 2023 requires state governments and Union territory administrations to prepare a consolidated record of forest lands within a period of one year, that the exercise was incomplete, and that in the interval land which is forest under the Godavarman judgment could be diverted for non-forest use under colour of the narrowed definition. The bench's directions, at paragraph 18 of the order as reported, were these. Pending completion of the Rule 16 exercise, the principles elucidated in the Godavarman judgment must continue to be observed, and the state governments and Union territories, while being guided by the statute and by Rule 16, shall peremptorily ensure compliance with the ambit of the expression forest as explained in that judgment. The Union of India, through the environment ministry, was directed to issue a circular to all states and Union territories in terms of the order. The states and Union territories were directed to forward the reports of the expert committees constituted under the Godavarman directions, recording the land identified as forest, by 31 March 2024, and the ministry was directed to maintain the record, digitise it, and make it available on its official website by 15 April 2024. The expert committees constituted under Rule 16 were directed to bear in mind the work of the earlier Godavarman committees, and were left at liberty to expand the ambit of forest lands worthy of protection. And the bench added a specific lock on the most contested of the new Explanation entries: any proposal for the establishment of zoos and safaris referred to in the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, owned by the Government or any authority, in forest areas other than protected areas, shall not be finally approved save and except with the approval of the Supreme Court.

### Can a zoo or safari be approved inside a forest while the challenge is pending?

Not finally. The rewritten Explanation to Section 2 takes government-owned zoos and safaris in forest areas other than protected areas outside the meaning of non-forest purpose, but the interim order of 19 February 2024 provides that no such proposal shall be finally approved except with the Supreme Court's approval, so the exemption cannot be completed while the direction stands.

Three things about the February 2024 order must be held apart, because public discussion of the case persistently merged them. The order is not a stay: the Amendment Act remains in force in its entirety, its renaming operates, its preamble stands, its machinery sections operate, and Section 1A is valid law whose validity the order does not question. The order is not a judgment on the merits: the bench decided nothing about the constitutional challenges, which include arguments about legislative competence, federal structure and the relationship between a statute and a prior construction of that statute by the Court, and the petitions remain pending for final hearing. And the order is not indefinite in its own terms: its trigger for release is the completion of the Rule 16 consolidated record exercise, an event in the administrative world rather than in the courtroom, so that the duration of the suspension lies substantially in the hands of the state administrations whose pace the Court has since had to police. A legislature enacted a definition; a court directed that the definition not be the one applied until a records condition is met; and the records condition then became the real subject of the litigation, which is where the trail leads next.

## Commencement under litigation: the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act 2023 in its first hundred days

The choreography of the amendment's first months explains how the suspension was built before any judge directed it. The Amendment Act left its commencement to notification, a standard device that here acquired unusual weight. The challenge was filed and notice issued in October 2023, while the Act was still uncommenced, so that the litigation preceded the law's operation and the Union went into the first hearing knowing the definition was contested before it had governed a single parcel. The commencement notification followed on 2 November 2023, appointing 1 December 2023, and was accompanied by the framing of the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Rules, 2023 under the amended Act, replacing the Forest (Conservation) Rules, 2022, and carrying in Rule 16 the consolidated record obligation on which so much would turn. On 30 November 2023, the eve of commencement, the Union's law officer gave the assurance recorded by the bench of Justices Gavai and Narasimha: no intention to dilute the Godavarman scope, and no precipitative action on the amended definition until guidelines under the new provisions were notified. The amendment therefore commenced, on 1 December 2023, already wrapped in a recorded executive assurance that its central provision would not be precipitately acted upon, an assurance given to a court in pending proceedings and later expressly reiterated by the Court itself in its order of 4 March 2025. When the three-judge bench in February 2024 converted the substance of that assurance into directions binding on every state and Union territory, it was formalising a pause the executive had already volunteered, which is one reason the order cannot accurately be described either as a judicial ambush of a functioning amendment or as a stay extracted from a resisting government. The suspension was, in its origin, consensual in form and judicial in force, and it hardened into its present shape only as the records exercise, the consensual foundation of the pause, failed to complete on anything like the schedule the pause assumed. Commencement provisions are usually the least read words of an amending Act. In this trail they are where the outcome was decided first.

## May Parliament answer a judgment? The separation of powers question

Beneath the coverage dispute sits a question of constitutional method on which serious opinion divides, and the article states both sides of it because the final hearing will turn on it. The government's position rests on the orthodox doctrine of legislative revision. A court construing a statute declares what the statute means; the legislature, if it prefers a different meaning, may amend the statute, and the amendment is not an appeal against the court but a new exercise of legislative power which courts then apply. The doctrine has limits that the government's own advisers would concede: the legislature must actually change the legal foundation rather than merely declare the judgment erroneous, it must act within its competence, and it cannot, by a bare declaration, reverse the outcome between the parties to a decided case. Section 1A, on the government's account, satisfies every condition. It does not say the Godavarman order was wrong; it enacts a coverage rule for the future, grounded in a stated policy about records, certainty and national goals, and the order's inter partes consequences are untouched. If that account is right, the only remaining questions are the ordinary ones of competence and of rights based challenges, and the amendment's prospects are strong.

The petitioners' answer is that the Godavarman order is not an ordinary construction awaiting revision, for three reasons that they pressed cumulatively. The first is institutional: the order did not merely gloss a word and retire; it set up an identification machinery, state expert committees and a continuing supervision through the Central Empowered Committee, under which rights and liabilities have been worked out for twenty seven years, and a legislature displacing the definition arguably displaces the foundation of that entire supervisory structure without addressing it. The second is constitutional in flavour: the dictionary definition was adopted in a judgment that treated forest protection as engaging the right to a wholesome environment under Article 21 and the duties reflected in Articles 48A and 51A(g), and where a construction rests partly on constitutional ground, the space for legislative reversal narrows, because the legislature cannot amend away the constitutional premise by ordinary statute. The third is methodological: the appropriate judicial response to an overbroad construction is ordinarily to seek review or a larger bench, and a legislative override that arrives without engaging the environmental findings on which the construction rested invites the charge that the form of revision is being used to avoid the substance of adjudication. The government replies, in substance, that none of this converts a statutory definition into a constitutional constant: Article 21 jurisprudence has never held that any particular statutory coverage line is constitutionally compelled, the amendment leaves the Act's protective machinery intact for the recorded estate, and a legislature that had to persuade a bench before it could define its own statute's reach would have ceded the legislative power itself. The interim order, notably, decides none of this. It holds the ring on the factual ground of incomplete records, which either side can describe as vindication: the government, that the Court found no basis to stay a duly enacted law; the petitioners, that the Court would not let the law operate on the ground until the ground was verified. The final hearing will have to choose, and the choice will be reported and analysed in the jurisprudence cluster of this series when it comes. This article's function is narrower: to leave the reader able to state, fairly and in each side's own strongest terms, exactly what the choice is.

## Rule 16 and the records crux

Subordinate legislation rarely decides the fate of an amendment, but the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Rules, 2023 come close. Rule 16(1) provides that, for the purpose of the Explanation of government records in Section 1A(1), the state governments and Union territory administrations shall, within a period of one year, prepare a consolidated record of such lands, including the forest like areas identified by the Expert Committee constituted for this purpose, unclassed forest lands or community forest lands on which the provisions of the Act shall be applicable. The rule is the bridge between the amendment's documentary test and the world the 1996 order had mapped. On paper, it instructs every state to do, under the new statute, substantially what the Godavarman expert committees were instructed to do under the old one: walk the categories, identify forest-like areas, unclassed forests and community forests, and consolidate them into a record. If that exercise were completed faithfully, much of the practical gap between the dictionary definition and Section 1A would narrow, because land identified into the consolidated record would stand recorded, and recorded land is what Section 1A covers. The petitioners' deeper objection survives the bridge: identification by a state committee under a rule is revocable and variable in a way a judicial definition is not, and the category of forest that no committee identifies remains outside. But the interim architecture the Court built treats the Rule 16 record as the event on which the new definition may begin to operate, so the exercise acquired a constitutional significance its drafters can scarcely have intended.

### What changes when a state completes its Rule 16 record?

Completion is the condition to which the interim direction is keyed: the direction that states apply the Godavarman definition operates pending completion of the consolidated record exercise under Rule 16. A completed, digitised record therefore moves the applying definition toward Section 1A for the lands it settles, though the challenge to validity remains pending.

The exercise did not complete itself. The February 2024 order had already had to chase the antecedent material, the Godavarman expert committee reports, with deadlines of 31 March 2024 for the states and 15 April 2024 for digitised publication, an indication that the identification work ordered in 1996 was still, in places, unconsolidated twenty eight years on. Through 2024 the record remained incomplete, and in 2025 the Court moved from direction to pressure. By an order dated 3 February 2025, the Court restrained the Union and the states from taking any step that would lead to a reduction of forest land unless land for compensatory afforestation was first provided, converting the interim protection from a definition rule into an area rule: whatever the definition, the total is not to shrink without replacement land. By a further order dated 4 March 2025, a bench of Justices B. R. Gavai and Augustine George Masih recorded that in several states the expert committees required for the Rule 16 exercise had not even been constituted, directed every state and Union territory in default to constitute its committee within one month and to complete the exercise, together with the identification directions associated with the Lafarge Umiam line of orders, within six months and report to the Union, directed the Union to consolidate the state reports and place the position before the Court, and warned that chief secretaries and administrators would be held personally responsible for lapses. The Additional Solicitor General, Aishwarya Bhati, reporting compliance, told the Court that only Sikkim, Gujarat and Odisha had completed the mandated identification process, and that West Bengal, Ladakh, Jammu and Kashmir and Lakshadweep had not formed their committees. The bench also reiterated the Union's recorded assurance of 30 November 2023 and the directions of February 2024 and February 2025. The petitions remain pending, listed for further hearing, and no final judgment on the validity of the Amendment Act has been delivered.

This is the practical crux the brief for this article identified, and the record supports it: the dispute is as much about administrative capacity as about legislative intent. A definition that runs on records can be no better than the records, and India's forest records are the product of settlement operations of very uneven vintage, of zamindari abolition transfers recorded in haste, of community tenure systems in the north-east that colonial and post-colonial record-keeping engaged with only partially, and of expert committee identifications that were ordered in 1996 and pursued at radically different speeds by different states. The 2023 amendment assumed a record that could bear the weight of being the definition. The litigation since has been, in large part, the discovery process for whether that assumption holds, conducted in open court with deadlines.

## The identification gap the amendment inherited

Between the 1996 order and the 2023 Act lies a twenty seven year administrative history that both sides used, because it proves opposite things depending on where the emphasis falls. The order directed every state to constitute an expert committee to identify forests within the dictionary meaning, and the committees were duly constituted in most states. Their work then proceeded unevenly. Some states completed identifications and placed identified deemed forest areas on record; others produced reports that were never fully acted upon; in several, the identification of forest-like areas remained entangled with the revenue record itself, because the committee could only identify as forest-like what survey and settlement documents gave it purchase on. Successive benches in the Godavarman proceedings returned to the incompleteness with directions and deadlines across the years, and the environment ministry, when the amendment was challenged, was in the uncomfortable position of defending a record-based definition while the Court observed that the Union did not hold a complete compilation of the states' own expert committee reports, the very reports on which recorded identification rested. The February 2024 order's insistence that those reports be forwarded within weeks and published in digitised form within two months is the clearest judicial statement of the gap.

For the government, the gap is the argument: a definition that depends on identification exercises which remain incomplete after twenty seven years has failed as an administrative instrument, whatever its ecological elegance, and a legislature is entitled to prefer a test that an officer can apply from documents in hand. For the petitioners, the gap is equally the argument, in the other direction: the identification exercises are incomplete in part because completion is politically costly, since identified forest is land withdrawn from the pool available for diversion, and a reform that responds to incomplete records by making protection depend on records rewards the incompletion. The trail records the symmetry without pretending it resolves anything. What can be said on the documents is narrower and firmer. The 1996 order created the category of deemed forest precisely because the official record and the ecological fact had diverged; the divergence was never administratively closed; the 2023 Act chose the record side of the divergence as the statutory test; and the Court's interim order holds the ecological side in application until a fresh record, built under Rule 16, is placed before it. Every participant's conduct since 2023 is consistent with that sentence, and no participant's rhetoric is.

The gap is not evenly distributed, and its geography is part of the merits. The record test behaves most consequentially where the formal record was always thinnest. In much of the north-east, forest land is held by communities and clans under customary tenure rather than by the state, large areas stand as unclassed forest in the official classification, and the apparatus of reservation and settlement under the Indian Forest Act, 1927 was applied late, partially or not at all. A coverage test that asks for a notification under that Act, or for a government record of the prescribed vintage, therefore asks the least developed record to carry the heaviest load in precisely the states with the largest share of forest cover by area, and the same states sit substantially within the one hundred kilometre border belt, so that the definition question and the exemption question compound each other there. The ministry's answer, that the Explanation embraces records of communities and councils recognised by the administration and that Rule 16 expressly directs the recording of community forest lands, is a real answer on the texts; its sufficiency depends on recognition and recording exercises that, in the north-east as elsewhere, the 2025 orders show to have been lagging. Elsewhere the pressure points differ but the structure repeats. Identified deemed forests in states such as Karnataka became politically contested once identification had consequences, with governments seeking to revisit identifications their own committees had made, while in the Aravallis of Haryana the forest character of land recorded under protective state legislation has been litigated for decades against intense development pressure at the edge of the national capital. The common thread is that identification is not a clerical act discovered to be incomplete; it is a distributive act, deciding which land is withdrawn from the development pool, and it is incomplete partly because completing it has always had losers who can organise. Rule 16's one year was, on this history, an optimistic figure when it was written, and the Court's progression from circulation directions in 2024 to personal accountability for chief secretaries in 2025 reads as a bench learning, in real time, the lesson the 1996 order's history already taught.

## The career of the deemed forest

The expression deemed forest appears nowhere in the principal Act, in either its 1980 or its amended form, yet it names the category over which the entire dispute is fought, and its career summarises the trail. The 1996 order's logic created it: if the Act applies to all land answering the dictionary meaning of forest irrespective of record, then land that is forest in fact but not in record is forest by deeming, by operation of the judicial test rather than by notification. State expert committees were the machinery for finding such land, and the committees' identified areas acquired, in administrative usage across the following decades, the label of deemed forests. The label then lived a double life. In ministry guidelines and state practice it was a working category, land to be treated as forest for clearance purposes pending or following identification; in the discourse of landowners and some state governments it became a term of complaint, denoting land captured by a test no statute contained, on identifications of uneven quality, sometimes sweeping in plantations, orchards and long-settled uses alongside natural forest. Both usages were accurate about different parcels, which is why the category resisted every attempt at a clean administrative settlement. The Lafarge Umiam Mining Private Limited v Union of India judgment of 2011, arising from limestone mining in the community forests of Meghalaya, refined the identification enterprise from within the Godavarman tradition itself, insisting that the identification of forest in areas of customary tenure proceed by a structured process in which scientific identification and the position of the local community both have a place; the order of 4 March 2025 expressly directed the Rule 16 committees to complete their exercise together with the directions in that line of authority, an instruction that quietly imports the Lafarge discipline into the new record the amendment requires. Section 1A is, in this light, the legislative abolition of the deemed forest as a route into the Act: after the amendment, land becomes covered by being declared, notified or recorded, and land that would formerly have been deemed forest is covered only if the Rule 16 record, or some other qualifying record, captures it. The interim order preserves the deeming route in practice until that capture is complete. No phrase in the case states the position more economically than the category's own history: deemed forest is what the dictionary test protects and the record test does not, and the distance between the two tests is the size of the category.

## What the amendment did not change

An amendment trail should record continuity with the same care as change, and the 2023 Act left the working core of the principal Act untouched. Section 2's approval bar remains the operative control: subject to the new coverage clause and the widened Explanation, no state government or other authority may direct that a reserved forest cease to be reserved, that forest land be used for a non-forest purpose, that forest land be assigned by lease or otherwise outside government, or that naturally grown trees be cleared for reafforestation, except with the prior approval of the Central Government. The advisory committee under Section 3 remains the statutory adviser on grant of approval. Sections 3A and 3B, the 1988 penalty and departmental liability provisions, stand unamended, so that contravention of Section 2 remains punishable with simple imprisonment extending to fifteen days. The rule-making power stands. Around the Act, the satellite architecture built by litigation and later legislation also stands: the compensatory afforestation regime and the Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016 that institutionalised its money, the net present value charges developed in the Godavarman proceedings, and the two-stage clearance process administered through the ministry, none of which the Amendment Act addresses. A project proponent whose land is inside the Act faces, after 2023, the same approval journey as before. The amendment changed the gate, not the road behind it, and arguments that it dismantled the clearance system, or that it changed nothing that matters, both fail against the text: it redrew the boundary of the estate to which the clearance system applies, and left the system itself intact.

The transitional position follows from the same continuity. The Amendment Act contains no provision unsettling approvals granted under the Act before its commencement, and diversions approved under the pre-amendment dispensation continue to be governed by the conditions on which they were granted, including their compensatory afforestation and net present value obligations. Proposals in the pipeline at commencement, and proposals made since, are processed under the compound position described in this article: the statute as amended supplies the text, the interim direction supplies the applying definition, and the ministry's circular following the February 2024 order instructed state administrations and nodal officers to conduct the coverage inquiry accordingly. A state officer examining a diversion proposal therefore performs, in sequence, the record inquiry Section 1A prescribes and the factual inquiry the direction preserves, and a proposal that fails the second inquiry cannot be approved on the strength of the first, however complete the record may be. That double inquiry is the daily administrative meaning of the suspension, and it will persist, on the Court's stated terms, until the Rule 16 record for the state in question is complete and the litigation concludes; practitioners should note that neither the ministry's guidelines under Section 1A(3) nor any state-level clarification can displace it, since the direction binds as an order of the Court in pending proceedings and not as an interpretation any administrator is free to revisit. The ministry's consolidated guidelines and clarifications under the amended Act, issued for the guidance of state administrations and user agencies, operate inside the same constraint: they organise the clearance process, restate the categories of the amended text and prescribe the documentation a proposal must carry, but a guideline is an executive instrument and cannot authorise what the interim direction forbids or exempt what the direction preserves. Administrators therefore work from three layers at once, the amended statute for the text, the guidelines for the process, and the Court's orders for the definition that governs the file in hand, and a proposal paper that cites only the first layer misstates the law it invokes.

One continuity deserves separate statement because it is the one the interim order most affects. The Godavarman proceedings themselves, Writ Petition (Civil) No. 202 of 1995, were not closed by the amendment and were not the vehicle of the challenge to it; the challenge came in fresh petitions, and the old mandamus, with its committee and its accumulated directions on diversion, felling and identification, continues on its own track. The amendment therefore sits at the intersection of two live litigations, one supervising the Act's administration under the definition the Court gave it, and one testing the validity of the legislature's attempt to give it a different one. That intersection is without precedent in this series, and it is the reason the article's verdict must be about the mode of the amendment's suspension rather than about its merits, which no court has yet judged.

## Which definition governs a parcel: the practical position

For a landowner, a project proponent or a state officer, the theoretical contest resolves into a sequence of questions about a specific parcel, and the sequence can be stated with precision. The first question is whether the parcel falls within Section 1A as enacted: notified or declared forest under the Indian Forest Act, 1927 or another law, or recorded as forest in a government record within the Explanation's meaning on or after 25 October 1980, subject to the proviso for conversions authorised on or before 12 December 1996, and subject to the Section 1A(2) exemptions. The second question is whether, independently of Section 1A, the parcel answers the dictionary meaning of forest as the Godavarman order expounds it, because while the interim direction stands, a parcel in that position is to be treated as inside the Act for the purpose of any diversion, whatever the record says. The third question is whether the proposal falls within a specific judicial lock, most obviously the bar on final approval of zoos and safaris in forest areas other than protected areas without the Supreme Court's approval, or within the February 2025 area direction restraining steps that reduce forest land without compensatory land first provided. In practice the second question dominates: until the Rule 16 record for the state is complete and the litigation concludes, an adviser who answers a coverage question from Section 1A alone is answering it wrong, and an adviser who answers it from Godavarman alone is ignoring the statute that will govern if the challenge fails. The honest professional answer is the two-layer one this article has built, stated parcel by parcel. The general position described in this article is legal information, not advice on an individual parcel, and questions of coverage, limitation and forum on a live proposal require advice on the specific record of the specific land.

### How does a landowner find out whether a parcel is inside the Act?

Begin with the record, then test the ground. The record inquiry asks whether the parcel is notified forest, or recorded as forest in a government record on or after 25 October 1980, and whether a conversion before 12 December 1996 applies. The ground inquiry asks whether the parcel answers the dictionary meaning of forest, made operative by the interim direction.

Three worked parcels show the machinery moving. Take first a tract of sal forest in a district where the settlement record is old and incomplete, never notified under the 1927 Act and never entered as forest in a qualifying record, though the tract is continuous with a reserved forest and has been treated as forest by the local community for generations. Under Section 1A as enacted, the tract is outside the Act, and a diversion of it would need no central approval. Under the interim direction, the tract answers the dictionary meaning, the state is bound to treat it as forest, and diversion without approval would contravene the direction and expose the approval, if any state authority purported to give one, to challenge. Take second a parcel of private agricultural land on which the owner raised a eucalyptus plantation a decade ago and since matured. Under the pre-2023 dispensation the plantation's canopy invited the argument that the land had become forest in the dictionary sense; under Section 1A(2)(b), trees and plantations raised on land not covered by Section 1A(1) are outside the Act, and the interim direction, which preserves the Godavarman definition, does not convert a raised plantation on non-forest land into a reserved category, because the 1996 test was concerned with forests, not with every stand of planted trees on private farmland. Take third a proposal for a road of strategic character within the one hundred kilometre belt along the Line of Actual Control, crossing notified forest. Section 1A(2)(c)(i) places forest land proposed for a strategic linear project of national importance concerning national security outside the Act, subject to the Central Government's guidelines and compensatory planting conditions under Section 1A(3); the interim direction preserves the Godavarman definition pending the records exercise, and the February 2025 direction restrains steps reducing forest land without compensatory land provided, so a proponent who treated the exemption as a complete release from process would be misreading the compound position. Readers assembling the three definitions for a board, a classroom or a Gram Sabha briefing can carry the comparison in [a deck opened through the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which displays presentation files in the browser without desktop software, keeping the artifact table's final row, in force and not applied, in front of the audience it most concerns.

## Four stations of the trail

The amendment trail, assembled, has four stations, and its direction of travel reverses twice. At the first station, 1980, Parliament created a control without a definition: a short Act that made central approval the price of releasing forest land, administered against the recorded estate because no other boundary was imaginable in the drafting room. At the second station, 1988, Parliament widened the control within the same silence, adding leasing, clearing for reafforestation and the plantation crops to the approval net and attaching a small penalty, still without saying what a forest is. At the third station, 1996, a court supplied the missing definition from the dictionary and from the fact of tree cover, and the control widened beyond anything the recorded estate had contained, administered thereafter through committees, clarifications and a continuing mandamus. At the fourth station, 2023, Parliament at last enacted a definition, and the definition it enacted was narrower than the one the court had supplied and organised on the opposite principle, record rather than fact. The trail's lesson is about definitions of natural things in statutes. A legislature that declines to define the central object of its Act does not keep the question; it lends the question to administrators first and to courts eventually, and the answer it receives will be built by the borrower for the borrower's purposes. When the legislature returns to reclaim the question after twenty seven years, it does not return to a blank page: it legislates against an entrenched construction, an administrative apparatus built on that construction, and a body of reliance, ecological and commercial, accumulated under both. The 2023 Act is therefore best read not as a routine amendment but as a reclamation, and its troubled commencement is the price of reclaiming late. Whether the reclamation succeeds is the question the pending challenge will answer, and the answer will matter beyond forests, because the same pattern, a silent statute, a judicial definition, a legislative reply, is available in every field where Parliament has left a natural or social category undefined and the courts have filled the silence.

## The trail's verdict: a suspension of a new kind

The series taxonomy of legislative non-arrival has grown by one. Earlier articles in this series have traced statutes that were enacted and never notified, statutes notified without the machinery to run them, statutes repealed in form while their power survived elsewhere, and codes commenced before their rules existed. The Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023 fits none of those. It was notified, commenced on a date appointed under its own first section, and is complete with its rules. Its central provision is nevertheless not the provision being applied, because a court, exercising interim power in a pending challenge and acting on the state's own recorded assurance, directed that the predecessor definition continue until an administrative condition is fulfilled. The mode is judicial, conditional and, in form, temporary; its duration is set by the speed of state record-keeping, which the Court's orders of 2025 suggest is measured in years rather than months. A definition of forest that is law and a different definition that governs: that sentence, which the brief for this article framed as the namable claim, is not rhetoric. It is a precise description of the legal position of every forest parcel in India while the Ashok Kumar Sharma petitions remain pending and the Rule 16 records remain incomplete.

On the merits, the article's verdict is necessarily divided, because the merits are divided. As a piece of legislative craft, the amendment is coherent: it identifies the uncertainty it targets, chooses a documentary test that an administrator can apply, grandfathers the conversions that predate the judicial expansion, conditions its exemptions, and builds its own bridge back to breadth in the Explanation's wide meaning of government record and in Rule 16's instruction to record forest-like, unclassed and community forests. As a settlement of the question it purports to settle, it has so far failed, and failed in a way its drafters should have foreseen: a definition whose justice depends entirely on the completeness of records that were known to be incomplete, and known to be incomplete because the Court had spent decades saying so, was always going to be met by an interim order keyed to the completion of those records. The strongest available reading of the whole episode is that the amendment and the interim order are two halves of one unfinished transaction. Parliament asserted, what no one disputes it may attempt, that coverage of a central statute is for the legislature to define. The Court asserted, what no one disputes it may do in a pending challenge, that the definition will not change on the ground until the factual foundation the legislature's own rules prescribe has been laid. What remains is the part neither institution can supply for the other: states must finish the record, and the Court must then decide, on a complete record, whether the narrowed definition is constitutionally sustainable. Until those two events occur, in whichever order they occur, the trail of the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980 ends where this article began, with an amendment in force and not applied, and with the oldest question in the statute's life, what is a forest, still answered by a judgment from 1996 rather than by the Act that bears a new name.

The possible shapes of the ending can be stated structurally, without prediction, because the interim architecture has already defined the alternatives. If the challenge fails and the amendment is upheld, Section 1A becomes the operative coverage rule from the release of the interim direction, the completed Rule 16 records become the working map of the Act, and the Godavarman definition passes into the history of the statute as the construction that governed for the decades between 1996 and the judgment, with the treatment of diversions approved in the interim period to be governed by the terms of the final order. If the challenge succeeds in whole, the amendment falls and the dictionary definition resumes its place as the construction of the unamended Act, and the Rule 16 record survives, if at all, as an administrative artefact of a repealed-by-judgment scheme. If the Court takes the middle course available in such challenges, sustaining the amendment while reading the record-based test together with the identification exercise, for instance by treating identified forest-like, unclassed and community forest land as within coverage as a matter of construction, the two definitions this article has compared will converge into a composite whose content only the judgment can supply. Which of these endings arrives matters greatly to every interest this article has described; that the ending is not yet available to be described is the final fact of the trail, and the article leaves it there, as the Court has.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did the 2023 amendment narrow the Forest Conservation Act?

The narrowing is the work of the new Section 1A, inserted by the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023. Before the amendment, the Act's coverage was governed by the Supreme Court's 1996 ruling in the Godavarman case, under which any land answering the dictionary meaning of forest was inside the Act irrespective of ownership, notification or record. Section 1A confines coverage to two documentary categories: land declared or notified as forest under the Indian Forest Act, 1927 or any other law in force, and land recorded as forest in a government record on or after 25 October 1980, with a carve-out for land whose use was changed under an authorised order on or before 12 December 1996. Forest that exists in fact but appears in no notification and no qualifying record falls outside the Act, and further categories are exempted by Section 1A(2).

### Q: Why was the Forest Conservation Act renamed?

Section 3 of the Amendment Act substituted Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam for Forest (Conservation) Act in the short title of the principal Act. The government presented the Hindi title, conventionally translated as the Forest (Conservation and Augmentation) Act, as expressing the Act's widened purpose: conservation joined with augmentation or enhancement of forest and tree cover, in line with the climate targets recited in the preamble the same amendment inserted. The renaming belonged to a wider 2023 pattern of Hindi short titles for central statutes. Members from non-Hindi speaking states objected that a Hindi-only name for a law administered in every state was non-inclusive and inaccessible to many of the people who must use it, and dissent on the name was recorded separately from dissent on the substantive changes.

### Q: Which lands were exempted by the 2023 forest amendment?

Section 1A(2) of the principal Act, as inserted in 2023, takes specified categories outside the Act. Forest land alongside a rail line or a government maintained public road, providing access to a habitation or to a rail and roadside amenity, is exempt up to 0.10 hectare in each case. Trees, plantations and reafforestation raised on land outside Section 1A(1) are exempt. Forest land within one hundred kilometres along international borders, the Line of Control or the Line of Actual Control, proposed for a strategic linear project of national importance concerning national security, is exempt. Land up to ten hectares for security related infrastructure is exempt, and land up to five hectares for defence projects, paramilitary camps or specified public utility projects in notified Left Wing Extremism affected areas is exempt. Section 1A(3) subjects the exemptions to terms and conditions, including compensatory tree planting, that the Central Government specifies by guidelines.

### Q: Did the 2023 amendment overturn the Godavarman definition?

In legislative intent, the amendment was designed to displace it: Section 1A replaces the dictionary meaning test of the order dated 12 December 1996 in T. N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v Union of India with a test running on notification and government records. A legislature amending a statute does not, in form, overturn a judgment; it changes the text the judgment construed, and it is for the courts to say whether the change is valid and what it does to the earlier construction. In operation, the displacement has not taken effect. By the interim order of 19 February 2024 in the challenge to the amendment, every state and Union territory must continue to apply the Godavarman definition until the consolidated record under Rule 16 of the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Rules, 2023 is complete, and the challenge itself remains undecided.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court order in February 2024 about forests?

By an interim order dated 19 February 2024 in Ashok Kumar Sharma, Indian Forest Service (Retired) and Others v Union of India, Writ Petition (Civil) No. 1164 of 2023, a bench of Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud and Justices J. B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra directed that, pending completion of the Rule 16 record exercise, the principles of the Godavarman judgment must continue to be observed and states and Union territories must ensure compliance with the ambit of the expression forest as explained in that judgment. The Union was directed to circulate the order to all states and Union territories. States were directed to forward the reports of the expert committees constituted under the Godavarman directions by 31 March 2024, and the environment ministry was directed to digitise the record and place it on its official website by 15 April 2024. Proposals for zoos and safaris in forest areas other than protected areas were not to be finally approved without the approval of the Court.

### Q: Has the narrowed forest definition taken effect?

No. The Amendment Act commenced on 1 December 2023 and Section 1A is valid law that has not been stayed or struck down, but the interim order of 19 February 2024 directs every state and Union territory to apply the Godavarman dictionary definition until the consolidated record under Rule 16 is complete. That exercise remains incomplete. In orders of February and March 2025 the Supreme Court restrained any step reducing forest land without compensatory land first provided, directed states in default to constitute expert committees within one month and finish the exercise within six months, and warned that chief secretaries would be personally responsible for lapses; the Union reported that only Sikkim, Gujarat and Odisha had completed the identification process. The definition enacted in 2023 is therefore in force and, by judicial direction, is not the definition being applied.

### Q: What is the Rule 16 record of forest lands?

Rule 16(1) of the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Rules, 2023 requires the state governments and Union territory administrations, within a period of one year, to prepare a consolidated record of the lands on which the Act applies, for the purpose of the Explanation of government record in Section 1A(1) of the Act. The consolidated record must include the forest like areas identified by the Expert Committee constituted for the purpose, unclassed forest lands and community forest lands. The record is the factual foundation of the amended definition, because Section 1A makes coverage depend on what a government record shows. It is also the event to which the Supreme Court's interim direction is keyed: the Godavarman definition continues to be applied pending completion of the record, and the Court's orders of 2025 imposed deadlines and personal accountability because completion had not been achieved.

### Q: How much forest land could lose protection?

The figure placed before the Supreme Court by the petitioners challenging the amendment is about 1.97 lakh square kilometres. Its basis is the Forest Survey of India's India State of Forest Report 2021, which records about 1.97 lakh square kilometres of forest cover lying outside the recorded forest area, within a total forest cover of 7,13,789 square kilometres; some reports of the petitions state 1.99 lakh square kilometres for the same contention. The petitioners argue that such cover is protected only through the Godavarman definition and falls outside Section 1A. The government disputes the inference rather than the survey: the Forest Survey's Director General cautioned that cover outside the recorded area includes orchards, plantations and urban and rural green cover, and the ministry maintains that the wide meaning of government record and the Rule 16 consolidated record will capture genuinely forest-like land. No court has made a finding on the figure.

### Q: When did the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act 2023 come into force?

The Amendment Act, Act 15 of 2023, received the assent of the President on 4 August 2023, but assent and commencement were different events. Section 1(2) of the Amendment Act left commencement to a date the Central Government would appoint by notification in the Official Gazette. By notification S.O. 4765(E) dated 2 November 2023, the government appointed 1 December 2023, and the amendments, including the renaming of the principal Act and the insertion of Section 1A, operate from that date. The earlier dates in the trail are distinct: the Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 29 March 2023, the joint committee reported on 20 July 2023, and the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha passed the Bill on 26 July 2023 and 2 August 2023 respectively.

### Q: What does "government record" mean in the amended Forest Conservation Act?

The Explanation to Section 1A(1) defines the expression for the purposes of the coverage clause. A government record means a record held by the Revenue Department or the Forest Department of a state government or Union territory administration, or a record held by any authority, local body, community or council recognised by that government or administration. The breadth is deliberate and was central to the government's defence of the amendment: coverage is not confined to forest department notifications or settlement registers, and records maintained by recognised local bodies, communities and councils can qualify. Rule 16(1) of the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Rules, 2023 then obliges every state and Union territory to build a consolidated record of the lands the Act covers, including forest like areas, unclassed forest lands and community forest lands identified through the expert committee process.

### Q: Why does the date 12 December 1996 appear in the amended Act?

The date is the date of the Supreme Court's order in T. N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v Union of India, the order that laid down the dictionary meaning of forest. The proviso to Section 1A(1)(b) uses it as a grandfathering line: the coverage clause does not apply to land recorded as forest which had been changed from forest use to use for a non-forest purpose on or before 12 December 1996, in pursuance of an order issued by an authority authorised by a state government or a Union territory administration. Land lawfully converted under competent authority before the judicial expansion is therefore not pulled back into the Act by the record clause, while conversions after that date gain no such protection. Parliament fixed the boundary of its new coverage rule by the date of the judgment the rule was designed to displace.

### Q: Which activities did the 2023 amendment declare are not non-forest purposes?

The Amendment Act rewrote the Explanation to Section 2 of the principal Act. Non-forest purpose does not include work relating or ancillary to conservation, development and management of forests and wildlife, such as silvicultural operations including regeneration operations; establishment of check-posts and infrastructure for front line forest staff; establishment and maintenance of fire lines; wireless communications; construction of fencing, boundary marks or pillars, bridges and culverts, check dams, waterholes, trenches and pipelines; establishment of zoos and safaris referred to in the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, owned by the Government or any authority, in forest areas other than protected areas; eco-tourism facilities included in the Forest Working Plan, Wildlife Management Plan, Tiger Conservation Plan or Working Scheme of the area; and any other like purposes the Central Government specifies by order. A new Section 2(2) separately allows surveys, including prospecting and seismic survey, to be kept outside non-forest purpose on terms the Centre specifies.

### Q: What did the Joint Parliamentary Committee do with the forest amendment Bill?

The Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 29 March 2023 and referred the same day to a Joint Committee of both Houses, a committee of thirty one members chaired by Rajendra Agrawal. The committee received 1,309 memoranda from experts, state governments, departments, public sector undertakings, ministries and the defence forces, among others, and examined the ministry's explanations on the objections received. Its report, laid before Parliament on 20 July 2023 and running to 201 pages, endorsed the Bill without recommending any change to its clauses. Four members filed notes of dissent, objecting among other things to the narrowing of coverage, the border and security exemptions, the treatment of zoos, safaris and eco-tourism, and the renaming of the Act. The Bill as reported was then passed by the Lok Sabha on 26 July 2023 and by the Rajya Sabha on 2 August 2023.

### Q: Why did the government say the 2023 forest amendment was necessary?

The reasons were stated in the Bill's Statement of Objects and Reasons, in the environment ministry's submissions to the joint committee and in the minister's speeches, and they form a consistent set. The government said the 1996 judgment had produced divergent interpretations of the Act's application to recorded forest land, private forest land and plantations, and that land already put to habitations, institutions and roads under competent approval continued to attract clearance demands. It said the dictionary test deterred tree planting on private and non-forest land, working against the Nationally Determined Contribution target of an additional carbon sink of 2.5 to 3.0 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent by 2030, the net zero target of 2070 and the goal of forest and tree cover over one-third of the land area. It also cited the needs of strategic and security infrastructure near the borders, which the exemptions in Section 1A(2) address within stated limits.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court direct in 2025 about identifying forests?

Two orders of 2025 carried the interim regime forward. By an order dated 3 February 2025, the Court restrained the Union and the states from taking any step that would lead to a reduction of forest land unless land for compensatory afforestation was first provided. By an order dated 4 March 2025, a bench of Justices B. R. Gavai and Augustine George Masih recorded that several states had not constituted the expert committees the identification exercise requires, and directed every state and Union territory in default to constitute its committee within one month, complete the exercise under Rule 16(1) of the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Rules, 2023, together with the identification directions in the Lafarge Umiam line of cases, within six months, and report to the Union, which was to consolidate the position and place it before the Court. Chief secretaries and administrators were warned of personal responsibility for lapses. The Union reported that only Sikkim, Gujarat and Odisha had completed the process.

### Q: Do the exemptions in Section 1A carry any conditions?

Yes. Section 1A(3) provides that the exemption under Section 1A(2) is subject to such terms and conditions, including the conditions of planting trees to compensate for felling of trees undertaken on the exempted lands, as the Central Government may by guidelines specify. Exemption from the Act therefore means exemption from its prior approval requirement for the listed categories and extents; it does not mean the land is released from every condition the Centre attaches to the category, and the content of the control depends on the guidelines issued for the purpose. Two qualifications from the litigation overlay the statutory position while the challenge is pending: the interim direction of February 2024 keeps the Godavarman definition in application generally, and the order of 3 February 2025 restrains steps reducing forest land without compensatory land first provided.

### Q: What power did the new Section 3C give the Central Government?

Section 6 of the Amendment Act inserted Section 3C into the principal Act. It provides that the Central Government may, from time to time, issue such directions to any authority under the Central Government, a state government or a Union territory administration, or to any organisation, entity or body recognised by the Central Government, a state government or a Union territory administration, as may be necessary for the implementation of the Act. The power is new; the Act contained no general directions clause before 2023. It is unqualified by any stated procedure in the text. Defenders treat it as ordinary implementation machinery for a statute the Centre must administer uniformly. Critics read it, together with the Centre's order-making powers over exemption guidelines, assignment terms and surveys, as one element of a pattern re-centring a Concurrent List subject on the Union, in a field where field administration and records remain with the states.

### Q: How was the Forest Conservation Act amended before 2023?

Once, by the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 1988, Act 69 of 1988. The 1988 amendment widened Section 2 of the principal Act in three ways. It extended the prior approval requirement to the assignment of forest land by way of lease or otherwise to any private person or to any authority, corporation, agency or other organisation not owned, managed or controlled by Government. It extended the requirement to the clearing of naturally grown trees on forest land for the purpose of using the land for reafforestation. And it expanded the Explanation's definition of non-forest purpose to name cultivation of tea, coffee, spices, rubber, palms, oil-bearing plants, horticultural crops and medicinal plants. The same amendment inserted Section 3A, making contravention of Section 2 punishable with simple imprisonment extending to fifteen days, and Section 3B, on offences by government departments and authorities. Parliament amended the Act on no other occasion between 1980 and 2023, although the courts, the rules and the guidelines transformed its operation in the interval.

### Q: What remains undecided in the challenge to the 2023 amendment?

Everything on the merits. The petitions in Ashok Kumar Sharma and the connected cases raise the constitutional validity of the Amendment Act, including questions of legislative competence in a Concurrent List field, the federal balance between the Centre and the states, the bearing of the right to a wholesome environment on a statutory definition of forest, and the extent to which a legislature may displace a long-standing judicial construction around which an identification machinery and decades of administration have been built. The Supreme Court's orders so far are interim: the order of 19 February 2024 and the orders of 2025 regulate the position while the case is pending, restrain reduction of forest land, and press the states to complete the Rule 16 record, but they decide no issue of validity, and the amendment has neither been stayed nor struck down. The final hearing may uphold the amendment, strike it down, or sustain it with a reading that qualifies Section 1A, and no outcome should be treated as settled before judgment.

### Q: What is the difference between forest cover and recorded forest area?

The two expressions measure different things with different instruments, and the forest definition dispute turns on the difference. Recorded forest area is a legal and administrative category: land recorded as forest in government records, divided into reserved, protected and unclassed forests, which the India State of Forest Report 2021 puts at about 7.75 lakh square kilometres. Forest cover is a remote sensing category used by the Forest Survey of India: all land of one hectare or more with tree canopy density above ten per cent, irrespective of legal status, ownership or land use, which the same report puts at 7,13,789 square kilometres. The two maps diverge in both directions: recorded forest may carry little or no canopy, and canopy amounting to about 1.97 lakh square kilometres lies outside the recorded boundary. Section 1A runs coverage on the first map; the Godavarman definition reached toward the second.
