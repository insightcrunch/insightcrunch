On 19 July 1969, a Saturday, the acting President of India, V.V. Giri, signed an ordinance that transferred the undertakings of the fourteen largest Indian-owned commercial banks to the state before the weekend ended. Bank nationalisation 1969 entered Indian political vocabulary in that single act, and the standard accounts treat the event as economic policy: a reform of credit allocation announced by a Prime Minister committed to a socialist programme. The chronology of May, June and July 1969 supports a different and prior reading. The ordinance landed less than two weeks after the Bangalore session of the Congress Working Committee had named Neelam Sanjiva Reddy as the party's presidential candidate on 12 July 1969, against the known preference of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi. It landed less than a month before the presidential poll of 16 August 1969, in which Indira Gandhi would defy her own party's candidate. Between those two dates, the takeover of the banks did political work that no other available instrument could do at that speed. This article defends one claim and tests it against the record: bank nationalisation was the second move in Indira Gandhi's coordinated campaign against the syndicate, the group of senior Congress leaders who controlled the party organisation, and its economic function as state-directed credit allocation, though real and durable, was secondary to that political function.

## The Timing Puzzle: Bank Nationalisation 1969 as a Political Move

A policy reading of the July 1969 decision has to explain why a reform that had been available to Indian governments for two decades arrived on one particular Saturday, by ordinance, with Parliament due to reconvene on Monday 21 July 1969. An economic calendar cannot supply that explanation. The deposit data on which the takeover rested had been published by the Reserve Bank of India for years. The critique of concentrated bank ownership had been in print since R.K. Hazari documented the interlocking directorships of the corporate private sector in 1966. The Congress party's own ten-point programme, adopted in May 1967, had promised social control of banking, and the machinery of social control had been operating since February 1969 under Finance Minister Morarji Desai. Nothing in the economic situation of July 1969 was new. What was new was a presidential succession. President Zakir Husain died in office on 3 May 1969. Vice President V.V. Giri became acting President, and the election of a successor placed the one constitutional office capable of checking a Prime Minister in the gift of an electoral college in which Congress legislators predominated. The syndicate, the informal directorate of senior leaders that included Congress president S. Nijalingappa, K. Kamaraj, Neelam Sanjiva Reddy, Atulya Ghosh and S.K. Patil, intended the presidency for Reddy. Control of the presidency, added to control of the party organisation, would have left Indira Gandhi as the syndicate's tenant in the Prime Minister's office.

The sequence that followed is the evidence. Indira Gandhi's advisers began preparing the nationalisation proposal inside the Prime Minister's Secretariat in May and June 1969, before the policy had been admitted to exist. At the Bangalore session in July, she circulated a short paper of economic proposals, remembered as her stray thoughts note, which floated nationalisation as an idea still open for discussion while the drafting of the measure was already advanced in her secretariat. On 12 July 1969 the Bangalore session ratified Reddy's candidacy. On 16 July 1969 Indira Gandhi took the finance portfolio from Morarji Desai, the minister constitutionally responsible for banking and the most senior opponent of nationalisation inside the cabinet, and Desai resigned from the government. On 19 July 1969 the ordinance issued. On 20 July 1969 Giri resigned the acting presidency to contest the election as an independent candidate, a candidacy Indira Gandhi backed against her own party's nominee. On 16 August 1969 the electoral college voted, after Indira Gandhi had called on Congress electors to vote according to conscience, and on 20 August 1969 Giri was declared elected. Every date in that chain is a matter of public record, in the Gazette of India, in the Congress session proceedings and in the election returns. The chain has a direction. Each step either weakened the syndicate's instruments or strengthened the Prime Minister's independent position, and nationalisation was the step that converted a party dispute into a national contest between a Prime Minister identified with the poor and an organisation identified with privilege.

The instrument chosen confirms the reading. An ordinance under Article 123 of the Constitution is the executive's emergency legislative route, available when Parliament is not in session and circumstances require immediate action. A government confident of its party's support introduces a bill. A government whose party organisation is held by its opponents, and whose finance minister has just resigned in protest, uses an ordinance on the last working day before the session opens and before the acting President who is willing to sign it leaves office to contest the presidency. The haste was not administrative sloppiness. The haste was the strategy, because the political value of the act depended on its arriving between the Bangalore decision and the presidential vote, as a demonstrated fact about who governed India, rather than as a proposal the syndicate's parliamentarians could amend, delay or defeat. Inder Malhotra's biography Indira Gandhi, published in 1989, reconstructs this sequence from the testimony of participants and treats the July decisions as a single campaign. The campaign reading also explains what the policy reading cannot: why the government accepted the legal fragility of the ordinance route, struck down by the Supreme Court within seven months, when a slower bill drafted with the same care would have survived judicial scrutiny. A bill that survived would have arrived after the presidential election. The ordinance arrived in time.

### What did the fourteen banks control on the eve of nationalisation?

The fourteen banks taken over on 19 July 1969 were every Indian scheduled commercial bank holding deposits above fifty crore rupees, and between them they held deposits recorded at 2,632 crore rupees, operated 4,130 branches, and constituted the commanding height of Indian commercial banking, the channel through which the industrial houses of Bombay, Calcutta and Ahmedabad financed trade and manufacturing.

The list itself reads as a roll of the old commercial order: the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Punjab National Bank, the Bank of Baroda, the United Commercial Bank, Canara Bank, the United Bank of India, Dena Bank, Syndicate Bank, the Union Bank of India, Allahabad Bank, the Indian Bank, the Bank of Maharashtra and the Indian Overseas Bank. These were the institutions whose boards interlocked with the boards of the managing agency houses and the large industrial groups. The Reserve Bank's own enquiries, and Hazari's 1966 study of the corporate private sector, had documented the pattern by which a small circle of directors sat on the boards of the great banks and of the companies that borrowed from them, so that credit decisions followed the map of corporate connection rather than any public criterion of need. The political charge of July 1969 drew its force from that documented record. When Indira Gandhi told the nation in her broadcast of 19 July 1969 that the banking system had to serve the farmer, the small industrialist and the exporter rather than a few large houses, the claim named a structure her listeners could recognise, and the structure had names, boards and balance sheets. The takeover therefore carried a double legibility that a purely technical reform would have lacked: to economists it was a change in the ownership of credit, and to voters it was the dispossession of fourteen named citadels of privilege, executed in a single night by a Prime Minister whom the party bosses had tried to corner eleven days after they imposed their presidential candidate on her.

## The Fourteen Names: What the Old Banking Order Was

The fourteen institutions were not an abstraction called private banking. Each carried a founding history inside the commercial order the republic had inherited, and the roll of their origins shows why their takeover registered as a social event and not merely a financial one. The Punjab National Bank, founded at Lahore in 1894 with Lala Lajpat Rai among its early moving spirits, was the pride of the nationalist banking tradition. The Bank of Baroda dated from 1908 and the patronage of Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III. The Bank of India had been raised in Bombay in 1906 by the city's merchant houses. The Central Bank of India, founded in 1911 under Sir Sorabji Pochkhanawala, advertised itself as the first Indian commercial bank wholly owned and managed by Indians. The Indian Bank of Madras, founded in 1907, rose from the wreckage of the Arbuthnot crash that had ruined the presidency's savers. Allahabad Bank, founded in 1865, was the oldest joint-stock bank in the country. Canara Bank came from Mangalore in 1906, Syndicate Bank from Udupi in 1925, where T.M.A. Pai was among its founders, a lineage that placed the father of Indira Gandhi's adviser T.A. Pai inside the founding generation of one of the very banks on the list. The Union Bank of India, founded in 1919, had been inaugurated by Mahatma Gandhi. The Indian Overseas Bank, founded in 1937 by M.Ct.M. Chidambaram Chettyar, served the Chettiar networks of Southeast Asia. The United Commercial Bank, founded at Calcutta in 1943 by the industrialist G.D. Birla, embodied in a single charter the interlock the reformers condemned, a great house's own bank. Dena Bank dated from 1938, the Bank of Maharashtra from 1935 at Pune, and the United Bank of India from the 1950 consolidation of Bengal's smaller banks.

Two conclusions follow from the roll. The first is that the takeover dispossessed Indian capital, not foreign: the foreign banks, the exchange banks of the old imperial trade, were left untouched by the fifty-crore threshold, a selectivity the Supreme Court would later hold against the Act under Article 14 and which the government defended on the ground that the object was the domestic commanding height. The second is that the fourteen were woven into every region's commercial life, which is why their seizure in a single night was legible in every market town in the country by Monday morning. A reform of the Reserve Bank's refinancing rules would have been invisible outside the financial pages. The vesting of the Punjab National Bank and the Bank of Baroda in the state was a headline in Lahore-descended refugee colonies in Delhi and in the bazaars of Baroda alike. The drafters understood the semiotics of their instrument. They were not nationalising an industry in the abstract. They were taking named, loved, resented, century-old institutions, several of them monuments of the nationalist movement itself, and the shock of recognition that ran through the country was the medium in which the presidential electors cast their votes four weeks later.

## The Cast of the Succession: An Organisation in Other Hands

The political function of nationalisation becomes visible only against the distribution of power inside the Congress in the first half of 1969, and that distribution had been hardening since the succession struggles that followed Nehru's death, through [the succession machinery the syndicate had operated since 1964](/2019/03/15/after-nehru-1964-succession-question/). The syndicate was not a faction of opinion. It was a network of office: K. Kamaraj, the former Madras chief minister who had made and unmade successions from within the party presidency; S. Nijalingappa, who held the Congress presidency in 1969 and with it the machinery of tickets, funds and discipline; Neelam Sanjiva Reddy, the Andhra leader marked out for the presidency of the republic; S.K. Patil of Bombay, whose municipal and organisational base commanded the party's richest fundraising territory; and Atulya Ghosh of West Bengal, who managed the east. Around them stood Morarji Desai inside the cabinet, senior enough to be a rival prime minister in waiting. These men had installed Shastri in 1964 and Indira Gandhi in 1966, the second installation on the calculation that a leader without an independent base would govern on advice. By 1969 the calculation had failed in their eyes: the Prime Minister had built her own secretariat under Haksar, cultivated the Congress left and the chief ministers, and begun to treat the organisation as an instrument to be directed rather than obeyed. The presidential succession offered the syndicate its corrective. A President Reddy, installed for a five-year term, would hold the formal powers of government formation at the next crisis, and the working committee's majority could then discipline or replace a Prime Minister who had ceased to consult it.

Indira Gandhi's counter-position rested on assets of a different kind, and the assets explain her choice of weapon. She held the government: the secretariat, the finance ministry after 16 July, the ordinance power, the broadcast microphone of All India Radio. She held the Congress left, the younger parliamentarians and ministers, among them the adherents of the Young Turks tendency, for whom the ten-point programme was a charter and its non-implementation an indictment of the organisation. She held, after the Faridabad session of the All India Congress Committee in April 1969, a documented record of the party's own radical resolutions, adopted in open session, which the organisation's managers had no intention of executing. The Faridabad session matters to the July sequence because it completed the brief the Prime Minister would plead in the country: the party had resolved, the organisation had shelved, and the government would act. When Nijalingappa's working committee named Reddy on 12 July, in the face of her known preference for a consensus candidate and her public hints that V.V. Giri's claims deserved consideration, the organisational struggle became a constitutional one. She could not veto the nomination. She could change the ground on which the nomination would be judged by the electoral college, and the ground she chose was the ownership of the banks, the one subject on which the syndicate's leaders, tied to the commercial order of Bombay and Ahmedabad and to Desai's fiscal doctrine, could not follow her without ceasing to be themselves.

The Bangalore decision's manner compounded its substance. The nomination was carried by the majority of the party's parliamentary board over the Prime Minister's stated preference for a consensus and her resistance to Reddy's name, and it was announced as a settled instruction to the party's legislators, with the expectation that the head of the government would campaign for the man chosen to check her. Within the conventions the syndicate itself had observed since 1964, the move was regular. Within the politics of 1969 it was a declaration that the organisation regarded the Prime Minister as its employee on the largest question a party can decide, the occupancy of the republic's first office. Indira Gandhi's refusal to perform the campaigner's part, her absence from Reddy's canvass and her public neutrality between her party's candidate and the independent, were the visible signals, legible to every elector in the college, that the instruction would not be obeyed. The ordinance of 19 July was the material form of the same refusal, addressed to the same electors, in a register no one could misread.

## The Long Prehistory: Why Banking Survived the Nehru Settlement

The question of why the banks remained private until 1969 has its own record, and the record disciplines any claim that economic logic alone dictated the takeover's date. The founding state had not shrunk from public ownership of finance where it judged ownership necessary. The Reserve Bank of India was nationalised with effect from 1 January 1949 under the Reserve Bank (Transfer to Public Ownership) Act of 1948. The Banking Regulation Act of 1949 built the supervisory frame. The Imperial Bank of India was converted into the State Bank of India on 1 July 1955 under the State Bank of India Act, expressly to carry banking into the countryside the joint-stock banks neglected. The life insurance business was nationalised in 1956 through the Life Insurance Corporation. Each step stopped at the commercial banks themselves, and the stopping was deliberate. The All-India Rural Credit Survey, reporting in 1954 under A.D. Gorwala's committee, had found the private banks' contribution to rural credit negligible and had pronounced its famous verdict on the cooperative alternative, that cooperation had failed and cooperation must succeed, and the state's chosen instruments for the countryside became the cooperative structure and the State Bank's branch programme rather than confiscation of the joint-stock banks. Nehru's governments, which built public steel, public power and public insurance, treated the commercial banks as an engine to be regulated and cajoled, partly from fiscal prudence about compensation, partly from the judgement that the planning system could direct credit through the Reserve Bank's powers without owning the lenders. The Mahalanobis strategy of the Second Plan needed the banks' deposit mobilisation more than it needed their ownership.

The consequence of that forbearance was a banking system whose structure, on the eve of 1969, contradicted the republic's declared pattern at the point of maximum visibility. Scheduled commercial banking was dominated by institutions headquartered in the port cities, directed by boards interlocked with the industrial houses, and oriented to the finance of trade and large manufacture. The Hazari documentation of 1966 and the Reserve Bank's own returns gave the contradiction figures: agriculture's share of bank credit in the neighbourhood of two per cent, industry and trade absorbing the great bulk, districts without offices, population per office among the highest of any large economy. The contradiction was not discovered in 1969. It was the standing condition that the Congress left cited at every session and that the ten-point programme of May 1967 had promised to correct by social control. What requires explanation, therefore, is not the existence of the case for nationalisation, which was old, documented and officially conceded, but the conversion of that standing case into an act of state on a chosen Saturday. Economic history supplies the case. Only political history supplies the conversion, and the conversion is the event. The distinction also disposes of a lazy symmetry in the textbook treatments, which narrate 1969 as the year an idea whose time had come was implemented. Ideas whose time has come do not require the prior dismissal of the finance minister, the secret drafting of an ordinance in the Prime Minister's secretariat, or the signature of an acting President on his last full day in office.

## The Ground Prepared: Social Control and the Case Against the Banks

Bank nationalisation did not descend on an unreformed banking system in July 1969. It displaced a reform already in force, and the displacement is itself evidence for the political reading, because the displaced reform belonged to Morarji Desai. The Congress ten-point programme of May 1967 had committed the party to social control of banks, a formula that promised the substance of public direction without the transfer of ownership. The National Credit Council, constituted in December 1967 with the Finance Minister in the chair, was charged with assessing the demand for credit and indicating priorities among sectors. The Banking Laws (Amendment) Act of 1968, whose operative provisions took effect on 1 February 1969, reconstituted bank boards, required full-time professional chairmen, restricted advances to firms in which bank directors held interests, and armed the Reserve Bank with enlarged powers of direction. Desai presented social control as the sufficient answer to the charge of concentrated credit: ownership could remain private, he argued in substance, if governance, inspection and credit guidance were tightened. The formula also had a party function. Social control was the syndicate-aligned answer to the Congress left, a reform that conceded the vocabulary of the ten-point programme and withheld the deed.

By the middle of 1969 the formula was failing on its own terms, and the failure was documented rather than merely alleged. The study group of the National Credit Council chaired by D.R. Gadgil reported in 1969 on the organisational framework for implementing social control and recorded how thinly bank credit reached agriculture and the small borrower; commercial banks directed the overwhelming share of their advances to large and medium industry and to wholesale trade. Agriculture, which employed the great majority of the working population, received barely two per cent of scheduled commercial bank credit at the end of the 1960s, a figure the Reserve Bank's statistical publications of the period sustain. Branch geography told the same story. In June 1969 India had 8,262 bank offices for a population approaching 530 million, roughly one office for every 65,000 people, and the offices clustered in cities and in the commercially advanced regions, leaving entire rural districts without a single bank. The interlocking directorate problem, which the 1968 Act was supposed to dissolve, dissolved slowly, because the Act regulated the conduct of boards whose composition and loyalties it could only gradually alter. Indira Gandhi's circle drew the conclusion that served the political argument: social control had been tried by its own author and had produced reports, councils and amended statutes, but not a redistribution of credit that a villager could see. The conclusion was available to any reader of the Gadgil report. What Indira Gandhi added was the willingness to convert the conclusion into a confiscation of ownership, and to do so at the moment when confiscation doubled as a weapon in the succession struggle.

The ideological ground had been prepared earlier and deeper, in [the Avadi rhetoric-practice gap that nationalisation operationalised](/2019/02/05/avadi-resolution-socialist-pattern-1955/). The Avadi session of January 1955 had committed the Congress to a socialist pattern of society, and the Second Five Year Plan had built the heavy-industry core of that commitment in the public sector, but banking had been left in private hands at the commanding point where the plan's credit was allocated. The gap between the Avadi vocabulary and the ownership map of Indian finance was fourteen years old in 1969, and every Congress radical knew its dimensions. Nationalisation closed the most visible remaining gap between the party's declared pattern and the structure of the economy. That closure was the economic substance of the act, and this article does not minimise it. The argument is about order and function. The gap had been visible since 1955 without producing a takeover under Nehru, under Shastri or under Indira Gandhi's own first three years. It produced a takeover in the nine days between a presidential nomination the Prime Minister opposed and a presidential election she intended to win. The Avadi inheritance supplied the legitimation. The succession struggle supplied the date.

## The Secret Drafting: Haksar, Kumaramangalam and the Pai Papers

The preparation of the takeover inside the Prime Minister's Secretariat during May and June 1969 is the documentary spine of the political reading, and it rests on named archives rather than on reconstruction. P.N. Haksar, who had become the Prime Minister's principal secretary in 1967 and who directed the secretariat as the centre of her government, supervised the preparation. The Haksar Papers, preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, carry the correspondence of May to July 1969 through which the nationalisation proposal moved from a political decision to a draft instrument. The correspondence shows the sequence the political reading requires: the decision to prepare nationalisation preceded the public debate about nationalisation, and the public debate, when it came at Bangalore, was staged by a Prime Minister who already possessed the prepared measure. Mohan Kumaramangalam, the barrister and former communist who had become the most articulate ideologue of the Congress left around Indira Gandhi, supplied the argumentative architecture, the framing of bank ownership as the point where the socialist pattern either became real or confessed itself rhetoric. The under-cited source in the standard accounts is the correspondence of T.A. Pai, preserved in the T.A. Pai Papers. Pai was a professional banker, associated with Syndicate Bank of Karnataka, one of the fourteen institutions on the takeover list, and his letters document the technical input a working banker provided to the drafting: the deposit threshold that would define the list, the custodian arrangement by which each taken-over bank would continue business without interruption under a government-appointed custodian, and the compensation structure that a statute would have to carry. Pai entered Indira Gandhi's council of ministers in 1972, a trajectory that itself records how the drafting table of 1969 became the ministry of the following years.

The secrecy of the drafting had a functional cause that the sources make plain. A takeover of banks announced in advance invites the flight of deposits, the transfer of assets and the mobilisation of every affected interest in Parliament and in the press. The July operation was therefore constructed so that the first public knowledge of the decision would be the decision itself, effective at the midnight following the ordinance. That construction had a political dividend beyond its banking rationale. Because the measure existed before it was announced, the announcement could be timed. A government improvising a response to the Bangalore decision of 12 July could not have produced a legally operable takeover of fourteen banks, with named corresponding new banks, custodians and a compensation schedule, by 19 July. A government that had drafted the measure since May could choose its Saturday. The stray thoughts note of the Bangalore session belongs to this construction. Presented as tentative reflections on economic policy, the note placed nationalisation in the mouth of the conference as a possibility under discussion, and the discussion performed two services for its author. It measured the syndicate's reaction, which was dismissive, and it established a public record that the Prime Minister had raised the question inside the party before she acted, a record of procedural propriety wrapped around a prepared act of party war. Malhotra's 1989 biography, drawing on interviews with the surviving participants, treats the Bangalore note as deliberate misdirection, and the documentary sequence in the Haksar Papers, drafting first and consultation afterwards, sustains that treatment against the charitable reading of the note as genuine hesitation.

One further feature of the preparation deserves emphasis, because it distinguishes the 1969 operation from a merely opportunistic strike. The drafting group worked on the assumption that the measure would be challenged in court and that its political effect had to survive the challenge's beginning, if not its end. The ordinance therefore vested each undertaking in a corresponding new bank with immediate effect, transferred staff and business intact, and left the old companies with compensation as their remaining asset, so that a judicial stay would confront a functioning public banking system rather than a suspended intention. When the challenge came, in petitions filed in the Supreme Court on 21 July 1969, the banks were already operating under custodians and the deposits of millions of account holders already stood behind the state's guarantee. The Court could strike the statute, and in February 1970 the Court did. The Court could not unscramble the political fact the ordinance had created in July 1969, a fact whose work was finished by the presidential count of 20 August 1969. The drafters had built the measure to win the interval that mattered. That is the behaviour of a political staff executing a timed campaign, and it is visible in the instrument itself to any reader of the ordinance text.

## Sixteen July: Removing the Finance Minister

The takeover required one personnel precondition, and Indira Gandhi executed it on 16 July 1969. Morarji Desai, deputy prime minister and finance minister since March 1967, was the author of social control, the cabinet's senior advocate of fiscal orthodoxy, and the syndicate's weightiest member inside the government. Banking policy ran through his ministry. So long as Desai held finance, a nationalisation measure would either die in his ministry or arrive in the Prime Minister's office bearing his amendments, and either outcome would advertise her subordination. On 16 July she took the finance portfolio into her own hands. Desai, offered the position of a deputy prime minister without the portfolio that gave the office substance, resigned from the cabinet. The standard accounts record the exchange as a humiliation engineered to force the resignation it produced, and Desai's own later writings treated the episode as a calculated affront. The political logic is legible without speculation about motive. The resignation cleared the ministry through which the ordinance would move, removed the one colleague whose dissent could give the syndicate a voice inside the room where the decision was announced, and presented the country with a three-day sequence that every newspaper reader could follow: the conservative finance minister dismissed on Wednesday, the banks taken over on Saturday. Opponents of the takeover were thereby positioned, before they had spoken a word, as defenders of the dismissed minister's order, and the syndicate's coming campaign for Reddy inherited the burden of appearing to avenge him.

The broadcast of 19 July 1969 completed the construction. Indira Gandhi addressed the nation on All India Radio on the evening of the ordinance and framed the act in the vocabulary of the ten-point programme: control of the commanding heights of credit, the end of the direction of bank finance by a handful of industrial houses, the obligation of the banking system to reach the farmer, the small entrepreneur and the exporter. The framing was addressed over the heads of the party organisation to the electorate at large, and its audience included, by design, the Congress legislators and the state legislators who formed the electoral college for the presidency. A legislator calculating how to vote on 16 August had been given, on 19 July, a demonstration of which side of the Congress quarrel commanded the country's applause. The popularity of the takeover was immediate and measurable in the response of the opposition parties of the left, which praised the act, and in the discomfort of the Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh, whose defence of property rights placed them beside the dispossessed bank boards. When the syndicate's managers canvassed for Reddy in the following weeks, they canvassed in a country where the Prime Minister had just performed the most popular economic act of the decade. The act's political function did not depend on any voter's view of credit allocation. It depended on the demonstration, which the broadcast sealed, that Indira Gandhi could act on the largest scale in Indian economic life without asking the organisation's permission, three days before the organisation's candidate needed the organisation's legislators to obey instructions.

### How could a single ordinance take over fourteen banks overnight?

The ordinance of 19 July 1969 vested the entire undertaking of each named bank in a corresponding new bank created by the same instrument, transferred every asset, contract and employee intact, replaced each board with a government-appointed custodian, and left the old company a shell whose only remaining asset was its claim to compensation, so banking business continued without interruption.

The device deserves a moment's attention, because its elegance served the timetable. The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, Ordinance 8 of 1969, did not wind up the fourteen banks or freeze their business. Each existing bank was paired with a corresponding new bank of the same name, constituted as a body corporate owned by the central government, and the undertaking of the old company, its branches, deposits, advances, staff and obligations, stood transferred to the new bank at the commencement of the ordinance. The chairman and directors of each old board vacated office by operation of the instrument, and the chief executive of each bank assumed the office of custodian of its successor, which meant that the man who had managed the bank on Friday managed the state's bank on Monday morning, under new instructions. Depositors faced no closed doors and no altered account. The old companies survived as legal persons for one purpose, to receive the compensation the schedule assigned them, and were forbidden by the Banking Regulation Act framework from carrying on banking business under their names. By the time the writ petitions reached the Supreme Court on 21 July 1969, the petitioners were asking the Court to reverse a completed transfer of the country's credit system, with the deposits of the public inside the successor banks. Courts asked to unscramble such a transfer move slowly, and this Court, for all its eventual severity toward the statute, never ordered the banks returned during the seven months the case was heard. The ordinance form, so often described as the operation's legal weakness, was in this respect its armour.

## The Presidential Test: August 1969

The measure's function was discharged in the presidential election, and the election's mechanics show how completely the July act had rearranged the contest. Giri resigned the acting presidency on 20 July 1969, the day after signing the ordinance, and Chief Justice Mohammad Hidayatullah discharged the President's functions until the new President took office. Giri contested as an independent, supported by the two communist parties, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, the Samyukta Socialist Party and other opposition formations that a month earlier would not have combined behind a candidate acceptable to the Prime Minister. Reddy contested as the official Congress candidate with the organisation's full machinery and the support of the Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh on the other flank. Between the two stood the Congress legislators of Parliament and the state assemblies, instructed by the working committee to vote for Reddy and possessed, most of them, of careers that depended on the organisation's favour. Indira Gandhi's intervention dissolved that dependence in public. She declared that the vote should be a free vote of conscience, a formula that released every Congress elector from the whip while aligning the released vote with the candidate she favoured, and the July demonstration supplied the argument each wavering legislator could carry home: the Prime Minister had the country, and a vote for the organisation's candidate was a vote against the woman who had given the banks to the people. Polling took place on 16 August 1969. The first count gave Giri votes to the value of 401,515 against Reddy's 313,485, with C.D. Deshmukh, the former finance minister standing with Swatantra and Jana Sangh support, at 112,769, and on the transfer of the second preferences of the eliminated candidates Giri was declared elected on 20 August 1969, the first presidential election in the republic's history to be decided on transferred preferences and the first in which the ruling party's official candidate was defeated. The syndicate had commanded the nomination, the organisation and the whips, and had lost the college. The instrument that had broken its command was not a rival organisation. It was a policy act, performed by ordinance between the nomination and the poll.

## The Ordinance Route and the Constitutional Pattern

The decision to legislate by ordinance placed bank nationalisation inside a longer constitutional pattern that the founding generation had established, [the executive-amendment pattern as precedent](/2019/01/14/first-constitutional-amendment-1951/). The First Amendment of 1951 had taught Indian governments that property provisions in the Constitution would be adjusted by parliamentary power when those provisions obstructed a programme the government treated as central, and Articles 31A and 31B, with the Ninth Schedule, were the instruments of that adjustment in the land reform context. The pattern's premise was that the economic settlement of the republic was a political question on which the elected power would prevail over the property clause, by amendment where necessary. Indira Gandhi's July 1969 operation worked the same premise with a different instrument. Where Nehru's government had used amendment after judicial obstruction, hers used the ordinance before parliamentary obstruction could assemble, and the difference between the instruments is the difference between the two prime ministers' situations in their parties. Nehru commanded the Congress. Indira Gandhi, in July 1969, commanded the government and the country, and her opponents commanded the Congress. The ordinance route is unintelligible as a first preference of a secure government and perfectly intelligible as the weapon of a Prime Minister whose majority in the Lok Sabha was dependable for supply but whose party organisation was in hostile hands and whose finance minister had just walked out.

The deeper constitutional frame is [the rights-development founding tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), the founding settlement's unresolved contest between the property rights the Constitution guaranteed and the developmental state the Constitution directed. The Constituent Assembly had written both commitments into the text: Article 19(1)(f) guaranteed the citizen's right to acquire, hold and dispose of property, Article 31 required compensation for acquisition, and Article 39(b) and (c) directed the state to distribute the ownership of material resources so as to subserve the common good and to prevent the concentration of wealth. For twenty years the courts and the governments had disputed which commitment governed when they collided, through the land reform cases, the First, Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments, and the Golak Nath judgment of 1967, in which the Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend the fundamental rights at all. Bank nationalisation drove the largest single transfer of private property since zamindari abolition directly into that unsettled field. The syndicate's defenders of the measure's illegality and the government's defenders of its necessity were arguing, in the vocabulary of 1969, the founding argument itself. The political-strategic reading does not dissolve that constitutional substance into tactics. The claim is narrower and stronger: the constitutional confrontation was foreseeable, was foreseen by the drafting group, and was accepted as the price of an act whose decisive work would be complete before the confrontation could be adjudicated. The Court's eventual judgment therefore belongs to the political story as an anticipated cost, not as an accident that befell an economic reform.

## Reading the Ordinance: The Instrument as Evidence

The ordinance text, a mandatory primary source for this article, repays reading as a political document, because its clauses encode the operation's priorities in the order of their drafting. The instrument's first work is definitional and total: the undertaking of an existing bank is defined to embrace the whole of its business, property, assets, rights and liabilities, so that nothing of value could linger in the old company around which a resistance might organise. The second work is substitution: for every existing bank the ordinance constitutes a corresponding new bank, a body corporate with the same name and the central government as its owner, and vests the undertaking in the successor at the ordinance's commencement, which is to say at the moment of promulgation, before any affected person had read the Gazette. The third work is personnel: the chairman and every director of an existing bank vacate office by the instrument's own force, the chief executive steps into the office of custodian of the successor bank, and the services of the staff stand transferred, so that no board meeting, no shareholder resolution and no act of cooperation by any officer of the old order is required anywhere in the transfer. The fourth work is erasure: the existing bank, stripped of its undertaking, its officers and its staff, is left incompetent to carry on banking business and disabled from using the word bank in its name, surviving only as a claimant for compensation and liable to dissolution by government notification. A reader who works through the clauses in order watches a complete commercial order abolished and replaced inside a single document of a few pages, without a gap of an hour in which the old order could act.

The drafting choices that later failed in court are equally legible in the text, and they mark the boundary between the operation's two audiences. For the audience of July 1969, the depositors, the legislators, the country, the instrument needed speed, totality and continuity, and it achieved all three. For the audience of February 1970, the eleven judges, the instrument needed a compensation scheme whose principles engaged the value of what was taken and a classification whose rationale could survive Article 14, and it achieved neither. The compensation schedule, computed in the secretariat from the returns at hand, named an amount for each company and stopped there, offering no reasoning a court could test and omitting the goodwill and leasehold values a court would insist on counting. The fifty-crore line, drawn for administrative clarity, left the foreign banks and the smaller Indian banks trading freely in the very business the fourteen were forbidden, and the instrument offered no account of why the public control of credit required that asymmetry. Both defects were the residue of the timetable, as this article has argued, but they were also something more specific: they were the points at which the drafting group's instruction, to produce an instrument that would work on the Saturday, diverged from the instruction a government litigating at leisure would have given, to produce an instrument that would work in the courtroom. The text of Ordinance 8 of 1969 is thus the single best exhibit for the article's thesis, because the thesis is written into its silences. The ordinance does not read as the first chapter of a banking code. It reads as the record of a decision that had to be irreversible before it could be examined.

## The Money Itself: Deposits, Compensation and the Quiet Weekend

Between the drama of the ordinance and the drama of the courtroom stood the banking operation itself, and its smoothness was a political fact of the first order. The fourteen banks held deposits recorded at 2,632 crore rupees, the savings of millions of account holders, and a takeover mishandled could have produced the run on the banks that the drafting group's secrecy had been designed to prevent. No run occurred. The Reserve Bank under Governor L.K. Jha stood behind the successor banks, the custodians kept every branch open on Monday 21 July 1969, and the depositors' discovery that the state's name stood behind their accounts calmed rather than alarmed the market towns. The government's parliamentary management matched the banking management. The Bill that replaced the ordinance passed through a monsoon session in which the opposition's fire concentrated on compensation and constitutionality rather than on the principle, because the principle, as the debate's tone recorded, had been conceded by public opinion before Parliament assembled. Minoo Masani's Swatantra Party defended property with ability and without resonance. The communist members supported the takeover and demanded its extension. The measure the syndicate's parliamentarians might have strangled in committee in June was, by August, a statute whose opponents were arguing about the price.

The price was the operation's legal underside, and it is where the drafting group's priorities show most plainly. The Act of 1969 named, in its schedule, the compensation payable to each taken-over company, a total in the region of eighty-seven crore rupees for the fourteen undertakings, payable in government securities, with the amounts derived from a valuation that excluded the goodwill the banks had accumulated and the value of their unexpired leases. A drafting group instructed to maximise the statute's survival would have bought safety with a generous and conventionally computed compensation, since the property jurisprudence of the 1950s and 1960s, from the West Bengal settlement case of 1954 through the metal corporation case of 1967, had shown the Court annulling acquisitions whose compensation principles were irrelevant to value. A drafting group instructed to produce an instrument by a Saturday chose the schedule that could be computed from the returns already in the government's possession, and accepted the exposure. The exposure was not hidden from the cabinet. It was the arithmetic residue of the timetable, and the cabinet paid it knowingly, because the compensation clause would be litigated in 1970 while the presidency was decided in 1969. When the tribunal litigation over the successor statute's compensation ground forward in the following years, it did so inside a banking system whose ownership was no longer in practical dispute. The money question, which dominated the courtroom phase of the event, was thus the phase the operation could most afford to lose, and in the event it was the only phase lost.

The conduct of the successor banks in their first weeks supplied the government's publicists with their closing argument of the summer. Contemporary press accounts of the week after the ordinance recorded account holders reassured rather than alarmed, fresh deposits entering the taken-over banks, and none of the closures or queues of a panic at any of the 4,130 branches. The custodians, drawn from the banks' own chief executives, issued the standard assurances and then something more valuable to their new owner: the visible continuity of the counter, the same clerks, the same ledgers, the same marble halls, under a board that answered to the Republic. By the time the presidential electors voted, the takeover had acquired the most persuasive of political properties, normality. The extraordinary had been absorbed into the week's routine, and a vote against the Prime Minister's candidate had become, by the alchemy of the interval, a vote to disturb a settlement the country had already accepted as fact.

## The Week in Parliament: The Ordinance Defended and the Act Passed

The monsoon session that opened on Monday 21 July 1969, two days after the ordinance, staged the constitutional argument about the method before it staged the argument about the merits, and the order of the arguments tells its own story. Opposition members protested at the session's opening against the promulgation of a far-reaching ordinance on the eve of a summoned Parliament, a device they characterised as a contempt of the House: the executive had legislated on the largest economic question of the decade at the one moment when the legislature could not answer, and presented the returning members with a completed transfer as the premise of their debate. The criticism was constitutionally serious and politically helpless, and both properties were intended by the device. A government that had waited for the session would have faced the amendment, the delaying division and the syndicate's whips in the lobbies. A government that had acted on the Saturday faced a debate about a fact, and the debate's centre of gravity shifted accordingly, from whether the banks should be taken to what the taking should cost and under what safeguards it should proceed. The Lok Sabha debates of the session record the migration plainly: the Swatantra members argued compensation and the property clause, the Jana Sangh argued centralisation, the communist members demanded the takeover's extension to the foreign banks and to general insurance, and the treasury benches, with the Prime Minister holding the finance portfolio in person, defended the ordinance as the servant of the ten-point programme the party itself had adopted. Morarji Desai, three weeks out of the cabinet, defended the principle of social control against the confiscation he had refused to author, in the isolation of a man whose policy had been repealed by his own government.

The Bill's passage converted the fait accompli into law with the presidential poll as its deadline. The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Bill was taken up, debated and passed in the session's opening weeks, and the Act received assent on 9 August 1969, seven days before the electors voted, with its commencement deemed to be 19 July. The deemed commencement was the clause that locked the sequence shut: whatever the presidential election's outcome, and whatever the courts might later say about compensation, the ownership of the fourteen banks would in law have changed on the Saturday, before any other actor in the drama had moved. Parliament's role in the making of Act 22 of 1969 was therefore real but posterior, the ratification of a decision whose political work was already under way, and the members who complained of the sequence understood their own posteriority. The complaint has echoed in the Indian debate about ordinances ever since, whenever a government reaches for Article 123 on the eve of a session, and the July 1969 precedent is the standing exhibit in that argument, cited by the critics of ordinance government as its founding abuse and by its defenders as the proof that the instrument exists for moments when delay is itself a decision. Both citations concede the point this article makes about the instrument's function in 1969. The ordinance was chosen because time was the substance of the decision, and time, in July 1969, belonged to whoever moved first.

## R.C. Cooper and the Court's Answer of February 1970

Parliament converted the ordinance into statute with the speed the presidential calendar required. The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Bill was introduced in the monsoon session, and the Act, Act 22 of 1969, received the President's assent on 9 August 1969, one week before the presidential poll, and was deemed to have come into force on 19 July 1969, the date of the ordinance it replaced. The challenge to the Act came from Rustom Cavasjee Cooper, a shareholder and director of the Central Bank of India and a shareholder in other taken-over banks, whose petition under Article 32 reached the Supreme Court within days of the ordinance and was then directed against the Act. An eleven-judge bench heard the case, and on 10 February 1970 the Court delivered its judgment in R.C. Cooper v Union of India, reported at (1970) 1 SCC 248. By a majority of ten to one, with Justice J.C. Shah writing the leading judgment and Justice A.N. Ray dissenting, the Court struck down Act 22 of 1969 as unconstitutional. The judgment is conventionally filed as a property-rights landmark. Read against the political sequence, it is also the judiciary's first full answer to the governing style that July 1969 inaugurated, an executive that treated constitutional form as an obstacle course to be run at speed.

The majority's reasoning struck at the Act's design rather than at the idea of nationalisation, and the distinction structured everything that followed. The Court held, first, that a shareholder could maintain a petition where the law's impact on the company was in substance an impact on the shareholder's own fundamental rights, and that Cooper's rights under Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31(2) were directly impaired by the extinguishment of his company's undertaking. The Court held, second, that the Act practised hostile discrimination forbidden by Article 14: the fourteen named banks were forbidden to carry on banking business, while other Indian banks below the threshold and foreign banks operating in India were left free to carry on the identical business, and the classification bore no reasonable relation to the Act's declared purpose of controlling credit. The Court held, third and most consequentially, that the compensation the Act offered failed Article 31(2), because the principles the statute prescribed for valuing the undertakings excluded components that were part of the property taken, including the value of goodwill and of unexpired leases, and fixed a sum by a method that made the compensation illusory in relation to the assets transferred. A law that took the property and named a price arrived at by irrelevant principles did not satisfy the Constitution, whatever label Parliament attached to the price. Justice Ray's dissent, which would have dismissed the petition at the threshold and treated the compensation question as a legislative province, stood alone. The ten-judge majority had, in effect, told the government that the commanding heights could be taken, but not in this manner and not at this price.

The government's defence had rested on the doctrinal settlement it believed the Fourth Amendment of 1955 had achieved, that the adequacy of compensation for acquired property was not justiciable and that a law fixing an amount concluded the matter. The majority reopened that settlement by the route the Court's own intervening cases had kept open. The judgment reasoned that the right to property under Article 31(2) was not satisfied by the recital of an amount where the principles by which the amount was reached took no account of the components of the property taken, and that a shareholder whose company was extinguished as a banking entity suffered a direct invasion of his own rights under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31, not a remote reflection of the company's loss. On classification, the majority pressed the point the drafting timetable had left exposed: if the Act's purpose was the public control of credit, a statute that prohibited fourteen named banks from banking while licensing every other bank in the country, Indian and foreign, to continue the identical business had selected its victims by a criterion unrelated to the evil, and Article 14 condemned the selection. The reasoning mattered beyond the banking system. R.C. Cooper restored the judiciary's authority to examine the substance of a taking at the moment when the executive had begun to treat that authority as spent, and it did so in a judgment whose author, Justice Shah, was within a year to be passed over in the politics of judicial appointment that the confrontation set running. The case thus stands at the head of the line that runs through the privy purse judgment of 1971, the Kesavananda Bharati decision of 1973 and the supersession of judges that followed it, the line along which the executive and the Court contested the republic's balance until the Emergency settled the contest by force. The drafters of July 1969 had anticipated a collision. The scale of the collision course, on which their anticipation proved to be an underestimate, is the subject the pillar's later articles inherit.

### What exactly did the Supreme Court strike down in February 1970?

On 10 February 1970 an eleven-judge bench struck down the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act of 1969 by ten votes to one, holding that the Act violated Article 14 by discriminating against the fourteen named banks, and Articles 19(1)(f) and 31(2) because its compensation principles excluded goodwill and lease values and therefore named an illusory price.

The judgment's political effect ran opposite to its legal effect, and the divergence is the analytical heart of this article. Legally, the government lost the statute. Politically, the government gained an adversary more useful than the syndicate: a Court that could be represented as standing between the people's programme and the people's welfare, defending the compensation claims of bank shareholders against a Prime Minister who had given the banks to the nation. Indira Gandhi's response to the judgment was not retreat but escalation by re-enactment, described in the following section, and the escalation found its audience prepared. The syndicate, which might have exploited the judgment as proof of the Prime Minister's recklessness, was by February 1970 a separated party, Congress (Organisation), fighting for survival after the November 1969 split, and its defence of the Court read to the public as a defence of the banks. The Court had constrained the form of the political move, exactly as the brief of this article's argument predicts the drafters expected, and the constraint arrived seven months after the move's function was complete. Giri was President. The Congress had split on the ground the July campaign had chosen. The statute was expendable in a way the sequence was not.

## Re-enactment: The Constraint of Form and the Survival of Substance

The government's answer to R.C. Cooper was delivered within four days. On 14 February 1970 a fresh ordinance re-acquired the undertakings of the fourteen banks, and Parliament then enacted the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, Act 5 of 1970, which received assent in March 1970. The new Act repaired the defects the majority had named: the compensation method was recast, the discriminatory bar on the old companies' business was adjusted, and the transfer was re-effected on the repaired basis, effective again from 19 July 1969, so that no interval of private ownership reopened. The sequence ordinance, Act, judgment, ordinance, Act, all inside eight months, is the executive-judicial collision in miniature. The Court had demonstrated that it could annul the product of the Prime Minister's fastest instrument. The Prime Minister had demonstrated that annulment cost her government four days. Between those two demonstrations lay the question that would govern the following decade of Indian constitutional politics: whether any institution could impose a durable constraint on a Prime Minister who commanded Parliament, the ordinance power and the country's applause, and who treated each judicial defeat as a mandate to amend the terms of the contest.

The amendment route opened promptly. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment of 1971 restored Parliament's power to amend the fundamental rights, answering Golak Nath. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment of 1971 rewrote Article 31(2) to replace compensation with an amount fixed by law, and inserted Article 31C, which gave laws enacted to implement the directive principles in Article 39(b) and (c) precedence over Articles 14, 19 and 31. Article 31C was drafted in the shadow of the bank case: the distribution of ownership of material resources, the stated ground of nationalisation, was the very subject the new clause placed beyond the property challenge that had destroyed Act 22 of 1969. When the Kesavananda Bharati bench of thirteen judges upheld the Twenty-Fourth Amendment and the first part of Article 31C in April 1973, while reserving the basic structure doctrine, the constitutional law of property had been re-founded on terms the July 1969 operation had demanded. The privy purse abolition and the confrontation over the presidential election belong to the same arc, and the arc's constitutional terminus, the Emergency of 1975, was visible in embryo in the pattern: an executive that answered institutional resistance by enlarging the instrument, ordinance to Act to amendment, each answer lawful in form and cumulative in effect. R.C. Cooper is therefore misread when it is filed as a defeat for nationalisation. The nationalised banks were never returned, the compensation litigation wound through tribunals on the repaired statute's terms, and the judgment's durable legacy was to teach the executive precisely which constitutional provisions required amendment. The Court constrained the form. The substance, the state ownership of the commanding heights of credit, survived intact and survives in the structure of Indian banking.

## The Economic Architecture: What the Takeover Built

The political reading defended in this article does not require the economic consequences of nationalisation to be small, and they were not small. The takeover built an architecture of state-directed credit that operated for decades and that Atul Kohli analysed in State-Directed Development, published in 2004, as the financial arm of the Indian state's developmental project. The architecture's elements were assembled in sequence. The Lead Bank Scheme of December 1969, which followed the study group chaired by F.K.F. Nariman and the Gadgil group's recommendations, assigned each district to a designated bank charged with surveying credit needs and leading the expansion of banking in its territory. Branch licensing policy under the Reserve Bank pressed the nationalised banks into the unbanked countryside, and the numbers record the result: bank offices rose from 8,262 in June 1969 to 60,220 by March 1991, and rural offices rose from 1,833 to 35,206 across the same span, taking the average population served by an office from roughly 65,000 people down toward 12,000. The priority sector framework, articulated by the Reserve Bank in the early 1970s and hardened into targets, directed the enlarged system: banks were first advised in 1974 to raise priority sector advances to 33.3 per cent of net bank credit by March 1979, and the target was raised to 40 per cent in 1980. Reserve Bank series place the priority sectors' share of net bank credit at about 14 per cent in 1969 and at 37.7 per cent by the early 1990s, with agriculture's share rising from the neighbourhood of two per cent toward the mandated eighteen per cent sub-target. The Regional Rural Banks, created by ordinance in September 1975 and placed on a statutory footing in 1976, extended the design into locally staffed institutions for the village borrower. Devesh Kapur's Diaspora, Development, and Democracy of 2010 examines this system as a political economy in which the state's control of credit became a standing instrument of distribution, patronage and electoral mobilisation alike.

### How did nationalisation redraw the map of Indian banking?

Bank offices rose from 8,262 in June 1969 to 60,220 by March 1991, rural offices multiplied from 1,833 to 35,206, and the Lead Bank Scheme assigned every district a bank responsible for its credit survey and expansion, converting a banking network concentrated in port cities and industrial towns into a branch system that reached the district town and the village.

The architecture's existence is the strongest evidence for the economic-policy reading, and the adjudication in the following section takes that evidence at full strength. Two features of the architecture, however, bear the fingerprints of its political origin. The first is the speed of the branch expansion relative to its commercial logic: the nationalised banks opened offices in locations chosen by district surveys and licensing policy rather than by deposit potential, because the system's purpose included demonstrating the state's arrival in the countryside, a demonstration with electoral value in every district where a branch opened under a board that carried the government's name. The second is the location of control. Credit allocation under the architecture ran through institutions owned by the central government, supervised by a Reserve Bank whose governor served at the government's pleasure, and answerable to a finance ministry held, at the decisive moments, by the Prime Minister herself or by her appointees. The architecture centralised the discretion it democratised. A farmer in a district town gained access to a loan officer; the terms on which the system lent, the sectors it favoured and the forbearance it practised were set in Delhi. Kohli's account of state-directed development and Kapur's account of credit as political instrument converge on this duality from different directions. The system widened the franchise of credit and narrowed the franchise of decision about credit, and both movements served the consolidation of the Prime Minister's office as the commanding institution of the Indian state, a trajectory the pillar hub traces as [Indira's four-phase office-rebuilding trajectory](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/).

The deposit side of the architecture transformed as thoroughly as the credit side, and the transformation had its own political meaning. A banking system that had collected the savings of the cities was instructed, through branch licensing and the lead bank machinery, to collect the savings of the countryside, and the small depositor in the district town became a stakeholder in the nationalised order in the most literal sense: the state's banks held his money, paid his interest and stood in his high street under the government's name. Deposit growth through the 1970s, outpacing the growth of the economy itself as monetisation deepened, recorded the success of that mobilisation in the Reserve Bank's returns. The political reading notes what the returns cannot say, that every new account opened in a village branch was also a small treaty between a household and the state that had taken the banks, renewed at every deposit and every withdrawal, in a currency of trust that no party organisation could match with its membership registers.

The architecture also answered a need of the moment that the economic histories understate, the financing of the agricultural transformation then under way. The high-yielding varieties programme had carried Indian agriculture into a new dependence on purchased inputs, fertiliser, seed, diesel and water, and purchased inputs require seasonal credit on a scale the moneylender and the cooperative could not jointly supply, a pressure that [the food crisis politics of the late 1960s](/2019/03/10/1960s-food-crisis-indian-politics/) had already converted into a governing emergency. Nationalised branch expansion and the priority sector framework delivered institutional credit into that gap, and the delivery bound the beneficiary, the borrowing farmer of the Green Revolution districts, to the state that had nationalised his creditor. The binding was not cynical and it was not incidental. A development programme and a constituency were constructed by the same instrument, which is what the nesting of functions means in practice: the economic architecture worked, and its working reproduced the political supremacy that had created it.

One constituency of the architecture is habitually left out of the economic summaries, the bank employees themselves, and their place in the settlement reinforces the nesting argument. The All India Bank Employees' Association had campaigned for the nationalisation of the banks since the 1950s, on grounds of service conditions, security and the public purpose of banking, and the unions greeted July 1969 as a victory won by their own long advocacy. The nationalised system then employed, promoted and pensioned a workforce that ran into the hundreds of thousands, organised in unions whose federations became a durable element of the left-of-centre coalition the Prime Minister's politics assembled. The architecture thus reproduced its founding coalition inside its own payroll, tellers and officers whose livelihoods were the state's gift and whose industrial organisations defended the settlement against every later proposal of reversal or dilution. A purely economic instrument does not usually acquire a garrison. The nationalised banks acquired one at every district headquarters in the country, and the garrison's existence is part of the explanation for the settlement's survival through the decades in which its economic rationale was most sharply questioned.

## The Adjudication: Function Before Consequence

The named disagreement this article must settle is between two readings of the same dated facts. The economic-policy reading, represented by the banking-history tradition of R.K. Hazari's successors and by the Reserve Bank's own institutional histories, treats nationalisation as a credit reform: the state took the banks because private ownership had failed to allocate credit according to developmental need, and the proof of the reading is the architecture the takeover built. The political-strategic reading, represented in different registers by Inder Malhotra's 1989 biography and by the analysis defended here, treats nationalisation as the second move of the anti-syndicate campaign: the state took the banks in July 1969 because the Prime Minister required a demonstration of autonomous power between the Bangalore nomination and the presidential poll, and the proof of the reading is the timing, the secrecy, the personnel precondition and the instrument. The readings are not symmetrical competitors for the same explanatory space. They answer different questions, and the article's adjudication turns on keeping the questions separate. The economic reading answers the question of consequence: what did the takeover do to Indian banking? The political reading answers the question of function: what was the takeover for, at the moment it was done, in the hands of the person who did it? Confusion between the two questions is what allows the consequence to be offered as a refutation of the function. It is not. A weapon can build a house after the battle and remain a weapon in the order of explanation.

Tested on the evidence, the political function is primary by every criterion that distinguishes function from consequence. The criterion of timing: the act occurred at the single point in the decade when its author faced institutional extinction, inside a nine-day window fixed by her opponents' decisions, and at no earlier point in the fourteen years since Avadi when the economic case was equally available and equally documented. The criterion of preparation: the measure was drafted in secret in the victor's secretariat from May 1969, with a banker's technical assistance recorded in the T.A. Pai Papers, before the public debate that supposedly generated it. The criterion of personnel: the act required the prior removal of the finance minister who owned the alternative policy, and the removal was executed three days before the act, not three months after a policy review. The criterion of instrument: the ordinance, chosen over a bill for reasons only the presidential calendar explains, was signed by an acting President hours from resigning to become the author's candidate. Against these four criteria, the economic reading offers the architecture, which is genuine, and the argument from declared intention, which the broadcast supplies and which no serious adjudication can treat as independent evidence, since the declared intention of a timed political act is part of the act. Kohli, whose 2004 study is no brief for the political reading, nonetheless situates the post-1969 banking system inside a governing project in which the concentration of economic discretion in the Prime Minister's office was itself the development strategy's operating condition. Kapur's 2010 analysis of credit as an instrument of political distribution completes the nesting: the consequences were real because the function was served. A takeover executed for consolidation built institutions that consolidated, and the institutions then allocated credit in patterns the economic historians rightly study. Function first, consequence second, the consequence enabled by the function: that ordering is the article's defended claim. Readers testing the two readings against the primary texts can lay the ordinance and the judgment side by side in an online [PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and check every dated step of the sequence against the instruments themselves.

Two objections to the political reading deserve direct answer, because they are the strongest the record offers. The first is the objection from post hoc rationalisation: that every successful political act acquires a strategic genealogy in retrospect, and that Indira Gandhi may have nationalised the banks from conviction, with the presidential advantage a windfall she exploited rather than a purpose she pursued. The documentary sequence answers the objection. Conviction does not explain secrecy maintained against the cabinet's own finance minister. Conviction does not explain a drafting process that began in the month the presidency fell vacant and matured in the week of the nomination. Conviction, above all, does not explain the stray thoughts performance at Bangalore, in which the author of a completed draft presented its contents as an open question to the body she was preparing to defy. The second objection is the counterfactual: that social control was failing and any government would have reached nationalisation by 1970 or 1971, so the timing proves nothing. The counterfactual fails on the personnel evidence. The government that would have had to reach nationalisation in the ordinary course was a government whose finance minister was Morarji Desai, the author of the alternative, whose party president was Nijalingappa and whose working committee had just demonstrated its intentions. In the ordinary course there was no ordinary course: there was a succession struggle, and the takeover was one of its battles. Strip the struggle away and the standing case for nationalisation returns to the condition it had occupied since 1955, conceded by all, executed by none.

### Who gained and who lost inside the Congress in July 1969?

Indira Gandhi gained an independent national constituency and the finance portfolio; the Congress left gained the policy ascendancy it had sought since Avadi; Morarji Desai lost office and the authorship of banking policy; and the syndicate lost the initiative, entering the presidential contest as defenders of dismissed ministers and dispossessed bank boards before an electorate applauding their opponent.

## The Country's Answer: Press, Markets and the Organised Interests

The response of the country in the week after 19 July 1969 is part of the evidence, because the act's function depended on the response being what it was. The organised interests divided exactly as the drafters would have wished. The Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry protested against the confiscation of the banks and warned of its effect on confidence and investment, and the Bombay share market marked down bank and industrial scrips in the sessions that followed the ordinance. The Swatantra Party, the party of property founded by C. Rajagopalachari in 1959, condemned the takeover as an assault on the constitutional guarantee and on the savings of the middle class, and its leaders carried that argument into the monsoon session debates. The Jana Sangh attacked the measure as a communist-inspired centralisation. Against that line-up stood the communist parties, the socialists, the Congress left and, on the evidence of the press of the week, a broad public that received the takeover as a blow struck at privilege on its behalf. The English-language press divided on its familiar lines, the Indian Express hostile to the method, other papers applauding the deed while deploring the ordinance, and the division itself served the author: a measure that the newspapers debated as a revolution was a measure whose author no organisation could plausibly reduce to a spokeswoman. The political scientists' vocabulary for what occurred is plebiscitary: a leader outflanked by an intermediate organisation appeals across it to the mass electorate, and converts a policy into a referendum on the leadership itself. July 1969 is among the founding Indian instances of the form. The syndicate understood the form too late, and its counter-moves, the expulsion of November and the grand alliance of 1971, were organisational answers to a plebiscitary fact, which is a principal reason they failed.

## The Historians' Quarrel: Personality, Institution and the Uses of 1969

The scholarly disagreement the article adjudicates has a canonical form in the literature of the Indira years. Bipan Chandra and his collaborators, in the standard nationalist history India Since Independence, read the July 1969 decisions within a narrative of personality and courage: a young Prime Minister, cornered by the conservative bosses of her party, seized the initiative with radical measures and broke the organisation's grip, and the bank takeover stands in that telling as the emblematic act of a leader discovering power. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution of 1999, reads the same years institutionally: the Constitution's seamless web of commitments, among them the directive principles' mandate against the concentration of wealth, supplied the standard against which the actions of governments and courts alike are to be measured, and the property amendments and judgments of the period are moves in a constitutional argument that no single personality authored. Francine Frankel's India's Political Economy of 1978, the earliest of the three and in some respects the most penetrating, refused both consolations. Frankel treated the radicalism of 1969 as the strategy of a leader who, lacking an organisation, manufactured a constituency by dramatic redistributive gestures whose institutional follow-through her own governing style then starved, a reading in which the political function of nationalisation is not a hidden motive but the explicit mechanism, and its economic half-life the predictable consequence. The adjudication defended here stands closest to Frankel, with one amendment drawn from the two decades of evidence she could not see. The follow-through was not starved. The branch network, the priority sector regime and the rural banks were built and endured, because the consolidation the act performed required durable institutions to hold the constituency it created. Where Chandra personalises and Austin constitutionalises, the record of May to August 1969 supports the colder reading: a succession struggle decided by a policy weapon, whose policy consequences were then pursued with genuine tenacity because the weapon's owner intended to keep the ground it had won. Personality supplied the nerve. The institution of the Prime Minister's secretariat supplied the draft. The Constitution supplied the battlefield. The function was power, and the consequence was a banking system.

## The Split Consummated: November 1969

The presidential result made the formal division of the Congress a matter of weeks. Nijalingappa's working committee, having failed to discipline the Prime Minister before the poll, moved against her after it: on 12 November 1969 the Congress president expelled Indira Gandhi from the party for indiscipline, and the organisation's claim to be the Congress passed to the group that took the name Congress (Organisation), while her supporters constituted themselves as the Congress (Requisitionist) and carried the majority of the parliamentary party with them. The sequence's logic should be stated plainly, because it completes the demonstration the July act began. In June 1969 the syndicate had held the organisation, the funds, the tickets and the presidential nomination, and the Prime Minister had held the government and an unexecuted programme. In November 1969 the organisation expelled her, and discovered that expulsion was a weapon that wounded its holder: the legislators followed the Prime Minister, the state units divided in her favour in most of the country, and the organisation retained the name, the headquarters' older loyalties and the allegiance of the commercial constituencies the July act had dispossessed. What the syndicate could not supply its followers was a cause with a future in it. Bank nationalisation had taken that ground. The party of the July takeover could present itself, and did present itself through the midterm poll of 1971, as the party that had broken the banks for the poor; the party of the organisation presented itself as the party of the dismissed and the dispossessed. The split of November 1969 was registered in the party's registers on a date in November. It had been effected in the country on a Saturday in July, by an ordinance.

## The Second Wave and the Settlement That Endured

The architecture received its extension on 15 April 1980, when Indira Gandhi's returned government nationalised six further banks holding deposits above 200 crore rupees, the Andhra Bank, Corporation Bank, the New Bank of India, the Oriental Bank of Commerce, the Punjab and Sind Bank and Vijaya Bank, under the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act of 1980. The second wave is instructive for the adjudication precisely because its political circumstances differed from 1969. No succession hung on the April 1980 decision, no finance minister was dismissed in its preparation, and no ordinance raced a presidential calendar. The measure proceeded as the completion of an established policy by a Prime Minister restored to office, and its comparatively quiet passage confirms by contrast what was distinctive about 1969: not the taking of banks, which by 1980 was an administrative inheritance, but the taking of banks as a timed act of party war. The Janata government of Morarji Desai, in office from 1977 to 1979 with every partisan reason to reverse his humiliation, left the nationalised structure untouched, and that abstention is the settlement's certificate of durability. Governments of every subsequent complexion administered the system, argued over its targets and, after the Narasimham Committee reported in 1991, admitted new private banks beside it, without restoring the fourteen to their former owners. The one failure among the twenty, the New Bank of India, was merged into the Punjab National Bank in 1993, inside the public system.

The costs of the architecture belong in the record beside its achievements, and the critics who enumerate them, from the Reserve Bank's own later histories to Kapur's analysis, are not answered by ignoring them. Directed credit decayed in places into instructed credit: the loan melas of the early 1980s, at which a minister of state for finance distributed sanction letters as public spectacle, displayed the system's vulnerability to the electoral cycle in its crudest form. Profitability across the nationalised banks thinned through the 1980s under the weight of concessional obligations, administered interest rates and forbearance toward favoured borrowers, and the exchequer recapitalised the banks in successive tranches through the 1990s as the Narasimham reforms exposed the accumulated weakness. Politicised appointment to bank boards, the transfer of inconvenient officers and the telephone direction of individual loans became standing complaints of the system's own managers. The adjudication in this article absorbs these costs without damage to its claim, because the claim concerns the order of functions in 1969, not the virtue of everything the order produced. A consolidation that lasts will generate the pathologies of the power it concentrates, and the pathologies of Indian public banking are, on the reading defended here, among the long shadows of the July settlement rather than evidence against its political character.

The settlement that endured, then, is double, and the doubleness returns the argument to its starting point. As banking, nationalisation endured because it built institutions that served depositors and borrowers at a scale the old system had never attempted, whatever the costs in directed credit, political lending and periodic recapitalisation that the system's critics enumerate and that this article does not dispute. As politics, nationalisation endured because it settled the question of July 1969 beyond appeal: the Prime Minister who could take the banks in a weekend could not be reduced to the organisation's tenant, and the Congress that emerged from [the 1969 split's structural pivot](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/) was rebuilt around that demonstrated supremacy. The economic historians and the political historians of the event have each hold of one half of the settlement. The halves are not equal in explanatory order. The political act came first, on a Saturday in July when nothing in the deposit statistics had changed and everything in the succession had, and the economic system that fills the textbooks is what that act, having won its battle, proceeded to build.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why were banks nationalised in 1969?

The official reason, stated in Indira Gandhi's broadcast of 19 July 1969, was that the banking system served a handful of industrial houses through interlocking directorships while agriculture received barely two per cent of bank credit and whole districts had no bank office. The timing had a political cause. The Congress Working Committee had named Neelam Sanjiva Reddy as its presidential candidate on 12 July 1969 against the Prime Minister's preference, and the takeover, prepared in secret since May, demonstrated her command of policy between that nomination and the presidential poll of 16 August 1969. The economic case was genuine and documented since R.K. Hazari's work of 1966. The date was chosen by the succession struggle inside the Congress.

### Q: Which banks were nationalised in 1969?

Fourteen banks were taken over on 19 July 1969, every Indian scheduled commercial bank holding deposits above fifty crore rupees: Allahabad Bank, the Bank of Baroda, the Bank of India, the Bank of Maharashtra, Canara Bank, the Central Bank of India, Dena Bank, the Indian Bank, the Indian Overseas Bank, the Punjab National Bank, Syndicate Bank, the United Bank of India, the Union Bank of India and the United Commercial Bank, later renamed UCO Bank. Between them the fourteen held deposits recorded at 2,632 crore rupees and operated 4,130 branches. Each bank's undertaking vested in a corresponding new bank of the same name owned by the central government, and its business continued without interruption under a government-appointed custodian.

### Q: What was the R.C. Cooper case?

R.C. Cooper v Union of India, reported at (1970) 1 SCC 248, was the Supreme Court's judgment of 10 February 1970 on the challenge brought by Rustom Cavasjee Cooper, a shareholder and director of the Central Bank of India, against the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act of 1969. An eleven-judge bench held by ten votes to one, Justice J.C. Shah writing the leading judgment and Justice A.N. Ray dissenting, that the Act violated Article 14 because it barred the fourteen named banks from banking while leaving other banks free to trade, and Articles 19(1)(f) and 31(2) because its compensation principles excluded goodwill and unexpired lease values. The government re-enacted nationalisation within weeks on a repaired basis.

### Q: Did bank nationalisation help India economically?

The measurable record is substantial. Bank offices rose from 8,262 in June 1969 to 60,220 by March 1991, rural offices from 1,833 to 35,206, and the priority sectors' share of net bank credit rose from about 14 per cent in 1969 to 37.7 per cent by the early 1990s, with agriculture's share climbing from roughly two per cent toward its mandated sub-target. Deposit mobilisation deepened across the countryside, and the Lead Bank Scheme and the Regional Rural Banks carried institutional credit into districts the old system had ignored. Against this stand the costs the critics enumerate: directed lending that decayed into political lending, thin profitability, and the exchequer's recapitalisation of the banks through the 1990s. The gains in reach are not seriously disputed; their price is.

### Q: Why did Morarji Desai oppose bank nationalisation?

Morarji Desai, deputy prime minister and finance minister from March 1967, was the author of the alternative policy of social control, enacted through the Banking Laws (Amendment) Act of 1968 and operating from February 1969. Desai held that reconstituted boards, professional chairmen, restrictions on advances to directors' firms and Reserve Bank direction could discipline private banking without confiscating ownership, and he regarded outright nationalisation as fiscally burdensome, administratively reckless and unjust to shareholders. His opposition was therefore doctrinal and proprietary at once: the takeover repudiated his own reform after five months of operation. Indira Gandhi took the finance portfolio from him on 16 July 1969, three days before the ordinance, and Desai resigned from the cabinet rather than serve without it.

### Q: What was the second wave of bank nationalisation in 1980?

On 15 April 1980, three months after Indira Gandhi returned to office, the government nationalised six further banks under the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act of 1980: the Andhra Bank, Corporation Bank, the New Bank of India, the Oriental Bank of Commerce, the Punjab and Sind Bank and Vijaya Bank. The qualifying threshold was deposits above 200 crore rupees, four times the fifty-crore line of 1969. With the State Bank group already public, the two waves left the overwhelming bulk of Indian commercial banking in state ownership. The 1980 measure proceeded as the completion of an established policy, without the secrecy, the dismissed finance minister or the presidential calendar that had marked the first wave in July 1969.

### Q: How did bank nationalisation help Indira politically?

The takeover of 19 July 1969 fell between the Bangalore nomination of Neelam Sanjiva Reddy on 12 July and the presidential poll of 16 August, and it converted a party dispute into a national referendum on Indira Gandhi's leadership. It demonstrated that she could act on the largest scale in economic life without the organisation's permission, positioned the syndicate as defenders of dismissed ministers and dispossessed bank boards, and gave Congress legislators a public argument for defying the whip when she called for a conscience vote. V.V. Giri, the independent candidate she backed, defeated the official candidate on transferred preferences. In November 1969 the organisation expelled her, and the legislators followed her, because the July act had already transferred the country's allegiance.

### Q: When was bank nationalisation announced, and what legal instrument carried it out?

Bank nationalisation was announced on Saturday 19 July 1969 and carried out by the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, Ordinance 8 of 1969, promulgated that day by acting President V.V. Giri under Article 123 of the Constitution. Indira Gandhi explained the decision in a broadcast to the nation on All India Radio the same evening. Parliament replaced the ordinance with the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, Act 22 of 1969, which received assent on 9 August 1969 and was deemed effective from 19 July. The ordinance route mattered: Parliament was due to reconvene on Monday 21 July, and the acting President willing to sign was due to resign on 20 July to contest the presidential election.

### Q: What deposit threshold decided which banks were taken over on 19 July 1969?

The ordinance took over every Indian scheduled commercial bank whose deposits exceeded fifty crore rupees. Fourteen banks stood above the line, holding aggregate deposits recorded at 2,632 crore rupees and operating 4,130 branches between them. Banks below the threshold, and all foreign banks operating in India, were left in private hands, a selectivity that served the drafting timetable, since the list could be compiled from the Reserve Bank's published returns, but which the Supreme Court later condemned in R.C. Cooper as hostile discrimination under Article 14. The threshold device was repeated in the second wave of 15 April 1980, when the line was set at deposits above 200 crore rupees and six banks crossed it.

### Q: What was social control over banks, and why was it judged a failure by 1969?

Social control was the Congress government's alternative to nationalisation, promised in the ten-point programme of May 1967 and enacted under Finance Minister Morarji Desai. The National Credit Council, constituted in December 1967, was to indicate credit priorities, and the Banking Laws (Amendment) Act of 1968, effective from 1 February 1969, reconstituted bank boards, required full-time chairmen and restricted lending to firms connected with bank directors. By the middle of 1969 the verdict of the government's own machinery was against it: the Gadgil study group of the National Credit Council reported that credit still bypassed agriculture and the small borrower, agriculture held barely two per cent of bank credit, and the interlocked boards were dissolving only gradually. Indira Gandhi's circle concluded that regulation without ownership changed reports, not behaviour.

### Q: Who was T.A. Pai, and what did he contribute to the design of bank nationalisation?

T.A. Pai was a professional banker associated with Syndicate Bank, the Udupi institution his father T.M.A. Pai had helped found in 1925 and one of the fourteen banks on the takeover list. His correspondence, preserved in the T.A. Pai Papers and under-cited in the standard accounts, documents the technical input he supplied to the secret drafting of May and June 1969 inside P.N. Haksar's secretariat: the deposit threshold that would define the list of banks, the custodian arrangement by which each taken-over bank continued business without interruption under a government appointee, and the structure of the compensation a statute would have to carry. Pai entered Indira Gandhi's council of ministers in 1972, a trajectory that records how the drafting table of 1969 became the government of the following years.

### Q: What compensation were the shareholders of the fourteen banks offered?

The Act of 1969 fixed the compensation for each taken-over company in a schedule to the statute, a total in the region of eighty-seven crore rupees for the fourteen undertakings, payable in government securities. The sums were computed by a method that excluded two components of the property taken: the value of the banks' goodwill and the value of their unexpired leases. That exclusion destroyed the statute. In R.C. Cooper the Supreme Court held in February 1970 that principles of compensation which ignored relevant components of the undertaking produced an illusory price and violated Article 31(2). The repaired Act of 1970 recast the compensation basis, and the resulting claims were fought through tribunals in the following years while the banks themselves remained in public ownership.

### Q: Why did V.V. Giri's signature matter to the timing of the ordinance?

An ordinance under Article 123 requires the President's promulgation, and in July 1969 the presidency was held in an acting capacity by V.V. Giri, the Vice President, after President Zakir Husain's death on 3 May 1969. Giri was willing to sign the nationalisation ordinance, and his willingness had a deadline: he intended to resign the acting presidency on 20 July 1969 in order to contest the presidential election as an independent candidate, the candidate Indira Gandhi backed against her party's official nominee, Neelam Sanjiva Reddy. The ordinance was therefore promulgated on Saturday 19 July, Giri's last full day in office, and he laid down the acting presidency the following day. A week's delay would have placed the instrument before a different signatory and inside a sitting Parliament.

### Q: How did the government restore nationalisation after the Supreme Court struck down the 1969 Act?

Within four days of the R.C. Cooper judgment of 10 February 1970, the government promulgated a fresh ordinance on 14 February 1970 re-acquiring the undertakings of the fourteen banks, and Parliament then enacted the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, Act 5 of 1970, assented to in March 1970. The new statute repaired the defects the majority had named: the compensation method was recast to answer the Article 31(2) objection, and the discriminatory features identified under Article 14 were adjusted. The transfer was re-effected from 19 July 1969, so no interval of private ownership reopened. The banks were never returned to their former owners at any point in the litigation, and the successor statute was not struck down.

### Q: What role did P.N. Haksar play in preparing bank nationalisation?

P.N. Haksar, the Prime Minister's principal secretary from 1967 and the director of the secretariat that functioned as the centre of her government, supervised the secret preparation of the takeover during May and June 1969. The Haksar Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library preserve the correspondence through which the proposal moved from political decision to draft instrument before the policy had been publicly admitted to exist. Working with the ideologue Mohan Kumaramangalam and the banker T.A. Pai, whose papers record the technical design of the deposit threshold, the custodian arrangement and the compensation structure, Haksar's secretariat produced a legally operable measure that could be promulgated on a chosen day. The secrecy prevented deposit flight and denied the syndicate and the banks any interval in which to organise against the act.

### Q: What was the Lead Bank Scheme that followed nationalisation in December 1969?

The Lead Bank Scheme, introduced by the Reserve Bank of India in December 1969, assigned every district of the country to a designated lead bank, charged with surveying the district's credit needs, identifying growth centres, and coordinating the expansion of banking offices and lending in its allotted territory. The scheme followed the recommendations of the study group chaired by D.R. Gadgil on the organisational framework for social control and of the committee chaired by F.K.F. Nariman on branch expansion. It converted nationalisation from an act of ownership into a machinery of territorial responsibility, and it underwrote the branch expansion that carried bank offices from 8,262 in June 1969 to 60,220 by March 1991, with rural offices rising from 1,833 to 35,206 across the same period.

### Q: Was the State Bank of India one of the banks nationalised in 1969?

No. The State Bank of India was already a public institution fourteen years before the 1969 takeover. It was created on 1 July 1955 under the State Bank of India Act, which converted the Imperial Bank of India into a state-owned corporation expressly charged with extending banking into the countryside. The Reserve Bank of India had itself been nationalised with effect from 1 January 1949. The fourteen banks of July 1969 were the remaining large private commercial banks, and their takeover completed a sequence the founding state had begun in 1949 and 1955 but had declined, under Nehru and Shastri, to finish. Life insurance had been nationalised separately in 1956 through the Life Insurance Corporation.

### Q: What happened to the private banks that were not nationalised in 1969?

Banks below the fifty-crore deposit threshold remained in private ownership, as did the foreign banks operating in India, whose exemption the Supreme Court criticised in R.C. Cooper as discrimination under Article 14. The surviving Indian private banks operated inside the transformed system, subject to the same Reserve Bank direction, branch licensing and priority sector expectations as the nationalised banks, and several grew into substantial institutions over the following decades. Six of the larger survivors crossed the higher threshold of 200 crore rupees in deposits and were taken over in the second wave of 15 April 1980. Private banking on a new footing returned only after the Narasimham Committee reforms of the 1990s licensed a fresh generation of private banks alongside the public system.

### Q: When were priority sector lending targets formalised for Indian banks?

The priority sector idea, that banks should direct fixed shares of credit to agriculture, small industry and other neglected borrowers, was articulated by the Reserve Bank of India in the years immediately after nationalisation and given a descriptive framework in the early 1970s. Formal quantification followed in 1974, when the banks were advised to raise priority sector advances to 33.3 per cent of net bank credit by March 1979. In 1980 the target was raised to 40 per cent of net bank credit, the level that structured Indian bank lending for decades, with sub-targets for agriculture and weaker sections inside it. Reserve Bank series place the priority sectors' share at about 14 per cent in 1969 and 37.7 per cent by the early 1990s, the statistical signature of the nationalised system's operation.

### Q: Did the Janata government of 1977 reverse bank nationalisation?

No. The Janata government of Morarji Desai, in office from March 1977 to July 1979, left the nationalised banking structure untouched, although Desai had resigned from Indira Gandhi's cabinet in July 1969 in the quarrel that preceded the takeover and had every partisan reason to undo it. No subsequent government restored the fourteen banks to their former owners either. The settlement's durability across governments of opposed complexions is part of the evidence for its character: what began in July 1969 as a timed act in a party succession struggle hardened into the permanent structure of Indian banking, administered after 1991 alongside new private banks licensed under the Narasimham reforms, with the one failed institution among the twenty, the New Bank of India, absorbed into the Punjab National Bank in 1993.
