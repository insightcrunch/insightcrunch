In March 1829 a twenty year old conductor stood before a Berlin choir of several hundred singers and raised his baton over a score that had not been performed in public for generations. Felix Mendelssohn had spent years preparing the St Matthew Passion of Johann Sebastian Bach, and the evening is where classical composers history often pretends to begin, with a forgotten master rescued at a single stroke. The record shows something less tidy and more interesting. Keyboard players and theorists had studied Bach in manuscript throughout the intervening decades, copying his fugues and learning counterpoint from them, so no rescue was required; what changed in 1829 was the scale of the public and the machinery of performance around the music. The Western classical tradition is not a sequence of geniuses but a sequence of problems, and each composer the tradition remembers is remembered because they solved one the previous generation could not.

That claim governs everything that follows. A problem, in this sense, is a gap between what musicians wanted to make and what their tools, employers, notation, instruments or audiences allowed them to make. The monk who needed a chant remembered identically in every monastery faced a problem of memory. The opera composer who needed an audience to feel a text rather than admire a singer faced a problem of expression. The symphonist who needed four movements to hold together for forty minutes faced a problem of architecture. Each solution became the next generation's starting condition, and the problems changed accordingly. Read this way, the history of classical composers is a history of institutions as much as a history of individuals: churches that paid salaries, courts that demanded ceremony, publishers who needed saleable parts, conservatories that trained players, and halls that gathered strangers willing to pay for a seat. The names survive because the solutions were strong enough to be taught, printed, performed and argued over after their makers were gone.

One documented figure makes the scale plain, and it is worth stating plainly because it corrects the usual picture of lonely invention. Joseph Haydn, across a career spent largely in one aristocratic household, composed more than one hundred symphonies, most of them for an orchestra he directed daily and knew player by player. Output on that scale was not a miracle of temperament. It was the product of a contract, a standing ensemble, a steady demand for new music at a prince's table, and a composer free to test an idea on Monday and revise it by Friday. Where those conditions existed, composers flourished in numbers. Where they collapsed, whole regional traditions thinned within a generation. The sections that follow take the conditions first and the names second, because that is the order in which the music itself was made.

## What classical music means and why the label fits badly

The word classical carries at least three meanings, and confusing them is the source of most muddled writing on the subject. In its narrowest sense it names a period, roughly the decades from 1750 to 1820, when balance, clarity and periodic phrasing governed European art music. In its broadest sense it names the whole Western art tradition, from medieval chant to the modern concert hall, set against popular and folk repertories. In its commercial sense it names whatever a record shop, a radio station or a concert promoter files under one heading, which is a matter of shelving rather than of style. This article uses the broad sense, because the story it tells begins centuries before the period that borrowed the name.

The label fits badly for a plain reason: classical music is not a single style at all. It is a market and institutional category. A Gregorian chant, a Machaut motet, a Bach fugue, a Mozart opera, a Brahms symphony and a Stravinsky ballet share almost nothing in sound, technique or social function. What they share is a mode of production and survival. Each was written down by a trained composer, transmitted in notation, preserved by an institution, and performed by specialists for listeners who treated the written work as the durable object. Richard Taruskin, whose Oxford History of Western Music appeared in 2005, presses exactly this point: the tradition is defined less by a sound than by literacy, by the habit of fixing music in writing and treating the fixed text as the work itself. Once that definition is accepted, the boundaries of the subject fall where the institutions fall, at the monastery, the chapel, the court, the opera house, the conservatory and the hall.

That definition also explains the company the category keeps. The ranking argument over which individuals stand highest belongs to a separate debate, and readers who want the widest frame for it can start from the series survey of the [greatest musicians of all time](/2010/06/07/greatest-musicians-of-all-time/), which sets composers beside performers across every division. The present article has a different job. It asks how the category was built, who paid for it, what tools shaped it, and why a few hundred names came to stand for a thousand years of music making. A category assembled by institutions can also be reassembled by them, which is why the later sections on the canon and on exclusion matter. Nothing about the repertory heard in a concert hall was inevitable. Every piece in it survived a chain of copying, teaching, publishing and programming decisions, and each link in that chain had alternatives that were dropped.

A further awkwardness is that the people inside the tradition rarely used the label themselves. Medieval singers spoke of chant and of musica. Baroque writers spoke of the stile antico and the stile moderno, the old style and the new. Haydn and Mozart were Kapellmeisters and opera composers, professionals filling posts and commissions. The word classical was applied backward, by nineteenth century historians and promoters who needed a name for a repertory they were busy canonising. J. Peter Burkholder, in A History of Western Music, the textbook through which generations of students have met the subject, treats period labels as tools for organising a survey rather than as facts found in the music. That caution runs through this article. A period boundary is a convenience drawn by historians after the event, not an event itself, and no composer woke on a given morning inside a new era.

The periods named in this article, with the problem each had to solve, can be set out in a single view.

## The patronage economy of church, court and aristocracy

For most of the span covered here, music was bought the way buildings were bought, by institutions with standing needs and the income to meet them. The Church was the largest employer. Cathedrals, collegiate churches and wealthy monasteries maintained choirs, choir schools and organists, and the liturgy required new music on a calendar that never paused: masses for feasts, motets for processions, chant for every day of the year. A composer in a cathedral post had singers at hand, boys being trained at the institution's expense, and a reason to write every week. The great Franco Flemish singers who dominated fifteenth century music, men such as Guillaume Dufay and Johannes Ockeghem, moved between church and court posts across Europe because both kinds of employer were bidding for the same scarce skill.

Courts were the second engine. A prince's chapel was a statement of rank, and rulers measured themselves against rivals partly by the splendour of their music. The Habsburg and Burgundian courts, the Italian city states, the French crown under Louis XIV and the German principalities all maintained musical establishments whose size was a matter of policy. Haydn's decades at Esterhaza under the Esterhazy princes show the arrangement at its most complete: lodging, livery, a salary, an orchestra, an opera house, and an obligation to produce. His contract regulated his conduct and his output in detail, and the security it gave him was the platform for an experimental career of unmatched length. When he called himself isolated from the world and forced to become original, he was describing a workplace.

Aristocratic patronage in the narrower sense, one wealthy individual commissioning one work, ran alongside employment and often mattered more than the salary. Commissions carried dedications, and dedications were currency: a composer offered a set of quartets to a prince or a publisher's patron in hope of a gift, a post or a pension. Beethoven's three great aristocratic supporters in Vienna, Prince Lobkowitz, Prince Lichnowsky and Prince Kinsky, combined with the Archduke Rudolph in 1809 to guarantee him an annuity precisely so that he would stay in Vienna rather than accept a post elsewhere. The arrangement frayed with currency devaluation and the deaths of guarantors, and Beethoven spent years in litigation over it, yet the principle is clear. Where a composer had no market, patrons created a private substitute for one, and the music written under that shelter could afford to be difficult in ways that music written for ticket buyers could not.

Church, court and aristocracy also set the terms of taste. A patron who paid for a mass wanted it splendid and correct; a patron who paid for an opera wanted a plot that flattered the dynasty; a patron who kept a private orchestra wanted novelty at a rate no public promoter would risk. Taruskin treats patronage not as background colour but as a causal force, arguing that the forms themselves, the length of a movement, the size of an ensemble, the choice of a text, answer to who was paying and what the payment was for. That is the working assumption of this article. Follow the money and most of the stylistic story explains itself; ignore it and the same story dissolves into a parade of temperaments.

## What a composer's job actually consisted of

The modern image of the composer, alone with a blank page and an idea, would have puzzled most of the figures in this history. Composing was one duty among many, and rarely the one that took the most hours. A Kapellmeister directed the music of a court or church: he rehearsed the ensemble, chose the repertory, copied or supervised the copying of parts, maintained instruments, auditioned singers, taught the choirboys, negotiated with suppliers of strings and reeds, and answered to a steward for every candle burned at a rehearsal. Johann Sebastian Bach's years in Leipzig, from 1723 to 1750, were spent as Thomaskantor, responsible for music in four churches, for the Latin school attached to St Thomas, and for a weekly supply of cantatas that he composed, copied, rehearsed and directed while teaching classes that had nothing to do with music. His letters of complaint about pay, about the quality of the schoolboys assigned to his choir and about funerals that paid better than composing are the letters of an overworked municipal employee.

Composition itself was embedded in that workload rather than set apart from it. A cantata was needed for Sunday; a keyboard piece was needed for a pupil's lesson; a concerto was needed because the court's visiting virtuoso required one by Thursday. Much of what survives as the canon began as working material, and its survival was accidental in ways the canon story prefers to forget. Bach reused his own music constantly, refitting a secular cantata as a sacred one and a concerto movement as a cantata chorus, because the deadline was real and the material was good. Handel built operas and oratorios partly from borrowed and reworked material, his own and other composers', at a speed set by the London theatrical season. None of this diminishes the results. It explains their shape: clear structures that rehearsed quickly, scoring fitted to the players actually available, and lengths set by the service or the evening rather than by an abstract ideal.

Teaching was the third leg of the job, and for many composers the most reliable income. Keyboard lessons to the children of merchants and officials paid in cash and in connections. Mozart's finances in Vienna rose and fell with his pupil list; Beethoven's lessons to aristocratic amateurs brought him into the houses that later guaranteed his annuity; Chopin, who disliked public performance, built his Paris career substantially on teaching the daughters of wealthy families at high fees. Teaching also transmitted style. A composer's pupils carried his fingering, his phrasing and his taste into the next generation, and the lines of descent from teacher to pupil are one of the most dependable maps of how the tradition actually moved.

Finally, the composer was often his own publisher, copyist, agent and impresario. He negotiated with engravers, corrected proofs, sold subscriptions to forthcoming editions, arranged benefit concerts, hired halls and haggled over ticket prices. The skills were commercial, and the historical record is full of composers who had them and composers who did not, with consequences that show in their incomes rather than in their scores. Understanding the job as a job is the first step toward understanding the music, because nearly every formal habit described in the sections ahead began as a practical answer to a working week.


## Staff notation and what it made possible

### Why is classical music written down at all?

Music is written down because memory cannot hold a complex work steady across distance, time and many performers at once. Notation fixes pitch and rhythm outside any single mind, so a choir in one city can sing what a composer in another city imagined, and a work can outlive everyone who first performed it intact.

Before notation, music lived where it was sung. A melody could travel only inside a singer, and it changed as it travelled, because human memory edits what it carries. The decisive invention of the Western tradition was not an instrument or a form but a writing system. Early medieval chant books marked the rise and fall of a melody with neumes, signs written above the text that reminded a singer who already knew the tune of its contour. Neumes were a memory aid, not a code a stranger could read. The staff changed that. Guido of Arezzo, an eleventh century Italian monk, is credited in his own writings with a system of lines and spaces that fixed each pitch at a definite height, together with syllables for teaching singers to find intervals. A singer who had never heard a chant could now sing it correctly at sight, and Guido boasted that his method cut years from a chorister's training.

Precision grew by stages. Lines multiplied into the five line staff; note shapes acquired rhythmic values; the mensural notation of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries allowed composers to specify durations in exact proportion, so that voices could move at different speeds against one another in calculated patterns. By the time printing arrived, notation could carry a four part mass, a keyboard fantasia or a full opera score from a composer's desk to any trained musician in Europe. Each gain in precision widened the gap between what could be imagined and what could be executed, and composers pushed into the space the writing opened. Polyphony of real complexity is unthinkable without it: no choir holds six independent lines in memory while a composer revises the third. Notation also created the work as an object. A written piece could be owned, sold, dedicated, stolen, criticised, revived and taught, and it could wait in a library for a century and speak again unchanged.

Christoph Wolff, whose archival research on Bach and his world set a standard for documentary method, shows in his Bach biography how deeply the written page structured a composer's working life, from the composing score to the copied parts to the printed edition aimed at amateurs. The page was at once workshop, warehouse and shop window. That triple function is the tradition's foundation. Every later development in this article, the canon included, rests on the fact that the music could be stored outside living memory and inspected by people who had never met its maker.

## Oral transmission and what notation displaced

Notation displaced a way of knowing, and something real was lost in the exchange. Oral transmission is not a defective version of writing; it is a different technology with different strengths. A singer trained by ear learns a repertory as a set of flexible formulas, phrases that can be stretched, ornamented, combined and adapted to a text, an occasion or a voice. Improvisation is not decoration in such a culture but the normal mode of performance. Medieval chant itself was sung from books only after centuries of oral practice, and scholars of chant have long debated how far the written versions capture a fluid sung reality that varied from region to region. The earliest chant manuscripts differ among themselves in telling ways, which suggests that the page froze one local practice and let the others fade.

The losses show wherever the written and oral worlds met. Notation recorded pitch and duration with growing precision, but it recorded almost nothing of timbre, of ornament, of tempo flexibility, of the thousand small decisions a singer makes in a phrase. Baroque performance depended on improvised ornamentation that the score deliberately left blank, and the figured bass, the shorthand at the heart of baroque accompaniment, was an oral skill written as a prompt rather than as instructions. Keyboard players realised harmonies from numbers the way jazz musicians later worked from chord symbols, by training and habit rather than by reading each note. As notation grew more prescriptive across the nineteenth century, those improvising skills withered. Composers wrote out the ornaments, then the cadenzas, then the expressive markings, and the performer's role narrowed from partner to executor.

The displacement was never total, and the tradition is healthiest where the two modes still overlap. Folk musicians inside and beside the classical world kept tunes by ear; church organists improvised; teachers passed bowing and fingering habits hand to hand in a chain no book records. Charles Rosen, the pianist and writer whose analyses of style rest on a performer's knowledge, repeatedly insists that a score is an incomplete instruction set which only a living practice can complete, and that treating the page as the whole of the music mistakes the map for the journey. The point matters for how the canon is judged later in this article. What notation preserved, it preserved superbly. What it could not preserve, whole repertories held by memory and habit, vanished without a trace, and the surviving record is biased toward the literate institutions that wrote things down.

One survival from the oral world deserves separate notice, because it shaped how written music was performed for centuries: the art of diminution, the division of a written long note into a run of shorter ornamental notes according to rules every trained singer and player learned by example. Sixteenth century treatises by teachers such as Silvestro Ganassi and Diego Ortiz set the patterns down, precisely because print had begun to threaten the oral chain that carried them, and their books are best read as rescue operations for a skill that lived in throats and fingers. A modern performer who sings a Renaissance line exactly as printed, note for note, is therefore not reproducing the period's practice but departing from it, since the printed line was the skeleton the performer was expected to clothe. The page and the practice were partners, and the history of their long negotiation is the history of musical literacy itself.

## Medieval foundations: plainchant and early polyphony

The tradition's documentary history begins with chant, the sung prayer of the Latin Church. Plainchant was a vast, ordered repertory of melodies for the Mass and the daily Office, built over centuries, standardised under Carolingian pressure when Charlemagne's court pressed Roman chant upon the Frankish church, and eventually written down in the neumatic books that survive from the ninth century onward. Chant was monophonic, a single unaccompanied line, and its forms were governed by text and ritual rather than by a composer's ambition. Yet it gave the tradition its first durable assets: a theory of modes that organised pitch, a body of melody every musician knew, and an institutional home in which music was a daily professional obligation. The chant repertory also became raw material. For a thousand years composers built new works on chant melodies, quoting a phrase in one voice while other voices wove around it, so that the oldest layer of the tradition runs as a thread through the newest.

Polyphony, music of simultaneous independent lines, grew from practice into art between the ninth and the thirteenth centuries. Early organum added a second voice moving in parallel with the chant; later organum let the added voice move freely above a chant note held to great length; the Notre Dame school in Paris, associated with the named composers Leonin and Perotin around the turn of the thirteenth century, produced chant based works of unprecedented scale and rhythmic order. Perotin's four voice settings for the Paris cathedral were public monuments in sound, and the treatises that describe them mark the moment when individual composers become visible as authors of particular works. In the fourteenth century the motet joined sacred and secular texts in different voices at once, and Guillaume de Machaut, poet as well as composer, supervised copies of his complete works, an early sign of the composer as a self conscious author with a catalogue to protect.

The medieval centuries also built the theoretical frame the whole tradition inherited: the classification of consonance and dissonance, the rhythmic modes, the rules of counterpoint that students still learn in altered form. Richard Taruskin opens his Oxford History with the written record itself, warning that a history of literate music is a history of what writing happened to keep. The warning fits the medieval evidence exactly. What survives is cathedral and court music, the repertory of institutions with scriptoria. The sung music of villages, of work, of domestic life left almost no trace, and any account of the period that forgets that silence will mistake the archive for the world.

## The Renaissance, imitation and the mass

### Who was the first great classical composer?

No single first figure exists, because the answer depends on the test applied. If the test is mastery across every genre of an age joined to a named body of surviving works, Guillaume Dufay is the strongest candidate, while Leonin and Perotin stand earlier as the first named composers of written polyphony.

Between about 1450 and 1600 the centre of musical Europe was a network rather than a capital. Singers and composers trained in the Low Countries and northern France, the Franco Flemish school, took posts in Italian chapels, Spanish cathedrals, the imperial court and the papal choir, carrying a shared technique with them. That technique was imitation: a melodic idea stated in one voice and answered in each other voice in turn, so that a texture of four, five or six lines could be woven from a single thread. Imitative counterpoint gave composers a way to make long spans coherent without a text to carry them, and it gave listeners the pleasure of recognition as the idea passed from voice to voice. Josquin des Prez, the most celebrated composer of the age, handled imitation with a clarity and expressive tact that made his music a model for a century; Martin Luther, no neutral witness, praised Josquin as the master of the notes, saying that other composers had to do as the notes wished while Josquin did as he wished with them.

The mass was the period's large form, and its construction shows the age's habits of mind. Composers built mass cycles on borrowed material: a chant melody laid out in long notes in one voice, a secular song used the same way, or, in the parody technique, the whole woven texture of an existing motet taken over and refitted to the mass text. Borrowing was not a failure of originality. It was a recognised craft, a way of placing a new work inside a tradition and displaying ingenuity against a known frame. J. Peter Burkholder built a scholarly career partly on this subject, and his studies of borrowing in the music of Charles Ives rest on the longer view that Western composers have always composed against existing music. The Renaissance evidence supports him. Originality in this period meant transformation, not ex nihilo invention, and the modern cult of the wholly new theme is a later value read backward.

Printing arrived in the middle of the story and changed its economics. Ottaviano Petrucci issued the first substantial book of polyphonic music printed from movable type in Venice in 1501, and within decades printed partbooks carried the Franco Flemish repertory into churches and homes that could never have afforded manuscript copies. A repertory could now be international in fact as well as in personnel. The Renaissance also widened the secular side of the ledger: the madrigal in Italy turned poetry into music of intense word painting for small groups of skilled amateurs, and the chanson, the villancico and the English madrigal each gave a vernacular literature its musical dress. By 1600 Europe possessed a trained profession, a shared technique, a printing trade and a repertory that crossed every border. The stage was set for a quarrel about what music was for.


## The baroque problem of expressing affect

Around 1600 a group of Italian musicians and intellectuals set out to solve a problem they stated with unusual clarity. Polyphony, for all its craft, buried the words. A listener hearing four voices sing four different syllables of the same text at once could admire the weave and miss the meaning, and the Florentine experimenters who gathered around Count Giovanni de Bardi wanted the opposite: a single voice, declaiming a text with the rhythms and inflections of passionate speech, over a spare accompaniment that supported the words without competing with them. Their model was an imagined Greek tragedy, their method was the new accompanied solo singing they called recitative, and their unintended invention was opera. The period that follows, conventionally dated from 1600 to 1750 and called baroque by later historians, organised itself around expression: the doctrine that music's purpose was to move the affections, the settled states of feeling that a piece could embody and a listener could be made to share.

Claudio Monteverdi gave the quarrel its slogan. Defending his madrigals against the theorist Giovanni Maria Artusi, who had attacked their rule breaking dissonances, Monteverdi's brother published the composer's position in 1607: there was a prima pratica, a first practice in which harmony governed the words, and a seconda pratica, a second practice in which the words governed harmony. A dissonance was justified if the text's feeling required it. The principle sounds modest and is not. It licenses the composer to break the inherited rules whenever expression demands, and it relocates authority from the rule book to the dramatic moment. Monteverdi's own operas and late madrigals show what the license could do, stretching harmony and vocal line to follow grief, rage and desire with a freedom that still startles.

The affect doctrine had a systematic side as well as a liberating one. Baroque theorists catalogued figures, standard musical gestures for grief, joy, anger and longing, and expected a movement to sustain one governing affection rather than wander among several. Unity of affect explains the period's characteristic forms: the aria that holds a single emotional state and turns it in the light, the dance movements each stamped with one rhythm and character, the concerto movement that drives one idea to its limit. What looks from a distance like limitation was a deliberate aesthetic of concentration. The composer solved the problem of expression by specialisation, one feeling fully inhabited at a time, and the solution held for a century and a half before a later generation found it constraining and wanted contrast and development instead.

Alongside the theory ran a revolution in venues and genres. The first public opera house opened in Venice in 1637, selling tickets to anyone who could pay, and opera became a commercial industry with seasons, stars and claques. Instrumental music stepped out of the church and the dance suite into the concerto and the sonata. The violin family, perfected by the Cremona makers, gave composers an instrument of unprecedented range and projection. By 1750 the baroque settlement, figured bass, affective unity, the opera house, the concerto, had built the platform on which the classical style would stand, even as younger musicians began to find its rhetoric old fashioned and to say so in print.

## Basso continuo and the rise of functional harmony

Underneath baroque music sits a practice so pervasive that the period is sometimes named for it: the basso continuo, the continuous bass. A continuo group, typically a cello or bassoon on the bass line with a keyboard or lute filling in harmony, accompanied nearly everything, vocal and instrumental, sacred and secular. The bass line was printed with numbers beneath the notes, the figures of figured bass, indicating which chords to build above each bass note. The player realised the harmony in performance, choosing voicing, spacing and ornament by trained habit. The system was a shorthand, and like all shorthand it assumed a shared professional culture: everyone in the room knew what the numbers meant and what style allowed.

The continuo matters to this history because it reorganised how composers thought. When a bass line with its implied chords runs under everything, harmony stops being the by product of independent voices weaving and becomes a framework in its own right, a progression the upper parts decorate. Across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries that framework hardened into functional harmony: the system in which chords have roles, tonic as home, dominant as tension demanding return, subdominant as departure, and a grammar of standard progressions links them. Jean Philippe Rameau gave the system its theory in his Treatise on Harmony of 1722, deriving chords from a fundamental bass and treating inversions as forms of a single chord. Practice and theory reinforced each other, and by the middle of the eighteenth century a composer anywhere in Europe could rely on any trained listener following a harmonic argument across a long span. Functional harmony is the load bearing structure of the classical and romantic repertory, the reason a symphony movement can travel far from its home key and be felt to arrive back as an event.

The continuo habit also trained musicians to think in charts and diagrams, since a figured bass part is itself a compressed visual summary of an entire texture. That habit of compressing a performance into a chart has a long afterlife in teaching and analysis. When the continuo faded around the middle of the eighteenth century, composers began writing out in full what players had once supplied by convention, and the score grew thicker, more prescriptive and more completely the composer's own. The gain was control. The loss, as the section on oral transmission noted, was a generation of improvising skills that the written page no longer asked for.

## Opera's birth as a separate institution

Opera began as an experiment at court and became, within forty years, a business with its own buildings, contracts, star system and scandals. The Florentine works of the years around 1600, Jacopo Peri's Dafne and Euridice and Giulio Caccini's rival Euridice, were court entertainments for weddings and state occasions, performed once for an invited audience. Monteverdi's Orfeo of 1607, written for the Mantuan court, showed what a composer of the first rank could make of the new form, but the form's destiny was decided in Venice in 1637 when the Teatro San Cassiano opened its doors to a paying public. Commercial opera needed works that could run for a season, singers whose names sold seats, and plots that mixed the serious and the comic in proportions the box office rewarded. The institution, not the theory, shaped the art from that point forward.

As an institution opera differed from every other musical employer in this history. A church needed music for a liturgy that repeated; an opera house needed novelty for a public that chose. Impresarios leased theatres, assembled companies on seasonal contracts, commissioned librettos and scores against deadlines, and bore the profit or the loss. Singers, the castrati above all, commanded fees that dwarfed composers' salaries, and arias were tailored, substituted and carried from production to production in a singer's portfolio like luggage. Handel's London career shows the system at full stretch: royal subsidy, rival companies, imported stars, audience factions, bankruptcy and reinvention, ending with his turn to English oratorio, a form that kept the musical forces and dropped the staging costs that had ruined the operas.

Opera also became the tradition's most international and most argued over genre. Italian opera in Italian travelled to every court in Europe; French opera under Jean Baptiste Lully at the court of Louis XIV built a rival national style with the orchestra and the dance at its centre; reformers from Christoph Willibald Gluck onward periodically declared the whole institution corrupt and tried to return it to dramatic truth. Giuseppe Verdi worked inside the Italian industry at its industrial height, negotiating with publishers and censors house by house, a story told in the article on [Giuseppe Verdi's life and music](/2018/04/07/giuseppe-verdi-life-and-music/). Richard Wagner, a generation later, concluded that the commercial institution itself was the obstacle and built his own theatre at Bayreuth to escape it, a story taken up in the article on [Richard Wagner's life and music](/2018/03/07/richard-wagner-life-and-music/). No other genre forced composers to think so hard about audiences, money and power, because no other genre cost so much to put on a stage.

## The concerto and the instrumental genres

Instrumental music had to solve a problem of respect. For centuries the prestige genres were vocal, since the word carried meaning and the Church carried the budget, and purely instrumental pieces were functional: dances for dancing, signals for ceremony, preludes to settle a choir's pitch. The baroque concerto changed the standing of instruments by giving them drama. The concerto principle is contrast embodied: one player or a small group set against a full ensemble, solo against tutti, brilliance against mass. Arcangelo Corelli's concerti grossi, published in 1714 after decades of manuscript circulation, fixed the model of a small concertino group answering a larger ripieno. Antonio Vivaldi, working with the virtuoso orphan girls of the Venetian Pieta, turned the solo concerto into a compact three movement drama of ritornello returns and solo episodes, and composed hundreds of them at a speed set by an institution that needed constant novelty. Johann Sebastian Bach, studying Vivaldi's scores by copying and transcribing them, learned the ritornello logic that organises his own concertos and cantata choruses.

The instrumental genres multiplied around the concerto's example. The sonata settled into church and chamber types; the keyboard suite ordered dances into stylised listening music; the trio sonata, two high voices over continuo, became the period's universal chamber medium, playable in a parlour and presentable in a chapel. Each genre was a social arrangement as much as a form: who played, where, for whom, and at what fee. When the continuo thinned out after 1750, the string quartet emerged from the same chamber culture with four equal voices and no harmonic filler, a medium Haydn turned into the most demanding conversation in music. The pattern is the article's thesis in miniature. A genre is a solved problem, the problem of what a given gathering of players and listeners should do with an hour, and the solution outlives the gathering.

Genre also governed expectation, and expectation is a compositional resource. A listener who knows that a concerto's soloist will answer the orchestra, that a fugue's subject will return in every voice, that a dance movement will repeat its strains, hears each fulfilment and each delay as meaning. Composers of the first rank exploited the contract, postponing the expected return, disguising it, or granting it with a difference that redefined the whole movement. Charles Rosen built his account of the classical style on this interplay between convention and event, arguing that the conventions were not cages but the very conditions that made expressive deviation legible. The instrumental genres of the baroque handed the classical period a complete toolkit of such conventions, together with a public trained by opera and by church music to listen to instruments as if they were speaking.


## Haydn and the standardisation of the classical style

The style later called classical, balanced, clear, built on periodic phrases and functional harmony, was not decreed by a theorist. It was standardised in practice, and the musician who did most of the standardising was Joseph Haydn. His career is the patronage system's longest experiment: engaged in 1761 by the Esterhazy family, he spent nearly three decades at their palaces, rising to Kapellmeister over an orchestra and an opera company, composing for a known ensemble in a known room week after week. The conditions rewarded exactly the virtues the classical style prizes. A composer writing for players he rehearsed daily could test balances, learn what clarity cost, and refine a language until it could carry wit, drama and weight in the same movement.

The symphony shows the process at work. Haydn wrote more than one hundred, and across them the loose sequence of movements inherited from the opera overture and the orchestral suite settled into a durable plan: a substantial opening movement in sonata form, a slow movement, a minuet, and a fast finale. The string quartet shows it even more sharply. In the sets from the 1770s and the celebrated Opus 33 set of 1781, which Haydn advertised as written in a new and special way, the four voices share the thematic material as equals, the cello steps out of the bass line, and motivic development, the breaking of a theme into fragments and their recombination, becomes the engine of the argument. The quartet became a school for composers: Mozart studied Haydn's quartets and dedicated his own set to him, and the dedication describes them as the fruit of long and laborious study, a pupil's public acknowledgement of where the standard was set.

Standardisation is not the enemy of expression here; it is what made expression portable. Once audiences and players across Europe shared the expectations, a composer could write for strangers. Haydn's London visits of 1791 to 1792 and 1794 to 1795, undertaken as a free agent for the impresario Johann Peter Salomon after the Esterhazy establishment was reduced, proved the point commercially: the twelve London symphonies succeeded with a public that had never employed him, in halls he had never worked in, because the language was now common property. Jan Swafford, whose Vintage Guide to Classical Music of 1992 remains a widely used listener's survey, presents Haydn as the tradition's great normaliser whose experiments became everyone else's grammar. The description understates the case in one respect. Haydn did not only normalise the grammar; he demonstrated, across sixty years of output, how much variety the grammar could bear without breaking, which is why composers who revolutionised everything else kept his forms.

## Sonata form explained in plain language

Sonata form is the classical period's central invention, and it is simpler than its textbook reputation suggests. It is the shape of a large instrumental movement, most often the first movement of a symphony, quartet, sonata or concerto, and it works by setting up a departure and then accomplishing a return. A movement begins by presenting its material at home, in the tonic key: usually a first theme of character and energy, then a passage of transition that travels, then a second theme or group in a contrasting key, the dominant or the relative major, which often sounds more lyrical against the first. This presentation is called the exposition, and classical practice usually repeats it, so the listener hears the journey twice and knows the ground.

The middle of the movement, the development, takes the presented material away from home. Themes are fragmented, sequenced through distant keys, set against one another and driven through passages of instability whose point is tension: the music has left the place where its themes belong and has not yet found the way back. The return, the recapitulation, brings the exposition's material home again, with the decisive difference that the second theme now appears in the tonic rather than away from it. The argument closes because the material that had been heard in a foreign key is reconciled to the home key. A coda may follow, confirming the settlement or spending the movement's remaining energy. That is the whole design: statement, estrangement, reconciliation. Its power lies in making harmony itself dramatic, so that a key change is felt as an event with consequences and a return as a resolution earned rather than merely scheduled.

Two clarifications keep the explanation honest. Sonata form is a description built by theorists after the fact, codified in the nineteenth century from the practice of Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven; the composers themselves worked from habits and expectations rather than from a printed plan, and many great movements bend the pattern, omit the repeat, expand the coda into a second development or let the return arrive disguised. Charles Rosen's The Classical Style, published in 1971, made the strongest scholarly case that the form is best understood as a dramatic principle inside a living style rather than a mould into which music was poured, and his later book Sonata Forms pursued the argument in detail. Rosen's reading has the virtue of matching the evidence: the pattern is regular enough for listeners to feel its departures, and flexible enough that no two major composers use it identically. A plan that can be bent without breaking is a style, and a plan that cannot is a template; sonata form endured because it was the first of these.

## The Viennese hinge and the public concert

Between about 1780 and 1830 Vienna was the hinge on which the tradition swung from patronage to the public, and the city earned the position through density: an imperial court, a wealthy aristocracy with palaces and private orchestras, a publishing trade, piano makers, a large population of professional musicians, and audiences with the money and the habit of attendance. Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven all worked there, as did Schubert and a small army of teachers, copyists and instrument builders. The decisive change of the period is not a style but a venue. The public concert, a ticketed event in a rented hall before an audience of strangers, grew from an occasional venture into the central institution of musical life, and it rewired the composer's economics. A work no longer needed a patron's taste; it needed a public's.

Mozart's Viennese decade shows the new economy in both its promise and its cruelty. Arriving in 1781 after breaking with the Archbishop of Salzburg's service, he built a freelance career on piano concertos performed by himself at subscription concerts, on pupils, and on opera commissions, and for several seasons the system made him prosperous. When subscriptions thinned and the aristocracy's attention wandered, the same system left him borrowing and writing begging letters, because a freelance income has no floor. Beethoven, arriving a decade later, learned from Mozart's experience: he kept aristocratic patrons and their annuity while also selling works to publishers in several countries, playing the old economy and the new against each other with a tenacity his contemporaries found exhausting and his biographers find exemplary. The hinge figure is not one man but an arrangement: patronage did not vanish, it lost its monopoly.

The public concert changed the music as surely as it changed the money. A hall rewards projection, contrast and works that seize listeners hearing them once; it favours the concerto with its visible contest and the symphony with its public rhetoric. It also created the repeat performance and with it the idea of a repertory: works performed again because a public asked for them, not because a liturgy required them. Schubert's career stands at the hinge's far side as its limiting case, a composer sustained by a circle of friends, the Schubertiades in private rooms, with one public concert of his own works given in 1828 and a reputation built largely after his death by the advocacy of others, a story the article on [Franz Schubert's life and music](/2018/06/07/franz-schubert-life-and-music/) tells in full. Vienna's lesson generalises. When the audience becomes a market, fame becomes a mechanism with its own logic, and the canon sections later in this article describe what that logic selected.

The hinge also had a domestic side that the hall's history tends to eclipse. Most music in Vienna, as in every European city, was made in rooms rather than in public: aristocratic salons, merchants' drawing rooms, the amateur orchestras and quartet parties of officials and lawyers who played Haydn and Mozart for their own instruction. That domestic culture was the market for the printed sonata, the song and the four hand arrangement, and it trained the audience the public concert later inherited. A public does not appear from nowhere when a hall opens; it is recruited from people who already play, sing and subscribe. Vienna's distinction was to possess that recruiting ground in unusual depth, a whole class for whom music making was a normal accomplishment of education, and the density explains why the hinge turned there rather than in a larger or richer capital.

## The romantic break and the composer as individual

The nineteenth century inherited the classical forms and changed the composer's self understanding. Where Haydn had been a servant in livery and Mozart a freed servant struggling as a tradesman, the romantic composer presented himself as an individual whose inner life was the work's true subject. Beethoven is the pivot, as he is in so many of this article's stories. His Eroica Symphony of 1803 to 1804 doubled the scale of the form, drove development to unprecedented length, and was received by contemporaries as a declaration that a symphony could carry a world view. His deafness, documented in the Heiligenstadt Testament of 1802, a letter never sent, gave the century its founding image of the composer as a sufferer whose affliction sealed him inside his art. The image was not manufactured from nothing, and Beethoven cultivated his independence fiercely, but the century that followed turned his stance into a template: originality became the supreme value, and the composer's personality became part of what an audience bought.

The romantic programme reorganised musical life around that individuality. The miniature became a serious genre, the piano character piece and the song cycle carrying private feeling at a scale the salon and the home could hold. The virtuoso composer performer, Franz Liszt and Niccolo Paganini above all, made the individual's charisma a box office force. Criticism became a literary profession, with Robert Schumann founding a journal and inventing critical personae to argue the new music's case. Hector Berlioz wrote his own memoirs and his own reviews, conducting his reputation in prose as deliberately as in notes. Even the concert ritual changed to suit the cult: audiences sat in darkened, silent halls, a discipline unknown to the talkative eighteenth century, and the programme note was born to explain what the great man's work meant.

Alex Ross, in The Rest Is Noise, his 2007 history of twentieth century music, traces how the romantic image of the composer survived into an age that rebelled against almost everything else about the period, because institutions had been rebuilt around it: the conservatory curriculum, the subscription season, the recording catalogue. The romantic break also set the terms of the argument about meaning that the next section takes up. If the work expresses the composer's inner life, what exactly does a listener receive, and can music mean anything checkable at all? The nineteenth century split over that question, and the split organised its criticism, its factions and some of its finest music. What no side disputed was the premise the age had installed: the composer as an originating individual, a figure the earlier centuries, with their servants, craftsmen and chapel masters, would have recognised only in part.


## Program music and the argument about meaning

Instrumental music without words raised a question the vocal centuries could postpone: what, if anything, does it mean? The romantic generation divided into parties over the answer, and the division produced both manifestos and masterpieces. On one side stood program music, instrumental works that set out to represent something outside themselves: a story, a scene, a character, a sequence of events. Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique of 1830 arrived with a printed programme describing an artist's opium haunted obsession, and expected the audience to follow the music by it. Liszt's symphonic poems of the 1850s compressed the idea into single movements shaped by poems, paintings and heroes. Richard Strauss carried the method to its limit in the 1890s, representing a hero's life, a domestic day and the cries of a child in orchestral detail that audiences were meant to decode.

On the other side stood the doctrine of absolute music. The Viennese critic Eduard Hanslick gave it its classic statement in On the Musically Beautiful of 1854, arguing that music's content is formed sound in motion, that it cannot represent definite feelings or events, and that judging it by a story attached to it mistakes an association for the work. Brahms became the party's standard bearer in the polemics of the century, and the so called War of the Romantics set his circle against the New German School of Liszt and Wagner, with manifestos, leaked drafts and journalistic feuds. The war was partly a genuine philosophical dispute and partly a struggle for posts, publishers and audiences, and historians who treat it as pure philosophy miss how much of it was fought over the machinery of careers.

The balanced verdict the evidence supports is that the argument itself was the productive thing. Program composers, forced to make a narrative legible in sound, sharpened orchestration, thematic transformation and long range pacing. Absolute music's defenders, forced to justify music without a story, produced the analysis of form and the criticism on which the modern concert programme still draws. Many composers refused the choice in practice: Beethoven had already labelled the Pastoral Symphony more an expression of feeling than painting, a formulation that stands with one foot in each camp, and Mahler attached programmes to his early symphonies and then withdrew them, as if the story had been scaffolding to be removed once the building stood. The question was never settled because it is really two questions: what a composer intended, which is a matter of record, and what a listener can claim to hear, which is a matter of judgment. Keeping those apart dissolves most of the quarrel while preserving everything in it that was worth fighting over.

## Nationalism and the spread beyond Vienna

The nineteenth century's second great transformation was geographic. Music that had been centred on Italy, then on Vienna, became a field of national projects, as peoples under empires and peoples building states decided that a national music was a form of political existence. The mechanism was consistent across countries: collect the folk songs before industrialisation erased them, found a conservatory and an opera house in the capital, write operas on national legends in the national language, and teach the resulting repertory as the sound of the nation. Bedrich Smetana built Czech opera almost single handed, from The Bartered Bride of 1866 to the cycle of symphonic poems Ma vlast, My Country, of the 1870s, while Antonin Dvorak carried Czech idioms into the symphony and the string quartet and then, from his post in New York in the 1890s, advised American composers to build on their own folk sources with an authority no American voice then carried.

Russia produced the period's most self conscious national school. The group remembered as the Five, Mily Balakirev, Cesar Cui, Modest Mussorgsky, Nikolai Rimsky Korsakov and Alexander Borodin, set themselves against the conservatory training of Anton Rubinstein's circle, arguing that Russian music should grow from Russian folk song, Orthodox chant and oriental colour rather than from German models. Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky, conservatory trained and caught between the camps, absorbed the criticism from both and answered it in the work itself, which is one reason his position in the tradition earns its own section later in this article. In Scandinavia, Edvard Grieg distilled Norwegian dance and folk melody into a lyric style of international reach, and Jean Sibelius gave Finland a symphonic voice that his countrymen heard, during the years of Russian pressure, as the nation speaking. In England, a country teased by continental writers as the land without music, collectors such as Cecil Sharp gathered folk song from Somerset villages from 1903 onward, and Ralph Vaughan Williams built a compositional language on what the collectors saved.

Nationalism's ledger has two columns, and honesty requires both. It widened the tradition's map permanently, brought folk material of great beauty into the written repertory, and gave composers outside the old centres a reason and a route to write. It also narrowed ears, teaching audiences to hear music as the property of peoples, and it supplied states with a cultural instrument that the twentieth century would use for darker purposes. The folklorist's notebook and the ministry's programme were sometimes the same document. What the movement proves, for this article's argument, is that the tradition's centre was never a place; it was a set of institutions, and any society that built them, a conservatory, a publisher, an opera house, a collecting movement, could make the tradition grow in a new soil.

## The nineteenth century opera industry

Opera in the nineteenth century was the tradition's heavy industry, the sector with the largest buildings, the longest contracts and the most money at risk. Italy ran the system most completely. An impresario leased a theatre for a season, engaged a company, and commissioned new works against a calendar fixed by Carnival and Lent; the publisher Giovanni Ricordi and his successors turned scores into property, renting materials, controlling productions and building a catalogue that made the firm a power beside the composer. Verdi's career was made inside this machinery and against its frictions: censorship that altered plots and settings, houses that expected a new opera at punishing intervals, singers whose contracts entitled them to interpolate favourite arias. The works that resulted, from Rigoletto in 1851 to Falstaff in 1893, show an artist learning the industry's constraints so thoroughly that the constraints became instruments.

France ran a rival system on state lines. The Paris Opera, subsidised and regulated, staged grand opera on a scale no commercial house could match: five acts, a ballet, crowd scenes, historical subjects, spectacle engineered by the newest stage machinery. Giacomo Meyerbeer, a German composer who mastered the Paris system, set its template with Robert le diable in 1831 and Les Huguenots in 1836, and the template exported across Europe. Wagner worked in Paris, failed there, and spent a career defining himself against its values before building Bayreuth, where from 1876 his own festival theatre inverted every term of the industry: one composer, one house, works performed as a sacred trust, an audience of pilgrims. The festival was an anti industry that became an institution in its turn, with its own box office, its own dynastic management and its own orthodoxy.

The industry also professionalised everything around the stage. Conductors rose from time beaters to interpreters as scores grew complex; the Paris Opera's orchestra and the court orchestras of Germany became standing bodies of a size the eighteenth century never fielded. Singers trained in conservatories for a repertory that now reached back a century, since the industry discovered that revivals of proven works balanced the risk of premieres. That discovery, repeated in the symphonic world, is the seed of the canon: an institution that must fill a season learns to love works that have already filled one. By 1900 opera had become at once the most popular and the most conservative large institution in musical life, a paradox the recording age would deepen rather than resolve.

## The orchestra as an instrument in its own right

### What does an orchestra conductor actually do?

A conductor keeps a large ensemble together in time, shapes its balance and phrasing in rehearsal, sets tempo and character in performance, and carries responsibility for the interpretation the audience hears. The gestures are a visible language the players read; the deeper work is preparation, rehearsal decisions and judgment under pressure on the night itself.

An orchestra is a technology for combining sounds, and its history is a history of standardisation. The baroque orchestra was a flexible gathering built around strings and continuo, its wind parts filled by whoever the court employed. The classical orchestra settled into a recognisable constitution: strings as the core, flutes, oboes, bassoons and horns in pairs, trumpets and timpani for ceremony, with clarinets joining as the century turned. Beethoven's scores stretched that constitution from inside, demanding trombones, piccolo and contrabassoon for particular effects and pushing the string writing to the edge of the period's technique. Berlioz then treated the orchestra itself as the subject of study. His Treatise on Instrumentation of 1843 catalogued each instrument's range, character and combinations with a practitioner's precision, and his own scores, calling for forces of unprecedented size, made orchestration a branch of composition rather than a clerical task of dressing a finished idea.

Across the later nineteenth century the orchestra grew in every dimension. Wagner's Bayreuth pit concealed a vast ensemble and required new instruments built to his specification; Mahler's symphonies called for triple and quadruple wind, offstage bands and choruses; Strauss wrote for an orchestra of more than a hundred players and treated its sections as soloists in rotation. String sections standardised their bowing under leaders and conductors; the conductor's role itself professionalised, from Hans von Bulow's drilled Meiningen orchestra of the 1880s, which toured as a demonstration of what rehearsal could achieve, to the great permanent orchestras founded in these decades, the Vienna and Berlin Philharmonics, the Amsterdam Concertgebouw, the American orchestras of Boston and Chicago. Permanence mattered as much as size. An orchestra that plays together for years under consistent direction becomes an instrument with a memory, capable of a corporate style that a pickup ensemble cannot approach.

The mature orchestra changed what a composer could think. Writing for it was like writing for a single many voiced instrument whose capabilities were known, taught in conservatories and documented in treatises, and the symphonic repertory of the century is the record of composers discovering those capabilities one by one: the horn as distance, the cor anglais as desolation, divided strings as shimmer, brass as public proclamation. It also changed the economics of the tradition for good. A symphony now required a hundred salaries and a hall to hold them, which meant that only institutions could perform the central repertory, and institutions, as the canon sections argue, have memories and preferences of their own that shape what survives.


## The conservatory and professional training

Training made the tradition reproducible. For centuries musicians had been formed inside the institutions that employed them: the choir school raised the singer, the guild and the court raised the player, and a composer learned by copying masters and singing in their choirs. The conservatory separated training from employment and gave it a curriculum. The Paris Conservatoire, founded in 1795 from revolutionary reorganisation, set the pattern: state supported, examination driven, with professors for each instrument, classes in harmony and counterpoint, and prizes that made careers. Leipzig followed in 1843 under Mendelssohn's direction, London's Royal Academy of Music in 1822, and the later nineteenth century added conservatories in Vienna, St Petersburg, Moscow and across the Americas, each importing the model and adapting it to a national school.

The curriculum did quiet ideological work. It defined the repertory a student must master, and that repertory, Bach for counterpoint, Haydn and Mozart for form, Beethoven as the summit of the symphony, became a ladder every trained musician climbed, so that the canon was reproduced in each generation's fingers before it was debated in print. It standardised technique: a violinist trained in Paris and one trained in St Petersburg disagreed about style but shared a mechanics that let them sit in the same orchestra. It also professionalised the composer. Where Bach had been a church employee who composed and Haydn a servant who composed, the conservatory graduate was a specialist whose diploma certified composition as a trade, and the great teaching lineages, from Nikolai Rimsky Korsakov's St Petersburg classroom, which produced Stravinsky, to Nadia Boulanger's Paris studio of the twentieth century, which trained composers from several continents, became channels through which styles travelled.

Access widened and narrowed at once. Conservatories admitted students by examination rather than by birth into a musical family or a choir school's charity, and women entered in growing numbers as performers, the Paris Conservatoire awarding its prizes to women instrumentalists across the nineteenth century, while composition classes and the careers beyond them stayed largely closed, a closure the sections on exclusion examine. The conservatory also created the professor composer, a new economic type: Anton Rubinstein, Tchaikovsky at Moscow, Rimsky Korsakov at St Petersburg and Brahms's friend Joseph Joachim in Berlin all drew salaries from teaching institutions, which steadied incomes that performance alone could not. By 1900 the tradition possessed a complete educational machine, able to train the players its orchestras needed, the singers its opera houses needed and the composers its publishers needed, and to certify them in a language every employer understood.

The conservatory's quietest product was the audience. Its public examinations and prize concerts taught cities what accomplished performance sounded like; its graduates staffed the orchestras whose standards rose with each intake; and its curriculum, by requiring students to perform the repertory of two centuries, manufactured listeners who knew that repertory from inside their own hands. When the graduates dispersed, to provincial orchestras, cathedral choirs, military bands, schoolrooms and opera pits, they carried the curriculum with them as a portable standard, which is why a competent orchestra could be assembled in a new city on another continent by 1900 and why its programmes resembled those of Leipzig or Vienna. Standardisation has a cost in local colour, and regional schools of playing did flatten as the conservatory model spread, but the gain was a genuinely international profession whose members could cross borders with their training intact and be understood at the first rehearsal.

## Printed scores, publishers and the market

Print turned music from a service into a product, and publishers became the tradition's second set of gatekeepers. Manuscript copying had always limited circulation: a work existed where a copy had been made, and copies cost scribes' wages. Engraving and then lithography cut that cost across the eighteenth century, and firms in Leipzig, Vienna, Paris and London built businesses on the printed page. Breitkopf and Hartel of Leipzig, founded in the eighteenth century and industrialised in the nineteenth, issued complete editions that made a composer's whole output available for study; Artaria in Vienna published Haydn and Mozart; Pleyel in Paris was composer, publisher and piano maker in one person, a vertical integration the industry never forgot. The publisher decided what was worth engraving, and engraving favoured what amateurs could play at home, which is why the piano, the domestic instrument of the century, sits at the centre of the trade: sonatas, dances, arrangements of operas and symphonies for piano duet filled the catalogues, and a symphony reached most of its hearers first as a four hand arrangement in a parlour.

Copyright, or its absence, shaped what composers earned and what they wrote. In a Europe of separate states and weak international protection, a successful work could be reprinted across a border without payment, and Beethoven's furious negotiations with publishers in several countries at once, offering the same work in different forms to different firms, were a rational defence against a system that let value leak. Publishers also shaped posterity directly. The collected edition, the Denkmal and the complete works series of the later nineteenth century, chose which composers received monuments in print. The Bach Gesellschaft edition, issued between 1851 and 1900, put Bach's surviving output into libraries in fifty volumes and made the modern study of his music possible; similar editions for Handel, Mozart and Beethoven followed, funded by subscription and national pride. A composer without an edition was a composer scholars could not easily read, and therefore could not easily teach, perform or canonise.

For a scholar or student assembling a growing shelf of editions and reference scores into a private research collection, keeping that material organised matters as much as owning it, and a lecture or recital deck built from such a collection can be checked quickly in a browser through the [ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which opens presentation files without installed software. The market's final effect was on form itself. Music written for sale to amateurs tends toward the performable, the moderately difficult, the excerptible; music written for a patron or a state institution could ignore the parlour entirely. The nineteenth century repertory balances on that tension, and the balance explains why the same composer might write a ferociously difficult concerto for himself to perform and a set of easy dances for the trade in the same season.

## The virtuoso and the touring economy

The virtuoso is the patronage economy's opposite number: a performer whose income comes not from a post but from a public that pays to witness difficulty overcome. The type is older than the word. Baroque courts marvelled at singers and violinists of freakish accomplishment, but the touring economy belongs to the nineteenth century, when railways, printed publicity and the public concert let a single performer sell the same astonishment in forty cities. Niccolo Paganini set the template in the 1820s and 1830s, touring Europe with a technique so far beyond his contemporaries that rumours of supernatural assistance circulated as free advertising; he managed his own fees, his own programmes and his own legend, and retired wealthy, an outcome no salaried Kapellmeister could match. His compositions, concertos and the twenty four Caprices for solo violin published in 1820, were written as extensions of his own hands, and they set technical benchmarks that violinists measure themselves against as a matter of professional routine.

Franz Liszt industrialised the model at the piano. His tours of the 1840s invented much of the modern recital: the solo piano evening, the programme mixing his own works with transcriptions of operas and symphonies, performance from memory, the instrument placed so the audience could see the player's profile and hands. Lisztomania, the word Heinrich Heine coined for the frenzy of his audiences, was a market phenomenon as much as an artistic one, complete with merchandise in the form of portraits, gloves and locks of hair pursued by admirers. Clara Schumann, whose touring career sustained her family across six decades, shows the economy's other face: a virtuoso whose playing was noted for depth rather than display, who programmed Bach, Beethoven and her husband's music when display pieces would have paid better, and whose diaries record the railway timetables, fees and illnesses of a working life on tour with a plainness that corrects the glamour.

The touring economy fed back into composition. Concertos became vehicles designed around a soloist's brand; cadenzas, once improvised, were written out as fixed displays; and a two tier repertory emerged, works the virtuoso carried and works orchestras played without one. It also created the star interpreter as a canon forming force, since a touring virtuoso's repertory choices taught audiences across a continent which names mattered, a function the ranking of the [greatest pianists of all time](/2025/05/07/greatest-pianists-of-all-time/) examines from the performer's side. The economy never disappeared; it merged with recording in the twentieth century, when a performer's tours sold records and records sold tours, and the balance between the two became the standard arithmetic of a performing career.

## The modernist rupture and the fracture of tonality

### Why did tonality break apart?

Tonality broke apart because composers pushed its own logic of chromatic tension further than the system of resolution could comfortably contain, while new scales, folk sources and a deliberate search for fresh sound offered exits. The fracture was a process across decades, driven from inside the tradition, not a single act of rebellion.

By 1900 functional harmony carried a load it had not been designed for. Wagner's Tristan und Isolde of 1865 had built an entire drama on longing that harmony expressed by postponing resolution, chord leaning on chord without the relief of a settled tonic. Liszt's late piano pieces thinned harmony to ambiguity; Debussy, drawing on whole tone and modal scales and on the Javanese gamelan he heard at the Paris Exposition of 1889, composed in successions of sonorities valued for colour rather than direction. Richard Strauss drove orchestral harmony to the edge of intelligibility in Salome of 1905 and Elektra of 1909, works whose dissonance served dramatic extremity with complete technical control. Arnold Schoenberg, who had begun as a Brahms and Wagner heir, concluded in his Harmonielehre of 1911 that the distinction between consonance and dissonance was one of degree, and his works of 1908 and 1909 dispensed with a home key altogether, in what he later called the emancipation of the dissonance.

The rupture was therefore a family quarrel, not an invasion. Schoenberg's twelve note method, formulated in the early 1920s, was an attempt to restore order after the fracture, a new grammar for a music that had lost its old one, and he presented it as the continuation of the German tradition rather than its execution. Igor Stravinsky took a different exit: The Rite of Spring of 1913 kept pitch organisation that listeners could follow while revolutionising rhythm, accent and orchestral sound, and his later neoclassicism rebuilt compositions on eighteenth century models with twentieth century harmony. Charles Ives, working in America outside the European institutions, arrived at polytonality and collage by his own road and was largely unheard until mid century. Alex Ross structures The Rest Is Noise around this plurality, treating the twentieth century not as one road away from tonality but as many musics competing in one fractured landscape. His framing fits the documentary record better than any single line of progress: tonality did not die, since film scores, musical theatre and much concert music kept it, but it lost its monopoly, and with the monopoly went the shared grammar that had let a listener in 1800 follow a new symphony at first hearing.

For the tradition as a whole the rupture changed the audience's contract. The public concert and the conservatory had been built on a common language; modernism asked listeners to learn new languages piece by piece, and a lasting split opened between the repertory institutions would programme and the music living composers wrote. That split, between a museum of masterworks and a present that had seceded from it, is the condition the canon sections now examine, because the canon is where the split became permanent policy.

## What recording did to the standard repertoire

### Which classical works are performed most often?

The most frequently performed works cluster in a narrow band: Beethoven's symphonies and piano concertos, Mozart's late symphonies and operas, Bach's Brandenburg Concertos, Handel's Messiah, Vivaldi's Four Seasons, Tchaikovsky's symphonies and ballets, and Rachmaninoff's piano concertos lead orchestral programmes, because institutions programme music that audiences recognise and players already know deeply well.

Recording arrived as a novelty and reorganised the tradition around permanence. The phonograph's early decades, from the 1890s to electrical recording in 1925, captured the period's performers in fragments: Enrico Caruso's records from 1902 onward proved that a voice could sell a machine, and the three minute side taught a generation to know operas as detachable arias. The long playing record of 1948 changed the unit of listening from the excerpt to the work, and the symphony, the string quartet and the complete opera entered the home as repeatable objects. A listener could now hear a Mahler symphony ten times in a month, a familiarity no concert life could supply, and study a score against a performance at will. The effect on standards was profound: recording created the idea of the definitive performance, and conductors and soloists became comparable across decades because their work survived beside their rivals'.

The repertory effect cut both ways, and the evidence supports both readings because both happened. Recording froze a core. Record companies, like opera houses before them, balanced risk with proven titles, and the catalogue filled with multiple versions of the same hundred works while thousands of performable pieces went unrecorded and therefore unheard, since a work no orchestra programmes and no label records effectively leaves the tradition's working memory. At the same time recording widened the archive beyond any concert hall's reach. The complete Bach cantatas, the operas of Handel, the symphonies of forgotten contemporaries and the early music repertory all became available through recording projects that no season of concerts could have financed, and the early music movement's period instrument performances spread through records before they filled halls. Which effect dominates depends on the measure: by sales and by programming share the core narrowed into a standard repertoire of remarkable stability, while by availability the recorded archive became the largest library of performed music the tradition has ever possessed.

Recording also changed who counts as a great musician. Performers joined composers in the canon, their interpretations preserved as documents, and a conductor's or pianist's recorded legacy became an argument that outlived the memory of any single evening. The standard repertoire that resulted, the narrow band of works every orchestra plays and every label reissues, is a joint creation of the concert hall's economics and the record catalogue's, and it sets the problem the next sections address directly: how a small repertory came to stand for a thousand years, who assembled it, and who was left outside the door.


## How the canon was assembled and by whom

The classical canon, the settled list of masterworks and masters that programming assumes, was assembled during the nineteenth century by identifiable people working through identifiable institutions. It was not discovered, in the way a coastline is discovered, and it was not decreed by a single authority. It accumulated. The first mechanism was the repeat performance. As public concerts multiplied, promoters learned that dead composers outsold living ones, because a known name reduced risk, and by the middle of the century the programmes of the Leipzig Gewandhaus, the Vienna Gesellschaft concerts and the London Philharmonic Society were dominated by works their composers could no longer conduct. The museum culture of music, an art that had lived on novelty for a thousand years, was a nineteenth century invention, and historians of concert life have documented the shift season by season in programme archives.

The second mechanism was print. The collected editions described earlier gave scholars and performers reliable texts; biographies gave the texts a story. The nineteenth century invented the composer biography as a popular genre, and its narratives, the suffering Beethoven, the angelic Mozart, the demonic Paganini, attached themselves to the works and taught audiences how to listen to them. The third mechanism was the conservatory curriculum, which fixed the ladder of study. The fourth was criticism and history writing itself: E. T. A. Hoffmann's reviews of Beethoven from 1810 onward established the rhetoric of the masterwork, and the first general histories of music organised the past as a road leading to the canon's door. Richard Taruskin argues across his Oxford History that the canon is best understood as a repertory of works a culture decided to keep performing for reasons that mix the musical with the institutional, and that pretending otherwise, pretending the list simply reflects timeless quality sorted by time, conceals the decisions that made it. Readers who want the ranking question argued on its own ground can find it treated directly in the article on the [greatest composers of all time](/2025/04/07/greatest-composers-of-all-time/), which takes up the comparative case that this pillar deliberately sets aside.

Two corrections protect the story from its own myths. The first concerns Bach. The popular account has his music forgotten until Mendelssohn's 1829 performance rescued it, and the account is wrong in the way the opening of this article noted: professionals studied Bach continuously, Mozart and Beethoven learned from his scores, the Well Tempered Clavier circulated in manuscript among keyboard players as a matter of course, and printed editions of his keyboard works appeared from the first years of the nineteenth century. What Mendelssohn revived was the public performance of the large choral works, a genuine event that later retelling inflated into a resurrection. The second correction concerns Handel, whose English afterlife ran the opposite course: never dropped from performance, his oratorios were monumentalised by giant festivals, the Crystal Palace Handel festivals of the later nineteenth century fielding choirs in the thousands, until the scale itself became a reason to restore smaller forces. The canon, in short, was built by promoters, publishers, teachers, biographers and festival committees, each acting for reasons that made sense locally, and the list they built bears the marks of every one of those reasons.

Assembly also had a geography, and the map it produced was narrower than the music Europe actually contained. The canon was edited in Leipzig, Vienna, Paris and London, and it privileged the repertories those cities performed: the Austro German symphonic line from Haydn to Brahms was installed as the tradition's main road, while Italian opera, French music and the whole Iberian and Scandinavian output were filed as side roads of local colour. Composers internalised the map and wrote toward it, which is one reason a Czech or a Russian symphonist of the nineteenth century worked so hard to be performed in Vienna and Leipzig; a premiere there was a certificate the home institutions could not issue. The main road was a real achievement and a real distortion at once, and the national movements described earlier in this article were, among other things, protests against its tolls.

## Who was excluded from the canon and why

A canon assembled by institutions excludes as institutions do: not usually by verdict on individual works, but by controlling access to the training, the posts, the publishers and the platforms from which works enter circulation. The strongest form of the aesthetic merit argument deserves to be stated first, at its full strength. Its advocates, among them Charles Rosen in his critical writings, argue that the canon is a record of survival under repeated testing: works were performed, studied and compared across generations by musicians free to drop them, and the ones that remain are the ones that repaid the closest attention with the richest substance. On this account, exclusion needs no conspiracy and no sociology; it is what the disappearance of thousands of competent, performed and then forgotten works by well placed male contemporaries already proves. Most music fails the test of time, and the canon is the residue of a severe and largely honest filtration.

The strongest form of the institutional access argument answers at the same level. Its advocates, among them the musicologist Marcia Citron, whose Gender and the Musical Canon of 1993 gave the argument its fullest scholarly form, hold that filtration can only act on what reaches it. A composer denied advanced training, denied a post with an orchestra or a choir, denied a publisher's engraving budget and denied performance in the halls where reputations were tested did not fail the test; the test was never administered. On this account the canon accurately records the quality of the music that institutions circulated, and tells us almost nothing about the music they never let circulate, so that survival is evidence about the pipeline as much as about the works. Each argument identifies a real mechanism. Filtration demonstrably destroyed the reputations of the well connected; access demonstrably decided who could be filtered at all.

The question is therefore about documentation before it is about judgment. Exclusion operated at several depths. At the shallowest, works were written, performed and then unpublished, their manuscripts scattered at a composer's death. Deeper, works were written and never performed, because no institution would programme them. Deepest, the training itself was refused, so the works were never written at all, and no archive can recover a symphony that its composer was never taught to score. What would have to be true for the merit argument to settle the matter is evidence that access was broadly open and filtration alone did the sorting; the record of admissions, posts and publishing shows it was not. What would have to be true for the access argument to settle it is a surviving body of excluded work equal in craft to the canon and demonstrably suppressed; some such bodies exist, as the next section shows, and many are lost beyond recovery. Between those two unmet conditions the historical judgment has to rest, and this article rests it there rather than awarding a verdict the evidence cannot carry.

## Women composers and the documentary record

The documentary record for women composers is a record of institutions making exceptions and then forgetting they had made them. The exceptions are early and distinguished. Hildegard of Bingen, a twelfth century abbess, composed a large cycle of chants and the Ordo Virtutum, a sung morality play that stands among the earliest surviving music dramas, and her works survived because her community copied and kept them: the institution, for once, was hers. Barbara Strozzi, a seventeenth century Venetian singer, published eight volumes of music between 1644 and 1664, more cantatas in print than most male contemporaries managed, working the one channel open to her, the commercial press and the private academy. Francesca Caccini served the Medici court as a composer and performer and wrote the earliest surviving opera by a woman, La liberazione di Ruggiero, performed in 1625. Each case shows the same pattern: where a woman held a protected position, a convent, a court, a family firm, the work exists. Where she depended on the open institutions, the chapel posts, the Kapellmeister tracks, the conservatory composition classes, the doors were closed and the record stops.

The nineteenth century multiplied both the output and the obstruction. Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel composed more than four hundred works, including a piano cycle, Das Jahr, of a scale and ambition her brother Felix's published output rarely attempts, yet her family held that publication was improper for a woman of her station, and a handful of her pieces appeared under Felix's name in his own collections. Clara Schumann composed a piano concerto performed at the Gewandhaus in 1835, when she was sixteen, and then watched the century's expectations redirect her genius into performance and teaching; her diaries record her own internalised verdict that a woman must not desire to compose, which is the sound of an institution speaking through its subject. Louise Farrenc, professor of piano at the Paris Conservatoire from 1842, fought the institution from inside, winning equal pay for her post after a documented campaign, and composed symphonies and chamber works that Paris heard and then shelved. In England, Ethel Smyth forced her operas onto German and then London stages against resistance she chronicled with unusual frankness in her memoirs, and her Mass in D filled the Albert Hall in 1893.

The recovery of this record since the 1970s has been archival work: catalogues, editions, recordings and dissertations establishing what was written before arguing about what it is worth. That order of operations is the correct one, and the record as it stands already supports one firm conclusion. The scarcity of women in the standard canon tracks the scarcity of women in the posts, the classes and the publishing contracts, with a precision that no theory of innate distribution of talent has ever explained, while the works that survive from the protected enclaves show no corresponding gap in craft. The question the evidence leaves open is quantitative and unrecoverable: how much was never written. A tradition that defines itself by its written archive should be the first to admit what an archive cannot show.

## Composers outside Europe inside the Western tradition

The Western tradition was written in Europe, but it was never only European in its personnel or its geography, and the composers who worked inside it from outside expose how its institutions actually behaved. Joseph Bologne, the Chevalier de Saint Georges, born in Guadeloupe in 1745 to a French planter and an enslaved African mother, became one of the finest violinists and conductors in Paris, led the orchestra that commissioned Haydn's Paris symphonies, and composed violin concertos and operas that Parisian audiences applauded. His career shows both the possibility and the ceiling: talent and patronage carried him to the centre of the institution, while his birth kept him from the opera directorship for which he was proposed, in a documented episode in which leading singers petitioned against serving under him. His music then fell out of the repertory for reasons that track the same line.

The Americas built the tradition's institutions on new ground and produced composers the European record treated as provincial. William Billings of Boston published the first collection of music by an American born composer in 1770 and wrote fusing tunes that American singing schools kept alive by oral transmission when print culture moved on. In Brazil, Jose Mauricio Nunes Garcia, a priest and composer of mixed ancestry, directed music at the Rio de Janeiro court from 1808 and composed hundreds of sacred works in the classical language, a complete parallel Kapellmeister career conducted at a distance from every European centre. In the United States, the nineteenth century's conservatory system slowly opened: Florence Price, trained at the New England Conservatory, had her First Symphony performed by the Chicago Symphony Orchestra in 1933, the first symphony by an African American woman played by a major American orchestra, and her manuscripts then spent decades in an abandoned house in Illinois before their recovery in 2009 set editions and performances in motion.

These cases complicate any simple map of centre and margin. Bologne worked at the centre and was marginalised inside it; Nunes Garcia worked at a distance and built a centre of his own; Price worked inside the credentialing system and was filtered out by its programming habits after her death, until an accident of real estate returned her archive to view. The mechanism is the same one this article has followed throughout: institutions decide what is taught, printed and repeated, and the map of the canon is the map of those decisions. What distinguishes the composers of this section is that their documentation survived against the gradient, in family hands, in church archives, in an empty house, which is evidence about the archive rather than about the size of the company they keep. The tradition they joined was genuinely shared; the memory of it was not shared equally, and the repair of that memory is documentary work, edition by edition and performance by performance, of exactly the kind the recovery of women composers required.


## Where Bach sits in the tradition

Johann Sebastian Bach occupies a position no other composer shares: he is the tradition's chief example of a summit reached inside the old system at the moment the new one was replacing it. His career ran through the patronage economy in its classic forms, court posts at Weimar and Cothen and the municipal church post at Leipzig, and his output was fitted to those posts with a craftsman's exactness: cantatas for the Leipzig Sundays, keyboard suites for pupils and patrons, concertos for the Cothen court orchestra. In his lifetime his reputation rested on his organ playing and his learned counterpoint, and younger composers already found his style old fashioned beside the lighter galant manner then in fashion. He placed himself knowingly at the end of a line: the Art of Fugue, left unfinished at his death in 1750, is a systematic exploration of one theme's contrapuntal possibilities that reads as a summation of the polyphonic centuries.

His afterlife inverted his lifetime standing, by the mechanisms this article has described. His sons and pupils carried his keyboard works into the new era as teaching material, so the Well Tempered Clavier became the pianist's and the composition student's daily bread; Mozart encountered his fugues in Baron van Swieten's Vienna circle in the 1780s and rewrote his own counterpoint in consequence; Beethoven studied him; the collected edition of the nineteenth century made the whole output legible; and the 1829 Berlin performance of the St Matthew Passion, prepared by a young Mendelssohn whose family had treasured the score, announced the large choral works to the public concert world. The full account of the life behind that position is given in the article on [Johann Sebastian Bach's life and career](/2010/08/07/johann-sebastian-bach-life-and-career/). Christoph Wolff's biography, Johann Sebastian Bach: The Learned Musician, published in 2000, consolidated the modern documentary picture, presenting Bach as a scholar composer whose learning was a professional programme rather than a temperament. In the settled judgment of the tradition, Bach is the proof that the service economy could produce the deepest art: the cantatas were weekly work, and the weekly work became the standard by which counterpoint itself is judged.

## Where Mozart sits in the tradition

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart is the tradition's test case for the hinge between service and the market. Trained inside the court system as a child prodigy exhibited by his father Leopold across the courts of Europe, employed unhappily by the Archbishop of Salzburg, he broke for Vienna in 1781 and spent his final decade as the first great composer to live by the mixed freelance economy of concerts, pupils, commissions and publishing. His position in the tradition rests on a completeness no other figure matches in quite the same way: he mastered every genre the period offered, opera, symphony, concerto, quartet, mass, solo sonata, and left in each a group of works against which the genre is still measured. The piano concertos of the Viennese years were written as his own performing vehicles and turned a display genre into symphonic drama; the operas with Lorenzo Da Ponte, The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni and Cosi fan tutte, made ensemble singing carry psychology that spoken theatre envied.

His afterlife was the fastest canonisation in the tradition's history, and the most distorted by anecdote. The Requiem, left unfinished at his death in 1791 and completed by his pupil Franz Xaver Sussmayr, attracted legends of a mysterious commissioning messenger that the documents reduce to a widowed count's vanity project; the early death at thirty five and the pauper's grave of popular imagination, in reality a standard Viennese communal burial, built the myth of the neglected genius that the romantic century required. The documentary record shows something more useful: a professional whose earnings in good years were substantial, whose difficulties were those of a freelancer in a volatile market, and whose widow Constanze managed his posthumous reputation and the publication of his works with a business sense the myth never credits. The life is told in full in the article on [Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart's life and career](/2010/10/21/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-life-and-career/). Mozart's settled position is as the classical style's most complete embodiment, the composer in whom the standardised language speaks with the least friction and the greatest range, which is why every later argument about the style begins by deciding what to do with him.

## Where Beethoven sits in the tradition

Ludwig van Beethoven is the hinge figure made flesh, the composer whose career divides the tradition's social history and whose works divide its stylistic one. He arrived in Vienna in 1792 as the patronage system was weakening, kept the system's benefits by securing the annuity of 1809, and added the market's benefits by driving hard bargains with publishers across Europe, so that his income, though contested and litigated, rested on both economies at once. The deafness that closed over him from the turn of the century, documented in his conversation books and in the Heiligenstadt Testament, removed him from the performing career that had launched him and concentrated his reputation in the written work, an accident of biography that the romantic century read as destiny and turned into the template of the composer as suffering individual described earlier in this article.

The works enforce the position. The nine symphonies trace an arc from the classical inheritance, the First and Second still speaking Haydn's and Mozart's language, through the Eroica's expansion of scale and argument, to the Ninth's introduction of voices into the symphony in 1824, an act that reset the genre's ambitions for a century. The thirty two piano sonatas and the sixteen string quartets became the tradition's twin testaments, the quartets above all dividing into the early sets that master the classical conversation, the middle sets that dramatise it, and the late quartets that contemporaries found unintelligible and posterity enthroned as the summit of the literature. Reception followed the pattern: bewilderment, advocacy by committed performers and critics, curricular adoption, canonisation. The full narrative belongs to the article on [Ludwig van Beethoven's life and career](/2011/01/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-life-and-career/). Beethoven's settled position is double. Musically, he is the composer who proved the standardised forms could bear unlimited weight, and therefore the licence for everything the nineteenth century attempted. Socially, he is the proof that a composer could live as an independent artist answerable to purchasers rather than to a master, and every conservatory student who has since aspired to that life has lived, knowingly or not, inside his settlement.

## Where Chopin sits in the tradition

Frederic Chopin holds the narrowest and most concentrated position of any major figure in this article: a composer who wrote almost entirely for one instrument, in small and medium forms, for a public of salons and pupils rather than of halls, and who nonetheless altered the technique and the expressive range of that instrument permanently. His career was built in Paris from 1831, inside the aristocratic and banking salons, sustained by teaching fees from wealthy pupils and by publishers in Paris, Leipzig and London who issued his works in parallel editions. He performed rarely in public and disliked the large hall, yet his influence travelled through the piano itself: every pianist who followed had to pass through his writing, its new figurations, its pedalling, its marriage of Italian operatic singing line to keyboard texture.

His position in the tradition is defined by a double debt and a double gift. The debts run to John Field, whose nocturnes gave him a genre, and to the bel canto opera of Vincenzo Bellini, whose long breathed melody he translated into piano cantabile; the gifts run forward to Liszt, who carried the idiom to the concert hall, to the French school that led to Debussy, who edited his works and acknowledged the ancestry, and to the whole conception of the piano as a singing instrument rather than a percussive one. The documentary record of his life has its own hazards, since Franz Liszt's early biography of Chopin, written as an act of homage by a rival pianist, coloured the portrait with a poetising varnish that later scholarship has had to scrape away, which is why this article treats Liszt on Chopin as a subject to be examined rather than as a source to be followed. The life itself is told in the article on [Frederic Chopin's life and career](/2011/03/21/frederic-chopin-life-and-career/). Chopin's settled position refutes, by counterexample, the assumption that historical weight requires large forms and public genres. He proved that a composer could work at the scale of the room and the single instrument and still change what every successor had to learn, a proof the tradition has needed for every miniaturist since.

## Where Tchaikovsky sits in the tradition

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky is the tradition's clearest case of a reputation fought over by the institutions that made it. Trained at the St Petersburg Conservatory, employed as a professor at Moscow, freed to compose by the patronage of Nadezhda von Meck, whose annuity from 1877 supported him for thirteen years on the single condition that they never meet, he stood at the crossing of every system this article has described: conservatory and anti conservatory nationalism, private patronage and the publishing market, Russian subject matter and European form. The Five distrusted his conservatory grammar; the conservatory distrusted his emotional directness; Western critics of the following century condescended to the symphonies as hysterical while audiences made them the most performed in the repertory, a split between critical and public verdicts that his case shares with no composer so completely.

The works that hold his position are the last three symphonies, the First Piano Concerto, the Violin Concerto, the operas Eugene Onegin and The Queen of Spades, and the three ballets, Swan Lake, The Sleeping Beauty and The Nutcracker, which between them rebuilt ballet music from functional accompaniment into symphonic composition that choreographers serve rather than use. His documentary record carries a warning of the kind this series flags wherever it occurs: the Soviet editions of his letters expurgated passages bearing on his private life, and the standard account of his final days was shaped for decades by an official narrative, so the letters and the death must be read as subjects under custodianship rather than as transparent sources. The life is treated in full in the article on [Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky's life and career](/2011/06/07/pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky-life-and-career/). His settled position is the symphonist of the personal utterance inside classical form, and the standing demonstration that a composer's standing can be carried for a century by performers and audiences against the settled opinion of critics, until the critics, as they have largely done, come round.

The custodianship point generalises beyond one biography. Every figure in this pillar reached posterity through an editor, a widow, a pupil or a state publishing house with interests of its own, and the strength of Tchaikovsky's position is that the filters are documented well enough to be corrected for. His music survived its custodians because performers and audiences kept the works in circulation while the texts were being managed, a reminder that the performed tradition and the printed record are separate archives that check each other. Where the two archives agree, history can be written with confidence; where they part, as in his case, the disagreement itself is the most instructive document, and the honest article reports the seam rather than stitching it shut.

## What the tradition is still arguing about

The argument that runs under the whole history, and that no generation has closed, concerns what the tradition is for. One account, given its sharpest form by Richard Taruskin in essays collected across his career, holds that the canon functions as a museum whose real purpose is the cultivation of a literate public's taste, and that its health should be judged by the honesty with which it admits its own constructedness and keeps admitting new claims. An opposing account, voiced by performers and scholars such as Charles Rosen when he defends the repertory's core, holds that the canon is a working library whose concentration is its virtue, since depth of shared knowledge, many musicians knowing the same works intimately, is what allows performance and criticism to be arts of interpretation rather than of perpetual introduction. What would have to be true for the first account to prevail is a programming culture that rotates the archive widely without losing the audience that funds it; what would have to be true for the second is evidence that concentration deepens rather than narrows the art, and the record of the last century supplies examples to both sides, which is why the argument continues.

Two further disputes remain structurally open. The performance dispute asks how the music should sound: the period instrument movement, armed with documentary research into tempo, pitch, vibrato and articulation, demonstrated that the standard modern performance of Bach or Mozart is a romantic inheritance rather than a neutral default, while its critics answer that instruments and techniques changed because musicians wanted what the changes gave. The authorship dispute asks what a work is: whether the score fixes the music and performance serves it, or whether the music lives in a practice that scores only partly capture, a question the oral transmission sections of this article show to be as old as notation itself. Jan Swafford's listener's guides return repeatedly to the practical form of the puzzle: a newcomer asks where to begin, and every honest answer smuggles in a position on all three disputes.

The tradition's own history suggests the arguments are not a symptom of decline but the mechanism of its survival. A repertory that is argued about is taught, programmed, published and recorded; the works that dropped out of the tradition are not the ones that lost arguments but the ones about which no argument was ever started. The sequence of problems with which this article began has not ended, because each solution redefined the conditions for the next generation: notation created the work, patronage created the professional, the market created the public, the conservatory created the curriculum, recording created the archive, and exclusion created the standing question of whose problems the tradition has never yet been asked to solve. The next solutions will come, as the earlier ones did, from musicians working inside institutions they did not design, on problems they did not choose, leaving results their successors will mistake for inevitabilities until the documents are read again.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is classical music and when did it begin?

Classical music is the Western art music tradition defined by written composition: music fixed in notation by trained composers, preserved by institutions and performed by specialists. The label is used broadly for the whole tradition and narrowly for the period from about 1750 to 1820, and confusing the two senses causes much muddle. As a documented practice it begins with the chant repertory of the medieval Church and the first written polyphony of the ninth to the thirteenth centuries, when notation made music storable and teachable across distance. It has no single birthday, because it grew by accumulation: chant, then polyphony, then the mass, the opera, the concerto and the symphony, each layer built by institutions that paid musicians to write.

### Q: What are the main periods of classical music?

The conventional periods are medieval, Renaissance, baroque, classical, romantic and modern, and they are historians' conveniences rather than events. Medieval music, to about 1450, covers chant and early polyphony. The Renaissance, about 1450 to 1600, is the age of imitative polyphony and the mass. The baroque, about 1600 to 1750, brought opera, the concerto and functional harmony. The classical period, about 1750 to 1820, standardised the symphony, the quartet and sonata form. The romantic nineteenth century expanded the orchestra, the forms and the composer's individual role. The modern period, from the early twentieth century, fractured the shared tonal language into many. Composers at every boundary mixed old and new, so the labels organise a library better than they describe a life.

### Q: How did composers earn a living before public concerts?

Before ticketed public concerts became common in the later eighteenth century, composers earned their living from posts and patrons. Churches employed cantors, organists and chapel masters on salary; courts kept Kapellmeisters and orchestras as marks of rank; aristocratic households engaged musicians as servants with lodging, livery and fees. Around employment ran a second economy of individual commissions, dedications offered in hope of gifts, teaching income from private pupils, and payments from publishers, though publishing paid irregularly and piracy was common. Bach's income at Leipzig combined a municipal salary, teaching and fees for weddings and funerals. Haydn's security at Esterhaza was a servant's contract of unusual completeness. The freelance career, living by concerts and sales, only became possible when publishers, public halls and a paying audience existed to support it.

### Q: What did staff notation make possible that memory could not?

Staff notation made music independent of any individual memory. A melody held by memory changes as it passes from singer to singer and dies with its last carrier; a melody fixed on a staff can be read by a stranger in another city, corrected against the composer's text, taught to a whole choir at once and stored for centuries. Notation also made complexity possible: several independent voices moving in calculated rhythm against one another cannot be composed, rehearsed or revised in the head alone. It created the musical work as a durable object that could be owned, sold, borrowed, criticised and revived, and it let composers plan large structures, a mass, an opera, a symphony, whose spans exceed what any performer holds in mind unaided. Memory remained essential to performance, but composition became a literate art.

### Q: What is the difference between baroque and classical style?

The difference lies in texture, phrase and harmonic behaviour. Baroque music, roughly 1600 to 1750, is built on the basso continuo, a running bass line with improvised harmony, beneath voices and instruments of equal contrapuntal energy; its phrases flow in long spinning lines, its rhythms drive steadily, and a movement usually sustains one governing affection. Classical style, roughly 1750 to 1820, drops the continuo, clarifies the texture into melody and accompaniment, and builds phrases in balanced questions and answers of two, four and eight bars. Contrast becomes structural: themes oppose each other, keys argue, dynamics change by sudden steps as well as by gradual swell. Where baroque music presses forward continuously, classical music articulates, it breathes in periods, and its drama is the drama of departure and return within a form the listener can follow.

### Q: What is sonata form?

Sonata form is the standard shape of a large instrumental movement in the classical period, found in the first movements of symphonies, quartets, sonatas and concertos. It has three great parts. The exposition presents the main themes, first in the home key and then, after a travelling transition, in a contrasting key. The development takes fragments of those themes through distant keys in passages of instability and tension. The recapitulation brings the themes back with the decisive material now stated in the home key, so the movement's argument closes, sometimes with a coda to confirm the settlement. The design makes harmony dramatic: leaving home, struggling, returning. Composers treated it as a flexible principle rather than a mould, bending, expanding and disguising it, which is why it organised a century of music without producing two identical movements.

### Q: Who standardised the symphony?

Joseph Haydn standardised the symphony across a career spent directing the Esterhazy orchestra, for which he wrote more than one hundred works in the form. From the loose inheritance of the opera overture and the orchestral suite he settled the four movement plan that became the norm: a substantial sonata form opening, a slow movement, a minuet and a fast finale, with thematic development as the engine of the long movements. Working with the same players for decades let him test every balance and proportion empirically. Mozart absorbed the standard and carried it to a new expressive finish, and Beethoven inherited it as a fully formed public genre ready to be expanded. Haydn's London symphonies of the 1790s proved the standard was portable, succeeding with audiences and players who had never worked with him, because the plan had become common musical property.

### Q: Why did composers stop working for patrons?

Composers stopped working for patrons because alternative income appeared, not because patronage was abolished in a single moment. Public concerts created ticket revenue; publishers created a market in printed music; a growing middle class created pupils and subscribers; and copyright, though weak, began to make a successful work an asset. The transition runs from the later eighteenth century into the nineteenth, and its figures are transitional men: Mozart resigned a court post to freelance in Vienna and felt both the freedom and the exposure; Beethoven kept an aristocratic annuity while selling to publishers across Europe, standing in both economies at once. Patronage also changed its character rather than vanishing, becoming the individual commission, the wealthy protector such as Nadezhda von Meck, and eventually the state subsidy and the foundation grant. The salaried servant composer died out because the market could pay more and demand differently.

### Q: What changed when concerts became public events?

Public concerts changed who music was for and therefore how it was written. A patron's chapel served a household and a liturgy; a ticketed hall served strangers who chose to attend, and a work had to seize listeners hearing it once, without ceremony to carry it. Projection, contrast and memorable themes gained value; the concerto and the symphony, public genres of display and rhetoric, rose to the centre. Programming changed too, since promoters discovered that dead composers with settled reputations sold seats more safely than living ones, and the repeat performance created a repertory where none had existed. The audience itself became an institution, with critics to guide it, programme notes to explain the works and subscription series to bind its loyalty. Composers gained a public and lost a master, exchanging one set of constraints for another.

### Q: How should a beginner start listening to classical music?

A beginner should start with short, vivid works and a small number of names, and should listen actively rather than broadly. A practical route is to begin with pieces built on clear contrasts and stories: Vivaldi's Four Seasons, Handel's Messiah choruses, a Mozart piano concerto and a Beethoven symphony such as the Fifth or the Seventh, then to hear each piece several times, since familiarity is what turns a wash of sound into an argument the ear can follow. Live performance teaches faster than recording alone, because the eye explains the orchestra's geography of sound. Reading one short guide alongside listening helps; Jan Swafford's Vintage Guide to Classical Music was written for exactly this reader. The habit that matters is repetition with attention: one work known well opens more doors than fifty works heard once.

### Q: Is Bach or Beethoven the greater composer?

The comparison is real but miscast as a contest, since the two composers solved different problems supremely. Bach's claim rests on completeness inside a system: no one has matched the union of contrapuntal mastery, structural planning and expressive range he achieved within the church and court genres of his time, and his keyboard works remain the foundation of compositional training. Beethoven's claim rests on transformation: he took the classical forms Bach's century bequeathed, expanded their scale and argumentative weight, and remade the composer's social position in the process, so that the nineteenth century's idea of music itself descends from his example. Listeners who value depth within order choose Bach; listeners who value drama and becoming choose Beethoven. Critics from Charles Rosen onward have treated the question as a way of declaring what one thinks music is, which is why it survives every attempt to settle it.

### Q: Why are so few women named in the classical canon?

The documentary record gives a structural answer. Composing for the canon's central genres required training in counterpoint and orchestration, a post with performers at hand, publication and repeated performance, and each of those gates was held against women by the institutions that controlled them. Choir schools, chapel posts and composition classes were closed or grudging; families of standing treated a daughter's publication as impropriety, as Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel's career shows; publishers and orchestras programmed almost no women. The surviving exceptions prove the mechanism, since women with protected positions, Hildegard in her convent, Barbara Strozzi at the Venetian press, Clara Schumann on the concert stage, left substantial bodies of work. Marcia Citron's Gender and the Musical Canon sets out the scholarly case: the canon records what the pipeline circulated, and the pipeline decided before any judgment of quality could be made.

### Q: How was the classical canon actually assembled?

The canon was assembled across the nineteenth century by the joint action of promoters, publishers, teachers, critics and festival committees. Concert promoters learned that dead composers outsold living ones and built programmes into a repertory. Publishers issued collected editions, Bach's between 1851 and 1900, which gave scholars reliable texts and effectively chose who could be studied. Conservatories fixed a ladder of study pieces that reproduced the list in every trained musician's fingers. Biographers popularised the lives, and critics from E. T. A. Hoffmann onward supplied the rhetoric of the masterwork. No single body decreed the result, and no single standard of quality explains it, since the same decades dropped works by composers of proven craft whose editions, advocates or institutions failed. Richard Taruskin treats the canon as a repertory a culture decided to keep performing, for reasons musical and institutional at once.

### Q: What is program music?

Program music is instrumental music that sets out to represent something outside itself: a story, a character, a landscape, an episode or a sequence of feelings attached to a narrative. The composer usually supplies the programme in words, as Berlioz did for the Symphonie fantastique of 1830, whose printed story of an artist's obsessive love the audience was expected to follow movement by movement. Liszt's symphonic poems shaped single movements after poems and heroes, and Richard Strauss carried depiction to virtuoso extremes in works such as Ein Heldenleben. Program music stands opposite the doctrine of absolute music, defended by the critic Eduard Hanslick, which holds that music's content is formed sound and nothing else. Most composers occupied ground between the poles, using a programme as scaffolding for structures that also satisfy purely musical logic.

### Q: Should a symphony be heard in one sitting?

A symphony should be heard in one sitting whenever possible, because its form is an argument across movements. The opening movement sets out themes and keys whose meanings complete themselves only in the finale; the slow movement and the dance movement are placed as contrasts in a planned sequence of weights and speeds; and many symphonies quote or transform earlier material later on, effects that vanish when the movements are separated. A single sitting also matches the conditions the music was written for, the concert evening, and the recorded performance preserves that span. There is no rule against hearing a movement alone, and a middle movement excerpted can still give pleasure, but the experience is that of a chapter read out of sequence. New listeners gain most by choosing a shorter symphony, a Haydn or Mozart work of twenty five minutes, and keeping the whole arc intact.

### Q: How do I read a concert programme?

Read a concert programme in layers. Begin with the running order and the timings, which tell you the evening's shape and where the interval falls. For each work, note the composer, the dates, the movements and any tempo markings printed, since the movement plan is the map you will follow by ear; a movement list lets you count where you are in the form. Then read the programme note selectively: the first paragraph usually gives the work's circumstances, and the descriptive passages name themes and events to listen for, which are more useful before the performance than after. Biographies of the performers can be left for the interval. Practical habits help as well: check whether the hall asks for silence between movements, keep the programme on your lap rather than rustling it at quiet moments, and save your verdicts for the applause.

### Q: What did nationalism do to classical composition?

Nationalism turned composition into a project of cultural state building across the nineteenth century. Composers collected folk songs before industrial change erased them, founded national conservatories and opera houses, wrote operas on national legends in the national language and built styles from folk rhythm and melody. Smetana and Dvorak created a Czech school, Grieg a Norwegian lyric idiom, Sibelius a Finnish symphonic voice, and the Russian Five set folk sources against imported German models. The movement widened the tradition's map permanently and carried folk material of great beauty into the written repertory. It also taught audiences to hear music as the property of peoples and gave states a cultural instrument with uses the twentieth century would darken. Its deepest effect was institutional: any society that built the machinery, a conservatory, a publisher, an opera company, could grow the tradition in new soil.

### Q: How did recording change which pieces get played?

Recording changed the repertory in two directions at once. It narrowed the working core, because record companies, like concert promoters, balanced risk with proven titles, and the catalogue filled with competing versions of a standard hundred works while thousands of performable pieces went unrecorded and slipped from the tradition's working memory. It widened the archive beyond any hall's capacity, because recording projects could finance what no season could: the complete Bach cantatas, Handel's operas, early music on period instruments and the works of forgotten contemporaries all became available to any listener with a record player. Recording also created the definitive performance as an idea, making conductors and soloists comparable across decades and adding performers to the canon beside composers. The standard repertoire heard in halls is the joint product of programming economics and catalogue economics, each reinforcing the other's caution and the other's reach.

### Q: Which composer is best to study first?

The best first composer to study is the one whose problems are easiest to hear being solved, and for most students that is Haydn or Mozart. Haydn teaches form: his quartets and symphonies show themes being stated, fragmented and recombined with a clarity that rewards score study, and his career demonstrates how the classical style was standardised. Mozart teaches completeness, the same language spoken in opera, concerto, symphony and chamber music at the highest level, so that one study opens every genre. A student of counterpoint should begin with Bach instead, and a pianist with Chopin, since the first study should match the student's instrument and questions. J. Peter Burkholder's A History of Western Music, the standard survey text, supports any of these routes with listening guides. What matters is sequence and depth: one composer studied properly teaches the method for studying all the rest.

### Q: How do I follow a score while listening?

Following a score while listening is a skill built in stages. Start with a piano reduction or a miniature score of a work you already know by ear, ideally a Haydn quartet or a Mozart symphony, and begin by tracking only the broadest landmarks: find the bar numbers, the rehearsal letters and the double bars that mark the form's joints, and keep your place by movement and page rather than by note. Next, follow one line only, the first violins or the melody, letting the rest wash past, and mark where the main themes return. With practice the eye starts to group notation into phrases and to read harmony from the bass line. Using a recording with pause and repeat is legitimate training, since the aim is to connect symbol and sound. Fluency arrives slowly, but even partial score following transforms listening, because the form becomes something you can see happening.
