## Where Does Kermode Place the Play in Shakespeare's Development?

Frank Kermode's *Shakespeare's Language*, published in 2000, places Romeo and Juliet firmly among the early works: a play of the middle 1590s, written before the great change that Kermode detects in Shakespeare's verse around 1600, and therefore a supreme example of the manner that change displaced. In the early manner, as the critic describes it, verse is patterned before it is pressured. Lines tend to complete themselves, rhymes arrive on schedule, speeches are built as set pieces that an audience can admire as feats, and the characteristic figure is antithesis: love against hate, light against dark, youth against age, each term balanced against its opposite in symmetrical clauses. The lovers' first speeches could be lifted from the action and read as poems, because in a strict sense they are poems, sonnets and conceits inserted into a play that has not yet learned, or not yet chosen, to make its verse think.

That placing is the starting point of this article, and the verdict can be stated at once. Kermode's map is right and his ranking is wrong. The description survives every test that editors and metrists have applied to the text: the play is saturated with rhyme to a degree no later tragedy approaches, its big speeches are disproportionately formal, and its verse does move by balanced period and completed line rather than by the broken, elliptical, mid-course syntax of Hamlet or King Lear. What does not survive scrutiny is the value sign attached to the description. To call this manner early, in Kermode's scheme, is to call it preliminary, a brilliance awaiting discipline, and the play itself refutes the demotion. Romeo and Juliet is not a tragedy that happens to be written in the decorative style of its decade. It is a tragedy about that style: about young people imprisoned in received forms of speech, learning at terrible speed which forms can carry feeling and which collapse under it, while the older generation speaks nothing else. The formality Kermode files under youth is the play's subject, its mechanism, and finally its indictment.

The argument that follows proceeds in the order the evidence demands. It first establishes what Kermode wrote, where the play actually sits inside *Shakespeare's Language*, and what his developmental scheme claims. It then tests the scheme against the text scene by scene, against Harry Levin's rival account of the same formality, against Caroline Spurgeon's census of images, against the defence of the early style mounted by Russ McDonald, and against the practical verdict of the editors who print the play. The complication named in every serious discussion of Kermode, whether a developmental history of a style must undervalue whatever comes first in it, is faced directly at the end. The conclusion reached there is a split one, and it is defended throughout: keep Kermode's chronology, discard his ladder.

## Who Was Frank Kermode, and What Kind of Book Is Shakespeare's Language?

Frank Kermode (1919 to 2010) was born on the Isle of Man and became, across a career spent at Manchester, Bristol, University College London, Cambridge, Harvard, and Columbia, the most widely read British literary critic of his generation. He held the King Edward VII chair of English literature at Cambridge. He edited The Tempest for the Arden Shakespeare in 1954, an edition that stayed in print for decades, and he wrote books that reached far beyond any single field: *Romantic Image* in 1957, *The Sense of an Ending* in 1967, a study of how fictions organise time that influenced readers who had never opened a Shakespeare edition, and *The Genesis of Secrecy* in 1979. In 1965 he edited *Four Centuries of Shakespearian Criticism*, an anthology that put Harry Levin's essay on Romeo and Juliet between covers for a generation of students, a fact worth holding in reserve, because the two critics whose accounts of this play's form are compared later in this article were not strangers. Kermode had already canonised Levin's reading once, as an editor, before his own book quietly repositioned it.

The book of 2000 was a summation, and its authority rests on the half-century of Shakespeare reading behind it. Kermode came to the plays as an editor before he came to them as a theorist of style: the Arden Tempest of 1954 gave him the editor's habits of collation, glossing, and attention to the word on the page, habits that never left his criticism. His *Romantic Image* of 1957 studied the modern inheritance of the Romantic symbol, and with it the long afterlife of the image as a critical category, the very category whose counters and chartists the later book would resist. Through the 1960s and 1970s his essays on Shakespeare, on narrative, and on the endings of fictions kept returning to a single conviction, that form is where meaning hides. When the survey of *Shakespeare's Language* speaks with such economy about a whole phase of the verse, it is spending capital accumulated across fifty years of reading, editing, and reviewing, which is why its compressions carry weight even where this article overturns their conclusions.

*Shakespeare's Language* appeared in 2000, published by Farrar, Straus and Giroux in New York and by Allen Lane in London, a compact book of some 250 pages written when its author was eighty. Its declared audience was not the profession. Kermode aimed the book, as its first reviewers noted, at a non-professional audience with an interest in Shakespeare that had not, in his view, been well served by modern critics, and the book carries no scaffolding of footnotes, no bibliography wars, none of the defensive apparatus of the scholarly monograph. The conviction behind it was quoted in the Publishers Weekly notice of the book: every other aspect of Shakespeare is studied almost to death, but the fact that he was a poet has somehow dropped out of consideration. The volume is the remedy its author proposed for that neglect: a reading of the whole career through the verse alone, on the premise that the true biography of the playwright, and the only one worth caring about, is lodged in the plays.

The endorsement the publishers carried came from the poet and translator Richard Howard, who judged the book the first one should read about Shakespeare's plays, and with those plays. Sales and reprintings bore the judgement out; few works of Shakespeare criticism in the period reached so many readers outside universities. That reach matters for the present subject, because *Shakespeare's Language* is the channel through which a very large readership absorbed a particular picture of Romeo and Juliet: the picture of a gorgeous early exercise, a lyric efflorescence from the years before the real Shakespeare, the Shakespeare of Hamlet and Lear, arrived. Whether the picture is fair is precisely the question this article adjudicates, and the adjudication has to begin by being exact about what the book does and does not contain.

### What question does the book set out to answer?

The book asks how and why Shakespeare's verse changed so drastically across his career that passages in the late tragedies remain difficult after centuries of commentary, and it answers that something extraordinary happened around 1600, after which the language reached a new level of achievement and of difficulty at once.

Everything in the volume, including its brief treatment of the early plays, is organised by that question, and Romeo and Juliet enters the argument as evidence from the far side of the divide: a famous, accomplished, much-loved specimen of what the verse was like before the change, quoted to fix the baseline from which the later difficulty departs.

Two features of the book's construction govern everything that can honestly be said about its treatment of the play. The first is proportion. Roughly the first third of *Shakespeare's Language* is a general survey of the earlier work, the narrative poems and the sonnets included, hunting for signs of what was to come; the remaining two thirds give close, play-by-play studies of individual works, and those studies begin with Julius Caesar and run through Hamlet, Measure for Measure, Othello, Macbeth, King Lear, and onward to Pericles, Cymbeline, and The Tempest. Hamlet alone receives some thirty pages. Romeo and Juliet receives no chapter. The play is handled inside the opening survey, in the company of the other pre-1600 work, and its function there is emblematic rather than exhaustive. Any account that speaks of a Kermode chapter on Romeo and Juliet inflates the record; the documents show a survey treatment, brilliant and compressed, and the present article follows the record. The compression is itself a datum for the verdict, because an emblem cannot argue back, and part of what this article does is supply the argument the emblem was never given room to make.

The second feature is method. Kermode reads by ear and by syntax. His evidence is the shape of sentences: where they break, where they balance, how much subordinate machinery they carry, whether the sense arrives with the line or spills past it. He permits himself one crude instrument, the proportion of prose to verse across the career, noting that prose swells toward forty per cent in some early work and recedes as the great verse tragedies take over; but he treats counting in general with open suspicion, a stance that becomes important when Caroline Spurgeon's side of the story is reached. For Kermode, language is known in its action, in passages, or it is not known at all.

## What Changed in Shakespeare's Language Around 1600?

The hinge of Kermode's book is a single claim, stated in its most quoted sentence: the playwright became, between 1594 and 1608, a different sort of poet. Connections between early and late work remain detectable, as they do in the study of any artist, but the manner and the purpose of the activity were transformed, and the context in which the verse operates became altogether more complex and ambiguous. The dates matter. The transformation Kermode describes is not a lifetime's gradual ripening but a concentrated event, centred on the turn of the century, and he fixes its markers with unusual precision: Hamlet, written around 1600, and the strange compressed poem "The Phoenix and the Turtle", printed in 1601, stand at the gate. What comes after the gate has qualities the earlier work does not reach for. The verse grows denser and more elliptical. Syntax stops marching and starts doubling back, qualifying, interrupting itself. Speeches cease to declare and begin to think, with the thinking left visibly unfinished in parentheses, broken constructions, and metaphors that outrun their occasions. Kermode's word for the generative habits underneath the change is matrices: recurrent linguistic patterns, fundamental to the procedures of the middle and late plays, of which the most striking in Hamlet is a steady stream of doubles, words and actions paired, echoed, and inverted until doubleness itself becomes the medium of the drama.

The summary Kirkus Reviews gave the argument, quoting the book directly, runs that increasing complexity in the verse of the plays matches increasing subtlety in their construction, and the matching is the point. In the early work, as Kermode reads it, the verse and the construction are simple together: scenes are built from display speeches, feelings are announced in completed periods, and the rhetoric is explicit, doing openly on the surface what the later verse learns to do by implication and reticence. His opening comparison sets the poles. At one end stands the verbosity of Titus Andronicus, the young playwright pouring out Senecan copia; at the other, the taciturnity of Coriolanus, a hero whose refusals to speak are themselves the eloquence. Between those poles the whole career is stretched, and Romeo and Juliet is pinned, with the other work of the middle 1590s, much nearer the Titus end: not crude, Kermode never suggests crude, but explicit, patterned, and pleased to show its workings.

Three consequences follow for any play written before the gate, and all three fall on Romeo and Juliet with particular weight. First, the style is treated as a period style before it is treated as a chosen one. What the play's verse does is what verse of its moment, handled by a very gifted practitioner of the lyric mode, generally did, and the individual decisions of the dramatist recede behind the phase. Second, difficulty becomes a criterion of value by the back door. If the post-1600 verse is greater partly because it is harder, because it demands interpretation and rewards it, then the pre-1600 verse, which asks chiefly to be admired, stands convicted of a lesser ambition by definition. Third, the early play becomes useful to the argument mainly as a contrast medium, the bright flat surface against which later depth can be measured. A reader who meets Romeo and Juliet only through *Shakespeare's Language* will know exactly where the play belongs in the history of a style, and will have been given almost no reason to ask what the style is doing inside the play, for the lovers, at the speed of four days.

### Why does Kermode treat Hamlet as the turning point?

Hamlet is the turning point because it is the first play, in Kermode's reading, where the verse sustains the new procedures at full length: the doubling of words and actions, the soliloquy become an instrument of thought rather than a set speech of self-description, and a density of figure that forces interpretation rather than reception.

Earlier plays show flashes of the manner, and the survey hunts for those flashes deliberately, but in Hamlet the flashes become the weather. The tragedy of 1600 or 1601 is therefore the gate through which the career passes, and every play before it, Romeo and Juliet included, is read by the light of a standard it could not have known it was failing to meet.

That retrospective structure is the scheme's strength and its danger in one. The strength is empirical: nobody seriously disputes that the verse of King Lear behaves differently from the verse of the early comedies, and Kermode's ear for the difference is the finest of his generation. The danger is that a gate, once built, sorts everything on the far side into a single class called before. Romeo and Juliet, The Merchant of Venice, Richard II, and Much Ado About Nothing become specimens of a phase, their very different uses of patterned language flattened into one condition, the early manner. The play under discussion suffers most from the flattening, because its patterning is not generic. It is specific, systematic, and increasingly, as the action proceeds, put under a strain the play itself designs. That specificity is what the next sections recover.

## The Early Style as Kermode Describes It: Rhyme, Balance, and Display

Before the play itself is tested, the early manner needs a precise description, since the whole dispute turns on what, exactly, Kermode is claiming about it. Drawing his account together, the early style in *Shakespeare's Language* has five marks. The verse is heavily end-stopped: sense and line end together, so that speeches fall into audible units an audience can follow and applaud unit by unit. Rhyme is everywhere, not as occasional emphasis but as a structural habit, binding exchanges into quatrains and couplets. Similes are elaborate and comparisons extended, the decorative machinery of a rhetorical education worn openly; the young writer, trained in the grammar-school arts of amplification, amplifies. Speeches are set pieces in a strong sense, composed to be detachable: laments, descriptions, debates, and professions that arrive complete, hold the stage by their finish, and release it. And the governing figures are the figures of balance, antithesis above all, with its train of oxymoron, paradox, and mirrored clause, the natural rhetoric of a mind arranging experience in pairs.

None of this is caricature, and the first duty of a fair adjudication is to say that Kermode's five marks are verifiably present in Romeo and Juliet in concentrations the later tragedies never match. The rhyme count alone is decisive. By the counts repeated in modern editions, roughly eighteen per cent of the play's lines are rhymed, a share no other Shakespearean tragedy approaches and one approached elsewhere only by the rhyme-drunk comedies of the same years, Love's Labour's Lost and A Midsummer Night's Dream. The play opens with a sonnet, continues with a second sonnet for a Chorus at the head of Act 2, and builds its most famous love scene out of a third sonnet shared between two speakers. Its hero introduces himself in a cascade of oxymorons. Its grief, when grief comes, is discharged in formal laments with refrains. If a reader wanted a single exhibit for the proposition that Shakespeare's pre-1600 verse is patterned, rhymed, and set-piece built, this play would be the exhibit chosen, and it was so chosen, in effect, by Kermode's survey.

The technical substrate is the blank verse itself, which in the middle 1590s still behaves like a frame for rhetoric rather than a medium for thought: regular in stress, sparing with enjambment, its sentences fitted to its lines like verses to a tune. A fuller anatomy of that substrate, its shared lines, its stichomythia, and its controlled departures, belongs to the series study of [blank verse in Romeo and Juliet and how it works](/2024/12/06/blank-verse-romeo-and-juliet/), and is compressed here deliberately. What matters for Kermode is the consequence: in such a medium, language presents itself. It does not hide its joins. An audience of the 1590s, educated to relish exactly this, heard the joins as the pleasure. Only a critic writing after the later plays exist can hear them as a limitation, and the question of whether that hindsight is insight or anachronism is the question the developmental scheme cannot dodge.

## The Company the Play Keeps in Part One: Poems, Sonnets, and the Lyric Phase

A survey is known by its groupings, and the grouping Kermode's Part One imposes on the play is itself an interpretation that needs auditing. Romeo and Juliet is read there in the company of the narrative poems, Venus and Adonis of 1593 and The Rape of Lucrece of 1594, of the sonnets composed across the decade, of Love's Labour's Lost with its academy of wits fencing in rhyme, of Richard II with its ceremonial, almost liturgical verse, and of A Midsummer Night's Dream, the other great rhyme-engine of the middle 1590s. The company is coherent: these are the works in which Shakespeare's art is most visibly the art of the set lyric form, the stanza, the sequence, the patterned exchange. To file the tragedy among them is to decide in advance which of its features will count as essential, the ones it shares with the poems, and which will count as accidental, the ones it shares with the tragedies that came later. The filing is not dishonest; the shared features are real and dense. But a filing is a choice, and rival filings exist, as the play's presence in every history of English tragedy, rather than in histories of the lyric, silently attests.

The sonnet culture behind the grouping deserves its weight in the adjudication, because it is the phase's engine and the survey is right to make it central. The 1590s were the decade of the sonnet sequence, of Sidney's Astrophil and Stella in print from 1591, of Daniel, Drayton, and a hundred imitators, and the young Shakespeare was formed inside that fashion as few dramatists have been formed inside any. Venus and Adonis made his name as a poet before the stage made it as a playwright, and the sonnet was the period's supreme instrument for the examination of desire: fourteen lines in which a lover could argue with himself in figures. Romeo and Juliet imports that instrument entire, and the survey's instinct, to read the play as the sonnet culture's dramatic monument, catches something no account of the play can afford to lose. Where the instinct hardens into the placing's weakness is in its treatment of importation as identity. A sonnet inside a play is not a sonnet. It is a sonnet with another person in it, timed by an entrance, overheard by a chaperone, and followed by a sword fight, and the differences the stage makes to the form are the differences the survey's grouping systematically discounts.

Richard II, the grouping's most instructive member, shows the discount at work. The survey reads that play's ceremonial verse, its rhymed rituals of deposition and lament, as the same early manner in a graver key, and so it is. Yet the ceremony in Richard II is the play's subject in exactly Levin's sense: a king unmaking himself in the forms that made him, formality collapsing under the weight of the meaning it carries. Nobody reads the deposition scene as a symptom of its author's youth, because the history play's intentions are granted in advance. The tragedy of Verona is owed the same grant, and the fact that the survey extends it to the history and withholds it from the tragedy is the grouping's quietest and most consequential decision. Restoring the grant does not move the play out of its phase. It restores the phase's best works to the dignity of intention, which is where this article's verdict will finally rest.

## Romeo and Juliet Inside Part One of Shakespeare's Language

What Kermode actually does with the play can now be stated with the record straight. In Part One of *Shakespeare's Language*, the general survey of the work before the turn, Romeo and Juliet takes its place among the exhibits of the lyric phase, the years in the middle 1590s when the playwright was also producing the narrative poems' legacy, the sonnets' discipline, Richard II's ceremonial verse, and the great rhyme-saturated comedies. The reading the survey gives the tragedy, paraphrased as the brief for this article requires, runs as follows. The play is the early manner at its most achieved and most exposed. Its love poetry is genuinely beautiful, and its beauty is of the kind the phase specialised in: figured, symmetrical, and self-regarding, verse that knows it is verse. The lovers speak in forms, sonnet, conceit, aubade, lament, because in the early manner character expresses itself by mastering the available forms rather than by breaking them. Set speeches carry the play's weight, and the greatest of them, Mercutio's Queen Mab fantasy, is a bravura turn that halts the action to exhibit what language can do when released from errand. The tragedy's rhetoric is explicit: what a feeling is, the verse says, in balanced and memorable statement. Nothing is withheld for the audience to excavate, because excavation is the pleasure of the later manner, not of this one.

As a characterisation of a phase, this is acute, and this article does not pretend otherwise. But three properties of the survey treatment distort the play before the argument even begins, and the distortions compound. The first is that the play is never allowed to be an exception. Every general survey needs its exhibits to behave, and the survey's Romeo and Juliet behaves: it is lyric, patterned, explicit. The moments in the text where the verse behaves in ways the phase should not produce, the compressed terror of Juliet's potion speech, the fractured syntax of the tomb, are not weighed, because a survey has no scale for single scenes. The second distortion is comparative framing. In Part One the play is perpetually being read toward Hamlet, as an earlier state of an art whose later state is the book's real subject; the reading is therefore always slightly impatient, always noting what has not yet arrived. The third is subtlest. By treating the early style as a stage in the development of an author, the survey converts the play's formal choices into biographical symptoms. The sonnet the lovers share stops being a decision this tragedy makes about its lovers and becomes evidence about where its author had got to. Biography swallows drama, and it is precisely that swallowing which Levin, McDonald, and the editors, in their different idioms, refuse to perform.

### Does Shakespeare's Language give the play a chapter of its own?

No. The close, single-play studies that form the bulk of the book begin with Julius Caesar and proceed through the tragedies and late plays written after the turn of the century; the works before 1600, Romeo and Juliet among them, are treated together in the opening survey, Part One.

The play's absence from the chapter list is not an oversight but a consequence of the book's design, and it explains both the brilliance and the thinness of what the survey says about the tragedy: the play is present as the early manner's finest exhibit, not as an object of sustained attention in its own right.

It is worth pausing on the justice of that design before challenging its effects. Kermode's book is about a change, and a change is proved by contrasts. To linger over Romeo and Juliet at Hamlet's length would have dissolved the book's argument in its own counter-evidence, because the play, examined closely, keeps suggesting that the early manner was already doing more than the scheme allows. The survey's brevity is therefore not laziness. It is the price of the thesis, and naming the price is the beginning of the adjudication: a thesis that must keep its finest early exhibit at arm's length has confessed something about the exhibit.

A word about the placing's precision is owed before the testing begins, because the developmental scheme's confidence can hide how much of the chronology it presumes. Romeo and Juliet is dated by consent to the middle 1590s, printed in a short quarto in 1597 and in the fuller Second Quarto of 1599, the copy editors trust. No document fixes the year of composition; the placing rests on the verse tests, on the source poem's currency, and on the play's evident kinship with the Dream and Richard II. Kermode's survey inherits that consensus and builds on it without anxiety, which is reasonable, but the consequence for the adjudication should be stated: the claim under examination is a claim about a phase of roughly five years, not about a season, and within a phase that narrow, manner and matter are unusually hard to separate. A dramatist writing a sonnet-culture tragedy for a company fresh from a sonnet-culture comedy would sound exactly as this play sounds whether he was twenty years from his maturity or standing in it. The phase explains the instrumentation. It cannot, by itself, explain the playing.

## Testing the Placing Against the Text: Sonnets, Set Pieces, and the Strain

Apply Kermode's own tests to the play, scene by scene, and the description holds impressively far. The Prologue is a sonnet, fourteen lines announcing the whole plot in the most closed form the period possessed: "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, 5 to 6). Form precedes feeling; the audience receives the ending inside a pattern before it receives a single character. Romeo's first extended speech is the oxymoron cascade, "O brawling love, O loving hate, / O any thing, of nothing first create! / O heavy lightness, serious vanity" (1.1.176 to 178), a young man performing lovesickness in the received figures of Petrarchan antithesis, and Benvolio answers in rhyme of almost mechanical regularity, "one fire burns out another's burning, / One pain is lessen'd by another's anguish" (1.1.46 to 47), counsel delivered as a rhyming machine for the curing of love by love. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, "O, then I see Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4.53), swells into the period's most famous detachable fantasy, ninety lines of miniature pageant that Romeo must interrupt by force of friendship: the set piece, exactly as Kermode describes the early habit, obeying its own momentum rather than the scene's need. The lovers' meeting completes the pattern in the most formal device of all, a sonnet built between two voices, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this" (1.5.92 to 93), to which Juliet replies inside the same form, "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much" (1.5.96), and the exchange ends with the flattest and most famous of theatrical jokes about form itself, her verdict that he kisses by the book (1.5.110).

So far, the survey is simply right, and the defence of the play must not waste itself denying the obvious. The testing begins where Kermode's account stops, at the observation that the play keeps staging its own forms as problems. The sonnet at the feast is shared, which no sonnet in the Petrarchan tradition is: the form built for a solitary worshipper addressing an absent lady is invaded by the lady, who caps his quatrains, corrects his theology, and turns his monologue into a negotiation. The oxymoron cascade of the first act is diagnosed inside the play as a symptom, by the Friar, who hears in Romeo's figures the sound of a love located in the eyes rather than the heart, and by Mercutio, whose mockery of the Petrarchan Romeo, the ape, the madman, the lover sighing like a schoolboy, treats the early style as a costume its wearer must be laughed out of. The lamentations over Juliet's body in Act 4 Scene 5, the most mechanically formal passage in the play, a quartet of grief with refrains, are undercut even as they sound, because the audience knows the grief is premature and the corpse is sleeping; the form performs itself for an audience positioned to judge it. Kermode's phase-style is present in every one of these scenes. What the survey misses is that the scenes are about the style, and that the play's intelligence is directed at it with a precision that looks, from any later vantage, very like the beginning of the very self-consciousness the book reserves for the years after 1600.

The strain shows most where the tragedy darkens. Juliet's defence of Romeo in Act 3 Scene 2 begins inside the old machinery, "Beautiful tyrant, fiend angelical! / Dove-feather'd raven, wolvish-ravening lamb!" (3.2.75 to 76), oxymoron piled on oxymoron, and then does something the early manner is not supposed to do: it thinks its way through the figures and out the other side, arriving at a plain resolution the figures were preventing. Her potion speech in Act 4 Scene 3 abandons balance altogether for a syntax of interruption and imagined horror, "My dismal scene I needs must act alone" (4.3.19), fear rendered as broken sequence rather than completed period. Romeo in the tomb speaks lines that close like the old verse and open like the new at once: "Eyes, look your last! / Arms, take your last embrace!" (5.3.112 to 113). The honest verdict on the text is therefore double. Measured across the whole play, Kermode's description wins: the patterning is real, dense, and unmatched later. Measured at the play's pressure points, the description is incomplete in a way that matters, because the patterning is visibly being used, tested, and at the climaxes partially discarded by a dramatist who already understands its limits from inside. A style that a play interrogates is not a stage in an author's development in the simple sense. It is a subject.

One further test belongs to the Friar, because his couplets are the early manner's most concentrated deposit in the play and the survey's description fits them perfectly, up to the point where the play turns them. Laurence enters speaking in rhymed sententiae, "The earth that's nature's mother is her tomb; / What is her burying grave, that is her womb" (2.3.9 to 10), wisdom delivered in the closed, balanced, proverbial form the phase perfected, and he marries the lovers under the same dispensation: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder" (2.6.9 to 10). No passages in the play better deserve Kermode's adjectives: figured, symmetrical, explicit, complete. Yet their dramatic function is the opposite of display for its own sake. The Friar's couplets are the old order's theory of the world, balanced antithesis as a philosophy, earth against tomb, womb against grave, delight against end, and the tragedy's action is the refutation of the philosophy by the events it fails to govern. The man of couplets devises the plot's machinery and loses control of it; his balanced world produces the unbalanced tomb. Form here is not the young author's habit. It is a character's creed, staged, trusted, and destroyed, and a survey that cannot distinguish a creed from a habit will misread every couplet the Friar speaks.

## The Orchard and the Aubade: Where the Verse Learns to Argue

Two scenes deserve a slower testing than the survey's pace allows, because they are the middle term the developmental account has the most trouble pricing: scenes in the full early dress of image and pattern that nevertheless behave like the later verse, arguing, qualifying, and discovering their meanings as they go. The orchard scene of Act 2 Scene 2 opens inside the purest display the manner affords, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2 to 3), the beloved processed through the phase's entire machinery of light in four lines. What follows, though, is not a set speech but an argument conducted at cross purposes with an unseen opponent, and its hinge is a piece of logic wearing lyric dress: "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33). Juliet's question is a syllogism in waiting, and the scene proceeds to work it, distinguishing name from thing, essence from accident, with a rigour the sonnet tradition she inherited never required of its solitary speakers. The verse remains patterned; roses, stars, and tides of tears arrive on schedule. But the pattern has been put to work as the medium of an inference, and inference, in Kermode's own scheme, is the later manner's defining activity. The scene does not refute his description of the phase. It shows the phase's instruments performing the succeeding phase's labour, inside the phase, on the phase's most famous stage.

The aubade of Act 3 Scene 5 completes the demonstration from the opposite direction, beginning in the most conventional device the lyric repertoire possessed, the dawn song, and converting it into a forensic dispute about evidence. "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5.1) opens a scene in which the lovers litigate a bird: lark or nightingale, herald of the morning or singer of the night, with the metre of the argument tightening as the light strengthens and the price of a wrong verdict rising to death. Form criticism can file the scene under the early manner, since the aubade is a set form with a pedigree from the troubadours, and the filing is correct. What the filing cannot capture is the scene's epistemology, its staging of two people testing a perception against a wish, correcting each other's figures, and surrendering a beautiful falsehood to an ugly fact. That is the drama of the post-1600 verse in embryo, thought conducted inside image, and it stands in the middle of the play the survey exhibits as the prehistory of such things. The developmental account, to remain honest, has to price these scenes as anticipations, and a career whose anticipations are this sustained begins to look less like a ladder than like a repertoire, drawn on as the matter demands.

## The Prose Counter-Register: The Evidence the Survey Underweights

Every account of the play's style in Kermode's manner is an account of its verse, and the survey's own favourite statistic, the proportion of prose to verse, points at a body of evidence the developmental reading of this play has never properly weighed. Romeo and Juliet carries a substantial prose estate: the servants' bawdy fencing that opens the action, the Nurse's great garrulous narratives, Mercutio's prose flights in the street scenes, Peter's clowning with the musicians over the apparently dead Juliet. Kermode's metric treats prose share as a rough index of earliness, swelling in the young work and receding before the great verse tragedies, and at the level of the canon the index behaves. Inside this play, though, the prose is not a residue of an undeveloped manner. It is a functioning counter-register, the play's second instrument, and its work is precisely the work the survey attributes to the later style: disenchantment, qualification, the testing of the verse's claims against a world that does not rhyme.

Consider the distribution. The verse of Verona is the language of the feud's ceremonies, the lovers' vows, and the Friar's sententiae: the official media. The prose belongs to the servants who fight the feud without owning it, to the Nurse who has buried a husband and a daughter and prices love in lunches and bruises, and to Mercutio, whose prose in the conjuration scene and the street exchanges is the sound of intelligence refusing the verse's terms. When the catastrophe has done its work, the play's grief is handed, for one strange scene, to prose entirely, as Peter and the musicians haggle over silver sounds and dump the tragedy's instruments into a clown's inventory. Levin ended his essay in that scene, among the musicians, and the instinct was sound: the play's own verdict on its verse splendour is delivered in prose, by hired men, over a body. A developmental account that files prose share under earliness cannot see this scene as the play's criticism of itself, yet there it stands, the counter-register given the last word before the Prince's rhymed judgement closes the action.

The adjudication this evidence forces is narrow but real. Kermode's prose metric is upheld as a canon-level gauge and set aside as a play-level reading, because in this text the prose is not what the young dramatist had not yet outgrown but what he had already built as the verse's internal opposition. The early manner, examined whole rather than in its verse alone, turns out to contain its own antithesis, a built-in second voice that says the patterned world is a performance and prices it. That is a sophistication the ladder model has no rung for, and it strengthens the verdict reached from the verse evidence alone by independent means: the play's relation to its period style is that of a user, not a product.

## Kermode Against Levin: Phase of a Career, or Subject of a Play?

Harry Levin's "Form and Formality in Romeo and Juliet" appeared in Shakespeare Quarterly in the winter of 1960, at pages 3 to 11 of the journal's eleventh volume, and it remains the most influential short account of the play's style ever written. Levin's argument, in the compressed form this comparison requires, is that formality is the play's very subject. The tragedy is built on antithesis at every level, youth and age, love and hate, day and night, form and nature, and its drama is the drama of the lovers' passage from the formal to the natural: from the sonnet's etiquette and the conceit's artifice toward a speech earned by experience. Rhyme, in Levin's reading, is bound to formality, thick in the ceremonious early scenes and thinning as feeling takes possession of the speakers. The play contains, on this account, a running critique of its own artificiality, and the critique is the meaning. A full anatomy of the essay, its figures, its Sidney borrowings, its strange ending among the musicians, belongs to the series study of [Harry Levin's 'Form and Formality' (1960)](/2026/12/04/harry-levin-form-and-formality/) and is held here to the single passage the comparison needs.

Set the two accounts side by side and their agreement is startling before their disagreement begins. Kermode and Levin describe the same text: the same rhymes, the same sonnets, the same balanced periods, the same oxymorons. Levin even supplies, in the rhyme-and-formality link, the kind of measurable correlation Kermode's survey elsewhere relies on. The quarrel is not over the evidence. It is over the address of the evidence. For Levin, the patterning is located in the play, placed there by a dramatist dramatising it; the formality is what the tragedy is about, and the lovers' escape from it is the tragedy's action in verbal form. For Kermode, the patterning is located in the career, in the phase the author had reached; the formality is what the verse of the middle 1590s characteristically did, and the play is its most famous carrier. The same sonnet is, in one reading, an event, and in the other, a symptom.

Kermode was not ignorant of the rival location. As the editor of *Four Centuries of Shakespearian Criticism* in 1965, he had himself reprinted Levin's essay for the student market, an editorial act of canonisation performed thirty-five years before *Shakespeare's Language* quietly declined to follow the essay's lead. The decline deserves a precise name. It is not rebuttal; Levin is nowhere argued with in the survey. It is absorption. The developmental frame takes Levin's facts, which are facts, and reassigns their cause, from the play's design to the period's manner, without ever quite saying that the design is not there. Absorption is harder to fight than rebuttal, because nothing has been denied, and that is why the adjudication must be made on grounds the frame itself cannot supply: the distribution of the patterning inside the play. If the formality were merely the period's manner, it would lie evenly over the text like weather. It does not. It thickens at the feast, in the fathers' ceremonious exchanges, in the public and parental registers, and it thins, breaks, and complicates exactly where the lovers' feeling is most their own, in the orchard's improvisations, in the aubade's argument about a bird, in the potion speech's collapse of syntax. Weather does not distribute itself by meaning. Design does.

### What is the real difference between the two accounts of the formality?

The difference is one of location and therefore of cause. Levin locates the play's formality inside the play, as its chosen subject, so that every sonnet and antithesis is evidence in an argument the drama is conducting. Kermode locates the same formality inside the author's career, as the manner of a phase.

Both locations explain real patterns; the distribution of the patterning across scenes, thickening where the old order speaks and breaking where feeling resists it, is the datum Levin's account predicts and the survey's cannot.

The biographical frame sharpens the irony of the absorption. Harry Levin (1912 to 1994) was Harvard's Irving Babbitt Professor of Comparative Literature, a comparatist whose *Contexts of Criticism* of 1957 and *The Overreacher* of 1952 had made him, by 1960, the American academy's most authoritative voice on Renaissance form. His Romeo and Juliet essay is nine pages long, the length of a lecture, and it does in nine pages what the survey declines to do in a book: it follows one play's forms through one play's action and finds the meaning in the itinerary. When Kermode reprinted the essay in *Four Centuries of Shakespearian Criticism* five years later, he certified it as one of the four centuries' essential readings, the judgement a whole generation of students inherited. The survey of 2000 neither cites it nor answers it; the developmental frame simply flows around the stone. Readers deciding between the two accounts should know that one of them was canonised by the other's author, and that the later silence is a change of framework, not a refutation conducted and won.

The verdict between them is therefore split by territory, and the split is honest rather than evasive. On the play, Levin wins. His account explains more of the evidence with less remainder: why the sonnet is shared, why the laments are hollow at the centre, why rhyme desertions track emotional truth, why Mercutio's prose and the Nurse's prose press against the verse like a second, disenchanted register. On the canon, Kermode wins, and it is not a small win. Levin's essay, confined to one play, cannot explain why the formality it anatomises is measurably denser here than in the tragedies of the next decade, why the same hand that built the shared sonnet was, within five years, building Hamlet's doubles. The developmental fact is a fact. What the adjudication refuses is only the inference the survey invites and never states: that because the manner is early, the play's use of it is naive. Between the two critics, the play is better served by being read twice, once as Levin's subject and once as Kermode's specimen, and worse served by either reading alone.

## The Counting Kermode Refused: Spurgeon's Census as His Test Case

One full passage must be given to Caroline Spurgeon, because her work is the measuring instrument Kermode's whole method defines itself against, and because this play is where their methods can be made to check each other. Spurgeon's *Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us*, published in 1935, founded a school on a simple, laborious procedure: count the images, play by play and cluster by cluster, and let the tallies reveal what a play is imaginatively made of. For Romeo and Juliet her census found the picture the whole century has repeated, a play saturated with images of light blazing against darkness, the lovers figured as sun, stars, lightning, and torches in a world of night, tomb, and envious moon. The census itself, its totals, its classes, and the revolution it started belong to the series study of [Caroline Spurgeon and the imagery revolution](/2027/03/05/caroline-spurgeon-imagery-revolution/), and stand here only as the exhibit in Kermode's argument about method.

Kermode's relation to that method is one of the most consistent positions in *Shakespeare's Language*: courteous, and immovable. Counting, in his practice, is what a critic does instead of reading. A tally of images cannot register what the language is doing, because language does its work in syntax, in the movement of a sentence through a mind, and a heap of counted suns says nothing about the sentence in which a particular sun rises. His own single concession to quantity, the proportion of prose to verse, is offered almost apologetically, as the crudest of gauges. Against Spurgeon the objection generalises into his famous suspicion of thematic keys of every kind, the suspicion that also governs his distance from G. Wilson Knight's pattern-first readings: a theme, once counted or charted, becomes a skeleton key that opens every door by ceasing to distinguish between them. The early style, for Kermode, will not be recovered by arithmetic, because what makes it early is not the frequency of its figures but the posture of its sentences, and posture is heard, not tallied.

One circumstance gives Kermode's refusal its peculiar force, and fairness requires it to be entered. He was not an outsider to the imagery tradition he resisted. His first Shakespeare book was the Arden Tempest of 1954, edited in the years when Spurgeon's method and Wilson Knight's pattern readings set the terms of the trade, and his introduction to that edition worked seriously with the play's imagery, its sea, storm, and music, as evidence of design. The scepticism of *Shakespeare's Language* is therefore a convert's scepticism, formed inside the school it questions, and it carries a convert's knowledge of where the method's bodies are buried: in the slide from a counted pattern to a total explanation, from the true observation that an image recurs to the false conclusion that recurrence is meaning. When the survey declines to count this play's suns and stars, it is declining a procedure its author had himself practised on The Tempest two generations earlier. The refusal deserves to be weighed as experience rather than temperament, which is why this article upholds it as a discipline even while rejecting it as a complete account of evidence.

The second of Kermode's famous cautions, his scepticism of thematic keys, belongs in this section because it governs how the play's patterning may legitimately be used, and Romeo and Juliet has suffered more from keys than almost any play in the canon. The tragedy has been unlocked, in successive critical generations, by haste, by light and darkness, by the stars, by sacrifice, by the feud as original sin, each key announced as the one the lock was cut for. Kermode's resistance to the whole procedure was formed early and never relaxed: a theme, on his showing, is what a work is found to be about by readers who need the finding to be portable, and the key that opens every door in a play usually turns out to have been cut from the door the critic entered by. His *Genesis of Secrecy* of 1979 gave the scepticism its hermeneutic form, distinguishing the meanings available to insiders from the residue that no interpretation exhausts, and the distinction travelled with him into the survey's practice: passages are honoured, quoted, and weighed, but no single pattern is permitted to stand for the whole. Applied to this play, the caution discredits in one stroke the haste thesis, the star-crossed thesis, and the light thesis alike, not because haste, stars, and light are absent, they saturate the text, but because each becomes false at the moment it claims to be the meaning rather than one of the play's materials. This article has used pattern evidence throughout, and it has used it under Kermode's discipline: patterns are cited as the play's means, never as its message, which is the only use of them that survives his critique and, on the evidence, the only use that survives the play.

The adjudication here cuts against the master in a way he would have recognised as fair. Kermode is right that counting explains nothing by itself: Spurgeon's light tally does not say why the light hurts, and her method, unguarded, licenses the thematic criticism he disliked, in which a play is reduced to its leading cluster and the cluster to a thesis. But the developmental claim at the centre of his own book is, in strictness, a claim of exactly the kind counting verifies. That the verse changed around 1600 is asserted from the ear, yet every corroboration the assertion has ever received is quantitative or quasi-quantitative: metrical tests, prose proportions, rhyme shares, the distribution of enjambment and mid-line breaks across the canon. The survey's placing of Romeo and Juliet in the early phase rests, at bottom, on the measurable fact that its patterning is denser than the later tragedies', which is a Spurgeon-shaped fact wearing Kermode's clothes. The methods are not rivals but instruments at different ranges: the census establishes where the weight lies, the ear establishes what the weight does. Kermode's refusal of the census is therefore upheld as a discipline for critics and rejected as a philosophy of evidence, and Spurgeon's play, the light-drenched tragedy her counters found, stands as independent confirmation that the patterning the survey describes is in the text and not in the phase's reputation.

## The Case Against the Ladder: Russ McDonald and the Defence of Rhetoric

The critic who differs, in the form this article's brief requires, is Russ McDonald, whose *Shakespeare and the Arts of Language* appeared from Oxford University Press in 2001, one year after Kermode's volume, and was grouped with it by reviewers as part of the same event: the return of language to the centre of Shakespeare study after decades in which the words themselves had been treated as a transparent medium for history, ideology, and theme. The two books share a cause and divide on the question that matters here. McDonald, professor of English at the University of North Carolina at Greensboro, rebuilds the world in which the early style was learned: the grammar-school curriculum of imitation, translation, and figure, in which rhetoric was not decoration added to thought but the very technology by which thought was produced, practised, and judged. Shakespeare, in the phrase the reviewers seized on, was the ideal student of Renaissance rhetoric, and the virtuosity of the early plays, the cascades of figure, the patterned exchanges, the relish for set forms, is what that training looked like when a genius underwent it. Where Kermode's survey sees a phase awaiting its supersession, McDonald sees a discipline achieving its ends.

The difference is best stated as a disagreement about what a pattern is. In the developmental account, pattern is what the young writer has not yet learned to subordinate: meaning rides on the surface because the verse has not yet acquired depth. In McDonald's account, pattern is a positive good in its own right, an aural and rhetorical pleasure the period's audiences were educated to take, and its presence in a play is evidence of an intention to please by those means, not of an inability to do otherwise. His own narrative of the career, it should be said plainly, is also developmental; his chapters follow a movement from the early enthusiasm for figurative virtuosity, through a middle-period suspicion of the harlotry of words, toward the last plays' renewed faith in language. The quarrel with Kermode is therefore not over whether the style changed. It is over the sign of the early term. For McDonald the arc is a story of changing relations to a permanent art; for the survey, as its readers have overwhelmingly received it, the arc is a story of growth out of a lesser manner into a greater. The reception is part of the evidence here, and it is unambiguous: whatever qualifications the survey contains, the picture it lodged in the culture is a ladder, with Romeo and Juliet on a lower rung than Hamlet, and it is the ladder, not the chronology, that McDonald's book refutes.

The Cambridge textbook's evidence deserves a paragraph of its own, because it changes what the shared sonnet, this article's recurring exhibit, is evidence of. On McDonald's showing, the figures that structure the lovers' first exchange, the antitheses, the balanced clauses, the conceit of pilgrim and saint sustained across fourteen lines, are not ornaments applied to a meaning that exists independently of them. They are the instruments by which the meaning is produced: the sonnet form obliges the two speakers to complete each other's rhymes, so that the harmony the scene celebrates is enacted by the verse's machinery rather than described by it. Where the developmental account sees two young people trapped inside a received form, the rhetorical account sees the received form doing precise dramatic work, welding strangers into a single metrical organism in ninety seconds of stage time, and it asks what account of poetic immaturity can accommodate an effect so calculated. The question has force precisely because the effect is the one Kermode himself isolated, the conversion of pattern into relation. By McDonald's standard the conversion is not the pattern's failure but its triumph, and the standard is drawn from the period's own pedagogy rather than from retrospective taste, which is what gives the differ its standing in this adjudication: it judges the early manner by the criteria the early manner was built to satisfy.

One boundary of the defence must be marked, since this article adjudicates rather than campaigns. McDonald's rehabilitation of rhetoric establishes that the play's patterning is masterful by its own arts' standards; it does not establish that Shakespeare was finished growing in 1595, and McDonald, whose book is itself organised as a passage from the arts of language toward their later transformations, does not claim otherwise. The differ defeats the ladder's valuation; it leaves the chronology, the drift of the verse tests, and Kermode's description of the two manners exactly where they stood. That division of the spoils is the one the verdict section formalises, and it is a division the survey's own author, who praised order and measure in other men's books all his life, is unlikely to have found unintelligible.

The play supplies its own witness for the defence, and her name in the brief for this article is Marjorie Garber. Garber's reading of the play, in the chapter of *Shakespeare After All* that the series treats in [Marjorie Garber's reading of Romeo and Juliet](/2026/12/25/marjorie-garber-romeo-and-juliet/), dwells on the play's dreams, names, and doubles, and on Mercutio as the play's internal critic of its own rhetoric, the mocker whose conjurations and fantasies expose the Petrarchan machinery by running it at full speed in the wrong direction. A style that a play equips with its own satirist is not a style its author is trapped inside. The Petrarchan conceit in Romeo and Juliet is quoted by the play, framed, and found wanting by characters whose judgements the action endorses, and a dramatist capable of that framing in 1595 is not awaiting the year 1600 to discover that language can be about language. Kermode, to be fair to the record, never claims the early playwright lacked self-consciousness; he claims the verse had not yet changed its procedures. But the distinction, fine in a seminar, collapses in the reading experience the book actually produces, and the verdict of this section is accordingly precise: McDonald wins the argument about value, Garber supplies the evidence from inside the play, and Kermode's description of the manner stands untouched by either, which is why the final adjudication can afford to be split.

### Does the play itself mock the style Kermode calls early?

Yes, continuously and by design. The Petrarchan machinery of the early manner, the oxymorons, the worship at a distance, the sonnet built for an absent mistress, is handed to Romeo as a costume and then laughed at by Mercutio, corrected by Juliet, and diagnosed by the Friar, until the hero's speech sheds it under pressure.

A play that satirises its period style from inside, and makes the shedding of that style the hero's education, cannot be filed as a naive specimen of the style: the manner is present in the text as material under judgement, not as the limit of its author's awareness.

One more witness belongs in this section, because he guards the flank McDonald leaves open. Brian Vickers, in *The Artistry of Shakespeare's Prose* (1968) and across a career of rhetorical scholarship, defended the period's patterned prose and verse as arts with their own criteria, against exactly the habit of reading them as failed versions of a later naturalism. Vickers and McDonald together represent the rhetorical turn's settled position: the early style is to be judged by the ends it pursued, ornament, copia, and pattern among them, and not exclusively by ends it had not yet adopted. Kermode, whose own prose is the most patterned of any critic in this story, would not have dissented from the principle in the abstract. The question the verdict section must answer is whether his book observes it in the particular case of this play.

## The Editor's Tribunal: What the Editions Do With the Patterning

Critics argue; editors act, and the acts are evidence. The editor required by this article's brief is Brian Gibbons, whose Arden Shakespeare edition of the play, the Second Series volume published by Methuen in 1980, was for three decades the standard scholarly text. Gibbons based his text on the Second Quarto of 1599, the good quarto, drawing where necessary on the First Quarto of 1597, and the choice itself is the first editorial verdict in the case. The fuller Q2 text is the more formal text: it preserves at length the patterned writing the compressed Q1 trims, the developed laments, the complete sonnet dialogue, the ceremonial exchanges entire. An editor who believed the patterning to be youthful surplus would have a thousand opportunities, in collation and in annotation, to treat it as such. Gibbons does the opposite. His introduction presents the play as a designed lyric tragedy whose formalities are its meaning-bearing structure, and his commentary patiently glosses the figures, the rhyme schemes, and the set forms as instruments to be understood, not excrescences to be excused. The lamentation scene of Act 4 Scene 5, the passage most often held up as the early style at its most mechanical, is defended in that edition by the conventions of formal lament it executes: grief in this culture had a liturgy, and the play performs the liturgy while the audience, knowing Juliet sleeps, watches the liturgy fail to reach its object.

Behind the Arden choice stands a textual fact that sharpens the tribunal's verdict. The First Quarto of 1597, the short text long dismissed as a bad quarto and in more forgiving accounts treated as a reported or adapted version, gives a measurably less formal play: speeches trimmed, patterning thinned, the verse's ceremonial surface worn down in transmission or abridgement. The formal Romeo and Juliet, the sonnet-built and lament-laden tragedy the survey describes, is specifically the play of the Second Quarto, the text the modern editorial tradition, Gibbons and Weis alike, has chosen to print. The choice is renewed by every editor who makes it, and it amounts to a standing professional judgement that the patterning is the play, not a husk around the play: strip the formality away, as the short text partly does, and what remains is widely agreed to be the lesser article. A developmental account that prices the formality as the phase's residue has to explain why the residue is the part no editor will surrender.

The tribunal does not sit in one edition only. Jill Levenson, editing the play for the Oxford Shakespeare in 2000, the very year of *Shakespeare's Language*, brought to her introduction the authority of her own earlier study of the play's phrasing, "The Definition of Love: Shakespeare's Phrasing in Romeo and Juliet", published in Shakespeare Studies in 1982, an essay that treats the play's verbal patterning as a system of definitions the lovers test and revise. René Weis, whose Arden Third Series edition followed in 2012, prints Q2 likewise and writes of the play's verbal artistry as a young master's deliberate construction. The professional consensus embodied in the editions is therefore unusually legible. Across forty years and three editorial generations, the men and women paid to know this text most intimately have treated its formality as the play's achievement, annotated it as design, and transmitted it entire. None of this refutes Kermode's chronology, and the editions do not pretend to; Gibbons himself dates the play to the middle 1590s and describes its style in terms a developmentalist can use. What the tribunal refuses is the demotion. In the daily practice of editing, the early style is not a rung. It is the text.

Set against the survey, the tribunal's evidence has one further use: it corrects the survey's implicit picture of the audience. Kermode's early manner is sometimes received as if its pleasures were the pleasures of a less sophisticated theatrical culture, an audience content with display because it could not yet follow thought. The editorial record reminds the argument that the play's first audiences included the readers of the sonnet sequences, the Inns of Court wits, and the spectators who made a hit of a tragedy whose heroine argues her lover out of his conceits in real time. Display, for that audience, was not the opposite of intelligence. It was one of its sports.

## Does the Developmental View Undervalue the Early Style?

The complication must now be faced in its strongest form, because it is the objection on which the whole adjudication turns. The charge against the developmental view is not that styles fail to change; it is that a history arranged as development smuggles a hierarchy into a chronology, and that four distinct weaknesses follow. The first is circularity. The dating of Shakespeare's plays rests partly on external records and partly on the styles themselves, so that a scheme which sorts plays by style and then reads the sorted order as growth risks proving its premise with its conclusion. Kermode's hinge is unusually well protected against this charge, since Hamlet's date and the Globe's chronology are documented independently of style, but the protection weakens at the level of the individual early play, whose place in the sequence is partly a stylistic judgement wearing a date's clothing. The second weakness is the metaphor itself. Maturity is a biological figure, and figures argue: what is mature makes the rest immature, and immaturity in criticism is never a neutral word. The third is the genre confound. A lyric tragedy steeped in sonnet culture will always look earlier, by the scheme's measures, than a sceptical problem play, even if the two were written in the same season, and the scheme has no internal way to separate the effect of kind from the effect of date. The fourth is the simplest and the most damaging in practice: the scheme teaches its readers where not to look. A style filed as a phase's manner stops being interrogated as a set of choices, and the choices in this play, as the testing section showed, are where the tragedy lives.

Against the charge, the developmental view has one defence that deserves its full weight, and it is that the alternative is worse. A criticism that refuses all chronology of style, treating every play as an unplaced monad, cannot explain the brute distributional facts: the rhyme shares, the prose proportions, the enjambment curves, the demonstrable difference between the sentence of 1595 and the sentence of 1606. Those facts exist, they are orderly, and order in time is development by any honest name. Kermode's version of the order is, moreover, the most humane available, because it insists that the connections between early and late remain detectable, that the early work is masterful already, in the survey's own concession that the pre-1600 plays were masterful works before they moved up a level. The ladder is in the reception more than in the text, and a scrupulous verdict must record that the book's sin, where it sins, is one of framing and proportion rather than of contempt.

The empirical spine of the corroboration is worth naming, since Kermode's ear is so often left to carry the argument alone. The nineteenth century's verse tests, crude instruments devised to settle the authorship and dating of disputed plays, already registered the direction of travel: rhyme declining, enjambment and feminine endings rising, light and weak endings multiplying as the career proceeds. Twentieth-century metrical scholarship, above all Marina Tarlinskaja's *Shakespeare's Verse* of 1987, put the drift on a statistical footing across the whole canon, and the drift runs the survey's way. Romeo and Juliet sits in her tables where Kermode's placing predicts, among the rhythmically regular, strongly end-stopped work of the middle 1590s. This matters to the adjudication because it confines the dispute to its true ground. Nobody in this article's argument, not Levin, not McDonald, not the editors, disputes the measurements or the chronology they build. The dispute begins at the next step, where a measured difference between periods is converted into a difference of rank between plays, and it is at exactly that step that the corroboration runs out, since no table of endings can register what a style is doing in a scene.

The play itself delivers the deciding exhibit, and it is the exhibit this article has been building toward. Juliet's career inside the tragedy recapitulates, at the speed of the plot, the entire movement Kermode spreads across fifteen years of authorship. She begins as the recipient of other people's forms, her mother's book of love, the Nurse's proverbs, Paris's stichomythic courtesies. She meets Romeo inside a sonnet and bends the sonnet. She argues in the orchard against the received machinery of names and vows. She passes through the oxymoron crisis of Act 3 Scene 2 and comes out speaking a plainer, harder idiom. And in the potion speech she produces, alone on stage, a passage of fractured, self-interrupting, imagination-haunted verse that meets every criterion the survey reserves for the post-1600 manner: the syntax of thought under pressure, the figure that outruns statement, the difficulty that demands interpretation. If the developmental scheme were the whole truth about this play, that speech could not be in it. Its presence does not refute the scheme's chronology, since one dramatist can anticipate his own future, but it destroys the scheme's use as a ranking of the play, because the play contains the standard by which it is ranked and deploys it at its climax. The early style is not undervalued by Kermode's attention; it is undervalued by his filing system, and the correction is to read the file as a map of where the play stands in a history, not as a judgement of how far it falls short of one.

### What inside the play most troubles Kermode's placing of it?

Juliet's potion speech in Act 4 Scene 3 troubles it most, because the speech exhibits, inside a tragedy of the middle 1590s, the very procedures the developmental scheme reserves for the verse after 1600: broken and self-correcting syntax, thought visibly outrunning statement, and a figurative density that forces interpretation rather than inviting admiration.

A single scene cannot overturn a chronology, and the speech is not offered as doing so. It overturns the ranking the chronology is made to carry, by proving that the dramatist of the early play already commanded the later manner when the drama required it, and withheld it elsewhere by choice of design rather than by limitation of growth.

## Verdict: Kermode's Placing, Weighed

The adjudication can now be drawn together, and it is a verdict in parts, because the account under judgement is itself in parts. As chronology, Kermode's placing of Romeo and Juliet is upheld without reservation. The play belongs where he puts it, in the middle 1590s, on the far side of the turn of 1600, written in the most patterned, rhyme-saturated, set-piece manner of the canon, and his description of that manner, its end-stopped periods, its governing antitheses, its explicit rhetoric, its lyric display, is the most efficient true account of the style available. As method, his refusal to substitute counting for reading is upheld as a discipline and qualified as a philosophy, since his own developmental fact rests on quantities his ear preferred not to name. His treatment of the play, as a matter of record, is a survey treatment, compressed, emblematic, and innocent of the sustained attention the book lavishes on Hamlet; readers should size its authority accordingly, and this article has supplied the attention the survey withheld.

As ranking, the placing is overturned. The inference from early to lesser, which the book's framing invites and its enormous readership absorbed, fails on the evidence of the play itself: on the distribution of its patterning by meaning, which Levin explained; on the play's internal satire of its own manner, which Garber documented; on the defence of the manner's art, which McDonald rebuilt from the period's rhetorical culture; on the practice of the editors, Gibbons, Levenson, and Weis, who transmit the patterning as design; and on the potion speech, which shows the supposed future of the style already present at the early play's climax. What survives, when the ladder is discarded and the map kept, is the thing Kermode's book was written to restore and did restore, ahead of the scholarship that followed it: the conviction that Shakespeare was a poet before he was an institution, that his language has a history, and that hearing the history is a condition of hearing the plays. Romeo and Juliet, read by that conviction and against its author's filing, emerges not as the beautiful preliminary the survey needed, but as the early manner's strongest argument for itself: a tragedy that took the most formal style of its decade and made the forms confess, one by one, what they could not hold.

For the reader deciding how to use Kermode on this play, the practical settlement is simple. Use him for the ear training and the chronology, for the discipline of asking of any passage whether its sentence completes or thinks, and for the map that sets the tragedy among the works of its five years. Decline the apology the map invites. When the survey's framing whispers that the patterning is the sound of a poet not yet grown, set against the whisper the play's own distribution of its patterning, its prose counter-register, its heroine arguing her way out of the period style in five days of stage time, and the united practice of its editors. The book remains what Richard Howard called it, the first one to read on its subject. On this play it should be read as the prosecution's finest speech, in a trial the defence, given its hearing here, wins on the points that decide reputations.

## The Book's Reception: A Bestseller, a Revival, and a Framing That Stuck

The reception of *Shakespeare's Language* explains why its placing of the play matters beyond the community of specialists, and the reception had a paradox at its centre. The notices were warm to the point of ceremony. Publishers Weekly called the book a work of beauty and grace that makes its readers want to reread the plays in its light. Kirkus Reviews, while praising its precision, lodged the reservation that has trailed the volume since: an excellent survey handicapped by its shortness, its chapters uneven, thirty pages for Hamlet against eight for Cymbeline, so that the argument's generalisations outrun their local evidence. That reservation is the review-page version of this article's central finding. A book whose method is the survey and whose loves are all post-1600 will be thinnest exactly where its thesis is most exposed, in the early work it must characterise to move beyond, and the play in this article is the largest single casualty of the thinness.

The paradox is that the thinness did not limit the influence; it multiplied it. Because the book was short, unfootnoted, and written to be read, it was read, in numbers no scholarly monograph on the play's style has ever approached, and its picture of the career passed into the common sense of the reading public and of the classroom. In the years immediately following, the Times Higher Education Supplement could group the volume with Russ McDonald's *Shakespeare and the Arts of Language* and with the essay collection *Reading Shakespeare's Dramatic Language* as the joint inaugurators of a rehabilitation, the return of close attention to words after decades in which language had been treated as a transparent window on history and ideology. The grouping is just, and it carries this article's final irony in concentrated form. The revival Kermode led restored the early style to serious attention even as his own account of that style taught the revival's readers to rank it second. McDonald's half of the revival is the corrective the first half needed, and the two books are best assigned, like the two critics in the Levin comparison, as a pair in which each supplies the other's defect.

For Romeo and Juliet the framing that stuck has had one benign effect and one malign one, and the verdict of this article can be stated in their terms. The benign effect is that a generation of readers learned from the survey to hear the play's verse historically at all, to notice its rhymes, its set forms, and its patterned periods as facts with dates attached, rather than as the transparent medium of a story everyone knows. The malign effect is that the same readers learned to apologise for what they heard, to treat the patterning as the charming limitation of a young poet on his way to better things, and to save their serious attention for the difficulty of the later manner. An account that produces both effects in those proportions is neither vindicated nor condemned by its reception; it is located. Kermode's placing of the play stands as the most influential statement of the developmental view, and the developmental view stands, after the evidence assembled here, as a true map with a false legend printed in its corner. The work of this article has been to redraw the legend.

There is a final, fitting symmetry in the book's fortunes. Kermode wrote *Shakespeare's Language* against the professional habit of reading through the verse to everything except the verse, and the book's commercial triumph proved the public's appetite for the words themselves. Romeo and Juliet, the most read and most taught of the tragedies, is where that appetite is largest and where the book's own reading is thinnest. Bringing the survey's question to the survey's exhibit, at the length the exhibit deserves, is therefore not an act of correction from outside the book's project. It is the project's completion, on the one play where its author, for reasons of architecture and design rather than of judgement, declined to linger.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Kermode summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Kermode summary is the table in this article setting out Frank Kermode's placing of Romeo and Juliet's style in *Shakespeare's Language*: the play's phase, its rhyme, its sonnet forms, its governing figures, its set speeches, its syntax, its difficulty, the lovers' shared idiom, and the play's position inside the book itself. Each row pairs one of Kermode's claims with the evidence the text supplies and with the standing the claim holds after the objections of Levin, McDonald, and the editors have been weighed. The pattern the summary exposes is a clean division in Kermode's account between measurements and rankings. Wherever his placing can be counted, in rhyme share, in formal structures, in proportions, it proves accurate. Wherever it must judge what the patterning is for, it weakens, and the weaker rows are precisely the ones on which the play's reputation has been made to rest.

### Q: What is Frank Kermode's Shakespeare's Language about?

*Shakespeare's Language*, published in 2000, is Frank Kermode's account of how Shakespeare's verse changed across the career, written for readers outside the academy and without scholarly apparatus. Its founding conviction is that every other aspect of Shakespeare has been studied almost to death while the fact that he was a poet has dropped out of consideration. The book argues that something extraordinary happened to the verse around 1600: the plays moved up to a new level of achievement and of difficulty at once, growing denser, more elliptical, and more resistant to paraphrase. Roughly the first third surveys the earlier work, the poems and sonnets included; the rest gives close studies of individual plays from Julius Caesar and Hamlet onward. Romeo and Juliet belongs to the survey, as the early manner's most famous exhibit.

### Q: What does Kermode argue happened to Shakespeare's language around 1600?

Kermode argues that around 1600 Shakespeare became, within the span of a few years, a different sort of poet, and he dates the transformation between 1594 and 1608 with its gate at Hamlet and the poem "The Phoenix and the Turtle". Before the gate, verse is patterned, explicit, and rhetorically displayed: balanced periods, completed lines, speeches built as set pieces, feelings announced in balanced antithesis. After it, the verse begins to think on stage. Syntax doubles back, interrupts and qualifies itself, metaphors outrun their occasions, and recurrent linguistic patterns Kermode calls matrices, such as the steady stream of doubles in Hamlet, become fundamental to the writing. The result is a language of new suppleness and muscularity that is also harder, for its first audiences as for later ones, and that rewards interpretation as the early manner rewards admiration.

### Q: Where does Kermode place Romeo and Juliet in Shakespeare's development?

Kermode places Romeo and Juliet in the early phase of the career, among the works of the middle 1590s that precede the great change of around 1600, and he treats it as that phase's supreme exhibit among the tragedies. In his survey the play represents the lyric manner at full stretch: a verse of conspicuous figure and formal display, built from sonnets, set speeches, balanced antitheses, and a density of rhyme unmatched in the later tragedies. The placing is chronological before it is critical, and Kermode is clear that the early work was already masterful. But the structure of his book gives the placing a gravitational pull toward demotion, because the plays he studies at length are all on the far side of the gate, and the play is left standing for a manner the rest of the volume exists to move beyond.

### Q: Does Shakespeare's Language contain a chapter on Romeo and Juliet?

No, the book contains no chapter on Romeo and Juliet. The single-play studies that make up the bulk of *Shakespeare's Language* begin with Julius Caesar and proceed through the works written after the turn of the century: Hamlet, Measure for Measure, Othello, Macbeth, King Lear, and the late plays. Romeo and Juliet is treated in Part One, the general survey of the pre-1600 work, alongside the other early plays, the narrative poems, and the sonnets. The distinction matters for weighing the book's authority on this play. What Kermode says about the tragedy is said in survey mode, compressed and emblematic, as evidence for the character of a phase, and it should be read with the discount that compression earns, however brilliant the compression is.

### Q: What does Kermode mean by calling the early plays rhetorical?

By calling the early plays rhetorical, Kermode means that their verse performs its meanings openly, with the machinery of classical and grammar-school rhetoric worn on the surface: amplification, balanced clauses, antithesis and oxymoron, elaborate similes, and speeches constructed as formal orations, laments, and debates. Feeling in such verse is declared in completed and memorable statement rather than discovered in the act of speaking. The term is descriptive before it is disparaging, since rhetoric was the period's trained technology of expression and Shakespeare was its most gifted student. It turns disparaging only when the developmental frame sets rhetoric against the reticence of the later style, in which implication replaces declaration. Romeo and Juliet is rhetorical in the strong sense throughout its first acts, and the play's interest lies in watching its speakers strain against the rhetoric that formed them.

### Q: Why does rhyme matter in Kermode's account of the play?

Rhyme matters because it is the most measurable signature of the early manner, and Kermode's placing of the play leans on the measurement even though his method distrusts counting. Roughly eighteen per cent of the lines of Romeo and Juliet are rhymed, a proportion no later Shakespearean tragedy approaches and one matched only by the rhyme-saturated comedies of the same years, Love's Labour's Lost and A Midsummer Night's Dream. Rhyme binds exchanges into quatrains, closes speeches into couplets, and turns the Prologue and the lovers' first meeting into sonnets. For Kermode it therefore certifies the play's phase beyond argument. Harry Levin adds the refinement the survey omits: rhyme in this play tracks formality, thickening in ceremonious scenes and thinning as feeling takes possession of the speakers, which makes the measurement evidence both of design and of date.

### Q: How does Kermode treat the sonnet the lovers share at their first meeting?

Kermode treats the lovers' shared sonnet, the fourteen lines built between Romeo and Juliet at the feast in Act 1 Scene 5, as a prime specimen of the early manner: a received lyric form imported whole into the drama, executed with perfect finish, and admired as a feat of construction. On his account the exchange shows the phase at its most accomplished, two speakers completing a sonnet's quatrains and couplet between them as courtship. The treatment is accurate about the form and, this article argues, incomplete about the event. A Petrarchan sonnet is built for a solitary voice addressing an absent mistress; in the play the mistress answers, caps the conceits, corrects the theology of pilgrims and saints, and converts monologue into negotiation. The form is not merely displayed. It is invaded by dialogue, which is the later manner's principle arriving early.

### Q: What does Kermode make of the Queen Mab speech?

Kermode makes of Mercutio's Queen Mab speech the type of the early set piece: a bravura fantasy, some ninety lines of miniature pageant describing the fairy who drives over sleepers' faces, which halts the plot in Act 1 Scene 4 to exhibit what language can do when it is released from errand. In his survey the speech exemplifies the young dramatist's willingness to let a speech obey its own momentum, growing from a taunt about dreams into an independent verbal performance that Romeo must break off by force. The characterization is just, and the speech remains the strongest single exhibit for the placing of the play in the display manner. Its context adds the qualification the verdict requires: the play frames the fantasy as Mercutio's performance, admired and interrupted in one gesture, so that the set piece is also a portrait of the set piece's seductions.

### Q: Why does Kermode treat the laments of Act 4 Scene 5 as formal display?

Kermode treats the laments over Juliet's apparently dead body as formal display because the scene is constructed as a set composition: the bereaved family and Paris take up the grief in patterned succession, with refrains, balanced exclamations, and the machinery of the formal lament executed complete, while nothing in the speeches advances knowledge or decision. In the early manner as he describes it, grief is an occasion for the rhetoric of grief, and the scene is that rhetoric's fullest performance in the play. The treatment captures the scene's construction exactly. What the survey frame leaves out is the audience's position, unique in the theatre: the corpse is known to be sleeping, so the display is heard as display, a liturgy performed over a living girl. Editors such as Brian Gibbons defend the scene on just that ground, as convention knowingly staged rather than feeling naively discharged.

### Q: How does Kermode's account of the play differ from Harry Levin's?

Kermode's account differs from Harry Levin's in where it locates the play's formality. Levin, in "Form and Formality in Romeo and Juliet" in Shakespeare Quarterly in 1960, locates the formality inside the play, as its subject: a tragedy about young people escaping received forms of speech, in which rhyme, sonnet, and antithesis are the drama's materials and its argument, thinning and breaking as the lovers find a language of their own. Kermode locates the same formality inside the author's career, as the characteristic manner of the phase before 1600, of which the play is the most famous carrier. The two critics describe identical evidence, the same rhymes, sonnets, and balanced periods, and divide over its cause. This article adjudicates the difference by distribution: the patterning thickens where the old order speaks and breaks where feeling resists it, which is the behaviour of a design, not of a phase's weather.

### Q: What did Kermode object to in Caroline Spurgeon's method of counting images?

Kermode objected that counting images cannot recover what a play's language is doing, because language does its work in syntax, in the movement of sentences through minds, and a tally of images, however accurate, registers none of that movement. Caroline Spurgeon's *Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us* of 1935 counted the images of Romeo and Juliet and found the play drenched in light against darkness, a true finding that nonetheless cannot say why the light hurts or how a given speech earns its radiance. Kermode's own practice permits one crude quantity, the proportion of prose to verse, and treats the rest of measurement with suspicion. The adjudication in this article is double: his objection stands against counting offered as criticism, and falls as a philosophy of evidence, since his own developmental claim rests on measurable facts about rhyme, prose, and metre that counting alone supplies.

### Q: Does Kermode think Romeo and Juliet is badly written?

No, Kermode does not think Romeo and Juliet is badly written, and the survey's placing should not be read as that judgement. He treats the early plays as the work of a master already, and the tragedy as the lyric manner's most achieved specimen, full of poetry he plainly admires. The demotion critics resist is structural rather than stated: because the book's close attention is reserved for the plays after 1600, and because difficulty functions in the argument as a mark of the verse's maturity, the early play is left honoured and unexamined at once, a beautiful preliminary to the real subject. Kermode's own qualifications, that the connections between early and late remain detectable and that the early work was masterful before it changed, are genuine. The verdict of this article is that the ladder lives in the book's framing and reception more than in its sentences.

### Q: What does Russ McDonald answer to the developmental view?

Russ McDonald answers, in *Shakespeare and the Arts of Language* of 2001, that the early style's patterning is not a symptom of immaturity but the product of the most sophisticated verbal training the period possessed. Rebuilding the grammar-school world of imitation and figure, in which rhetoric was the technology of thought itself, he presents Shakespeare as the ideal student of Renaissance rhetoric and the early virtuosity, the cascades of antithesis, the patterned exchanges, the relish of set forms, as that training achieving its ends. McDonald's own history of the career is also developmental, moving from early enthusiasm through middle suspicion of words toward the last plays' renewed faith in language. His quarrel with Kermode is therefore over the sign of the early term: achievement of a different kind, to be judged by the ends it pursued, not a lesser draft of the later manner.

### Q: How does Brian Gibbons's edition present the play's formal patterning?

Brian Gibbons's Arden edition of 1980, based on the Second Quarto of 1599 with support from the First Quarto of 1597, presents the play's formal patterning as the meaning-bearing structure of a designed lyric tragedy. The editorial choice itself speaks: the fuller Q2 text preserves at length the patterned writing that Q1 compresses, the complete sonnet dialogue, the developed laments, the ceremonial exchanges entire, and Gibbons prints and annotates that formality as an instrument to be understood. His introduction defends the much-mocked lamentation scene by the conventions of formal lament the scene knowingly executes, and his commentary glosses rhyme schemes and set forms as design throughout. Later editors, Jill Levenson for Oxford and René Weis for the Arden Third Series, sustain the same presentation. In the tribunal of the editions, the patterning Kermode files under a phase is transmitted as the play's achievement.

### Q: What is a linguistic matrix in Kermode's argument?

A linguistic matrix, in Kermode's argument, is a recurrent pattern of language that becomes fundamental to a play's or a period's procedures, generating speeches and scenes rather than merely decorating them. His clearest instance is Hamlet, where he finds a steady stream of doubles, words paired and actions mirrored, until doubleness itself is the medium in which the drama thinks. Matrices matter to his thesis because they give the change of around 1600 a mechanism: the later verse is not simply harder word by word, it is organised by deeper generative habits that the audience absorbs without being told. Set against the play in this article, the concept cuts both ways. Antithesis functions as the early manner's matrix, generating the Prologue's balances, Romeo's oxymorons, and the feud's mirrored households alike, which confirms Kermode's description of the phase while suggesting the phase had matrices of its own, and used them knowingly.

### Q: Why did Kermode write Shakespeare's Language for readers outside the academy?

Kermode wrote for readers outside the academy because he believed the modern profession had abandoned the very readers who keep Shakespeare alive, and had let the fact that Shakespeare was a poet drop out of its consideration while every other aspect of the work was studied almost to death. The book accordingly carries no footnotes, conducts no bibliography wars, and proceeds by quotation and judgement in prose a general reader can follow. Richard Howard's endorsement, that it is the first book one should read about Shakespeare's plays and with those plays, caught the intention exactly. The choice had consequences beyond reach. A survey written for the common reader compresses, and the compression fell hardest on the early plays, Romeo and Juliet among them, whose treatment as emblems of a phase is partly the price of the book's admirable economy.

### Q: What evidence inside the play complicates Kermode's placing of its style?

The strongest complicating evidence is Juliet's potion speech in Act 4 Scene 3, where a heroine of the supposedly early manner speaks in broken, self-interrupting syntax, her imagination outrunning statement in a passage that meets every criterion Kermode reserves for the verse after 1600. Around it stand lesser complications: the shared sonnet invaded by dialogue at the feast; Juliet's oxymoron cascade in Act 3 Scene 2, which she argues through and out of; the laments of Act 4 Scene 5 staged to be judged by an audience who knows the corpse sleeps; and Mercutio's running satire on the Petrarchan machinery his friend wears. Together these passages show the play designing with its manner rather than merely exhibiting it. They do not disturb the chronology. They dismantle the inference from early to naive on which the placing's received authority rests.

### Q: What survives of Kermode's account if the idea of maturity is set aside?

Almost everything of value survives. Set aside the metaphor of maturity, and the account still gives the canon its map: a demonstrable change in the verse around 1600, described with an ear no critic of the period matched, and a description of the early manner, its end-stopped periods, its governing antitheses, its explicit rhetoric, its lyric display, that remains the most efficient true characterisation of this play's style. It also leaves standing the book's founding conviction, vindicated by the scholarship that followed it, that the language itself is a primary object of study and that Shakespeare's true biography is lodged in the plays. What falls is only the ranking the metaphor smuggled in: the assumption that the manner of the middle 1590s is a preliminary to be outgrown rather than a settlement to be understood on its own terms.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Kermode summary to argue that the play's style belongs to a phase of Shakespeare's development?

An essay should use the InsightCrunch Kermode summary by arguing from its strongest rows and conceding its weakest in advance. Begin with the measurements, the rhyme share unmatched in later tragedy, the sonnet architecture, the end-stopped periods, and let them establish the phase beyond dispute, since Kermode's chronology on this evidence is sound. Then engage the rows where the summary records divided standing, the sonnet's invasion by dialogue, the laments staged for judgement, the potion speech's later manner, and present them not as refutations but as the play's own commentary on its phase, the dramatist already auditing the style his period gave him. Close on the adjudicated position: the style belongs to a phase, and the play belongs to the small company of works that understand their phase from inside. That structure earns the developmental claim by surviving the evidence against it.
