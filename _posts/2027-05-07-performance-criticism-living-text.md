Performance criticism begins from an embarrassment that page-bound study prefers to ignore: the most consequential interpretations of Romeo and Juliet have not been written in books at all. They have been rehearsed, cast, choreographed, cut, and played before audiences, and most of them have vanished with the runs that housed them. When Peter Brook staged the play at Stratford in 1947 and cut the final reconciliation of the two houses, he published an argument about the ending more forceful than most journal articles of the decade: that the feud's peace is a formality the tragedy has not earned. When John Gielgud and Laurence Olivier alternated as Romeo and Mercutio at the New Theatre in 1935, a single production contained two incompatible accounts of both roles, and reviewers spent months adjudicating between them. Performance criticism is the discipline that takes such events seriously as criticism: it treats each production as a reading, each directorial choice as a claim about the text, and each staging decision as evidence that can be examined, compared, and argued with. Its founding document for this play is Harley Granville-Barker's preface in the Prefaces to Shakespeare, written by a director who approached the text as a score for voices and bodies rather than as a poem for the study. Its institutional charter came half a century later, when J. L. Styan named the stage-centred turn in Shakespeare studies "the Shakespeare revolution" and James C. Bulman's 1996 collection Shakespeare, Theory, and Performance gave the method its theoretical vocabulary.

![A stage production of Romeo and Juliet treated as an act of criticism - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-15.webp)

The verdict argued through this article is that performance criticism has earned its central claim, with one discipline imposed on it. The claim is that meaning in this play is made in performance and not merely illustrated there: the text of Romeo and Juliet underdetermines nearly everything that decides what an audience actually experiences, from the distance between a girl at a window and a boy in an orchard to the mechanics of the wound that kills Mercutio, and every production must settle those questions in ways that amount to interpretation. The discipline is the one forced on the method by its sharpest opponents: Lukas Erne's demonstration that Shakespeare also wrote for readers, and W. B. Worthen's warning that performance criticism betrays its own insight whenever it describes a staging as a reading of a text that remains the senior partner. A method that began by liberating the play from the page ends, at its best, in the position Jill Levenson built into her Oxford edition of the play: performance records and printed documents are two archives of the same work, and neither is complete without the other. The cliche this series tracks fixes the lovers inside a book, embalmed in admiration. Performance criticism restores the play to the place where it was always most alive and most argued about: a stage, in front of witnesses, on a particular night that will never recur.

## What Performance Criticism Claims: Meaning Is Made in the Event

The method rests on a single observation about how this text behaves. Romeo and Juliet is dense with instructions it does not contain. The words specify that Mercutio is wounded under Romeo's arm, and Mercutio himself supplies the fact in the line "I was hurt under your arm" (3.1.112), but no stage direction in any early edition explains how three men were arranged so that the wound could happen, whether Tybalt aimed at Romeo and struck the intervener, whether Romeo's body shielded the thrust or caused it, or whether Mercutio even saw the blade. Every performance answers those questions with bodies in space, and the answer determines whether the audience has watched an accident, a betrayal, or a murder. The same gap runs through the play at every scale. The text of the orchard scene places Juliet above and Romeo below and lets Romeo say "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2), but it does not fix the height, the distance, or whether Juliet can see the man she is addressing as she asks "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33). A production that sets her close enough to touch builds a scene of intimacy overheard; a production that sets her far off and high builds a scene of worship across a divide. Both productions speak identical words. They do not say the same thing.

Granville-Barker's generation gave this observation its first systematic form by treating the playscript as a score. A score is complete as a document and incomplete as an experience: it fixes pitches and durations while leaving tempo, phrasing, and colour to performers, and no musician supposes that the performance merely illustrates what the notation already fully says. The performance critics of Shakespeare extended the metaphor into a critical method. Bernard Beckerman's Shakespeare at the Globe, 1599-1609 (1962) reconstructed the physical and contractual conditions under which the plays were first produced, the daylight, the thrust stage, the boy actors, the repertory pace, so that the texts could be read as documents designed for those conditions rather than as closet drama that happened to be staged. The consequence for Romeo and Juliet was immediate. Read as a score for a daylight stage with no scenery to change, the play's structure of short alternating scenes stops looking like restlessness and starts looking like engineering: the action flows because the stage never empties for a set change, and the verse carries the location because the platform cannot. What a page critic registers as pace, a performance critic can explain as a function of boards, light, and the two hours the Chorus itself advertises when it calls the play "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, 12). The play's own Prologue, that is, describes the work as a timed theatrical event before it describes anything else about it. Performance criticism takes the Chorus at its word.

### What does it mean to call a playscript a score?

It means the text is treated as a complete set of instructions for an event it cannot fully determine, so that tempo, spacing, casting, and movement count as interpretation rather than decoration. Just as two orchestras playing one symphony produce two arguments about it, two companies playing one script produce two readings, and the differences between them are evidence.

The claim scales from mechanics to meaning. Consider the lovers' first meeting, which the text builds as a sonnet shared between two speakers (1.5.92-109). On the page, the shared sonnet is a fact about verse form, and formalist critics have written finely about it. In the theatre it is also a fact about two bodies: the sonnet's fourteen lines must be distributed as an approach, a negotiation conducted in the middle of a dance floor, with Tybalt watching from the edge of the scene and Capulet's feast moving around the pair. A production must decide whether the lovers are aware of constructing the sonnet together, whether the formality is a game both understand, a spell neither controls, or a ritual the older generation's world imposes on them. Each decision is available in the text and none is dictated by it. The performance critic's point is not that anything goes. It is that the text's openness at such moments is structural, and that criticism which ignores the decisions productions are forced to make is ignoring a large part of what the play, as a working artefact, actually contains. J. L. Styan's The Elements of Drama (1960) had already begun treating dramatic meaning as something realised between text, actor, and audience rather than stored in dialogue; the performance criticism of later decades radicalised the insight by insisting that the audience's side of the transaction, the witnesses in the dark, are part of the evidence too.

Tempo is the whole-play form of the same principle, and it exposes the variable at the largest scale. The Chorus promises two hours of traffic; a company that plays the full second-quarto text at a speaking pace that keeps the verse intelligible will run well past that promise, and a company that honours the promise must cut or drive the verse at a speed that becomes its own interpretation. There is no neutral solution, only a choice among claims: the expansive production claims that the play's world needs room to breathe and its subplots room to register; the driven production claims that velocity is the tragedy's medium and that Verona should feel like a place where nobody has time to think. Granville-Barker's preface argues the second claim from the text's construction; the long, ceremonious productions of the pictorial stage argued the first with their scene changes and intervals. The audience's body adjudicates between them in the simplest way, by feeling the evening as urgency or as pageant, and that feeling, aggregated across a run and recorded in reviews, is among the oldest continuous data series the method possesses. When the performance histories in the following cluster trace the play's changing running times and cutting patterns across the centuries, they will be reading, in effect, the minutes of this single long argument about what the play is for.

This is also why the method insists on the production, rather than the isolated performance, as its unit of study, while never forgetting that productions are made of unrepeatable nights. A production is a sustained argument mounted over weeks of rehearsal: a text chosen and cut, a company cast, a Verona designed, a sequence of fights choreographed by a specialist whose work is as interpretive as any editor's punctuation. The performance critic reads that argument the way a literary critic reads an essay, checking its claims against the text it claims to serve. When the argument fails, the failure is legible in the same terms: a Romeo and Juliet that cannot make the audience believe the feud will kill anyone has misread Act 1, whatever its programme notes assert, and a production whose Juliet seems to choose death lightly has misread the potion soliloquy, in which Juliet works through a catalogue of horrors, "I have a faint cold fear thrills through my veins, / That almost freezes up the heat of life" (4.3.24-25), before she drinks. The text disciplines the staging; the staging interrogates the text. Performance criticism is the name for criticism that keeps both directions of that traffic open.

## Granville-Barker's Preface: Criticism Written From the Rehearsal Room

Harley Granville-Barker came to this play with credentials no previous critic of it had held. He had been an actor, a playwright of the first rank, and the director of the Savoy Theatre productions of 1912 to 1914 that had restored speed, continuity, and ensemble playing to English Shakespeare: The Winter's Tale, Twelfth Night, and A Midsummer Night's Dream played on an apron stage with the text largely intact and the scenes flowing into one another, against every habit of the pictorial, heavily cut, star-centred Shakespeare he inherited. Between 1927 and 1947 he published the Prefaces to Shakespeare, play by play appreciations written from that practitioner's knowledge, and the preface to Romeo and Juliet is among the most searching of them. No production of this play by Granville-Barker exists in the record; the preface imagines one, and the imagining is the criticism. Where a literary critic asks what a scene means, Granville-Barker asks what a scene needs: how it must be prepared, what the actor's body is doing while the verse is spoken, where the audience's attention has to be standing when the blow falls. The separate article on [Granville-Barker's Preface to the Play](/2026/11/27/granville-barker-preface-analysis/) examines his scene-by-scene rulings in their own right; the concern here is what his method founded.

The founding move is a change of evidence. Granville-Barker treats the feelings a scene reliably produces in a theatre, its building tension, its release, its fatigue, as data about the text's construction. His preface reads the tragedy as an engineered sequence of effects: the street brawl's noise prepared so that the Prince's entrance lands as authority rather than exposition, the feast's brilliance built so that Tybalt's suppressed rage glows through it, the long stillness at the centre of the duel scene, where the play's noise stops and its catastrophe begins. Such claims cannot be verified by quotation alone, and Granville-Barker does not pretend otherwise. They are verified, or refuted, in rehearsal and in the house, by whether the effect arrives when the preparation is made. This is why his preface keeps returning to pace. The play he describes is one whose meaning lives in its momentum: a tragedy of haste, "star-cross'd" and swift, in which the lovers' speed and the feud's speed are the same speed. A production that lingers, that lets scenes settle into pictures, is for Granville-Barker not a different flavour of the play but a misreading of it, a failure to perform what the text's construction demands. Criticism of this kind can be wrong, and can be shown to be wrong on a stage; that falsifiability is precisely what makes it criticism rather than appreciation.

His preface's attention to the play's double time shows the method at its most characteristic. The tragedy runs on two clocks at once, a public calendar of feasts, duels, and a wedding day fixed for Thursday, and a compressed private time in which the lovers live a whole marriage between a Sunday dance and a Thursday dawn. A reader can hold the contradiction in suspension; a company cannot, because its actors must decide, scene by scene, how much time a night, a journey to Mantua, a friar's errand is allowed to take, and the decisions either expose the play's haste as its subject or blur it into vagueness. Granville-Barker's insistence on unbroken flow is his answer: play the scenes into one another without scenic pauses, let the public clock be heard ticking inside the private one, and the play's famous speed stops being a defect of construction and becomes the felt experience of its theme. The advice is a century old and remains the single most consequential staging instruction the play has received, because it converts a bibliographical puzzle into an audience's sensation.

The Prefaces also modelled a new relationship between scholarship and the stage. Granville-Barker quarried the early texts and the theatre history of the play with a scholar's appetite, but he put every finding to a practitioner's question: what follows for the playing? His respect for the full text was itself a production argument, since his Savoy work had shown that Shakespeare played swiftly and continuously did not need the Victorian manager's scissors. The performance critics who followed him inherited both halves of that settlement: the archive matters, and the archive is not enough. A promptbook, an early quarto, a review of a dead production are evidence about events in theatres; the events are the object of study. It is worth registering what Granville-Barker did not claim. He did not claim that a production could mean anything its director pleased, and his preface polices the text's demands as strictly as any editor. His theatre was a place where the play's effects were tested, and the test could fail. The later history of performance criticism is, in large part, the history of institutions catching up with that position and then arguing about how far it should be pushed.

## The Shakespeare Revolution: How the Method Took Over the Institutions

For three decades after Granville-Barker, his position survived mainly as a practitioner's creed honoured by directors and quoted by the more theatrical reviewers. Its conquest of the academy came in the generation of J. L. Styan and Bernard Beckerman, and Styan gave the conquest its name. In The Shakespeare Revolution: Criticism and Performance in the Twentieth Century (1977), Styan argued that twentieth-century Shakespeare studies had been transformed by a stage-centred reorientation: criticism had learned to treat the plays as scripts for performance, to recover the conditions of the original staging, and to judge interpretations by their life in the theatre. Beckerman's work on the Globe's playing conditions and Styan's own analyses of dramatic experience gave the revolution its scholarly instruments. The phrase stuck because it described something verifiable in the record: journals founded performance sections, doctoral theses began citing productions alongside editions, and the Shakespeare Association's conferences acquired the panels on staging that an earlier generation would have considered journalism.

The revolution's second wave was theoretical, and it arrived with James C. Bulman's Shakespeare, Theory, and Performance (1996), the collection that marks the method's maturity and its first serious self-examination. Bulman's introduction honoured Styan while correcting him on the point that mattered most. Styan's revolution, Bulman argued, had kept one foot in the old world: it still treated Shakespeare's texts as stable and authoritative, their meanings immanent in the words, with actors and directors as interpreters of a significance that existed before they arrived. What that picture omitted was the radical contingency of performance, the unpredictable intersection of bodies, occasions, politics, and weather by which a night's meaning is actually made. The essays Bulman gathered, written from feminist, psychoanalytic, cultural materialist, and deconstructive positions, treated productions not as illustrations of readings achieved elsewhere but as the places where readings happen. Stage history, long tolerated as an antiquarian appendix to real criticism, attached itself to theory and was transformed by the attachment.

### What changed when critics began treating productions as evidence?

Three things changed at once. Productions acquired the status of arguments, citable and contestable like essays. The archive of theatre, promptbooks, reviews, photographs, designs, became a resource with standards of proof. And readers gained a new test for any claim about a scene: whether the claim survives contact with the physical facts of playing it, its sightlines, timings, and bodies.

The institutional settlement that followed can be measured in the apparatus of the discipline. Manchester University Press's Shakespeare in Performance series commissioned a volume on each play written from the production record; Jill Levenson's volume on this play appeared in 1987, and James N. Loehlin's Romeo and Juliet for Cambridge's Shakespeare in Production series followed in 2002, its commentary organised passage by passage around what productions had done with the words. The great editions absorbed the method, as later sections of this article show in Levenson's Oxford text. University syllabuses began setting production viewings beside textual study, and the criticism of a single famous staging could carry the weight of a monograph. None of this made performance criticism immune to error, and its own practitioners were the first to catalogue its temptations: the anecdote dressed as analysis, the single attended night inflated into a law, the director's programme note mistaken for the production's meaning. Styan's revolution, like most revolutions, produced an establishment, and the establishment's sacred cows came under fire from within, most searchingly from W. B. Worthen, whose objection is given its full hearing below. First, though, the method deserves to be watched at work on the scenes where it has most changed what can be said.

## The Verse in the Mouth: Delivery as Evidence

A playscript's least visible variable is the one audiences feel first: the sound of the verse as a particular company speaks it. The text of this play is unusually musical in its construction, built from sonnets, shared lines, stichomythia, formal laments, and set speeches that behave like arias, and every one of those forms poses a delivery question that print cannot answer. Is the verse to be spoken as thought, quick, irregular, and psychological, or as music, patterned and sustained, its architecture audible? The choice is a reading of the play's world. A company that speaks the lovers' exchanges as heightened music asserts that Verona's young people inhabit a formal order their passion strains against; a company that roughens the verse into naturalism asserts that the passion is the reality and the form its disguise. Granville-Barker's preface, with its insistence that the play's verse be taken at speed and its structures kept clear, argued the first position from the practitioner's side: the formality is the meaning's carrier, and mumbling it into psychology destroys evidence. The stage history of the play is, in part, the history of that argument being rerun by each generation's vocal fashions.

The Queen Mab speech is the repertory's standard test of the question, because the text gives Mercutio a detachable showpiece, "O, then, I see Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4.53), and then surrounds it with listeners whose reactions the actor must manage. Spoken as a party trick that darkens by degrees into something involuntary and frightening, the speech becomes a diagnosis of Mercutio: the wit possessed by his own invention, the play's gaiety showing its underside of obsession. Spoken as pure display, it becomes a celebration of the same wit, and Romeo's interruption, "Peace, peace, Mercutio, peace! / Thou talk'st of nothing" (1.4.95-96), lands as affectionate deflation rather than alarm. Reviewers of the 1935 production singled out Gielgud's Queen Mab above other attempts at the role, and the singling out is legible in performance terms: his musical delivery made the speech's turn from glitter to disturbance audible as a single arc, an effect available only in the ear and only on the night. No analysis of the speech's imagery, however fine, can substitute for that datum, because the datum is about what the speech does in time.

Delivery decides quieter questions everywhere. The lovers' dialogue is laced with shared verse lines, single pentameters split between two speakers, and the split is a tempo instruction the text does contain: the line completes only if the second speaker answers on the beat. Companies that honour the joins produce the famous effect of two minds thinking in one rhythm, the formal correlative of the shared sonnet; companies that pause for psychology between the halves produce two solitudes exchanging speeches. The laments over Juliet's supposed corpse in Act 4 Scene 5 pose the opposite test, a formal, almost operatic ensemble of grief, "O woeful day!" answered and re-answered, which modern companies often cut or ironise because its ritual shape embarrasses naturalistic taste. A performance critic reads the embarrassment itself as evidence: the scene's ritual is the text's argument that Verona's grief is a public performance long before it is a private feeling, and a production that plays the lament straight discovers the argument in its own discomfort. Even the Nurse's prose, sprawling and repetitive on the page, is a delivery problem with a thesis inside it: played for comedy alone, she is the play's licensed fool; played with her grief and her final pragmatic betrayal given full weight, she becomes the measure of what Verona's world does to loyalty. The verse and the prose are the score's finest notation, and the companies that play them are its first critics.

### Why is the Queen Mab speech a test of a whole production?

Because it concentrates every decision a company must make: tempo, tone, the turn from wit to disturbance, and the management of listeners onstage. A production that has thought about Mercutio will show the thinking inside those ninety lines, and one that has not will expose the gap there first, which is why reviewers reach for the speech as their sample.

## The Window, Not the Balcony: What Staging Adds to the Orchard Scene

No scene in the play demonstrates the method's thesis more cleanly than the orchard scene, because no scene's most famous feature exists in the text at all. Generations of spectators carry away an image of Juliet on a balcony, and the image is so potent that the scene is named for the architecture. Yet the early texts give Juliet a window, not a balcony: the second quarto's direction brings her in above, at her window, and the scholar Lois Leveen established in 2014 that neither the quartos nor the Folio mentions a balcony anywhere. The balcony is a contribution of the theatre, an accretion of staging tradition that hardened into supposed fact, the stage's own interpolation into the play the world thinks it knows. A page-bound criticism can note the error and correct it. Performance criticism asks the more interesting question: what work was the interpolation doing, that it proved so durable? The answer discloses a reading. The balcony externalises the scene's structure of rank and access: the beloved elevated, enclosed, framed like an icon in a shrine, the lover below in the dark among the trees, trespassing. The architecture stages the feud's geography in a single image, her father's house made visible as the obstacle, and it gives the audience the erotic charge of a threshold that can be looked across and spoken across but not crossed. The tradition kept the balcony because the balcony is an interpretation, and a strong one, of "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44): the words argue that surfaces and labels are nothing, while the staging shows two people for whom surfaces, walls, names, and houses are everything.

Once the accretion is recognised as an interpretation, its variations become legible as arguments. Height is a variable with meaning. A Juliet set barely above Romeo's reach turns the scene into a conspiracy of near-equals, dangerous because it is almost possible; a Juliet set high and remote turns it into devotion addressed upward, dangerous because it is impossible. Distance does similar work. The scene opens with Romeo already inside the orchard, having scaled the walls, watching Mercutio's mockery fade behind him and then watching Juliet appear before she knows he exists: "He jests at scars that never felt a wound. / But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.1-2). For the scene's first movement the audience shares Romeo's position as hidden witness to a soliloquy, and the production must decide how long Juliet remains unaware, how the revelation of his presence is timed, and whether her startled "How camest thou hither, tell me, and wherefore?" (2.2.62) is played as fear, delight, or the swift practicality the text supports. These are not decorations on a meaning settled in advance. They are the meaning's delivery system, and different deliveries have produced the prudish Juliet, the ardent Juliet, and the Juliet who runs the scene like a negotiation, each with the same lines.

Film, which the performance-history articles in this series treat in their own right, enters here only as the method's limiting case. Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film abolished the vertical axis altogether and set the lovers' exchange at the level of a swimming pool, Juliet descending to Romeo rather than Romeo gazing up: a directorial claim that the scene's truth is mutual immersion, not aspiration, and a claim argued entirely in staging. W. B. Worthen took that film as his closing exhibit in an essay on the authority of the text in performance, precisely because its liberties are so legible as interpretation. The pool and the balcony are the same critical gesture four centuries apart: the theatre answering a question the text poses and declines to settle. Performance criticism's contribution is to make such gestures citable, to insist that the archive of stagings is a record of the play being thought about by other means.

### Why does the orchard scene change more between productions than any other scene?

Because the text fixes its words and almost nothing else: a height without a measurement, a window without an architecture, a listener hidden for as long as a director chooses. Every era rebuilds the scene from its own assumptions about privacy, courtship, and danger, so its stagings form the densest record of the play being reinterpreted that the theatre offers.

## The Chorus Addresses the House: The Play's First Performance Critic

Before any director interprets this play, the play interprets itself to its audience, in the person of the Chorus. The Prologue sonnet performs the founding act of performance criticism two centuries before the profession existed: it tells the house what kind of event it is attending, how long the event will run, what pattern to watch for, and how to judge the performers' labour, closing with a request that the audience's patient ears attend what the actors' toil shall strive to mend. That is a critical contract, offered in the theatre, about the theatre. The second Chorus, opening Act 2, renews the contract with a summary of the story so far and a forecast of its difficulties, doing in fourteen lines what reviewers would later do in columns: situating the new movement against the old, and instructing the house where its sympathies should stand. A criticism that ignores the Chorus ignores the play's own demonstration that its meanings were designed to be mediated, framed, and pre-digested for witnesses, which is the performance condition Granville-Barker and his successors simply took seriously.

Productions vote on the Chorus with their scissors, and the votes are readable. To cut the Prologues entire is to claim that the play needs no frame and that its surprises should be allowed to ambush; the cost is the peculiar irony Shakespeare built, an audience that knows the ending from the first minute and watches knowledge itself become the tragedy's instrument, every happy moment shadowed by the announced grave. To keep the Chorus is to claim that foreknowledge is the meaning: Verona's citizens and the play's spectators share the condition of watching a catastrophe they can describe and cannot prevent. To reassign the Chorus, to a single actor, a company in unison, a figure doubled with the Prince or the Friar, is to editorialize further, lodging the play's foreknowledge in authority or in complicity. Luhrmann's film made the boldest reassignment of the modern record by handing the Prologue to a television newsreader, a choice Worthen analysed as the film's thesis about where public narrative lives: the feud as broadcast item, the star-crossed lovers as the day's story, announced in advance by the medium that announces everything. Each treatment is a piece of criticism about mediation itself, and each is legible only to a method that counts framing decisions as arguments.

The Chorus also disciplines the audience's relationship to the Friar's plotting and the lovers' secrecy, because a house that has been told the ending watches concealment differently: the orchard vows, the cell marriage, the potion plan are all performed under the shadow of announced failure, and the spectators are made complicit in the dramatic irony rather than merely surprised by outcomes. Productions that play the middle acts for suspense, trimming the Chorus and hurrying the omens, trade that complicity for shock, and the trade is a thesis about where the play's power sits. The performance archive preserves the whole spectrum of these decisions in promptbooks across four centuries, and the pattern they form is among the strongest exhibits for the method's value: left to themselves, companies keep rediscovering the Chorus question, because the text builds the question in and refuses, with unusual explicitness, to answer it for them.

## The Fight in Act 3: Choreography as Critical Argument

If the orchard scene shows staging adding what the text omits, the duel scene shows staging deciding what the text deliberately leaves undecided. The catastrophe of Romeo and Juliet turns on a few seconds of stage violence in Act 3 Scene 1, and the text's account of those seconds is a study in withheld information. Romeo enters committed to peace, refuses Tybalt's challenge in language Mercutio reads as dishonour, and interposes himself between the fighters at the moment of engagement. Tybalt's blade reaches Mercutio under Romeo's arm. Mercutio's own diagnosis is double: "A plague o' both your houses!" (3.1.99) distributes the blame to the feud itself, while "I was hurt under your arm" (3.1.112) fixes the physical fact that Romeo's peacemaking body was the wound's occasion. Whether it was also its cause, whether Tybalt lunged at Romeo and Mercutio intercepted, whether Romeo held Mercutio's sword arm, whether the whole exchange was horseplay that tipped, the text does not say. A fight director must say. The choreography of those seconds is a verdict on Romeo's culpability, on the feud's mechanics, and on the genre of the play the audience has been watching, delivered without a word of commentary.

Performance criticism learned to read such verdicts by comparing productions. In Franco Zeffirelli's handling, on stage at the Old Vic in 1960 and then in his 1968 film, the street violence begins in bravado, boys showing off with blades, and escalates past the point where anyone can stop it; Stephen Orgel praised the film's duel as a presentation of bravado getting out of control, and the praise identifies the reading exactly: the feud kills by momentum, not by malice, and Mercutio dies of a game. Peter Brook's 1947 Stratford production argued the opposite temperament: Brook stripped the play toward speed and brutality, and in his Verona the violence was never a game, only a habit waiting for its occasion. Between those poles every staging of the scene takes a position, and the position propagates through the whole interpretation. A production in which Tybalt is a murderer makes Romeo's answering kill, and his cry of "I am fortune's fool!" (3.1.131), a chapter in a revenge action. A production in which the wound is a freak of timing makes the same cry the play's thesis about accident. The words are constant; the tragedy is not.

The fight also supplies the cleanest test case for the traffic between performance criticism and its page-bound neighbours. Susan Snyder's landmark account of the play's form, examined in the article on [Susan Snyder: Comedy Turning to Tragedy](/2026/12/11/susan-snyder-comedy-to-tragedy/), argues that the play runs on comedy's machinery until Mercutio's death breaks the pattern and tragedy takes over the materials comedy assembled. That is a claim about structure made from the page. The theatre tests it nightly, because a production either feels like one kind of play before the duel and another after it, or it does not, and audiences register the hinge in their bodies before any critic names it. Directors who stage the first half's street scenes as genuine comedy, all swagger and display, and then let the duel curdle out of the same idiom are performing Snyder's thesis whether or not they have read her. Directors who darken the opening brawl, making the feud lethal from the first thumb-bite, "Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" (1.1.41), are performing a refutation. Performance criticism does not merely borrow such debates from literary scholarship; it maintains the laboratory in which they are continuously, publicly rerun.

### Can a fight director change the meaning of Mercutio's death?

Yes, within limits the text sets. The choreography decides whether the wound is accident, treachery, or the feud's routine product, and each decision redistributes blame among Tybalt, Romeo, and Verona itself. What choreography cannot do is acquit the scene of consequence: the text fixes the curse, the banishment, and the speed of everything after, whatever the blades decide.

## Verona Translated: Setting, Design, and Each Era's Divided City

A production's Verona is its first and largest claim about the play, made before a word is spoken. The text supplies a city defined less by geography than by a condition: two households, a watchful prince, streets that belong to the feud, and private interiors where the feud's writ runs differently. Every design translates that condition into a world the audience recognises, and the translation is an argument about what the feud is. Zeffirelli's Verona, on the Old Vic stage in 1960 and again on film, was a sunlit, crowded, working Italian city, its violence erupting out of festival noise and market-day heat; the reading embedded in the design is that the tragedy is a by-product of abundance, energy with nowhere lawful to go. Otomar Krejča's production at the Prague National Theatre in 1963, mounted inside Cold War Czechoslovakia, built the opposite Verona, a city of negligent age and disaffected youth, and Peter Brook, seeing it in Paris, declared it the best production of the tragedy he had seen; the superlative from the era's most exacting director records what the design had argued, that the play's true subject was the generations' failure to hear each other, staged where that failure was the local politics. Neither production altered a line to make its case. The cases were made in stone, cloth, light, and the ages of the crowds that filled the streets.

Modern-dress production extends the same logic to its limit, dressing the feud in the uniforms of whichever division the audience outside the theatre is living: gang colours, militia fatigues, the suits of rival firms. Performance criticism treats these settings neither as gimmicks nor as neutral updates but as hypotheses, testable in the event. A setting earns its place when it finds, in the text, the structures it claims to illuminate: the Prince's exhausted jurisprudence, "I have an interest in your hate's proceeding, / My blood for your rude brawls doth lie a-bleeding" (3.1.187-188), becomes a different speech in the mouth of a mayor, a general, or a police chief, and the audience learns which of those officers the production believes Escalus to be. A setting fails when it decorates without corresponding, leaving the verse to fight the wardrobe. The method's point, once again, is evidentiary: design choices that correspond unlock passages, and the unlocking can be checked against the text by any spectator with the book open afterwards.

Intercultural production carries the argument across languages, where the evidence changes kind. W. B. Worthen's later work on Shakespeare in performance gives extended attention to the Brazilian company Grupo Galpão, whose Romeu e Julieta played at Shakespeare's Globe in London in 2000 in a street-theatre idiom of circus, music, and popular Catholic festivity. A Brazilian Romeo inside the reconstructed Elizabethan playhouse is a controlled experiment in the method's central question: which of the play's meanings survive translation out of their language, their period, and their stage conventions, and which prove to have been local all along. Worthen's interest in the production was precisely its value as criticism by other means, a company reading the tragedy through the performance traditions of Minas Gerais and finding, in the feud's festivity and the lovers' carnival defiance, structures that Anglophone naturalism had underplayed. Performance criticism claims such productions for its archive on the same terms as Gielgud's: they are arguments, mounted in public, about what the play contains.

## Two Romeos in One Run: Casting as Interpretation

In the autumn of 1935, at the New Theatre in London, a single production of this play performed an experiment that performance criticism has used as evidence ever since. John Gielgud directed the production and, with Laurence Olivier, alternated the roles of Romeo and Mercutio across the run, with Peggy Ashcroft as Juliet throughout; the production ran for 186 performances. The alternation began partly as insurance, Gielgud doubting whether the younger actor could carry Romeo, but it became the production's meaning. The same text, the same sets, the same Juliet, and two Romeos who embodied opposed accounts of the part: Olivier's Romeo was passionate and physically impulsive, admired for its ardour and faulted for rough verse-speaking; Gielgud's was lyrical, musical, built out of the verse itself. Reviewers took sides and kept score. Ashcroft, who played opposite both, later judged the mixed pairing, Olivier's Romeo against Gielgud's Mercutio, the definitive one, which is itself a critical verdict of a kind no essay can replicate: the judgment of the actor inside the scene. For the performance critic, the 1935 alternation is the cleanest demonstration in the play's history that a role is not a fixed quantity the text hands down. Romeo, as a meaning, changed with the body and the voice that carried him, while every word stayed in place.

Casting is the production decision least disguisable as anything but interpretation, and this play exposes it with unusual force because the text is explicit about the fact that matters most: Juliet is not yet fourteen. Capulet states it in the second scene, "She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2.9), and the Nurse returns to the count obsessively. A production must therefore decide, in the visible bodies of its actors, whether the audience is watching children destroyed by an adult world's machinery or young adults destroyed by their own haste, and the decision reorganises every judgment the audience will make about parents, friar, and lovers. George Cukor's 1936 film cast Leslie Howard and Norma Shearer, whose combined age passed seventy-five, and made the tragedy a romance of mature sensibility; Franco Zeffirelli, first at the Old Vic in 1960 with John Stride and a twenty-three-year-old Judi Dench, then in his 1968 film with leads of seventeen and fifteen, made it a tragedy of adolescence, its catastrophe chargeable to the grown world that armed the children and then mislaid the letter. Neither reading is an embellishment on a neutral core. Each is a claim about where the play's guilt resides, argued in the only medium that can argue it: flesh.

The casting question reaches into the play's critical debates and gives performance a vote in them. Coppelia Kahn's feminist account of the play, treated in full in the article on [Coppelia Kahn: Coming of Age in Verona](/2026/12/18/coppelia-kahn-coming-of-age-verona/), reads the tragedy as a rite of passage administered by the fathers, in which Verona's sons can become men only inside the feud and its daughters are denied an adolescence altogether. A production tests that thesis with its casting and its handling of Capulet in Act 3 Scene 5, where the father's wooing of Paris curdles into threats and blows: stage the scene with a Juliet visibly a child and a Capulet whose violence fills the room, and Kahn's reading stops being a thesis and becomes the audience's experience; soften the violence into bluster and cast a Juliet who towers over her father, and the thesis strains. Performance criticism does not claim the stage settles such arguments. It claims the stage is evidence in them, of a kind that scholars ignore at the price of arguing about a play no audience has ever seen.

Doubling belongs to the same family of decisions, quieter than casting the leads and often as eloquent. A company that doubles the Chorus with the Prince makes the voice that frames the story the voice that closes it in judgment, an assertion that Verona's narration and Verona's law are one institution. A company that doubles Mercutio's actor into a later role keeps the play's lost wit visibly haunting the tragedy he no longer speaks in. The original company worked with a small body of sharers and hired men, and doubling was its economic necessity; the modern company inherits the device as a choice, and choices are the method's currency. Even costume carries the argument at this level of granularity: whether the lovers are dressed as their houses' heirs or dressed against them, whether Juliet's wedding clothes and her shroud are the same garment carried through, whether the feud has colours the audience learns to read before a word of exposition. Design and doubling rarely earn paragraphs in page criticism. In the theatre they are sentences in the production's argument, and the performance critic is the reader those sentences have.

## The Ending in the Theatre: Statues, Silence, and the Last Word

Every account of this play must finally answer for its last five minutes, and productions answer with their scissors and their courage. The text closes in a crowded tomb and an exposition: the Friar's long narration, the Prince's distribution of blame and pardon, the fathers' rivalry transposed into gold, Montague promising a statue of Juliet "in pure gold" and Capulet matching it, and the Prince's closing couplet, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). Page critics have quarrelled over whether this closure is earned. The theatre settles the quarrel afresh at every performance, because the ending's emotional truth is a function of how it is played: the statues can be staged as the feud's final obscenity, the old men still competing, now in grief and precious metal, or as the first disinterested act of two houses that have run out of children to spend. The handshake can be believed or shown as a photo opportunity for the Prince's Verona. Even the Prince's "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" (5.3.305) can fall as benediction or as a politician's summary over bodies he failed to protect, and audiences know the difference in their spines.

Peter Brook's 1947 Stratford production made the most famous directorial intervention in this ending: he excluded the final reconciliation of the families from his performance text altogether. The cut is a piece of criticism as exact as any paragraph. It asserts that the peace is unearned by anything the tragedy has shown, that the feud's machinery would digest the lovers' deaths as it digested everything else, and that an audience should leave with the waste undiluted by ceremony. One may dispute the assertion, and performance critics have, but its form deserves notice: Brook argued about the ending by means of the ending, in the medium the ending was built for. His governing maxim, that a production is only correct at the moment of its correctness and only good at the moment of its success, is the method's pragmatism in one sentence: staging claims are validated in the event, before witnesses, or not at all. Barbara Hodgdon's The End Crowns All (1991) supplied the theoretical frame for such work, treating Renaissance endings as transactions completed by spectators rather than by texts, the audience's assent or resistance being the final material the playwright writes in. A Romeo and Juliet whose reconciliation wins that assent has demonstrated something about the text's design; one whose statues curdle in the mouth has demonstrated something else. Both demonstrations are data.

Inside the ending sits a smaller performance that companies handle as nervously as the reconciliation itself: the Friar's narration. For some forty lines the Friar retells the entire play to the assembled city, "I will be brief, for my short date of breath / Is not so long as is a tedious tale" (5.3.229-230), and then performs the story the audience has just watched, in summary, for listeners who arrive too late. The passage is a standing temptation to the scissors, since it tells the house what it already knows, and the promptbook record shows it cut again and again across the centuries. Performance criticism reads the temptation and the resistance to it as a genuine critical fork. Cut the narration and the ending moves at the speed of discovery, the city's knowledge arriving as action. Keep it, and the ending acquires a second subject: the conversion of the lovers' private catastrophe into the city's public story, the first retelling of many, performed by the one man whose schemes made the story possible and whose confession is also his defence. A production that keeps the speech whole is arguing that the play ends not with the deaths but with the beginning of the legend, the moment Verona starts doing to the lovers what four centuries of theatres have done since: narrating them, framing them, and arguing about what their story means. The parallel is not fanciful. The Friar is the play's first performance critic, assembling evidence, assigning causes, and offering the Prince a reading of events whose adequacy the audience, alone in possession of the whole event, is positioned to judge.

### Why do some productions cut the final reconciliation?

Because the ending's peace is the play's contestable claim, and cutting is the strongest form disagreement can take. Brook's 1947 removal of the reconciliation read the feud as unrepentant and the statues as its last transaction. Keeping it is an argument too: that grief has bankrupted the old men, and that the text's closure should be tested, not pre-judged.

## Ghosting: Why No Audience Sees This Play Innocently

Marvin Carlson's The Haunted Stage: The Theatre as Memory Machine (2001) gave performance criticism its account of the audience's side of the event. Carlson's term is ghosting: the process by which everything in a theatre, the bodies, the materials, the language, the space itself, is received through memories of earlier encounters. A spectator does not meet a Romeo; a spectator meets this Romeo against the Romeos already carried in memory, the school production, the famous film, the beloved actor of a generation ago. Herbert Blau's observation, which Carlson takes up, is that in the theatre audiences are always seeing what they saw before, the present perception doubled by recollection. For most plays this is a condition of reception. For Romeo and Juliet it approaches the substance of the work itself, since no play in the repertory arrives more heavily freighted. Zeffirelli's teenage lovers of 1968 installed themselves in the memory of millions and have ghosted every subsequent stage pair, who are praised or blamed in the film's vocabulary of youth and sunlight whether they invoke it or fight it. A production that casts older lovers is not merely choosing an interpretation; it is declaring war on a ghost, and the audience's friction with the memory is part of the meaning the production makes.

Ghosting explains several otherwise puzzling features of the play's performance life. It explains why certain choices become traditions that companies inherit without deciding: the balcony itself is a ghost, architecture remembered from production to production until it feels like text. It explains why the alternation experiment of 1935 still organises discussion of the roles, Gielgud's Mercutio remaining the standard against which Queen Mab speeches are measured by people who never heard him. And it explains the peculiar authority of first encounters: the production through which a spectator first meets the play becomes their private text, against which editions are later judged. Performance criticism absorbs Carlson's point as a methodological caution and a resource. The caution is that no account of a production describes an object all its witnesses shared, since each witness watched a slightly different, ghost-ridden event. The resource is that the ghosts are themselves evidence: the history of what audiences remember, and misremember, is a record of the play's meanings in the culture, and for this play that record is unusually rich, unusually documented, and unusually continuous from the Restoration forward.

The multiplication of recordings has intensified ghosting into a condition companies must plan around. A spectator may arrive at a stage production having absorbed the famous film versions entire, scene by scene, so that the live event is received as a variation on a screened original rather than as the thing itself. Directors answer the condition in two opposed ways, and the opposition is a critical debate conducted in staging. Some productions quote the ghosts deliberately, restaging a beloved image, the balcony's composition, the film's sunlit piazza, so that recognition itself becomes part of the evening's meaning and the audience watches its own memory being handled. Others refuse every inherited image, stripping the feast, relocating the orchard, recostuming the feud, in an attempt to return the house to first sight. Neither strategy escapes Carlson's law, since refusal is also a relation to the remembered production, legible only against it. Performance criticism's task in such cases is to name the ghost in the room and grade the production's negotiation with it, which is work no page-bound method is equipped to do, because the object under study is not the text, and not even the production, but the charged space between the production and everything its audience has seen before.

## Original Practices: Reconstruction as a Form of Criticism

The most theatrical branch of the method tests readings by rebuilding the conditions under which the play was first playable. The reconstruction of Shakespeare's Globe on Bankside, opened in 1997 after Sam Wanamaker's long campaign, gave scholars something no archive could: a working instrument. Its productions, and the original-practices experiments mounted there and elsewhere in period clothing, shared daylight, and direct address, function as experiments in Dessen's sense, staging the conventions his books recovered from the texts and discovering which of them still operate. When the audience shares the actors' light, the soliloquy stops being a private meditation overheard and becomes a public act of persuasion, and scenes like Juliet's potion soliloquy change their nature: "O, if I wake, shall I not be distraught, / Environed with all these hideous fears?" (4.3.48-49) addressed outward to a visible crowd is a heroine recruiting witnesses, not a girl thinking aloud. The discovery is a critical result, obtained by construction rather than by argument, and it revises page readings that assumed the soliloquies were interior.

Casting supplies the experiment's sharpest variable. The first Juliet was a boy, a fact the text never mentions and the original audience never forgot, since the convention was the water they swam in. Original-practices casting restores the convention and lets a modern house measure what it did to the play's meanings: the erotics of the orchard scene mediated through a performed femininity the audience simultaneously credits and knows to be artifice, the Nurse's bawdy landing differently, Capulet's disposal of his daughter exposed as a transaction conducted entirely among males. Most companies treat the restored convention as one variable among many rather than as a recovered authenticity, and the performance critics who write about such productions are correspondingly careful: reconstruction cannot reproduce the first audience, whose ghosts were different, but it can falsify claims about what the text requires. A scene said to be unplayable without scenery that plays perfectly well on bare boards in daylight has had a scholarly myth removed from it. That removals of this kind are possible at all is the branch's contribution: some propositions about the play are not matters of taste, and the rebuilt theatre is where they go to be settled.

The Globe experiment also clarified the method's relationship to its own nostalgia. Worthen's writing on the rebuilt Globe treats the building neither as a time machine nor as a fraud but as a performance in its own right, a modern argument about the past built in oak and thatch, whose productions argue with the heritage industry as much as with the playtexts. That doubleness is the honest condition of all reconstruction criticism: the evidence it generates is real, and so is the modernity of the questions it asks. A Juliet in original dress, lit by afternoon sun, addressing a standing crowd, demonstrates things about Act 4 that no seminar can, and demonstrates them to spectators whose responses are formed by cinema and the proscenium arch. Performance criticism has learned to hold both halves of that sentence without embarrassment, which is a fair definition of its maturity.

## The Director as Critic: A New Author in the Theatre

Performance criticism could only arise when the theatre produced a figure whose work it could study, and that figure is the modern director. Elizabethan companies had no director in the modern sense; interpretation was distributed among the sharers, the playwright, and the book-holder, and a staging's argument belonged to no single mind. The director as a production's unifying intelligence is a creation of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and Granville-Barker stands at its headwaters in England: an actor-manager who conceived a Shakespeare production whole, its text, tempo, design, and ensemble, and who then wrote criticism explaining the conception. From him descends the figure the twentieth-century stage made familiar, the director as the production's author, whose name heads the reviews and whose reading the company embodies. Gielgud in 1935, Brook in 1947, Zeffirelli in 1960: the performance archive of this play is organised by directors' names because directors became the persons whose interpretations productions are.

The development doubled the method's evidence and its difficulties. A director's reading is recoverable in a way a distributed company's never was: it leaves interviews, rehearsal records, designs commissioned to a brief, and a promptbook that reflects one intelligence's decisions, so the critic can reconstruct an argument and hold it against the text with unusual precision. Brook's excision of the reconciliation, Zeffirelli's casting of adolescents, Krejča's generational Verona are all legible as propositions because a single mind can be inferred behind them. But the director's authorship also tempted the theatre's public language into the vocabulary Worthen diagnosed, the director as licensed libertine or sworn servant of the text, and tempted critics into treating the programme note, the director's own account of the reading, as the production's meaning rather than as one more interested testimony about it. The performed event routinely escapes its author's intentions, in the playing of a scene by actors who find more in it, in an audience that laughs or refuses to laugh where laughter was budgeted. The mature performance critic therefore treats the director as the production's first critic rather than its final one: the reading that rehearsal proposes, which the run then amends nightly in public.

This is why the method, at its best, changed the status of everyone in the rehearsal room. The fight director became a critic of Act 3, the designer a critic of Verona, the verse coach a critic of the play's music, and the actors, whose testimony Ashcroft exemplifies, critics with the standing of witnesses rather than illustrators. A literary culture that had reserved the title of critic for those who write about plays learned to extend it to those who answer plays with productions. The extension is the revolution Styan named, still completing itself wherever a staging is reviewed as an argument instead of an occasion.

## The Archive: Promptbooks, Reviews, and the Poverty of the Record

A method that studies unrepeatable events lives or dies by its archive, and performance criticism of this play is fortunate in its central scholarly monument. Preparing her edition of the play for the Oxford Shakespeare, published in 2000, Jill Levenson assembled profiles of some 170 promptbooks, the annotated working copies from which productions had been run, recording their cuts, moves, and staging notes from the seventeenth century to the twentieth; the profiles grew into a searchable database that her edition's readers can consult alongside the text. Levenson's own summary of the play's stage career identifies a rhythm in it: since the Restoration the play has been in production almost continuously, and five productions have dominated their eras, David Garrick's of 1748, Charlotte Cushman's of 1845 and 1846, Gielgud's of 1935, Brook's of 1947, and Zeffirelli's of 1960 on stage and 1968 on film. The pattern matters to the method because it converts the play's performance life from a mist of anecdotes into a sequence of documented settlements, each era's dominant reading identifiable and comparable. Her earlier volume on the play for the Shakespeare in Performance series (1987) and Loehlin's Shakespeare in Production edition (2002), which annotates passage after passage with what named productions did at that line, complete a shelf on which this play is served better than any other in the canon. G. Blakemore Evans's New Cambridge edition likewise built the play's stage history into its introduction and commentary, an editorial decision that would have been eccentric before the revolution and was natural after it.

The archive's poverty is as instructive as its riches, and the honest practitioners of the method insist on both. Levenson's own account notes that Gielgud's 1935 production, among the most consequential of the century, left few printed traces, and that Zeffirelli's Old Vic staging was improvised from a workbook with cuts only, so that two of the five era-defining readings survive mainly in reviews, photographs, and memoirs. The promptbook, the method's gold standard, records intentions and mechanics better than effects: it can show that a line was cut, not how the cut line's absence played; it preserves where an actor stood, not what the standing meant at speed, in light, before that crowd. Reviews preserve effects but through the distorting lens of a single witness with a deadline and a taste. Alan Dessen's work on Elizabethan stage conventions, Elizabethan Stage Conventions and Modern Interpretations (1984) and Recovering Shakespeare's Theatrical Vocabulary (1995), added the archive's oldest layer, the conventions legible in the texts themselves, the vocabulary of stage action that the dialogue encodes for companies who shared it. The mature method therefore works by triangulation: text, promptbook, review, photograph, and memoir checked against one another, with the gaps confessed. A performance claim that cannot survive triangulation is demoted to anecdote, which is the discipline's own name for its characteristic sin.

One further stratum of the archive deserves separate notice, because it is the stratum in which the performers themselves practise criticism: the testimony of actors. The Shakespeare on Stage interview series, in which leading actors reconstruct celebrated performances role by role, includes Judi Dench's account of playing Juliet for Zeffirelli at the Old Vic, a document that records decisions at a granularity no promptbook reaches: where a laugh was found, which speech resisted until a physical action unlocked it, how the balcony's business was solved in rehearsal. Ashcroft's comparative judgment of her two Romeos belongs to the same genre, verdicts delivered by the only witnesses who experienced the production from inside the fiction. Performance criticism grades such testimony as it grades reviews, as interested evidence of the highest value about intention and effect, never as neutral fact. But it insists on admitting the genre, because a method that studies interpretation cannot exclude the interpretations of the people who stood inside the scenes. The actor's memoir and the director's notebook are the rehearsal room's criticism, and the archive is incomplete, as well as poorer, without them.

### What evidence survives when a production closes?

More than sceptics allow and less than the method wishes: promptbooks and marked texts, reviews, photographs, designs, recordings where they exist, and the memoirs of participants, all partial and all biased in knowable directions. The discipline lies in triangulation, treating each survival as testimony from an interested witness and building only what their agreement, or their instructive disagreement, supports.

## The Reviewer and the Scholar: Two Tribes of Witness

Performance criticism draws on the testimony of two tribes whose relations have not always been cordial. The reviewer witnesses the event once, at speed, and writes for readers deciding whether to buy a ticket; the scholar witnesses it with the archive open, sometimes across a whole run, and writes for readers who may never see the production at all. The tribes distrust each other's instruments. Practitioners of the theatre have asked, in print and with some justice, how armchair directors who have never staged a scene can pronounce on sightlines and timings; scholars reply, with equal justice, that a deadline notice full of verdicts and empty of evidence is journalism wearing criticism's coat. The quarrel surfaced in the reviews of Worthen's Authority of Performance itself, where a theatre practitioner's exasperation at academic performance writing boiled over in terms the journals rarely print. The heat is diagnostic: both tribes claim custody of the same object, the meaning of a night's playing, and neither can produce the object itself in evidence.

The method's mature practice treats the two testimonies as complementary instruments with known errors, like the promptbook and the photograph. The review is the archive's seismograph for effect: it registers, however partially, what a production did to at least one attentive witness on a datable night, and a run of reviews across a production's life lets the scholar triangulate the house's experience far better than any single account. The scholarly study contributes what the reviewer cannot: comparison across decades, the promptbook record, the patience to track a single staging choice, a balcony's height, a cut Chorus, through its consequences in scene after scene. Shakespeare Survey's annual surveys of production and Shakespeare Quarterly's theatre reviewing built the institutional shelf where the two kinds of witness stand side by side, and the performance histories of this play by Levenson and Loehlin are unthinkable without both. What the method adds to the old quarrel is a standard of proof neither tribe can meet alone: a claim about a production should be able to say what was done, from the record; what it did to witnesses, from the testimony; and what it means for the play, from the argument. Claims that supply only the third element are the anecdotes the sceptics rightly mock, whether they appear in a newspaper or a monograph.

The audience itself is the third witness, and the hardest to depose. Its testimony survives in aggregate forms: the length of runs, the pattern of revivals, the scenes a culture keeps quoting in the staging's terms rather than the text's, the balcony that replaced a window in the world's imagination. Carlson's ghosting describes the mechanism by which those aggregates form, each house carrying the production into memory and misremembering it productively for the next. Performance criticism is the only branch of Shakespeare studies that counts this witness systematically, and for a play whose afterlife in the theatre dwarfs its afterlife in the study, the counting is not optional. The criticism of the coming performance-history cluster will lean on all three witnesses at once.

## The Objection: Does Performance Reading Slight the Text?

The complication the brief for this article demands be faced is real, and it has been pressed from two directions that do not agree with each other. The first direction is bibliographic. Lukas Erne's Shakespeare as Literary Dramatist (2003) marshalled the publication record to argue that Shakespeare, unlike the pure man of the theatre of critical legend, wrote with readers in mind: the long texts, he contends, were designed in part for the page and abridged for the stage, since at any plausible delivery rate they overflow the two hours the Prologue advertises. Romeo and Juliet is one of his three central exhibits, its two early texts, the short first quarto of 1597 and the long second quarto of 1599, displaying the difference between a theatrical abridgement and a literary fullness. If Erne is right in his strong form, then the founding story of performance criticism, the play as a score whose life is the stage, is at best half the truth: part of this text's life was always a reader's life, led in a study, at a pace no company could sustain. The objection bites hardest exactly where the method is proudest, in its handling of length, cutting, and pace, because it suggests that the full text performance criticism treats as its score was never intended to be played entire by anyone.

The second direction comes from inside the method's own house. W. B. Worthen's Shakespeare and the Authority of Performance (1997) examined how directors, actors, and performance critics talk about what they do, and found the old hierarchy surviving inside the new vocabulary. Directors describe themselves as poised between fidelity and creativity, as if the text's meaning were a fixed quantity to be served or betrayed; performance critics describe productions as readings, as if a staging were a kind of essay whose authority still derives from the document it interprets. Worthen's exhibits run from the rehearsal room to the criticism of G. Wilson Knight, whose work separated the literary depths of the plays from their stage realisation and thereby kept the stage in tutelage. His conclusion cuts the ground from both camps: performance is not the text's servant and not its rival translation, but a practice with its own authority, in which the text becomes significant only as embodied within changing conventions of playing. On this account, asking whether performance criticism slights the text is asking the wrong question, since the slight assumes a sovereign text whose meanings performance can only honour or diminish.

Adjudication requires separating the objection's strong and weak forms. Erne's evidence is genuinely damaging to the crude version of the performance thesis, the version in which print is a mere accident that befell a stage work, and the discipline is better for absorbing it: the living text of this article's title lives in print as one of its habitats, and Q2's surplus over any performable length is a fact about the play that performance criticism must accommodate rather than resent. But Erne's argument concerns the text's design and destinations, not the location of performed meaning; nothing in the publication record shows that a staged Mercutio's death means what the page says it means, and the 1935 alternation stands as permanent refutation of the notion that the document settles the event. Worthen's correction is absorbed in turn: the mature method drops the courtier language of fidelity, stops grading productions by their obedience, and treats the staging as an authority that cites the text rather than a commentary that serves it. What survives of the objection is a discipline, not a defeat. Performance claims must show their textual warrant, as Granville-Barker's did; they must grade their evidence, as Levenson's archive does; and they must concede the reader's play alongside the spectator's. Marjorie Garber's account of the play, for instance, built from its language of names, dreams, and doubles and examined in the article on [Marjorie Garber Reads Romeo and Juliet](/2026/12/25/marjorie-garber-romeo-and-juliet/), is a page reading of great power that no production has made and none needs to: the stage has other work. The balance this article defends is not a compromise but a division of jurisdiction, in which the text governs what can be claimed in its name and performance governs what the claims come to in the only place they were first tested.

One development since the objection was first framed has shifted its ground, and the method's honesty requires registering it. The unrepeatable event has become, in part, repeatable. Stage productions of this play are filmed, broadcast, and archived on a scale Granville-Barker could not have imagined, so that a scholar can return to a staging, check a memory against the recording, and cite a moment with something approaching the verifiability of a page reference. The gain is real and the method has taken it. But the recording is itself a further interpretation, its cameras choosing where the house's attention is supposed to have been, its edit confiscating the spectator's freedom to watch the listener instead of the speaker. Performance criticism therefore treats film of a stage production the way it treats a promptbook: as a document of the event made by an interested party, precious and partial. The objection's deepest point survives the technology. A performance's meaning was an event among witnesses, and witnesses, not artefacts, remain its primary evidence.

## From Performance Criticism to Performance History: The Bridge Forward

This article stands at a hinge in the series. The critical-history articles before it have treated methods that work on the page: schools of reading, landmark essays, the editors' settlements. The cluster that follows turns to the play's stage history proper, its four centuries of production from the first performances to the present repertory. Performance criticism is the bridge between those territories, and the crossing explains the difference between them. Performance history asks what happened: which text Garrick played in 1748 and how he rewrote the tomb so that Juliet wakes before Romeo dies and the lovers speak; how Cushman's Romeo reset the role's gendered meaning in 1845; which of the era-defining productions Levenson counts can still be reconstructed and from what remains. Performance criticism asks what the happenings mean: what account of the play each settlement embodied, what evidence it offers about the text's capacities, and how it should weigh in the continuing argument among readings. The first discipline supplies the second with its facts and receives from it its questions.

The handrail for the crossing is the rhythm Levenson identified in the play's stage career: the long dominance of a single settlement at a time, Garrick's version holding the stage for the better part of a century, Cushman's Romeo defining the role for a generation, then the twentieth century's quicker succession of Gielgud, Brook, and Zeffirelli as the culture's pace of reinterpretation quickened. A settlement, in this rhythm, is a production so persuasive that it becomes the text for its era, the form in which audiences, actors, and even editors encounter the play, until its assumptions harden into common sense and a new production rebels against it in the name of the words. Performance history will narrate those settlements one by one. Performance criticism's bequest to the narration is the habit of treating each not as a costume change but as a verdict: Garrick's Romeo, tender, expurgated, and reunited with Juliet for a dying conversation, is the eighteenth century's judgment on Shakespeare's severity; Cushman's is the nineteenth century's judgment on who may embody desire; Brook's cut ending is the postwar judgment on reconciliation itself. Read so, the stage history of the play is a second critical tradition running parallel to the printed one, older in places, and for long stretches more influential, since most human beings who have known this play have known it in a theatre or through a theatre's descendants on screen.

The traffic matters because each discipline corrects the other's characteristic failure. Performance history without criticism degenerates into chronicle, a procession of dates and casts in which Garrick's altered ending and a provincial matinee weigh the same. Performance criticism without history degenerates into the eternal present of the reviewer's column, each new production discussed as if the play had no past, its choices praised as discoveries that companies made, and unmade, generations before. Robert Weimann's Shakespeare and the Popular Tradition in the Theater (1978) modelled the union a generation ago, reading the playtexts through the playing conventions, the divided stage of locus and platea, that their first theatres supplied; Margaret Jane Kidnie's Shakespeare and the Problem of Adaptation (2009) extended the union by arguing that a work like this one is best understood not as a fixed object that productions approximate but as a process sustained by its performances, editions, and adaptations together. On Kidnie's account, which this article adopts, the living text is not a metaphor for the play's popularity. It is a description of the play's mode of existence, the only mode it has ever had.

## Learning From Failure: What Bad Productions Prove

The method's least discussed evidence is failure, and for this play failure is unusually informative. A production of Romeo and Juliet can fail in ways that map precisely onto the text's requirements, because the play's machinery is exposed: if the feud's danger is not established in the first scene's brawl, nothing later can be believed, and the tragedy collapses into a story about unlucky teenagers; if the feast does not dazzle, the lovers' meeting has no world to be stolen from; if Juliet's potion soliloquy is cut or rushed, her death loses its chosen, argued quality and becomes a plot event. The promptbook record Levenson assembled shows generations of companies discovering these requirements by violating them, cutting the musicians' scene and the Friar John episode for pace and then, in revival after revival, restoring pieces of the play's causal fabric that the cuts had starved. A cut is a hypothesis, and the archive preserves the results of experiments their makers did not know they were running.

Failure also polices the border between interpretation and imposition more honestly than polemic does. A concept imposed on the play from outside, a Verona dressed as a boardroom, a feud staged as a football rivalry, announces itself as cleverness and is judged, in the house, by a single test the method trusts: whether the correspondences keep paying out as the scenes accumulate, unlocking passage after passage, or whether the idea spends itself in the first act and leaves the verse to fend for itself for four more. The productions remembered as travesties are instructive failures of correspondence; the productions remembered as revelations are successes of it. This is why performance criticism can afford to be relaxed about directorial freedom in principle and strict in practice. The stage is a self-correcting institution over time, not because taste improves but because the text keeps outlasting the ideas imposed on it, and the record of what it outlasted is data about the text's own properties. No other critical method generates negative results of comparable clarity, since a mistaken essay merely sits on a shelf, while a mistaken production is refuted nightly, in public, at the box office and in the body of the house. The discipline that studies the refutations owns a kind of evidence the library cannot supply.

## Verdict: What Performance Criticism Contributes

The contribution can be stated in three parts. First, the method recovered a lost continent of evidence. The documents of performance, promptbooks, reviews, the testimony of actors such as Ashcroft judging her two Romeos from inside the scene, are now part of what a serious account of this play must answer to, and editions from Evans to Levenson have rebuilt themselves around that obligation. Second, the method changed what kind of claim criticism can test. Snyder's hinge, Kahn's fathers, Garber's names: page arguments all, but each makes predictions about what happens in a theatre at 3.1, at 3.5, at the feast, and the theatre's four centuries of nightly experiments are the largest body of results those predictions will ever have. A criticism that refuses the results is not being rigorous; it is being deaf. Third, and most quietly, the method restored humility to the study of a text that underdetermines its own life. The balcony that is not in the play, the wound whose mechanics are not in the play, the reconciliation whose sincerity is not in the play: performance criticism teaches its students to see the gaps as the play's working spaces rather than as defects, and to honour the generations of practitioners who have thought, in public and at risk, inside them.

There is a consequence for ordinary readers that the method's champions understate and this article will not. A reader who has absorbed performance criticism reads differently afterwards, because the gaps the method exposes are visible on the page once they have been seen on a stage. The unspecified height of a window, the unchoreographed seconds of a wound, the statues whose tone the text leaves to the house: these stop being absences and become the places where the play hands its work to its collaborators, actors, directors, and witnesses. Studying the play with the production record open beside the edition, as Levenson's Oxford text invites its readers to do, is a slower and less certain business than studying it as a self-sufficient poem. It is also, on the evidence assembled here, the only study that matches the object: a script that has never existed for a single hour except as companies and audiences were making it.

The objections survive as the method's conscience. Erne's readerly Shakespeare warns the performance critic that the archive of print is as real as the archive of the stage, and that some of this text's meanings ripen only in the slowness of study. Worthen warns the method against smuggling the text's sovereignty back in through the compliment of calling a staging a reading. Both warnings are compatible with the verdict this article has argued, and the verdict bears repeating plainly: a production of Romeo and Juliet is an act of criticism, answerable to the text and to the record, capable of being right about the play in ways no essay can be and of being wrong in ways no essay would dare. The play was built for that risk. Its Prologue does not promise a story of more woe as a thing to be read about; it promises two hours of traffic, an event with a duration, a company, and a crowd. Performance criticism is the discipline that finally believed it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch performance primer, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch performance primer is the table in this article that lists the elements a performance critic examines, from tempo and casting to fight choreography and the staging of the ending, each matched to the question it answers and the moment in the play where it decides most. The pattern it exposes is uniformity of structure beneath variety of detail: at every row, the printed text leaves a variable open, a production is forced to close it, and the closing constitutes an interpretation. Read down the table and the play stops looking like a fixed object variously decorated and starts looking like a set of questions that four centuries of companies have answered in bodies and space.

### Q: What does performance criticism study that literary criticism leaves out?

Performance criticism studies the event of the play in the theatre: the decisions of casting, tempo, space, movement, and design by which a production turns a script into an experience, and the evidence those decisions provide about the text. Literary criticism can establish what the words permit; it cannot establish what the words do at speed, in light, before an audience, when Mercutio's wound must be physically arranged and Juliet's window placed at a measurable height. The performance critic treats those arrangements as arguments, checks them against the text, and compares them across productions, recovering a continent of interpretation that the page-bound disciplines historically filed as anecdote.

### Q: How did Granville-Barker's Prefaces change what counted as criticism of the play?

Granville-Barker's Prefaces changed the evidence on which criticism of the play could rest. Writing as a director who had reformed English Shakespeare production at the Savoy Theatre, he treated the effects a scene reliably produces in a theatre, its tension, momentum, and fatigue, as data about the text's construction, and he judged interpretations by whether they could be played. His preface to this play reads it as an engineered sequence whose meaning lives in its pace, so that a slow, pictorial staging becomes not a taste but a misreading. After the Prefaces, a claim about the play could be tested in rehearsal, and criticism acquired a laboratory it has used ever since.

### Q: What did J. L. Styan mean by the Shakespeare revolution?

J. L. Styan meant the twentieth-century reorientation of Shakespeare studies toward the stage, which he named and chronicled in The Shakespeare Revolution (1977). The revolution he described recovered the plays as scripts written for performance, rebuilt knowledge of the original playing conditions, and admitted productions as objects of scholarly attention rather than as illustrations of conclusions reached in the study. Styan's own work, from The Elements of Drama onward, modelled the new attention to dramatic experience as something made between text, actor, and audience. Later performance critics, James C. Bulman among them, honoured the revolution while arguing that it had not gone far enough, since it left the text's meanings immanent and the performers as mere interpreters of them.

### Q: What does W. B. Worthen argue about the authority of performance?

W. B. Worthen argues, in Shakespeare and the Authority of Performance (1997), that performance is not authorised by the text and does not derive its meanings from it. Examining how directors, actors, and critics describe their work, he found the old hierarchy surviving inside the new vocabulary: directors poised between fidelity and creativity, productions praised as faithful or blamed as liberties, stagings described as readings of a document that stays in charge. Against all of that, Worthen holds that a text becomes significant only as embodied within the changing conventions of its performance, so performance has an authority of its own. His argument disciplines performance criticism from within, forbidding it to smuggle the text's sovereignty back through compliments.

### Q: What does Lukas Erne's literary dramatist thesis claim about this play's two early texts?

Lukas Erne's thesis claims that the play's two early texts were designed for different media. In Shakespeare as Literary Dramatist (2003), Erne argues that Shakespeare wrote with a readership in mind as well as a company, and that the long second quarto of 1599 preserves a literary version whose length overflows the two hours the Prologue promises, while the short first quarto of 1597 stands closer to a theatrical abridgement. Romeo and Juliet is one of his three central exhibits, alongside Henry V and Hamlet. The thesis matters to performance criticism because it denies that print was a mere accident befalling a stage work: part of this text's life, on Erne's evidence, was always a reader's life.

### Q: Why is a promptbook stronger evidence than a review for performance criticism?

A promptbook is stronger evidence because it records the production's working decisions rather than one spectator's impression of them. The promptbook is the annotated copy from which a staging was run: it preserves cuts, repositionings, cues, and moves in the order the company settled them, so a critic can reconstruct what a production actually did at a given line. A review preserves how the doing struck a single witness on a single night, filtered through deadline and taste, which makes reviews indispensable for effects and unreliable for facts. Jill Levenson's profiles of some 170 promptbooks for this play show the standard in action: claims about historical productions are built from the promptbooks first and corrected against reviews, photographs, and memoirs.

### Q: What is ghosting, in Marvin Carlson's sense?

Ghosting, in Marvin Carlson's sense, is the process by which an audience's memory of earlier performances shapes its reception of the present one. In The Haunted Stage: The Theatre as Memory Machine (2001), Carlson argues that everything in the theatre, bodies, materials, language, and space, arrives haunted by previous encounters, so that a spectator meets any new Romeo against the Romeos already carried in memory. For this play the effect is unusually strong: famous Juliets and Romeos, above all the teenage pair of Zeffirelli's 1968 film, haunt their successors, who are praised or blamed in a vocabulary those ghosts supplied. Performance criticism uses ghosting both as evidence about reception and as a caution that no two witnesses saw quite the same event.

### Q: What did Peter Brook's 1947 production argue about the play's ending?

Peter Brook's 1947 Stratford production argued, by the strongest means a director commands, that the play's ending should not be believed. Brook excluded the final reconciliation of the two houses from his performance text, so the tragedy closed without the fathers' handshake, the promised golden statues, and the ceremony of settlement. The cut is a verdict: the feud's machinery, on this reading, would digest the lovers' deaths as it digested everything else, and the peace is a formality the action has not earned. Brook's governing maxim held that a production is only correct at the moment of its correctness, and the cut embodies that pragmatism: the claim about the ending was made in the ending's own medium, before witnesses, where it could succeed or fail in public.

### Q: How did the 1935 alternation of Gielgud and Olivier turn one production into two readings?

The 1935 alternation turned one production into two readings by holding every variable constant except the actor. At the New Theatre, John Gielgud directed and exchanged the roles of Romeo and Mercutio with Laurence Olivier across a run of 186 performances, with Peggy Ashcroft's Juliet constant between them. The same words, sets, and staging therefore carried two opposed Romeos: Olivier's passionate and physical, faulted for rough verse-speaking, and Gielgud's lyrical and musical. Reviewers adjudicated between the pairings in print, and Ashcroft, judging from inside the scenes, later preferred Olivier's Romeo against Gielgud's Mercutio. For performance criticism the experiment is foundational evidence that a role's meaning is not fixed by its text, since the text demonstrably stayed still while the meaning moved.

### Q: Why do performance critics treat casting as an act of interpretation?

Performance critics treat casting as interpretation because the actor's body is evidence the audience cannot refuse. This play states its heroine's age in its second scene, not yet fourteen, so a company must decide in visible flesh whether the tragedy concerns children processed by an adult world or young adults ruined by haste, and that decision reorganises every judgment about parents, friar, and lovers. The record shows the argument being made in both directions: Cukor's 1936 film cast lovers whose combined age passed seventy-five, while Zeffirelli cast actual teenagers on stage in 1960 and on film in 1968. Neither choice decorates a neutral core. Each assigns the play's guilt to a different address.

### Q: What does the balcony Shakespeare never wrote show about performance tradition?

The balcony shows that performance tradition can overwrite the text in the memory of an entire culture. The early editions give Juliet a window and a position above the stage; the word balcony appears in none of them, as Lois Leveen's research confirmed, yet the scene is universally named for the architecture the theatre added. The interpolation endured because it interprets: it frames Juliet as an icon in a shrine, makes her father's house visible as the obstacle, and stages rank and access in a single image. Performance criticism reads such accretions as the tradition's own criticism, strong readings that hardened into supposed fact, and treats their persistence as evidence about what audiences have needed the scene to mean.

### Q: How can staging decide whether Act 3 Scene 1 is a comic accident or a killing?

Staging decides the question because the text withholds the mechanics on which the answer depends. Shakespeare fixes the outcome, Mercutio wounded under Romeo's intervening arm, and Mercutio's twin verdicts, the curse on both houses and the flat statement "I was hurt under your arm" (3.1.112), but he does not fix whether Tybalt aimed at Romeo, whether the fight was horseplay tipping over, or whether Romeo's body caused the hit it tried to prevent. A fight director must choreograph one of those possibilities, and each produces a different tragedy: freak accident, feud's routine product, or murder wearing accident's clothes. Zeffirelli's duel, praised for showing bravado getting out of control, argues the second; darker stagings argue the third. The words never change.

### Q: What can a production demonstrate that an essay about the play cannot?

A production can demonstrate what a scene does to a room full of witnesses in real time, which is a class of fact no essay can reach. An essay can argue that the play's first half is comic in structure; a production proves or refutes the claim by making an audience laugh for two acts and then feel the laughter confiscated at Mercutio's death. An essay can describe the orchard scene's distances; a production sets them and watches intimacy or worship result. Demonstration has a further virtue essays lack: it is public and falsifiable on the night. When a staging's preparation fails and the effect does not arrive, everyone present knows, and that shared knowledge is evidence of a kind print cannot manufacture.

### Q: What is the difference between performance criticism and performance history?

Performance history establishes what happened; performance criticism establishes what the happenings mean. The historian reconstructs which text Garrick played in 1748, how he altered the tomb scene, what Cushman's Romeo wore and cut, and builds the record from promptbooks, reviews, and accounts. The critic takes that record as evidence in an argument about the play: what account of the tragedy each era's dominant production embodied, what the embodiments reveal about the text's capacities, and how they should weigh against readings made from the page. The disciplines are partners with opposite temptations: history without criticism subsides into chronicle, and criticism without history reinvents, as discoveries, choices that companies made and discarded generations earlier.

### Q: Why do performance critics distrust the word faithful in accounts of productions?

Performance critics distrust the word faithful because it smuggles in a theory they reject: that a production's virtue is measured by obedience to a meaning the text already possesses. The word presumes the document is the senior partner and the staging its servant, graded by resemblance. W. B. Worthen showed how the vocabulary of fidelity and liberty organises reviewing even among writers sympathetic to performance, keeping the hierarchy intact inside the compliment. The performance critic asks a different question: not whether a staging resembles an imagined original, but what reading it makes, how strongly the text supports that reading, and whether the reading survives the event. Brook's cut reconciliation was unfaithful by design and remains one of the century's most argued-with interpretations.

### Q: Can a staging test a page critic's reading of a scene?

A staging can test a page reading wherever the reading makes a prediction about playing. Susan Snyder's argument that the play runs on comedy's machinery until Mercutio's death predicts an audience experience: laughter and comic rhythm confiscated at the duel, with the same materials returning in tragic form. Companies have run that experiment for decades, some darkening the first brawl and refusing the prediction, others letting the comedy play full and letting the curse land on a laughing house. Coppelia Kahn's account of Capulet's violence makes a similar prediction about Act 3 Scene 5, testable by any production that stages the scene's threats at full weight. The tests are not laboratories with controls, but they are public, repeatable, and cumulative, which is more than most readings ever face.

### Q: What role does the audience play in completing a performance's meaning?

The audience completes a performance's meaning because the event the text scores exists only in witnesses. Barbara Hodgdon's study of closure in Renaissance drama treats endings as transactions with spectators, whose assent or resistance is the last material the playwright writes in; a reconciliation that a house refuses to believe has a different meaning from the same words believed. Marvin Carlson's ghosting adds the mechanism: each spectator assembles the performance out of present perception and stored memory, so the meaning is finished in hundreds of private theatres at once. Performance criticism therefore counts reception as evidence, reviews, testimony, and the record of what endured, rather than treating the audience as a passive container for a meaning delivered complete.

### Q: What is the strongest objection to performance criticism, and how much of it stands?

The strongest objection is that the method studies objects that vanish, through records that lie, in defiance of a text that was partly written for readers anyway. Its bibliographic form is Lukas Erne's: the long second quarto was designed in part for the page, so the stage cannot claim the whole work. Its internal form is Worthen's: performance described as reading keeps the text sovereign. A good deal of both stands as discipline. The archive is partial, promptbooks record mechanics better than effects, and any honest practitioner confesses the gaps. But the objection fails as a refutation, because the evidence of 1935, of Brook's cut, and of the balcony tradition shows meaning being made where the objection says only illustration occurs.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch performance primer to argue that a staging is a reading of the play?

An essay should use the primer as a checklist of decisions, working row by row through the production under discussion and showing, for each element, what the text left open, what the production chose, and what interpretation the choice amounts to. The strongest essays pick the rows where the production's choice is boldest, a cut reconciliation, an unusually aged Juliet, a duel choreographed as murder, and test those choices against the text's wording and the record of rival productions in the archive Levenson and Loehlin assembled. The primer's pattern does the argumentative work: since every row shows a variable the script leaves open, a staging that closes all of them coherently has made a reading, and the essay's task is to state that reading and judge it.
