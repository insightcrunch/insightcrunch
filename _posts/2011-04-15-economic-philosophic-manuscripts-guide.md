The Paris Manuscripts are the most influential text in the Marxist tradition that their author never wrote, if writing means finishing, ordering, titling, and releasing a work to readers. What Karl Marx left behind in 1844 was a set of working papers: three incomplete manuscripts, partly written in ruled columns, with pages lost, breaks in the argument, and no instruction anywhere about the order in which the pieces belong. Everything a modern reader encounters between two covers, the title on the spine, the sequence of sections, the sense of a book that begins with wages and ends with a critique of Hegel, was supplied by editors working almost a century later. That single fact changes how the text should be read, cited, taught, and argued from, and it is the fact that nearly all commentary on the manuscripts passes over in silence.

This guide treats the manuscripts as what they materially are before treating them as what they philosophically say. It follows the papers from a Paris desk in the spring and summer of 1844, through their strange physical format and their eighty-eight years of obscurity, to the two rival editions of 1932 that arranged them differently and thereby gave the twentieth century two different young Marxes. It maps each manuscript and section, names the passages that carry the argument, decodes the hard places where readers usually stall, and gives a route through the text that a first reader can actually finish. The reward for this material patience is considerable, because the manuscripts reward it: once their status is understood, arguments that have run for generations about continuity and rupture in Marx's thought can be restated in a form where evidence, rather than editorial habit, decides them.

## What Kind of Text the Paris Manuscripts Are

Every other famous work in this tradition has a determinate shape that its author gave it. The Communist Manifesto was printed as a pamphlet in 1848, with a structure Marx and Engels chose and later prefaces that comment on the choice. Capital appeared volume by volume, and even its posthumous volumes, assembled by Engels from drafts, rest on manuscripts that Marx had spent years preparing for the press. The Paris Manuscripts have no comparable anchor. They were not prepared for the press at all. They are studies, excerpt-driven working papers in which a twenty-six-year-old thinker taught himself political economy by writing his way through it, arguing with Smith, Ricardo, Say, and Mill in the margins of his own summaries, and discovering, midway through the exercise, a set of questions that the economists could not ask.

The point is not merely biographical. A draft composed to clarify the writer's own thought obeys different rules from a book composed to persuade strangers. In a draft, terminology is still molten: a word can carry a technical sense on one page and a polemical sense three pages later, because the writer has not yet been forced by readers to fix it. In a draft, an argument can be abandoned mid-sentence without embarrassment, because nobody was promised its completion. In a draft, two incompatible formulations can coexist for weeks while the writer tests which one bends and which one breaks. Readers who approach the manuscripts with book-expectations import a stability the papers never had, and then either blame Marx for contradictions that belong to the workshop or credit him with a system that belongs to the editor's binding.

### Are the Paris Manuscripts a finished work?

No. They are three incomplete manuscripts written and abandoned in 1844, partly in ruled columns, with pages missing and no authorial ordering whatsoever. They were first published in 1932 in two differently arranged editions, and the sequence any reader encounters is an editorial decision, not a recovered intention.

The unfinished character of the manuscripts is visible in their smallest features. Sentences trail off. Promised discussions are announced and never delivered, most famously a treatment of rent that the first manuscript sets up and the surviving papers never complete. A passage in the third manuscript discusses a topic, breaks away, and resumes the discussion pages later in a different vocabulary. Marx himself numbered some pages with Roman numerals, a private filing system that later editors have used and argued over, but numbering pages is not ordering an argument: it tells a scholar that two sheets belonged to one bundle, not that the bundle was meant to be read as a continuous treatise. The distinction matters because the most common way of citing these papers silently upgrades them, quoting a sentence from the third manuscript as though it commented directly on a sentence from the first, when in the physical record the two sentences sit in separate workbooks written for separate immediate purposes.

Even the title is an editorial gift. Marx called these papers nothing at all. The Berlin scholarly edition of 1932 presented them under the descriptive German title Ökonomisch-philosophische Manuskripte, Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts, and the name stuck, generating the familiar variants: the 1844 Manuscripts, the Paris Manuscripts, the early manuscripts. Each variant quietly theorises the text. To call them the manuscripts of a year is to stress their place in a development. To call them Paris manuscripts is to stress the exile milieu in which they were written, the French socialist literature and the workers' associations of that city pressing in on a German émigré's reading. To call them early writings is already to have decided that there is a later writing against which they will be measured, which is precisely the question the text is so often conscripted to answer. A careful reader holds the title lightly and remembers that the author supplied none of it.

What the papers unquestionably are is a record of work in progress at the highest level of intensity. Across roughly five months Marx filled notebooks with excerpts from the economists, interleaved with commentary that grows steadily less deferential, until the commentary bursts the excerpt format altogether and becomes free-standing argument. The estranged labour discussion, the private property analysis, the critique of Hegel's dialectic, and the pages on needs and money all belong to this burst. Understanding the manuscripts means understanding that trajectory, from digestion to insurrection inside a single set of notebooks, and the trajectory is legible only if the reader knows which pages are excerpts, which are glosses, and which are the moments where the glossator stopped summarising other men's systems and began building his own.

## The Working Months in Paris

Marx arrived in Paris in the autumn of 1843, already expelled from one career: the Rheinische Zeitung, the Cologne newspaper he had edited, had been suppressed by the Prussian authorities, and he had married Jenny von Westphalen and crossed the border with a plan to publish a journal that could say what German journals could not. The Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher, the German-French Yearbooks produced with Arnold Ruge, managed a single double issue in February 1844 before collapsing amid disagreements and distribution failures. Its contents included Marx's essays On the Jewish Question and the introduction to his critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, texts that announced a turn from liberal criticism toward communism. The manuscripts belong to the months immediately after that collapse, when Marx, without a journal and without a salary, sat down to master the science that he had decided held the key to everything else: political economy.

Paris was the right city for the attempt and the worst city for tranquillity. It was the capital of European socialism in its French varieties, of Saint-Simonian engineers, Fourierist phalansteries, Proudhon's recent provocation on property, and a workers' movement whose secret societies and public banquets made revolution feel like a local industry. Engels, whom Marx had met briefly in Cologne, passed through Paris at the end of August 1844 on his way back to Germany from Manchester, and the ten days the two men spent together, largely in a café on the Place du Palais-Royal according to the standard accounts, sealed one of the consequential partnerships in intellectual history. Engels had already written an outline of a critique of political economy for the Yearbooks, a sketch Marx would praise for years. The manuscripts were substantially drafted before that visit, across the spring and summer, in a period when Marx's reading notebooks show him working through Smith's Wealth of Nations in French translation, Ricardo, James Mill's Elements, Say, Sismondi, and others, excerpting with the ferocity of a man who believed the enemy's science had to be learned from inside before it could be broken.

The material circumstances deserve a paragraph because they shaped the papers' form. Marx was poor, in debt, and living on advances, gifts, and the remnants of his wife's resources, in the city where he and Jenny's first child was born that May. Writing in notebooks rather than on loose sheets, reusing paper ruled into columns in the manner of account books and excerpt ledgers, keeping several lines of inquiry physically side by side on one page, these are the habits of a working scholar conserving materials and managing a self-imposed syllabus, not of an author fair-copying a treatise. When Prussia pressed France and the Guizot ministry ordered Marx out of the country at the start of 1845, he left for Brussels with the notebooks among his papers, and the critique of political economy he had contracted to write for the Darmstadt publisher Leske migrated into other projects, other notebooks, and eventually, by a road of more than twenty years, into Capital. The Paris papers stayed in the luggage, unrevised.

That abandonment is the first interpretive fact, and readers resist it because it feels like an accident imposed on a masterpiece from outside. The resistance is understandable but misplaced. Marx abandoned drafts throughout his life with a regularity that suggests method rather than misfortune: the vast manuscripts of the late 1850s known as the Grundrisse were left in a drawer, the first volume of Capital consumed and recast years of prior drafting, and whole books were written to clear the ground for arguments that then took different shapes. A thinker who works by writing, thinking with the pen in the strictest sense, produces strata rather than monuments. The Paris Manuscripts are the richest exposed stratum of the earliest layer, which is exactly why they are precious as evidence and exactly why they are treacherous as doctrine. The sections that follow take the stratum apart layer by layer, beginning with the feature any reader can see in a facsimile and almost no summary mentions: the columns.

## Ruled Columns: The Page as a Working Instrument

Open a reproduction of the first manuscript and the page does not look like the page of a book in progress. It is ruled into parallel vertical columns, three across, headed with the three great revenue categories of classical political economy: wages of labour, profit of capital, rent of land. The layout imitates the organisation of the science Marx was studying. Smith and his successors distributed the annual produce of a society among the classes that received it as wages, profit, and rent, and a student digesting that science could hardly find a better instrument than a page physically divided into the three shares, excerpts from the economists entered under their proper headings, contradictions between authorities made visible by adjacency. The column was Marx's spreadsheet, built from paper and rule lines, and like a spreadsheet it determined what could easily be thought with it: comparison, juxtaposition, the slow accumulation of quoted definitions that begin to accuse one another simply by standing side by side.

Marx's page numbers, written in Roman numerals, and the column headings in his hand give scholars the physical evidence from which the manuscript's assembly has been reconstructed. The first manuscript's opening pages proceed column by column through the three revenues, and the texture of the writing in these pages is distinctive: long excerpts, close paraphrase, interspersed with short, increasingly impatient comments. The economists are allowed to state their doctrines of wages, of profit, of rent, and the comments begin to ask what the doctrines assume about the human beings inside them. What happens to the worker, as a living person, in a theory that prices labour like a commodity among commodities? Why does the science treat the division of society into proprietors, capitalists, and labourers as a fact of nature? The questions start as marginalia. They do not stay marginal.

Then, at the point where the estranged labour discussion begins, the apparatus breaks. The columns give way to continuous prose. Marx stops distributing material under the three revenue headings and writes, across the full width of the page, an analysis that no longer belongs to any one column because it concerns the relation that produces the columns themselves: the worker's relation to the product, to the activity of production, to his own human capacities, and to other men. Editors and commentators have often treated this breakdown as a practical detail, a student tiring of his filing system. It deserves better. The breakdown of the format tracks the breakthrough in the argument with unusual precision. A page ruled for the categories of political economy can hold everything the science says about distribution; it cannot hold a question about production itself, about what labour is and what it has become, because that question dissolves the boundaries the columns exist to maintain. The form failed where the content outgrew it, and the failure is datable to the page.

The second and third manuscripts were not written in the same columnar manner throughout, and here a second physical fact enters: survival is uneven. The manuscripts as preserved are a fraction of what Marx wrote in Paris. The second manuscript survives only in its concluding pages. Other notebooks from the same months, the excerpt books in which Marx digested his reading, survive alongside the manuscripts proper and are sometimes confused with them, and a summary Marx made of the final chapter of Hegel's Phenomenology, a reading aid for his own use, was at one stage mistaken by editors for a fourth manuscript of his own composition. A reader who pictures three tidy booklets has the wrong picture. The right picture is a worktable: bundles of sheets, some ruled, some plain, some lost, covered with a young scholar's excerpting, glossing, and sudden sustained argument, the whole mass bearing no title, no contents page, and no farewell note.

For the practical reader, the columns teach a reading skill. Where the text is excerpt and paraphrase, Marx is reporting a science he has not yet finished judging, and his voice must be distinguished from the voices he is summarising, a distinction careless quotation habitually collapses. Where the columns break and the prose runs free, Marx is speaking in his own person with an argument he is discovering as he writes, and the sentences deserve the closest attention a reader can give, because they are the sentences on which a century of interpretation has been built. The next two sections walk the manuscripts in their reconstructed order, flagging that distinction page by page.

## The First Manuscript: Three Columns and a Door Left Open

The first manuscript, the longest of the three in its surviving form, is best read as two works spliced together: a digest of classical political economy on wages, profit, and rent, and, growing out of the digest's final column-entry, the analysis of estranged labour that has made the whole collection famous. The splice is the story. Readers who skip to the famous pages miss the logic that produced them; readers who stop at the digest miss the moment the student overthrows the syllabus. Both halves need attention, though not equal attention, and the manuscript map later in this guide assigns each stretch its proper weight.

Begin with the wages column. Marx excerpts the economists on the determination of wages and lets their consistency work against them. Wages, in the science he is summarising, settle around the cost of keeping the worker alive and reproducing the labouring class; the worker's share is pressed downward by competition among workers themselves, by the capitalist's interest in cheapening production, and by the division of labour that renders each labourer more replaceable than the last. Marx's comments in this stretch are already sardonic: political economy, he observes, regards the worker only during working hours, treats the human being as a beast reduced to the strictest bodily needs outside them, and knows the labourer in his non-working existence not at all. But the decisive feature of the wages pages is methodological rather than rhetorical. Marx accepts the economists' premises provisionally and shows the misery following from them not as an accident of greed but as a theorem of the system: given private property, competition, and the separation of labour from capital and land, the worker's degradation is deduced, not deplored. The young writer is learning to make a science testify against the society that science describes.

The profit column extends the exercise. Profit appears in the economists as the capitalist's reward, proportioned to capital rather than to any labour the capitalist performs, and Marx presses on the moral vocabulary the science cannot purge: the capitalist's advantage in exchange, the treatment of the worker's whole existence as an item of cost, the doctrine that what benefits capital benefits society, assembled from quotations until the quotations themselves become the indictment. The rent column, treating ground rent through Smith and Ricardo, explores how the landowner gains from social development he does nothing to create, from population growth, from improvements in agriculture, from the general rise of needs, so that the landlord's interest stands opposed, in Marx's summary of the science's own findings, to the interest of everyone else. Each column was to have been completed by a resolution: having let political economy state its results, Marx intended to exhibit the relation among the three revenues and the alienation at the system's root. The rent discussion breaks off incomplete. The door to the resolution opens in the wages material instead, in the section every edition prints under a heading Marx's editors supplied: estranged labour.

The columns repay a slower pass than first readers usually give them, because the method of the whole manuscript is invented inside them. Marx does not begin by denouncing the economists from an external standpoint. He begins by believing them, excerpting their doctrines with a student's fidelity, and then he watches what the doctrines, taken together and taken seriously, actually describe. The wages doctrine describes a class whose members compete their own price toward the level of subsistence and whose humanity, in the science's own account, is an obstacle to be managed: the worker appears in the theory as a commodity whose cost of production is food and training, and whose feelings, family, and inner life fall outside the account because the account has no column for them. The profit doctrine describes a revenue that grows with capital rather than with any exertion of its owner, so that the science must either invent a labour of superintendence to justify it or admit that ownership as such is remunerated. The rent doctrine describes a class enriched by the progress of the whole society, by roads it did not build, populations it did not raise, and improvements it merely permitted. In each case Marx's commentary takes the same form: he quotes the science against the society the science serves, and then he asks the anthropological question the science cannot formulate, what manner of life these arrangements produce in the human beings who staff them. By the time the columns break, the reader has been trained in the procedure the free prose will use. The estranged labour pages do not interrupt the digest. They are what the digest was for.

One specific feature of the wages material should be flagged for students, because it returns in the mature work and is often missed here. Marx notes that within the economists' own presentations, the worker's condition varies with the state of accumulation: in a declining society his misery is naked, in a stationary one it is chronic, in an advancing one it is, in the manuscripts' cruel summary, the misery that accompanies growing wealth, since the division of labour that enriches the whole renders the individual worker ever more one-sided, dependent, and machine-like. The point is directed against any reading of the science for which the worker's poverty is a temporary embarrassment of an otherwise beneficent progress. On the science's own showing, progress and degradation are the same process viewed from the two ends of the wage relation, and the young Marx's sardonic achievement in these pages is to make Smith and Ricardo say so in their own words. When, decades later, Capital describes accumulation producing wealth at one pole and misery at the other, the sentence's ancestry runs directly back to these columns, and the reader who has worked through them possesses the genealogy rather than merely the assertion.

### What makes the estranged labour section the hinge of the text?

Four things at once. The column format physically collapses there, the excerpting voice gives way to sustained first-person argument, the question changes from how income is divided to what labour itself has become, and every later theme of the manuscripts, private property, communism, species life, is derived from the analysis those pages begin.

The estranged labour analysis proceeds by taking a fact that political economy itself supplies, the fact that the worker becomes poorer the more wealth he produces, and refusing the science's habit of treating that fact as a natural law rather than a relation to be explained. Marx distinguishes four dimensions of the estrangement, and the series' treatment of the concept itself lives in the companion guide to [Marx on alienation](/2011/04/01/marx-on-alienation-complete-guide/), which owns the definitional questions. What belongs here, in a reader's guide to the manuscripts as a text, is the passage's construction. The argument moves from the product, which confronts the worker as an alien object embodying his own life drained into a thing, to the activity, which is experienced as coerced, external, and mortal rather than as self-expression; from the activity to the worker's species-being, his character as a being who produces freely and universally, knows his production as his own, and finds his individuality confirmed in a shared human world; and from species-being to the estrangement of man from man, the social relation in which the product and the activity accrue to another. The derivation has a deliberate order: each dimension is shown to follow from the one before, and the sequence ends by posing the question the rest of the manuscripts exist to answer. If the worker's labour and product belong to someone else, who is that someone, and what is the relation, private property, that the whole structure presupposes?

Notice what the passage does not do, because editorial habit often disguises it. The estranged labour section does not define its central terms with a lexicographer's care. Estrangement and alienation, Entfremdung and Entäusserung in the German, slide toward each other and apart again across the manuscripts, and translators have never fully agreed on how to mark the difference in English, some reserving alienation for one term and estrangement for the other, some treating them as stylistic variants of a single thought. Nor does the section argue from empirical investigation of factories: its evidence is the economists' own texts plus a phenomenological description of labour's meaning that owes as much to Hegel and Feuerbach as to Smith. It is a philosophical detonation inside an economics notebook, which is why it could be read, decades later, both as the humanist manifesto of a lost Marx and as a transitional exercise that the mature economist quietly discarded. Both readings find their sentences in these pages. Neither reading is entitled to pretend the other set of sentences is absent.

A further feature of the first manuscript deserves emphasis because students habitually miss it: the estranged labour discussion is unfinished as an argument, and its incompleteness is informative. Marx derives estrangement from the fact of political economy and then asks after private property, concluding that private property appears as the product and consequence of estranged labour, yet also as the means by which labour estranges itself, a circle he flags rather than resolves in the surviving pages. The promised fuller treatment, like the completion of the rent column, was never written. The manuscripts therefore hand their readers a derivation with its keystone sketched and its arch unbuilt, and the industry of interpretation that later supplied keystones of varying shapes, humanist, structuralist, Hegelian, economic, is in part an industry for finishing an arch its author walked away from. Remembering that the arch was never finished is not a counsel of despair. It is a reminder that the reader's first duty to this text is accuracy about what is actually on its pages.

## Inside the Derivation: How the Four Dimensions Follow

Because the estranged labour pages are so often summarised as a list, their character as a derivation, each stage earned from the last, deserves a reader's slow attention. Marx begins where a sceptical reader must begin, with the product. Labour embodies itself in an object; under the prevailing relation, that object confronts the worker as something alien and powerful in proportion as he produces more, so that his own life, poured into things, belongs to the things and to their owner rather than to him. The second stage follows by asking what the product's alienation implies about the activity that made it: if the result is not his, the producing was not his either, and labour is experienced as external compulsion, as activity that is suffered rather than lived, in which the worker feels himself only in his animal functions and feels his human functions as animal. The third stage widens the lens from the individual act to the kind of being who acts. Man is a species-being because he makes his life activity itself an object of will and consciousness, produces universally and freely, and forms things in accordance with the laws of beauty as readily as with the standard of need; estranged labour inverts that relation, making man's species life a mere means to his individual existence, so that the very capacity that defines him becomes the instrument of his reduction. The fourth stage lands on the social relation that the first three have been circling: if my product and my activity belong to an alien power, that power is not a god or a fate but another man, the non-worker, and estrangement between man and his species completes itself as estrangement between man and man.

The derivation's rigour should not be overstated, and the manuscripts' honesty about its gaps has already been noted: the transition from estrangement to private property, the question of which is cause and which effect, is raised inside these pages and deferred to the third manuscript's circling treatments rather than settled. Nor should the derivation's beauty be allowed to disguise its foundations, which are those of 1844: a Feuerbachian anthropology, a Hegelian logic of externalisation and return, and the economists' fact of the worker's impoverishment accepted as the starting datum. What a reader is watching, page by page, is a philosopher teaching himself to think like an economist without ceasing to think like a philosopher, and the seams of that education are the most instructive feature of the whole manuscript. The definitional questions the passage raises, what estrangement is, how its dimensions relate to the later theory, and where the concept now stands, are treated as doctrine in the series' companion guide to the alienation concept; here the point is architectural. The most famous pages of the Paris Manuscripts are not an essay. They are a proof under construction, with its scaffolding showing, and they reward the reader who studies the scaffolding.

## The Second Manuscript: A Text Known by Its Ending

Of the second manuscript, only the conclusion survives: a run of pages, numbered in Marx's Roman sequence, that takes up the relation between private property and labour and begins to ask how wealth and poverty are produced together by the same social arrangement. The bulk of the manuscript, in which Marx apparently worked through further economic categories and moved toward the discussion of communism that the third manuscript supplies, is lost. What the loss means in practice is that the middle of the reconstructed book, the bridge between the estranged labour analysis and the reflections on communism, money, and Hegel, is a bridge scholars infer rather than a bridge anyone can walk. Editions print the surviving fragment where the reconstruction places it, and the honest ones mark the gap. Casual summaries never mention it, which is why so many readers believe they have read a continuous work.

The surviving pages are not negligible. They contain a compressed and striking meditation on how private property, as it develops, generates both the refinement and the degradation of needs, how the system produces luxury and privation as paired outputs, and how the political economists' celebration of wealth conceals a science of renunciation preached to the worker: the less he eats, drinks, reads, loves, and lives, the more he saves and the greater his treasure, his capital. The passage anticipates, in germ, the third manuscript's treatment of needs and of money, and it shows Marx testing a rhetorical register, the bitter inventory of ascetic counsels, that he would deploy for the rest of his career. As evidence about the state of his thought in those months, the fragment is gold. As a load-bearing wall in a reconstruction of the whole, it cannot serve, because no one knows what stood around it.

The fate of the second manuscript also teaches the general lesson of this guide in its purest form. A text can be famous, canonical, assigned in seminars on several continents, and still be, in its material core, a torso. Scholarship is honest about torsos when it studies Sappho or the pre-Socratics, whose works survive in fragments quoted by others, and nobody pretends that a reconstruction of Heraclitus is Heraclitus's book. The Paris Manuscripts are not fragments of that extreme kind; the first and third manuscripts are substantial and coherent enough to sustain sustained reading. But the discipline appropriate to fragmentary evidence, name your edition, mark the gaps, distinguish the author's sequence from the editor's, applies here with real force, and a reader trained on these papers will carry the discipline profitably to every other posthumous text in the tradition, from the assembled volumes of Capital to the notebooks of Lenin and Gramsci.

## The Third Manuscript: Private Property, Communism, and the Answer to Hegel

The third manuscript is the most miscellaneous and, for many readers, the most rewarding of the three. In the standard reconstruction it gathers supplements to the argument about private property, an extended discussion of communism in its crude and developed forms, reflections on the division of labour and on needs, the celebrated pages on the power of money, and the critique of the Hegelian dialectic with which most editions close. None of this was arranged by Marx into the sequence the editions present. The third manuscript is a bundle of continuations, insertions, and fresh starts, written as the inquiry overflowed the first two manuscripts' frames, and the reader who knows that will read it as what it is: a mind returning to its central problem from several directions in succession, each return sharper than the last.

Begin with private property and communism, the bundle's argumentative centre. Having posed private property as both product and instrument of estranged labour, Marx asks what its supersession would mean, and his first move is negative and startling: a warning against the communism that merely generalises private property instead of abolishing its logic. That warning is the crude communism passage, treated in detail among the questions at the end of this guide. Against it he sets a positive conception in which the return of man to himself is described as the complete restoration of human qualities and senses, the point at which the rich human being and rich human need replace the economy of having with an economy of being. The anthropology of that conception, the doctrine of species-being that underwrites it, is examined in the companion article on how [Marx's idea of species-being](/2011/05/15/marx-species-being-explained/) works, and the present guide confines itself to the textual point: these are the manuscripts' most lyrical pages, the ones most often quoted by the humanist tradition, and they sit in the least finished portion of the least ordered manuscript, a fact both sides of the later quarrel have found convenient to forget at different moments.

The pages on needs and on money show a different register, satirical and concrete. Marx argues that the system of private property manufactures needs as industriously as it satisfies them, that the extension of products and needs becomes a contrived apparatus of inhuman appetites, and that money, as the universal mediator, inverts every quality it touches, making ugliness beautiful, incapacity capable, and the bonds of human life exchangeable. The money pages draw on Shakespeare's Timon of Athens and Goethe's Faust, quoted at length in the manuscript itself, and they demonstrate something about Marx's working method that the column format concealed: his literary memory was part of his analytic equipment from the beginning, and the mature work's allusiveness is not decoration added to economics but a habit of thought present in the workshop. Readers pressed for time sometimes skip the money pages as rhetoric. That is an error. The analysis of money as the alienated ability of mankind, the power that transforms real incapacities into their opposites through possession, is the estranged labour argument applied to the universal equivalent, and it foreshadows the mature theory of the value form more directly than many passages that announce themselves as theory.

### Which manuscript holds the critique of Hegel's dialectic?

The third. Its closing bundle examines the final chapter of Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit, credits Hegel with grasping labour as the essence of man and the self-creation of man as a process, then charges the dialectic with performing that movement only in abstraction, in thought alone.

The critique of the Hegelian dialectic, which most editions place last and many readers therefore treat as the manuscripts' philosophical summit, repays slow reading precisely because it is double. Marx credits Hegel with a discovery he calls the outstanding achievement of the Phenomenology: Hegel grasps the self-creation of man as a process, understands objectification as estrangement and as the supersession of estrangement, and so lays hold, in philosophical form, of the essence of labour. The charge follows immediately. In Hegel, the only labour that counts is abstract mental labour; the estrangement overcome is estrangement within thought; the rich, living, sensuous human being of the earlier manuscripts is replaced by self-consciousness talking to itself, and the real chains are left politely unmentioned. Feuerbach stands behind this verdict as its positive source: Marx's pages on Feuerbach in the same bundle, praising him for dissolving the religious world into its secular basis and for founding a criticism on man's sensuous, social existence, are among the warmest acknowledgements in all of Marx's writing. Within months, in the theses on Feuerbach drafted in Brussels, the warmth would cool into criticism. In the manuscripts the debt is still being paid with gratitude, and the sequence, Hegel criticised through Feuerbach, then Feuerbach criticised through practice, is the biographical hinge on which the young Marx turns into the writer of The German Ideology.

One more component of the third manuscript belongs in any faithful map: the short preface Marx drafted for the projected work of which these studies formed a part. In it he announces a critique of law, ethics, and politics to follow the critique of political economy, distinguishes his method from the dogmatic abstraction he attributes to other German critics, and explains that he argues from the premises of political economy itself, accepting its language and its laws in order to exhibit its contradictions. The preface is the nearest thing to an authorial statement of intent the collection contains, and editors have placed it at the front of the volume, where prefaces live. That placement is an editorial kindness with an interpretive cost: it invites the reader to treat the whole bundle as the executed portion of the announced programme, when the papers themselves show the programme dissolving and reforming as the work proceeded. Read the preface, by all means, but read it as a plan the workshop outgrew, not as a contract the manuscripts fulfilled.

## The Manuscript Map

Everything argued so far about the text's status can be compressed into a single reference table, and the table below is this article's findable artifact: the manuscript map. ## Eighty-Eight Years of Silence: Custody, Loss, and Recovery

Between the summer of 1844 and the year 1932, the manuscripts existed in the world without existing for it. They were not suppressed by any authority, not banned, not hunted, not dramatised by confiscation. They lay among Marx's literary remains, the vast accumulation of drafts, notebooks, and correspondence that passed at his death in 1883 to Engels, who mined the papers for the second and third volumes of Capital and mentioned the early studies rarely if at all, and from Engels to the executors and archives of German social democracy. There the bundles rested while the movement that claimed Marx's name built its doctrine from the texts it had: Capital, the Manifesto, the polemics, the published essays. Nobody in that movement, friend or enemy, quoted the estranged labour pages, assessed the critique of Hegel, or debated species-being, because nobody outside a small circle of custodians knew the pages existed. The history of Marxism before 1932 is, among other things, the history of what Marxism became without its author's most philosophical manuscript.

### What happened to the manuscripts after Marx left Paris?

They travelled with his papers to Brussels and London, passed at his death to Engels and then into the archives of German social democracy, and slept there largely unread for decades, known to a handful of custodians, until scholars preparing a complete edition brought them into print.

The recovery, when it came, was the work of the first great scholarly edition of Marx's writings, the Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe, universally shortened to MEGA, launched in the 1920s under David Ryazanov at the Marx-Engels Institute in Moscow. Ryazanov's project was archival before it was doctrinal: collect everything, photograph the manuscripts held in party archives in the West, transcribe them exactly, and publish the Nachlass whole. Sections of the manuscripts appeared in Russian in 1927 in the institute's archive journal, a partial unveiling that has largely been forgotten because the complete German publication of 1932 eclipsed it. Ryazanov himself was arrested in 1931 and did not live to see the volume through the press; the edition appeared under his successor's direction, a reminder that the recovery of the text and the politics of the recovering institutions were entangled from the first day. That entanglement is a fact about the edition, not a refutation of its scholarship, and the distinction will matter when this guide reaches the objection that the whole humanist Marx was a political construction.

Meanwhile the physical papers themselves had further travels ahead. The social democratic archive, fleeing Nazi Germany, was eventually sold to the International Institute of Social History in Amsterdam, where the originals of the manuscripts now reside and where scholars consult them. The itinerary matters for a practical reason: for decades, editors worked from photographs, transcripts of transcripts, and copies of varying reliability, and some of the textual disputes in the literature are disputes between a reading made from a photograph in Moscow and a reading made from the original in Amsterdam. Modern critical editing, embodied in the second MEGA, returned to the originals with photographic reproduction and full apparatus, and it is that return which underwrites the confidence with which the manuscript map in this guide assigns states and sequences. Where the older literature disagrees with the newer about what a page contains or where a bundle belongs, the newer usually has the better evidence, because it has seen the paper.

## Two Editions in a Single Year

The manuscripts entered print twice in 1932, and the doubleness of the entry is the most consequential bibliographical accident in the tradition's history. In Moscow and Berlin, the Marx-Engels Institute's edition presented the text in the third volume of the first part of the Gesamtausgabe, under the title that has since become canonical, Ökonomisch-philosophische Manuskripte aus dem Jahre 1844. It printed the three manuscripts in their reconstructed order, from transcriptions prepared with the institute's characteristic rigour, and it remains the ancestor of every scholarly text. In Leipzig, in the same year, the publisher Kröner issued a two-volume collection of Marx's early writings, Der historische Materialismus: Die Frühschriften, edited by the social democratic scholars Siegfried Landshut and Jacob Peter Mayer, in which the manuscripts appeared under a different title altogether, Nationalökonomie und Philosophie, National Economy and Philosophy. The Leipzig collection was affordable, portable, and aimed at the general educated reader. It reached Western European readers first and shaped the initial reception, in Germany and beyond, far more than the Moscow volume, which was expensive, scarce, and soon entangled in the catastrophe of European communism's relations with everyone else.

The two editions did not merely differ in price and audience. They differed in what they claimed the text was. Landshut and Mayer, working from imperfect transcripts and under commercial pressure, omitted the first manuscript altogether, so their readers met no wages, profit, and rent columns and no continuous path into the estranged labour analysis; they arranged the second and third manuscripts in a jumbled sequence; and they printed, as a supposed fourth manuscript, Marx's private summary of the last chapter of Hegel's Phenomenology, a reading aid containing no commentary of his own, thereby attributing to Marx's pen a stretch of near-Hegel in Marx's transcription. The Moscow edition, by contrast, printed the manuscripts complete and in order, with the estranged labour pages in their proper home at the end of the first manuscript. A reader of the Leipzig volume and a reader of the Moscow volume in 1933 were, in a strict sense, reading different works that happened to share most of their sentences.

### Why do the two first editions arrange the material differently?

Because neither editor had an authorial order to follow. The Moscow scholars reconstructed the bundles from Marx's page numbering and the column structure, while the Leipzig editors, working from faulty transcripts, omitted, transposed, and misidentified material. Different evidence and different purposes produced different books from identical paper.

The scholarly verdict on the two editions is not in serious dispute: Moscow's text was better, and every subsequent edition descends from its reconstruction or corrects it. The historical verdict is more interesting, because the worse edition did the early cultural work. It was through Landshut and Mayer's collection, and through essays responding to it, that the manuscripts detonated in German intellectual life in 1932 and 1933, in the last months of the Weimar Republic, just before the Nazi seizure of power scattered the very milieu that was reading them and drove the discussion into exile journals, French translation projects, and delayed echoes. Herbert Marcuse, then a young philosopher working between Freiburg and the Institute for Social Research, published one of the earliest substantial responses in the year of publication itself, an essay announcing that the manuscripts placed the foundations of historical materialism in a new light. The reading he pioneered, of an essentially philosophical, humanist Marx for whom the critique of economy was grounded in a theory of man's estranged essence, travelled with the exiles. It would resurface after the war, in France, in Yugoslavia, in the United States, and eventually everywhere, as the standing alternative to the doctrine of the communist parties. That a bestseller's flawed text midwifed this tradition is not an embarrassment to be concealed. It is the arrangement dependency operating at the scale of a century.

## The Arrangement Dependency

This guide has been building toward a single claim, and it is time to state it as plainly as the evidence allows. Because the manuscripts have no authorial order, the philosophical Marx a reader meets in this text is partly a product of the edition they hold, so any argument built on the sequence of the material must name its edition. Call this the arrangement dependency. It is the namable claim of this article, and it is deliberately narrower and more defensible than the slogans usually traded in this territory. It does not say the text means whatever an editor wishes. The sentences are Marx's, their wording is recoverable, and no arrangement can make the manuscripts into a treatise on rent or a defence of private property. It says that everything in an interpretation which depends on order, on what comes first and what crowns the argument, on beginnings and endings and climaxes, is partly an editorial artifact, and must be audited as one.

Consider the most common such dependence. Nearly all editions close the volume with the critique of Hegel's dialectic, and generations of readers have therefore experienced the text as culminating in philosophy, as though Marx worked through economics in order to arrive at a verdict on Hegel. That experienced culmination has been cited, by humanists, as proof that the young Marx's economics was philosophy all along, and, by their opponents, as proof that the manuscripts belong to a pre-economic phase he outgrew. Both citations lean on a placement Marx never made. The Hegel material belongs to the third manuscript's bundle, and its position at the end of the modern volume reflects an editor's judgement about where a bundle of supplements most usefully sits. The judgement is reasonable. It is also reversible, and a reader who began the volume with the Hegel critique, as a statement of method, and then read the economic studies as its application, would meet a noticeably different author without a single sentence being altered. That counterfactual is the arrangement dependency in miniature: same words, same paper, different Marx, decided at the binding stage.

Scholarly disagreement on these questions is genuine and should be reported evenhandedly. Editors of the second MEGA chose to present the manuscripts in a form that displays more than one possible sequence, printing the material so that the reader can follow the sheets in their archival order as well as in the traditional reading order, a solution some scholars praise as honesty embodied and others criticise as abdication, on the ground that an edition which will not choose has failed at an editor's first duty. Defenders of the traditional order reply that Marx's Roman page numbering and the column headings give the reconstruction more authorial warrant than the sceptical rhetoric allows, and that the broad sequence, columns, estrangement, communism, Hegel, corresponds to a real intellectual progression visible in the writing itself. Defenders of greater editorial intervention reply that progression is exactly what a good editor manufactures when tidying a workshop, and that the progression's smoothness is evidence against its innocence. This guide's verdict is the narrow one the evidence compels: the broad internal order of each bundle is well grounded, the order among bundles and the framing pieces is editorial, and therefore sequence-based arguments must specify which of the two kinds of order they exploit. An argument from the estranged labour section's internal derivation stands on Marx. An argument from the volume's climax stands partly on its editors. The tradition's most famous quarrel, between the humanist reading of a continuous philosophical Marx and Althusser's thesis of an epistemological break separating a young ideological Marx from a mature scientific one, is mapped in the article on [young Marx and mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/), and it cannot be responsibly joined by anyone who has not first asked which of its exhibits are sequence-dependent.

There is a discipline that follows, and it is simple enough to state as a rule for readers, writers, and teachers of this text: cite the manuscript, not the volume. A reference that names the bundle, the first manuscript's estranged labour pages, the third manuscript's money discussion, survives any rearrangement of the book on the shelf, because it points at paper Marx touched rather than at a page an edition happened to assign. A reference that names only a page number in one translation is a reference to an editor's pagination, valid in a classroom where everyone holds the same printing and worthless across the literature. The manuscripts, with their unstable order, their lost middle, and their rival births, are the tradition's standing lesson in this discipline, which the companion article on the [MEGA2 editorial apparatus](/2013/07/01/mega2-marx-manuscripts-guide/) extends to the whole corpus. Readers who internalise the lesson will find that it changes their handling of every posthumous classic, and that is one reason this text, unread for eighty-eight years, now functions as a training ground for scholarly conscience far beyond its size.

## Reception: The Marx Who Arrived Late

A text that sleeps for eighty-eight years does not enter a conversation; it interrupts one. When the manuscripts surfaced in 1932, Marxism was already an established doctrine with parties, orthodoxies, and a canon, and the canon had been formed entirely without them. The Marx of the movement was the author of Capital and the Manifesto, the economist and revolutionary strategist. Into that settled portrait the manuscripts introduced a young philosopher of estrangement, species-being, and human essence, writing in Hegel's vocabulary about the meaning of labour and the richness of human needs. The first readers who grasped the scale of the interruption, Marcuse prominent among them, argued that the new text supplied the philosophical foundation the doctrine had always implicitly claimed: historical materialism was not an economic determinism but a theory of human practice, grounded in an account of what labour is and what capitalism makes of it. The tradition that grew from that argument, Marxist humanism, has its own article in this series on [Marxist humanism](/2011/07/15/marxist-humanism-explained/), and its career runs from the exile journals of the 1930s through the philosophical thaw after 1956, when the death of Stalin and the secret speech denouncing him made a humanist Marx suddenly usable inside the socialist world itself, to the Yugoslav Praxis school, the Polish and Hungarian revisionists, and the Western New Left.

The counterattack, when it came, was aimed at exactly the manuscripts' authority. Louis Althusser, writing in Paris in the 1960s, argued that the young Marx of the manuscripts was not the founder of the mature science but its last brilliant prehistory: an ideologist still thinking in Feuerbach's humanist categories of essence and estrangement, which the Marx of Capital discarded in an epistemological break around 1845. On that reading, the manuscripts' philosophical glamour was a trap, and the humanist tradition built on them was a regression dressed as a recovery. The quarrel between these readings organised a generation of scholarship, and it is still the frame through which the manuscripts are most often introduced. A reader's guide is not the place to settle it, and this series assigns the verdict to the article on the two Marxes linked earlier. What a reader's guide must insist on is the prior, material point that both camps have sometimes slighted: the disputants were arguing over an unfinished, editor-ordered, partially lost workshop draft, and the confidence appropriate to conclusions drawn from such an object is different in kind from the confidence appropriate to conclusions drawn from a published book. Humanists who quoted the manuscripts as Marx's true testament and structuralists who dismissed them as juvenilia both treated the papers as more determinate than paper of this kind can be.

A second reception deserves record because it carried the text beyond scholarship. In the Anglophone world the manuscripts reached a wide audience through Erich Fromm's Marx's Concept of Man, published in 1961, which printed T. B. Bottomore's English translation of substantial portions alongside Fromm's own essay presenting Marx as a humanist thinker of alienation for an age anxious about conformity and organisation men. Fromm's volume was a publishing event: it put the estranged labour pages into paperback circulation among students, clergy, therapists, and general readers who would never open a volume of the collected works. Whatever scholars thought of Fromm's framing, and many thought it domesticated Marx into a moralist of modern dissatisfaction, the book fixed the manuscripts' popular identity for decades as the humane Marx, the Marx of alienation, the Marx one could quote without economics. First readers now often arrive at the text through that reputation, expecting a lyric of protest and finding, instead, ruled columns and Ricardo. Managing that expectation is part of reading the manuscripts well, and the route section below is designed with it in mind.

The French reception gave the text a second homeland. A French translation of the manuscripts appeared in the later 1930s in the collected philosophical works, and after the war the Parisian discussion, in journals of the existentialist and independent left, made estrangement a category of general philosophy rather than of Marxist scholarship alone: the young Marx became an interlocutor for Sartre's generation, a philosopher of man's self-loss to be set beside Kierkegaard and Heidegger, sometimes with the economics quietly amputated. Scholars have noted the irony that Georg Lukács, whose History and Class Consciousness of 1923 had constructed a theory of reification in ignorance of the manuscripts, encountered the text in Moscow before its publication while working at the institute, and recognised in it both a confirmation of his earlier construction and a standard against which, in his later self-criticism, he measured its faults. A doctrine's prehistory had been written, it turned out, in an archive, years before the doctrine: the episode remains the single most arresting illustration of how much of twentieth-century Marxism was elaborated in the dark, from partial texts, and how the archive's slow opening kept revising what the tradition thought it had always known. The manuscripts were the largest single instalment of that revision, which is why their story, told properly, is not a footnote to the tradition but one of its main plots.

Inside the socialist world itself, the manuscripts led a double life that no Western summary should omit. Officially, the text was an embarrassment of riches: published by the movement's own institutes, yet describing communism in a vocabulary of human essence and self-realisation that sat uneasily beside the doctrine of the party schools, and handling private property as a relation of labour rather than as a legal form to be abolished by decree. The East German collected works' decision to quarantine the manuscripts in a supplementary volume rather than the main sequence, noted in the editions section of this guide, was the institutional expression of that unease. Unofficially, the text was dynamite in the best hands. Yugoslav philosophers of the Praxis circle built an entire journal and school around the young Marx's categories in the 1960s, arguing from the manuscripts that a socialism of bureaucratic command reproduced estrangement under new management. Polish and Hungarian thinkers of the same generation, Leszek Kołakowski in his early work and the Budapest School formed around the elderly Lukács among them, drew on the manuscripts for critiques of their own regimes that those regimes correctly understood as fundamental. The pattern is worth stating plainly, because it completes the reception's symmetry: a text recovered by a Soviet institute became, within three decades, one of the sharpest instruments available to critics of Soviet-style society, and its estrangement analysis proved as serviceable against the administered factory as against the capitalist one. Whatever side a reader takes in the continuity quarrel, that career is a fact about the manuscripts' content and not only about their editors: doctrines do not usually turn so readily against the houses that publish them unless something in the text itself resists domestication.

## Translations and the Route Into English

The manuscripts were written in German, in a hand that editors have described with feeling, and every English reader meets them through translation choices that are neither neutral nor uniform. Three English versions matter. The first complete translation was Martin Milligan's, published in Moscow in 1959 by the Foreign Languages Publishing House and reprinted widely thereafter, often bound with the Great Books Foundation and later paperback editions; Milligan's is the version through which the Anglophone world first read the whole text, and its renderings set the vocabulary of the early Anglophone debate. Bottomore's translation, made for Fromm's 1961 volume, covered the principal philosophical portions with a fluency many readers still prefer, and its selections shaped what the general public took the manuscripts to be. The third version, by Rodney Livingstone and Gregor Benton, appeared in the third volume of the Marx and Engels Collected Works in 1975 and in the Penguin Early Writings of the same period, and it is the version most academic citation now follows. A scholarly reader should know which of the three is open on the desk, because their key terms diverge: estranged labour in one is alienated labour in another, species-being survives everywhere but is glossed differently, and the difficult word Entäusserung, which means both externalisation and renunciation or divestiture, is rendered in ways that tilt whole interpretations toward psychology or toward property law.

The instruction to prefer one translation absolutely is a false economy, and the honest advice is procedural. Read the argument in the Collected Works or Penguin version for consistency with the surrounding early writings and with most modern scholarship. Check any sentence on which an interpretation will be built against Milligan, and where the two diverge, treat the divergence as a warning light rather than an inconvenience: at that point the German is carrying a distinction English flattens, and the careful reader consults the German term, which scholarly editions print or note, before proceeding. For the estranged labour pages in particular, where the whole analysis turns on the double sense of labour as self-externalisation that is also self-loss, translation is not a preliminary to interpretation. It is the first act of interpretation, and editions that pretend otherwise are hiding their own work.

### How should a reader cite a text with no stable pagination?

By manuscript and section, with the edition and translator named. Pagination differs across every printing and order differs across editions, so a citation that names the bundle, the first manuscript's estranged labour discussion, say, and identifies its edition survives rearrangement, while a bare page number points at one printing only.

Because citation is where the arrangement dependency becomes practical, it deserves a tool as well as a rule. Readers building serious notes on these manuscripts do well to keep a citation card for every passage they use, recording the manuscript bundle, the section in the edition used, the translator, and the German headword where terminology matters, so that a quotation gathered from Milligan in January can be reconciled with a seminar's Penguin text in March without an afternoon of page hunting. This is precisely the kind of apparatus that a structured notebook serves better than a commonplace book, and it is the purpose for which readers of this series keep their manuscript cards in [a citation-linked study notebook on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html): the card preserves the source's identity through every rearrangement of the reader's own shelves, which is exactly the virtue the manuscripts' history teaches. The rule generalises beyond this text. Any work that survives in multiple editions, orders, or translations, and the Marxist canon is dense with them, rewards the reader who cites objects rather than pages.

One further translation problem is specific enough to name. The manuscripts' most quoted sentence patterns involve Marx's play on the verb to have and the condition of being, the rich man passage, and the formulations about the senses becoming human. English versions differ in whether they preserve the Hegelian resonance of these constructions or flatten them into moralising prose, and readers comparing a quotation found online with the version in their own edition will sometimes conclude, wrongly, that they are looking at different texts. They are looking at different translations of one unfinished German draft, mediated by editors who ordered it differently. At this point in the guide that sentence should no longer sound like a counsel of despair. It is simply the ground condition of reading this work, and the reader who accepts it early will waste less time and argue more accurately than the reader who discovers it late.

## The Hard Passages Decoded

Four knots account for most of the difficulty readers report with the manuscripts, and each knot loosens once its kind is recognised. The first is the vocabulary of objectification. Marx inherits from Hegel the thought that a human being becomes actual by externalising himself in objects, making a world that mirrors his powers back to him, and from Feuerbach the thought that religion is such an externalisation misunderstood, man's essence projected onto a God who then confronts man as an alien power. In the manuscripts, objectification as such is not the disease. Labour that objectifies itself in a product is man's species life doing what it does. The disease is the specific social form in which the objectification returns against the producer as an alien and hostile power, and the manuscripts use estrangement and alienation words for that pathological form, with a consistency that is real but imperfect, since the draft was never revised. Readers who treat every act of making as already estrangement invert the argument and make nonsense of the communism passages, where a world rich in objects, senses, and relations is the goal rather than the problem.

The second knot is the concept of species-being itself, which strikes many first readers as the text's most expendable metaphysics and is in fact its load-bearing anthropology. A species-being, in the usage Marx takes from Feuerbach and bends to his own purposes, is a being that treats its own life activity and its species as objects of its will and consciousness: man produces even when free from physical need, produces universally rather than under the goad of a single appetite, and knows the products of others and of nature as a shared world in which his individuality is confirmed. The claim doing the work is comparative and, in principle, arguable: estrangement is measured as the distance between what labour is in this species sense and what the wage relation makes of it. A reader need not buy the anthropology to follow the argument, but must see that without it the estranged labour pages lose their standard of measurement and degenerate into complaint. The mature work's relative silence about species-being, once the standard's disappearance is noticed, becomes itself a datum in the continuity quarrel rather than a mere change of fashion.

The third knot is the prose of the Hegel critique, where Marx summarises the Phenomenology's final movement, absolute knowing, in Hegel's own compressed idiom before turning it against him. The passage defeats readers who attempt it without Hegel at hand, and there is no shame in the correct procedure: read Marx's summary of a chapter as evidence of what Marx took from Hegel, namely the conception of self-creation through labour and negation, and postpone adjudicating whether the summary is fair to Hegel until a separate encounter with the Phenomenology itself. What the manuscripts require is the double verdict Marx delivers, that Hegel stands on the standpoint of modern political economy within philosophy, grasping wealth's subjective essence as labour while knowing only abstract, mental labour. Fair or not, that verdict is the programme of the manuscripts in one sentence: the economics of the columns and the philosophy of the bundles are one inquiry, the inquiry into labour as man's self-making and self-loss.

The fourth knot is tonal, and it is the one least often confessed. The manuscripts alternate, sometimes within a page, between the dry register of excerpted economics and passages of prophetic intensity about the human senses, the emancipation of the eye and ear, and the rich human being. Readers trained on either register alone tend to discount the other, economists skipping the lyricism as youthful excess, philosophers mining the lyricism and skipping the rent column as scaffolding. The alternation is the meaning. A thinker who could not yet write Capital was finding, in real time, the union of economic analysis and human science that he would pursue, under colder disciplines, for the rest of his life, and the seams where the registers meet are where the finding happened. Read across the seams, slowly, and the draft's unfinished state converts from defect to evidence: nowhere else in the corpus can the joining be watched as it occurs.

## The Manuscripts Among the Early Writings

No text is read well in isolation, and the manuscripts sit in a dense neighbourhood. Behind them stand the doctoral dissertation on Greek materialism, the journalism of the Rheinische Zeitung with its debates on the law on thefts of wood and the misery of the Moselle wine farmers, the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right written at Kreuznach in 1843, whose manuscript, like the Paris papers, waited decades for publication, and the essays of the German-French Yearbooks. Beside them, in the same Paris months, stand the excerpt notebooks on James Mill, where Marx's comments on money and mediation already rehearse the third manuscript's themes, and the reading of French socialist and communist literature that supplied the target of the crude communism passage. Ahead of them, within a year, lie the theses on Feuerbach, eleven compressed sentences drafted in Brussels in the spring of 1845 that criticise the very Feuerbachian standpoint the manuscripts had celebrated, and The German Ideology of 1845 and 1846, co-written with Engels, itself abandoned to what its authors later called the gnawing criticism of the mice and unpublished in full until the same year as the manuscripts, 1932, in the same two editions. The neighbourhood teaches a sobering lesson in textual humility: the early Marx, as the twentieth century received him, was substantially a construction of 1932, assembled by editors from papers the nineteenth century had left in drawers, and the scholarly task ever since has been to keep the construction visible while using it.

Placement in a reading path is the practical corollary. The series' own guidance on the order in which to read Marx is set out in the [Marx reading order guide](/2013/06/15/marx-reading-order-guide/), and this article confines itself to the manuscripts' position within that path. They are not the place to begin, despite their chronological priority among the major unpublished works, because their argumentative density presupposes the very economics they are digesting. They are the place to go after a first encounter with the Manifesto's programme and alongside, or just before, the opening of Capital, when the reader has enough political economy to hear the columns arguing and enough philosophy to follow the Hegel bundle. Read there, they illuminate both neighbours: the Manifesto's communism gains a philosophical depth its pamphlet form conceals, and Capital's analysis of the commodity and of labour gains a prehistory in which its human stakes were stated with a frankness the mature work disciplines into irony. Read too early, they produce the familiar undergraduate Marx of quotable estrangement and no economics. Read in their place, they produce the reader the series exists to make: one who can hold the young philosopher and the old economist in a single field of vision and argue, from evidence, about their relation.

## A Route Through the Manuscripts for a First Reading

The route that follows assumes a reader with a week of evenings and a standard modern edition, and it is built on the manuscript map's verdicts. Read the preface first, in five minutes, as a statement of intent, holding in mind that the workshop outgrew it. Then go directly to the estranged labour pages at the end of the first manuscript and read them twice: once for the movement of the argument from product to activity to species-being to man and man, and once with a pencil, marking every place where a term is used before it is explained, because that list of forward references is the reader's personal syllabus for the rest of the volume. From the estranged labour pages, step backward into the columns, reading the wages material with the estrangement analysis still warm, and skim profit and rent for the method rather than the detail: the point is to watch the economists' science generating the questions the free prose then answers. That backward step, against the editions' grain, is this guide's single most useful piece of practical advice, because it reproduces the manuscript's own genesis, digest first in fact but discovery first in understanding.

With the first manuscript inhabited, take the surviving pages of the second as a short bridge, read for the renunciation passage and the treatment of needs, and then give the third manuscript two full evenings. The first evening belongs to private property and communism: the argument that private property is the product and then the instrument of estranged labour, the attack on crude communism, and the positive statement of a communism beyond having. The second evening belongs to the money pages and the critique of Hegel, taken in that order, so that the concrete analysis of the universal mediator precedes the abstract reckoning with the dialectic and grounds it. Close the week by rereading the estranged labour pages a third time. The experience of that rereading, in which sentences that were opaque on first contact now carry the whole architecture visibly, is the manuscripts' built-in test of whether they have been read or merely visited. Readers who want the companion concept treatment once the text is familiar will find it in the series' guide to [Marx on alienation](/2011/04/01/marx-on-alienation-complete-guide/) already cited, which takes up the four dimensions as doctrine where this guide has taken them up as pages.

The route also has a social form worth recommending, because this text improves markedly in company. A reading group of three or four, meeting weekly over the same ground, divides the labour the manuscripts impose: one member tracks the economists being excerpted and reports where Marx is summarising and where he is judging, one tracks the Hegelian and Feuerbachian vocabulary and glosses its shifts, one keeps the edition question, noting every point where arrangement or translation might be steering the group's sense of the argument. Rotate the duties weekly so that no member becomes the house sceptic or the house enthusiast. Groups that read this way discover, usually by the third meeting, that their disagreements have migrated from slogans about the young Marx to specific pages and specific editorial decisions, which is the migration this entire guide exists to promote. A solitary reader can simulate the arrangement with a notebook divided into the same three watches, and the simulation, honestly kept, delivers most of the benefit.

Two disciplines make the route pay. Keep the edition question open as you read: whenever the prose feels suspiciously climactic or suspiciously transitional, ask whether the feeling belongs to Marx's bundle or to the editor's arrangement, and note the answer in the margin. And keep quotations honest at the moment of capture: a sentence copied into a notebook with its manuscript and section attached is an asset, while the same sentence copied with a bare page number is a future embarrassment. The manuscripts punish casual handling more than most texts because their afterlife has made them a quarry for every faction, and the reader who handles them carefully from the first evening is already doing the thing the whole tradition, at its best, exists to teach: letting a difficult object be what it is before making it useful.

## The Strongest Objection: Abandoned Notebooks and Doctrinal Weight

The objection must be stated at the strength its best proponents would choose. The manuscripts are the unfinished private papers of a twenty-six-year-old, abandoned without a backward glance, unknown to every figure in the tradition's formative century, and elevated to canonical status only in the 1930s and after, in circumstances saturated with politics: a Soviet institute burnishing its scholarly legitimacy, social democratic editors seeking a humane Marx against both fascism and Stalinism, and, after 1956, reform communists and New Leftists who needed a founding text for a Marxism with a human face and found the manuscripts ready to hand. On this view, the humanist Marx is not a discovery but a construction, assembled because the century required him, and the doctrinal weight placed on the notebooks, as the interpretive key to the whole corpus, inverts every sound rule of evidence: the tail of abandoned juvenilia wagging the dog of the published life's work. Proponents add that Marx had every opportunity, across forty subsequent years of publishing, to print, quote, or even mention these papers if he had thought them his testament, and that his silence, continued through the decades when he lovingly revised far lesser pieces, is itself the author's verdict on their status.

The reply begins by conceding what is true in the charge, because the charge's factual core is sound. The elevation of the manuscripts was politically motivated at several of its stages; motives in the history of reception are rarely pure, and the post-1956 humanist moment in particular cannot be understood apart from the crisis of the communist world. The concession, however, does not reach the conclusion its proponents draw from it, because it confuses two uses of a text that scholarly method keeps apart. In canonical use, a text serves as an authority whose statements bind interpretation of the whole, and the manuscripts cannot bear that use: unfinished, unordered, and unendorsed by their author, they settle nothing by themselves. In evidential use, a text serves as testimony about its author's development, assumptions, and working concepts, and for that use the manuscripts are superb evidence, all the better for being private, since a workshop draft shows the machinery that a published facade conceals. The error lies not in reading the notebooks but in promoting them, and the demotion of the manuscripts from testament to testimony, which this guide has practised throughout, answers the objection by accepting its standard.

The second half of the reply is independent of anyone's politics, and it is the half the objection's proponents answer least comfortably. The evidential picture changed in the middle of the century for reasons internal to scholarship: the Grundrisse, the vast draft of 1857 and 1858 in which the mature Marx, alone and unpublished, returned to themes of estrangement, species activity, and the rich individuality of a developed human being, became generally available in the postwar decades and in English in the 1970s. Whatever one makes of the continuity question, the Grundrisse's existence as a private bridge between the Paris notebooks and Capital is a documentary fact, not a factional requirement, and it forbids the clean story in which the philosophical concerns of 1844 were simply outgrown and discarded by the economist. The notebooks' weight, in other words, does not rest only on the uses to which the 1950s put them. It rests on the whole archive, slowly published across the twentieth century, in which the manuscripts take their place as the first exposed stratum of a lifelong working process. A text's authority is a matter of argument. Its evidence is a matter of record. The manuscripts, treated as record, survive the objection entire.

## The Standard Misreadings

Five misreadings recur so reliably that a reader can use them as a checklist against summaries found anywhere. The first presents the manuscripts as a book Marx wrote and chose not to publish, as though a finished Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts had been suppressed by prudence or by publishers. No such book existed; there were notebooks, and the choice framing invents both a completed object and a decision about it. The second misreading treats the printed order as Marx's architecture and argues from the volume's progression, economics ripening into philosophy, as from a design. The progression is substantially the editors', and arguments built on it must be discounted by exactly the arrangement dependency this guide has described. The third misreading attributes the manuscripts' long invisibility to suppression, by parties, by Engels, by a conspiracy of orthodoxy. The record shows custody, neglect, and the ordinary entropy of a huge literary estate, not a plot, and the conspiracy version flatters its believers by making the text's recovery a melodrama in which they play the rescuers.

The fourth misreading takes the estranged labour section as the whole text, quoting its four dimensions and ignoring the columns that generated them, the communism discussion that answers them, and the Hegel critique that frames them. Its effect is to shrink a study of political economy into a lyric of protest, and it is the misreading on which the manuscripts' popular reputation largely rests. The fifth and subtlest misreading treats the manuscripts as Marx's settled early position, a doctrine of 1844 that can be set, whole, against a doctrine of 1867. A workshop draft has no settled positions; it has experiments, some repeated and strengthened across the bundles, some tried once and dropped, some contradicted within twenty pages as the writer thought his way forward. The reader who asks of each striking sentence not only what it says but how often, in what company, and in which bundle it appears will be immune to most of the damage the five misreadings do, and will have acquired, as a by-product, the reading habits that every unfinished classic in the tradition demands.

A sixth misreading completes the set, and it is the one this guide has been organised against from its first paragraph: the belief that the manuscripts can tell us what Marxism really is, in independence from the tradition that actually formed. Marxism as a historical force was built, for its first half-century, entirely without this text, from Capital, the Manifesto, the polemics, and the organisational writings, and any account of the doctrine that silently substitutes the workshop of 1844 for that public history has ceased to describe the movement and begun to describe a counterfactual. The manuscripts can correct the tradition, challenge it, and enrich it; the reception history shows them doing all three. What they cannot do is replace it retroactively, because a doctrine's identity includes the texts its adherents actually read, and the estranged labour pages were not among them until the tradition was already old. Holding that boundary firmly is what allows the manuscripts their genuine authority, as evidence and as provocation, without the inflation that has so often made them a prize to be captured rather than a text to be read.

## The Excerpt Notebooks: The Reading Behind the Manuscripts

The manuscripts proper are only the visible ridge of a much larger body of Paris writing: the excerpt notebooks in which Marx digested his reading. Through 1844 he filled notebook after notebook with extracts, in French and German, from the economists and socialists he was studying, and the manuscripts grew directly out of that digestion, sometimes continuing an argument begun in the margins of an excerpt. The most famous of the notebook texts, his comments on James Mill's Elements of Political Economy, were written in the same months and breathe the same air as the third manuscript: money as the estranged mediator between man and man, production for exchange as a system in which human relations appear as relations between things, and the credit system as a moralised parody of trust. Generations of editors have printed the Mill comments near the manuscripts, and some early presentations blurred the boundary between notebook and manuscript altogether. The careful reader keeps the genres distinct. An excerpt notebook is a quarry and a gymnasium; a manuscript, even an unfinished one, is a construction site with an argument rising on it. Confusing the two produces the common error of citing a remark Marx copied from an economist, or tossed off against one, as a settled doctrine of the manuscripts.

The reading list itself instructs. Marx worked through Smith in the French translation, through Ricardo and his popularisers, through Say's treatise, through Sismondi's critique of the new economy's human costs, and through French writers on the condition of the labouring classes, among them Eugène Buret's study of working-class misery in England and France. He read as an enemy apprentice: the notebooks show him translating the science into his own questions as he went, so that by the time the columns of the first manuscript were ruled, the categories of wages, profit, and rent were already loaded with the anthropological charge that would detonate them. This is why the digest half of the first manuscript, so often skipped as derivative, is better understood as the record of a hostile takeover in progress. The science is being learned perfectly and forgiven nothing, and the estranged labour pages are unintelligible as an achievement unless the reader has watched the learning happen. Skim the columns, by all means, as the manuscript map advises. But know what is being skimmed: not juvenilia, but the sound of a tradition's foundations being quietly repossessed.

## What the Manuscripts Do Not Contain

Expectations management is part of honest guidance, and readers who come to the manuscripts from the mature work's reputation should be told plainly what is absent. There is no theory of value here: the labour theory is assumed from the economists rather than reconstructed, and the distinctions that organise Capital, between labour and labour power, between use value and value in their developed forms, are absent or embryonic. There is no theory of crisis, no account of the state beyond the announced intention to write one, no developed conception of history as a succession of modes of production, and no strategy, party, or programme: the political content is a philosophical communism of human essence, closer to the French and German socialist literature Marx was reading than to anything in his later practice. Class appears as the economists' revenue classes, proprietors, capitalists, workers, rather than as the historically formed agents of the later writings. Even the concept of estranged labour, the text's glory, is deployed without the analytical machinery that would later let Marx distinguish the forms of appearance of a relation from the relation itself.

None of this is a reproach, and the reader who registers the absences accurately gains two advantages. The first is protection against anachronism in both directions: against importing Capital into the notebooks, as though the young writer possessed in embryo everything the old one proved, and against quarantining the notebooks as a foreign country, as though the man who wrote them shared nothing with the author of the value form analysis but a name. The second advantage is a sharper sense of what the manuscripts uniquely do contain, which the mature work nowhere repeats with the same directness: a phenomenology of labour under private property, an account of needs as historical products, and a statement of communism as the recovery of the human senses and powers rather than as an administrative arrangement. The corpus is large enough for specialisation, and the manuscripts' speciality is precisely the territory the later texts assume, cite obliquely, or leave to one side. Knowing the absences is what allows the presences to be valued at their true weight rather than at the inflated price of fame.

## The Editors Themselves: Three Hands on the Text

The arrangement dependency has human agents, and a complete guide should give them faces. David Ryazanov, founder of the Marx-Engels Institute in Moscow, was the recovery's architect: a scholar of formidable learning who built, in the decade after the Russian Revolution, an archive that collected, photographed, and transcribed the Marxian Nachlass from repositories across Europe, and who conceived the Gesamtausgabe as a work of pure documentation that would serve scholars of every persuasion. The manuscripts' Russian preview of 1927 was his doing. His arrest in 1931, in the machinery of Stalin's purges of the old scholarly guard, removed him before the volume appeared, and the edition he had designed was completed under Vladimir Adoratsky, a successor of different character and lesser independence. The Moscow text of 1932 is therefore doubly mediated: by an institute working under a dictatorship's eye and by the loss, mid-course, of the one editor whose scholarly authority might have resisted that eye. None of this impugns the transcription's quality, which specialists respect. It does mean the edition's presentation of the manuscripts as a finished early work, titled, ordered, and bound like one, harmonised conveniently with an institutional need for monuments, and the reader is entitled to know the harmony existed.

The Leipzig editors came from the opposite pole of the socialist world. Siegfried Landshut and Jacob Peter Mayer were social democratic scholars, Mayer a historian of the labour movement, working for a commercial publisher in a republic entering its terminal crisis. Their collection was an act of intellectual politics of a democratic kind: to present the young Marx whole, the critic of Hegel, the manuscripts, The German Ideology, as the humanist foundation of a socialism they opposed both to fascism and to the communist orthodoxy that claimed Marx as its property. Their transcripts were faulty, their omission of the first manuscript a serious scholarly failure, and their misidentified fourth manuscript an embarrassment. Their historical effect, however, was larger than the Moscow volume's, because their book was priced for readers and their Marx answered the questions their moment was asking. When the Nazis took power within a year, the milieu that might have corrected the Leipzig text from within was destroyed or exiled, and the text's errors and emphases travelled with the exiles into French and American discussions. Three hands, then, and three different necessities, archival, political, and commercial, stand between the modern reader and the Paris desk. Naming them is not gossip about editors. It is the arrangement dependency with its causes shown, and it completes the case for the citation discipline this guide has urged: in a text transmitted by such hands, the reader who cannot say which edition a claim comes from does not fully know the claim.

## Later Editions and the Settling of the Text

The textual history did not end in 1932; it entered a long process of correction and standardisation whose milestones a researcher should know. In the German Democratic Republic, the Marx-Engels Werke, the standard East German edition, at first omitted the manuscripts from its numbered volumes and issued them in 1968 in a supplementary volume, a placement that itself testified to the text's awkward status within an orthodoxy unsure whether the humanist young Marx was an asset or a liability. The English Collected Works of 1975 gave the manuscripts their now-standard Anglophone scholarly form in the third volume, alongside the other writings of the young Marx, with the Livingstone and Benton translation. Most consequentially, the second Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe, the MEGA2 project launched in the 1970s as a fully historical-critical edition, published the manuscripts in 1982 in its first section's second volume with a fresh examination of the originals in Amsterdam, and chose the boldest editorial course in the text's history: rather than pretend to a single definitive sequence, it presented the material so that the reader can follow the manuscripts both in the order of their physical survival and in the traditional reading order, with the apparatus laying out the evidence for each arrangement.

That editorial decision is the arrangement dependency acknowledged at the level of the discipline's flagship edition, and its significance deserves emphasis. When the most exacting scholarly edition of an author's works declines to print one order as the order, the question of sequence has been officially returned to the reader, where, for this text, it always materially belonged. Scholars continue to argue the details: the exact placement of particular leaves, the reading of difficult handwriting at points where interpretations have been built on single words, the dating of the bundles relative to the excerpt notebooks. Such arguments are sometimes represented in popular accounts as pedantry. They are the opposite. In a text where an editor's comma can relocate an argument's emphasis and an editor's sequence can crown a different conclusion, philology is philosophy conducted by other means, and the manuscripts' readers inherit the obligation to take it seriously. The settling of the text, then, is real but partial: the wording is as secure as modern editing can make it, the broad bundle order is well evidenced, and the architecture remains, and will remain, a matter of declared editorial judgement rather than recovered fact.

## Three Passages That Carry the Argument

Close reading earns its name on particular pages, and three passages in the manuscripts carry a disproportionate share of the whole. The first is the opening movement of the estranged labour analysis, where Marx performs the manoeuvre that distinguishes his procedure from both the economists and the moralists. He begins from a fact of political economy itself: the worker produces wealth, palaces, beauty, and the more he produces the poorer he becomes, receiving back the means of bare subsistence while the world of things he has made grows in value as his own world falls. Where the economist registers this as a law of the system and the moralist protests it as an injustice of distribution, Marx asks what relation the fact expresses. The answer he constructs, that the product stands against the producer as an alien power because the labour embodied in it was never the producer's own, converts a datum of distribution into a theory of production. Readers should notice the logical shape, because Marx would use it for the rest of his life: accept the science's fact, refuse the science's framework for it, and derive the hidden relation the fact presupposes. The estranged labour pages are the first full performance of that shape, and their fame rests partly on the performance being visible here, in workshop conditions, rather than polished away in a published book.

The second passage is the money discussion in the third manuscript, and its form is as instructive as its content. Marx assembles quotations from Shakespeare and Goethe, Timon's curse on gold and Mephistopheles on the power of purchase, and treats the poets as witnesses whose testimony political economy must answer. Money, in the analysis he builds around them, is the alienated ability of mankind made portable: it transforms real incapacities into their opposites, makes the ugly man confident of love, the dishonest man respected, the fool taken for gifted, because it buys the qualities it confuses. The passage matters beyond its brilliance. It shows the young Marx already refusing the idea that a social power becomes innocent by becoming universal and impersonal, already treating the mediator, the thing through which men relate, as the place where the relation's character is decided. A reader who follows the money pages into Capital's treatment of the value form will find the same refusal, now disciplined by two decades of economics, and the line of descent between the notebooks and the book runs through this passage as directly as through any other.

The third passage is the verdict on Hegel in the critique of the dialectic, and specifically its double structure, gratitude and indictment fused in single sentences. Marx credits the Phenomenology with grasping the self-creation of man as a process and the essence of labour as objectification and its supersession, a credit no other young Hegelian paid so precisely. He then argues that Hegel's labour is only abstract mental labour, that the estrangement overcome in the Phenomenology is the estrangement of thought from itself, and that consequently the rich human content of the real process, the senses, needs, and activities of actual men, drops out of the dialectic it was supposed to animate. The double verdict is the manuscripts' methodological charter: take from a system the discovery it made in idealist form, convict the form, and rehouse the discovery in a material account of human activity. Whether the verdict is fair to Hegel remains a live scholarly question, and readers should hold it as a question. But the charter's influence is not in question, because it describes exactly what Marx spent the manuscripts, and arguably the rest of his career, doing to political economy itself.

## The Positive Doctrine: Senses, Needs, and the Rich Human Being

Commentary on the manuscripts concentrates on the negative analysis, estrangement, and treats the positive statements about unestranged human life as decorative lyricism around the argument's edge. That distribution of attention distorts the text, because the third manuscript's positive doctrine is what gives the negative analysis its standard and its stakes. Its central figure is the rich human being: not the man who has much, a figure the manuscripts treat with contempt as the summit of the having mode, but the man whose needs, senses, and relations are developed and many-sided, whose wealth consists in the range of his life activity and his capacity to appropriate the world through all his senses as his own work. Marx's claim that the forming of the five senses is a labour of the entire history of the world to date, paraphrased here because the sentence deserves a reader's own encounter with it, makes the senses themselves historical products: the eye that enjoys painting, the ear that requires music, are achievements of a developed human practice, degraded under private property to the single sense of possession. The emancipation the manuscripts imagine is therefore not ascetic. It is the recovery by all the senses of a world that estrangement had reduced to a stock of ownable things.

The doctrine of needs completes the figure. In the manuscripts' argument, needs are not a fixed natural schedule to which production ministers; they are historically produced, refined and degraded by the same social process that produces goods. The system of private property, Marx argues, extends products and needs into an apparatus of artificial appetite at one pole while reducing the worker's needs below the animal level at the other, so that civilisation within barbarism and refinement within privation are paired outputs of one machine. The surviving pages of the second manuscript had already sounded this theme in the bitter inventory of renunciations preached to the poor. Read together, the needs passages amount to the manuscripts' most prophetic economics: a critique not of consumption's level but of its manufacture, aimed at the producers of desire rather than at the desiring. Modern readers who meet the manuscripts expecting a period piece are often startled by these pages, and the startlement is instructive, because it reveals how much of the text's afterlife has been conducted on the estrangement pages alone. The positive doctrine is the part of the workshop that the mature work, with its stricter disciplines, declined to restate, and it remains the principal reason the manuscripts continue to be read by people who have no stake in the continuity quarrel at all.

## Drafts as Evidence: A Method for Reading Unfinished Marx

The manuscripts pose, in their sharpest form, a problem that recurs across the whole corpus, and a reader who solves it here acquires a method of permanent value. Marx left more unfinished paper than any comparably influential modern thinker: the Kreuznach critique, the Paris notebooks, The German Ideology, the Grundrisse, the successive drafts of Capital's later volumes, the ethnological notebooks, the mathematical manuscripts. Engels edited some of this mass into books; the twentieth century's editions exhumed the rest; and at every stage interpreters have had to decide what weight a draft bears against a published work, a letter, a marginalium. The disputes are famous precisely because the stakes are general. Read the drafts as the true doctrine and the published works become compromises; read the published works alone and the doctrine loses its genesis, its alternatives, and its author's second thoughts.

The method this guide has practised, and now states, has four rules. Rank by authorial investment: a work the author revised for the press outweighs a draft, a draft repeatedly mined by its author for later work outweighs a draft abandoned untouched, and the manuscripts score high on that second test because the later Marx returned to their themes, if not to their pages. Date everything: a formulation of 1844 is evidence about 1844, and becomes evidence about 1867 only when a bridge is shown, a later notebook, a letter, a reprise, which is why the Grundrisse matters so much to the continuity case. Distinguish genesis from doctrine: a draft may prove how a concept was reached without proving that the concept, as reached, was retained, and the manuscripts are unrivalled evidence of genesis whatever their doctrinal status. And name the mediation: every quotation from posthumous material should carry, at least in the scholar's own notes, the identity of the edition and editor through which it passed, because in this corpus the channel is part of the message. Applied with patience, the four rules dissolve most of the melodrama in the young Marx controversy. What remains is a genuine and difficult question about how a thinker's categories changed across twenty years, which is the kind of question evidence can actually settle, and the kind the manuscripts, properly handled, help to settle rather than merely to inflame.

## Why the Manuscripts, of All the Early Writings

A fair question hangs over the whole subject: why did this bundle, of all the early papers exhumed in the same decades, become the one around which movements, curricula, and controversies organised themselves? The Kreuznach critique of Hegel's philosophy of right is comparably substantial; The German Ideology is comparably famous; the Grundrisse is ten times longer. Part of the answer is timing. The manuscripts arrived in 1932, at the moment when European Marxism's official forms were hardening and its dissident forms were searching for credentials, and they arrived bearing exactly the vocabulary, essence, estrangement, humanism, that the dissidents required and the officials lacked. Part is usability: the manuscripts are short enough to teach, lyrical enough to quote, and philosophical enough to found positions on, a combination none of the other exhumed texts matches. Part is the drama of the object itself, the lost-and-found story, the columns, the abandoned desk in Paris, which gave journalists and lecturers a narrative handle that a treatise cannot supply. And part is the accident, examined in this guide, that the flawed but vivid Leipzig edition reached the exiles first, so that the text's interpretive traditions were seeded by readers of genius working from imperfect paper in catastrophic years.

The consequences for the text's public identity have been permanent. The manuscripts, rather than Capital, became the Marx of the 1968 generation's posters and paperbacks, the Marx cited in debates about work, consumption, and the quality of life by people who had never met a surplus value calculation, and the Marx against whom a later generation of scholars, keen to restore the economist, defined their own rigour. Both identities are partial, and the working rule for readers is to notice which Marx a given discussion is using and whether the manuscripts are being asked to do work they can bear. As testimony about their author's formation and as a phenomenology of estranged labour, they bear a great deal. As a system of Marxist doctrine, they bear almost nothing, because a system is precisely what their author never made of them. The fame is justified. The uses must be audited one by one.

## The Evidence on Both Sides: Continuity and Break

Because the manuscripts' most famous employment is as an exhibit in the quarrel between continuity and break, a reader's guide owes its readers the exhibits themselves, staged as fairly as the evidence allows and without the verdict this series assigns elsewhere. For continuity, the strongest exhibits are these. Marx's own retrospective in the preface to his 1859 Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy narrates a single sustained inquiry running from the Rheinische Zeitung through Paris and Brussels to the economic studies, presenting the Paris period as the beginning of the work rather than as a discarded prelude. The Grundrisse of 1857 and 1858, a private draft no faction commissioned, returns to the manuscripts' territory, estrangement, the development of human capacities as an end in itself, rich individuality, with a freedom the break thesis struggles to dismiss as mere vocabulary survival. And the published first volume of Capital contains the chapter on the labour process whose account of purposeful human activity, the worst of architects distinguished from the best of bees by imagination, breathes the manuscripts' anthropology even where the terminology has changed.

For the break, the exhibits are of a different kind and no weaker. Within a year of the manuscripts, the theses on Feuerbach criticise the standpoint the manuscripts had celebrated, and The German Ideology pours sustained scorn on the language of essence and estrangement as the stock in trade of the German ideology it exists to demolish. Across the mature work, the term species-being, the manuscripts' load-bearing concept, effectively vanishes, and with it the practice of measuring an economic relation against a philosophical anthropology; the later Marx measures relations against relations, and his explanations of exploitation require no doctrine of man's essence to function. Althusser's periodisation, whatever its exaggerations, rests on this observable shift of apparatus, and his challenge, to name the work the young Marx's central concept does in the old Marx's theory, has never received a comfortable answer. A reader who has worked through both columns of exhibits will understand why the quarrel has lasted: the continuity evidence is biographical and thematic, the break evidence is conceptual and methodological, and the two kinds do not cancel, because a man may pursue one inquiry all his life while rebuilding its foundations twice. That sentence is as far as fairness requires this guide to go; the argued verdict, with its deciding considerations, belongs to the article on [young Marx and mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/) already linked, and readers who stage the evidence honestly first will get more from it.

## Working With the Facsimile and the Apparatus

Researchers who go beyond the reading editions meet the manuscripts in their scholarly form, and a few words about that encounter belong in a complete guide. The originals rest in Amsterdam, at the International Institute of Social History, which holds Marx's and Engels's literary remains and supplies reproductions to scholars. The hand is difficult: a rapid German cursive of the period, dense with abbreviations, corrections, interlinear insertions, and cancellations, in which whole interpretations have turned on the reading of single words. The critical editions mediate that difficulty through an apparatus recording variant readings, editorial conjectures, and the physical evidence, paper, ink, pagination, on which dating and ordering rest. To use the apparatus is to read a second, quieter text beneath the first: the record of how the printed page was earned from damaged and disorderly paper. Students are rarely shown it, which is a pity, because an hour with the apparatus teaches the argument of this entire article more efficiently than any summary, the distance between the worktable and the volume made visible line by line.

Two practical counsels follow. Where a secondary work quotes the manuscripts in a form your edition does not show, suspect a different edition or translation before suspecting invention, and check the passage against the Collected Works text and, if the matter is serious, against the second MEGA's apparatus. And where an interpretation is built on the presence, absence, or placement of a single passage, verify the passage's physical status: whether it stands in a bundle's continuous sequence or in an insertion, whether its page numbering is Marx's, whether later editors moved it. Such verification is not pedantry. In a corpus where the flagship edition itself prints two orders, physical status is interpretive evidence of the first rank, and the habit of checking it, once formed on these manuscripts, will protect a researcher's work on every posthumous text the tradition contains, from the assembled volumes of Capital to the prison notebooks of Gramsci, whose editors faced the same disorder and made their own fateful arrangements.

## For students and exam candidates

Your task with this text is double: know the argument, and know the object's status, because examiners reward the second as the sign of the first being properly understood. The twenty pages that matter most are the estranged labour pages, the crude communism and communism discussion, and the critique of Hegel's dialectic, in that order of priority. When you quote, quote by manuscript and section, and if a question invites you to treat the manuscripts as Marx's philosophy in finished form, build the qualification into your opening lines: three unfinished manuscripts, abandoned in 1844, first published in 1932 in two arrangements, their order editorial. That single move distinguishes your answer from the summary-driven scripts around it. Keep the four dimensions of estrangement in their derivational order, product, activity, species-being, man from man, and be ready to state the strongest objection to the text's authority, the abandoned-notebooks argument, before you answer it, since the objection and its evidential reply are the standard high-mark territory on this topic.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Set the estranged labour extract, but set it as a manuscript before you set it as an argument. Ten minutes spent on the physical facts, the columns, the lost second manuscript, the two editions of 1932, the absence of an authorial title, inoculates a seminar against the two failures that plague discussion of this text: treating it as a sacred book whose sequence proves things, and dismissing it as juvenilia unworthy of the attention its influence demands. The most productive exercise is comparative and cheap to stage: give the class the same passage as printed in two editions or two translations, with the order or the key term differing, and let them discover the arrangement dependency in their own hands before you name it. Frame the humanist and break readings as a genuine scholarly quarrel with evidence on both sides, and require every contribution to specify which manuscript bundle its evidence comes from. The class will argue better about everything else afterward.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this text is philological, and its working rules follow from everything in this guide. Cite by bundle and section, name your edition and translator in any publication that builds an argument on wording, and treat any sequence-dependent claim, yours or another scholar's, as carrying an editorial component that must be argued rather than assumed. The second MEGA's presentation, with its dual ordering and full apparatus, is the reference point for the state of the evidence, and its departures from the 1932 Moscow reconstruction are the first thing to check when an older secondary work reports a reading that surprises you. Three live questions reward attention. The dating and internal order of the third manuscript's bundles relative to the excerpt notebooks remains refinable, and with it the exact genesis of the communism discussion. The relation between the manuscripts' anthropology and the Grundrisse's later categories is the strongest available test of the continuity thesis, stronger than any juxtaposition of the manuscripts with Capital alone. And the reception history, the passage of a flawed Leipzig text through exile networks into the post-1956 debates, is a model case of how editions make doctrines, documented well enough to support general claims about textual transmission in political traditions. Journalists covering anniversaries or controversies around Marx should take one discipline from this article: never describe the manuscripts as a book Marx wrote in a year without adding that he neither finished, ordered, titled, nor published it. That sentence of care costs nothing and prevents the most common error in the public record.

## How to Approach the Manuscripts: A Closing Verdict

The verdict of this guide can be stated in a sentence and then cashed out. Approach the Paris Manuscripts as the finest workshop record in the Marxist tradition: the place where its central inquiry can be watched forming, under the twin pressures of an economics being mastered and a philosophy being broken, in papers whose disorder is not an obstacle to understanding but a large part of what there is to understand. Read the columns for the method, the estranged labour pages for the discovery, the third manuscript for the reach, and the whole with its editorial history held constantly in view. Refuse both available sentimentalities: the piety that upgrades a draft into a testament because its sentences are beautiful, and the severity that dismisses a draft as juvenilia because its author had the discipline to outgrow parts of it. Marx abandoned these papers and spent forty years becoming the writer who no longer needed them in that form. The papers survived their abandonment, slept through the century that made their author's name, and woke into a second life stranger than most finished books achieve. A reader who knows exactly what they are, three manuscripts, ruled columns, a lost middle, two births in one year, an order supplied by editors and a Marx partly made by that order, is the only reader fully entitled to say what they mean.

A last word about the kind of pleasure this text offers, since guides should be honest about rewards as well as difficulties. The Paris Manuscripts are not efficient reading. They repeat themselves, break off, resume, summarise other men at length, and demand of the reader a tolerance for disorder that finished classics spare us. What they give in return is rarer than efficiency: the sight of a great intelligence at the moment of finding its subject, with the tools half made and the workshop door open. Readers who finish the route set out in this guide will own something no summary can transfer, a first-hand acquaintance with the papers themselves, their columns and gaps and sudden sustained flights, and a permanent immunity to the two lazy sentences with which the manuscripts are usually dismissed or enthroned. That immunity, in a field where lazy sentences about Marx circulate by the million, is worth the week's evenings the route requires.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the Paris Manuscripts about?

They are working papers in which Marx, digesting the classical economists in ruled columns, develops the analysis of estranged labour and asks what private property is and what its supersession would mean. The first manuscript moves from wages, profit, and rent to the four dimensions of estrangement; the third manuscript takes up private property, communism in its crude and developed forms, needs, money, and a critique of Hegel's dialectic. Their subject, beneath the economics, is what labour is and what it has become under private property, and their author's own later summary of the period's work would treat these studies as the point where his critique of political economy began in earnest.

### Q: Why were the Paris Manuscripts not published in Marx's lifetime?

Because they were never prepared for publication. Marx wrote them in the spring and summer of 1844 as private studies toward a projected critique of politics and political economy, then left Paris under an expulsion order, carried the notebooks to Brussels and London, and spent the following decades on other works, repeatedly reworking his critique into new manuscripts rather than returning to these. The papers remained among his literary remains, passing to Engels and then to party archives. Their non-publication was not suppression or refusal by publishers; it was the ordinary fate of workshop drafts in the hands of a writer who habitually abandoned strata of work as his argument moved on.

### Q: When were the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts first published?

In 1932, eighty-eight years after they were written, and twice in that single year. The Marx-Engels Institute in Moscow published them in the Gesamtausgabe under the title Ökonomisch-philosophische Manuskripte, printing the three manuscripts complete and in reconstructed order. In Leipzig, Siegfried Landshut and Jacob Peter Mayer published them the same year in their collection of early writings, under the title Nationalökonomie und Philosophie, in a cheaper edition that omitted the first manuscript, rearranged the rest, and reached Western European readers first. Parts had appeared in Russian in 1927. Every later edition descends from, or corrects, one of these two births.

### Q: Which parts of the Paris Manuscripts should you read first?

Begin with the short drafted preface for the author's stated intention, then go straight to the estranged labour pages that close the first manuscript, reading them twice. From there step back into the wages column to watch the economists' premises generate the estrangement analysis, skim the profit and rent columns for method, and then give the third manuscript's discussions of private property and communism, money, and Hegel your remaining time. The surviving pages of the second manuscript make a short bridge between the first and third. This order follows the text's own genesis, discovery first and digestion second, and it avoids the discouragement of opening with dense excerpt material.

### Q: Are the Paris Manuscripts a finished work?

No, and the question matters because so much commentary proceeds as though the answer were yes. The manuscripts are three incomplete working drafts, written and abandoned in 1844, partly in ruled columns, with the rent discussion unfinished, the second manuscript mostly lost, and no authorial ordering, title, or preparation for the press. What readers hold as a book is an editorial reconstruction, first performed in 1932 in two different arrangements and refined by later critical editions. Their unfinished state does not diminish their evidential value about Marx's development. It does mean that arguments from the volume's sequence or climax are partly arguments from its editors, and should be discounted accordingly.

### Q: Which translation of the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts is best?

There is no single best, only versions with different strengths, and the responsible practice is to know which one is on your desk. Martin Milligan's version, published in Moscow in 1959, was the first complete English translation and set the vocabulary of the early debate. T. B. Bottomore's rendering of the principal portions appeared in Erich Fromm's Marx's Concept of Man in 1961 and remains fluent and readable. The Livingstone and Benton translation, in the Collected Works and the Penguin Early Writings, is the version most scholarship cites. Read the argument in one, check any load-bearing sentence against another, and where they diverge, consult the German term before building on the passage.

### Q: What is the crude communism passage about?

It is Marx's warning, in the third manuscript, against a communism that abolishes private property only by generalising it. Crude communism, as he describes it, is driven by envy and the urge to level down: it wants to abstract from talent, possession, and culture rather than transform them, denies personality, and treats the community itself as the universal capitalist, with the category of worker preserved rather than superseded. Marx treats it as the first, still barbaric form of the doctrine, proof that a movement can negate private property while remaining inside its logic. Against it he sets a communism beyond having, the positive restoration of human qualities and senses.

### Q: Why are the manuscripts written in columns?

The first manuscript is laid out in parallel columns headed wages of labour, profit of capital, and rent of land because Marx was digesting classical political economy, which distributed society's produce among those three revenue classes, and the ruled page let him enter excerpts from Smith, Ricardo, and others under their proper headings, side by side, where their contradictions became visible. The column was a study instrument, closer to an account book or an excerpt ledger than to a book draft. Significantly, the format collapses where the estranged labour analysis begins, because that argument concerns the relation producing all three revenues and cannot be filed under any one of them.

### Q: Did the publication of the manuscripts change how Marx was read?

Profoundly. Before 1932 the tradition knew an economist and revolutionary whose major works were published ones, and doctrine had formed without the manuscripts. Their appearance supplied a philosophical young Marx, theorist of estrangement and species-being, and reorganised interpretation around the relation between that figure and the author of Capital. Herbert Marcuse's response in the year of publication opened the humanist reading; Marxist humanism carried the text through the post-1956 debates; Louis Althusser's counter-thesis of an epistemological break was framed against it. Even scholars who reject both camps work in a field the manuscripts created, arguing about a continuity question that could not have been posed in their absence.

### Q: What should you read alongside the Paris Manuscripts?

Read the Yearbooks essays, On the Jewish Question and the introduction to the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, for the position Marx brought to Paris, and his Paris comments on James Mill for the notebooks' closest companion. Then read the theses on Feuerbach and The German Ideology, written within the following two years, to watch the manuscripts' standpoint being criticised by its own author. Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity explains the anthropology the manuscripts borrow. For the mature echo, the Grundrisse's pages on labour and rich individuality are the essential bridge, and Capital's opening chapters show what became of the inquiry under stricter discipline.

### Q: How do editors reconstruct the order of an unfinished manuscript?

From physical and internal evidence, since the author left no instruction. Editors study the paper itself: Marx's Roman page numbering on the sheets, the column headings and their sequence, changes of ink and hand, the way a discussion breaks and resumes, and cross references inside the text. They compare the bundles with dated neighbours, such as the excerpt notebooks, to fix relative chronology. Where the evidence supports more than one sequence, honest editions say so; the second MEGA presents the manuscripts in dual orderings with the apparatus exposed. Reconstruction is therefore an argued hypothesis about paper, not a recovery of intention, and its conclusions carry the probabilities of the evidence rather than the certainty of an author's design.

### Q: What should a reader do when two translations of the manuscripts differ?

Treat the divergence as information, not noise. First, identify the term at stake: the manuscripts turn on words such as Entfremdung and Entäusserung, estrangement and externalisation, whose ranges English cannot reproduce one to one, so every translator chooses which distinction to preserve. Second, check the passage in a second translation and, where editions provide it, note the German headword. Third, ask whether your interpretation depends on the divergent wording. If it does, say so in your own writing, name both translations, and rest the argument on the sense the German supports. A difference between translations of an unfinished draft is the arrangement dependency at sentence level: a reminder that mediation is everywhere in this text.

### Q: Why does the third manuscript matter for the idea of private property?

Because it contains the manuscripts' most original move on the subject: the reversal of the expected explanation. Common sense and much socialist rhetoric treated private property as the cause and estranged labour as its effect. In the third manuscript Marx argues that, examined closely, private property is the product and consequence of estranged labour, labour's own alienated activity congealed into an institution, and then, once established, the means by which labour continues to estrange itself, means and consequence in a single circle he flags rather than resolves. The same bundle draws the conclusion for communism: superseding private property means transforming the labour relation itself, not merely redistributing its titles, which is why crude redistribution earns his scorn a few pages later.

### Q: What should a teacher explain before setting the estranged labour extract?

Four facts, briefly, before the text is opened. The extract comes from an unfinished private manuscript, not a book, and its author abandoned it at twenty-six. Its order and headings are editorial, supplied when the manuscripts were first published in 1932. Its vocabulary is molten: estrangement and alienation words slide in ways no translation fully stabilises. And its argument has a derivational order, product, activity, species-being, man from man, that students should track rather than sample. A class told these things reads the extract as evidence and thinks about what kind of object it is; a class not told them reads it as scripture or as juvenilia, and argues past the text either way.

### Q: How did the manuscripts first reach readers in English?

Slowly, and through selections before wholes. Scholarly discussion in English drew on the German editions and on journal renderings of portions during the 1950s, and the first complete English translation was Martin Milligan's, published in Moscow in 1959. The wider breakthrough came with Erich Fromm's Marx's Concept of Man in 1961, which printed T. B. Bottomore's translation of the principal philosophical portions with Fromm's humanist introduction and put the estranged labour pages into paperback circulation for a general audience. The Livingstone and Benton translation followed in the Collected Works and Penguin Early Writings of 1975, giving English readers the scholarly standard text most citations now follow.
