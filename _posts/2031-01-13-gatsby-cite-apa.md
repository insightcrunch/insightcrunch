How to cite The Great Gatsby in APA turns on a single claim. Citing the novel right in APA: APA's author-date style needs care with a 1925 novel read in a modern edition, so the original-and-reprint date convention matters, the correct handling of the publication years being where APA citations of Gatsby most often go wrong. That single sentence carries the whole article. Every other detail below is machinery for getting that one decision right across the reference list, the in-text citation, and the source types that orbit a student essay: the novel itself, an edition or reprint, a journal article, a book chapter, and a digital text. How to cite The Great Gatsby in APA is a question with a short answer and a long explanation, and this guide gives both, with the short answer first and the long explanation worked through example by example until no plausible student situation is left uncovered.

![Student writing an APA reference entry for The Great Gatsby - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-39.webp)

The short answer is the worked reference entry. In APA seventh edition, the edition of the novel that sits on the student's desk is cited by the year of that edition, and the novel's first publication is recorded in a parenthetical note at the end of the entry:

Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925)

Every in-text citation to the novel then carries both years, separated by a slash, with the original year first: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) in parenthetical form, or Fitzgerald (1925/2004) in narrative form. A page number joins the parenthetical whenever words are quoted directly: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93). Those four forms, the reference entry, the parenthetical in-text citation, the narrative in-text citation, and the page-located quotation citation, cover the overwhelming majority of student needs, and the rest of this guide exists so that each form is understood rather than merely copied.

The reason the two-date convention exists is not decorative. APA is an author-date system, which means the date in the citation tells the reader when the text under discussion entered the world. For a novel published in 1925 and read in a reprint from 2004, a single date misleads in one direction or the other: the 1925 date alone pretends the student consulted the first edition, and the 2004 date alone pretends the novel is a twenty-first-century text. The convention splits the difference honestly. The year inside the parentheses next to the author's name names the version actually used, which is what lets the reader find that version, and the note at the end of the entry names the original publication, which is what lets the reader place the work in its historical moment. The in-text slash carries both pieces of information into the sentence without a footnote. Once that logic is clear, the convention stops feeling like a fussy rule and starts feeling like the natural way to tell the truth about which book was read and when it was written.

### Where does the original publication note sit in the reference entry?

The original publication note sits at the very end of the reference entry, after the publisher, as a parenthetical sentence: (Original work published 1925). Nothing follows it, and it is never placed inside the date parentheses after the author's name.

The position matters because each position in an APA entry carries a defined meaning. The date parentheses after the author name identify the version the writer consulted, which is the version the reader would go and find. The publisher that follows the title confirms that version. The note at the end then adds a historical fact about the work itself: it first appeared in 1925. Swapping those roles, putting 1925 in the date position and 2004 in the note, would tell the reader to look for the first edition while holding a modern reprint, and the citation would fail its most basic job, which is to let a reader retrace the writer's steps. Students who memorize the entry as a rhythm, author, version year, title, publisher, original-work note, almost never misplace the note, because the rhythm itself enforces the logic.

A common point of confusion is whether the note changes when the student read a different reprint, perhaps a classroom edition with an introduction or a digital text. It does not change in its wording. Whatever the version year is, the note still reads (Original work published 1925), because the novel's first publication is a fixed fact about the work. What changes is the year in the date parentheses and the publisher element, which track the version actually used. A 1995 Scribner classroom edition would be cited with (1995) after the author's name and the same closing note, and the in-text citation would read (Fitzgerald, 1925/1995). The convention is stable across editions precisely because the two pieces of information it carries, version consulted and original publication, are kept in their own lanes.

### How do you write a narrative in-text citation for the novel?

A narrative in-text citation weaves the author and both dates into the sentence grammar: Fitzgerald (1925/2004) argues, or In Fitzgerald's (1925/2004) account. The slash pair stays inside the parentheses, original year first. The author's name does the grammatical work, so it never repeats inside the parentheses.

The narrative form exists for sentences where the author's name does grammatical work, and the choice between parenthetical and narrative is a style decision, not a correctness decision. A student who writes "The green light has been read as an emblem of American aspiration (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004)" is making a parenthetical citation, while a student who writes "Fitzgerald (1925/2004) frames the green light as an emblem of American aspiration" is making a narrative one. Both are correct, both carry the same information, and an essay will naturally mix them. The narrative form becomes especially useful when the same paragraph cites the novel several times, because repeating the author's name in the sentence and letting the dates ride in parentheses reads more smoothly than a chain of identical parentheticals.

One subtlety deserves attention. In a narrative citation, only the year travels with the name in the parentheses; the page number for a quotation still belongs in the parenthetical that follows the quoted words. A correct narrative quotation looks like this: Fitzgerald (1925/2004) describes the valley of ashes as a "fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat" (p. 27). The two parentheticals divide the labor: the first identifies the work, the second locates the words. Students who try to combine them, writing Fitzgerald (1925/2004, p. 27) describes, produce a sentence that reads awkwardly and misplaces the locator away from the quotation it locates. The habit to build is simple: narrative citation for the work, trailing parenthetical for the page, each doing one job.

The reference entry is worth studying as an object in its own right before moving to the other source types, because every element in it is doing defined work and every punctuation mark is load-bearing. Take the entry again:

Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925)

The author element inverts the name and reduces the given names to initials, with a period after each initial and a comma between surname and initials. The date element puts the version year in parentheses, followed by a period that belongs to the sentence structure of the entry. The title is italicized, set in sentence case so that only the first word and proper nouns are capitalized, which is why "great" and "gatsby" appear lowercase in the middle of the title even though the book's cover prints them in title case. The publisher appears without decoration, no "Inc." or "Ltd.", just the name as it appears on the title page, followed by a period. Then the original-work note closes the entry. Every one of those choices can be checked against the APA manual, and checking them once, deliberately, is worth more than copying the entry a dozen times without understanding it.

Sentence case on the title trips up more students than any other formatting detail, because it contradicts what the book cover shows and what other citation styles require. MLA uses title case for book titles; APA uses sentence case for book titles in the reference list. The rule applies to the entry only. When the title appears in the student's own prose, normal title capitalization returns: "In The Great Gatsby, Fitzgerald..." The student who writes *The Great Gatsby* in title case inside the reference list has not made a content error, only a formatting one, but it is the kind of formatting error that signals unfamiliarity with APA to a marker, and it is avoidable with one deliberate check.

The initials in the author element raise their own small question: why F. S. and not Francis Scott? APA abbreviates all author given names to initials in the reference list, without exception for famous authors. The in-text citation never uses the initials at all, only the surname. So the reference list says Fitzgerald, F. S. and the essay says (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004), and the two forms never exchange parts. The only situation in which initials enter an in-text citation is when two different cited authors share a surname, in which case the first initial disambiguates them, as in (F. Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) versus (Z. Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) if a student were citing Zelda Fitzgerald's work alongside her husband's. That situation is rare in a Gatsby essay, but the rule is worth knowing because surname collisions are the one case where the clean author-date pair grows a third element.

Punctuation inside the entry follows a plain logic: a period ends each element, and the parenthetical note is itself a sentence, which is why it carries its own closing period inside the parentheses. The version-year parentheses carry the period after them because the date element is complete and the title element is beginning. Students sometimes drop the period after the date parentheses or after the publisher, producing an entry that looks almost right but reads as careless under a marker's eye. The check is mechanical: author element, period; date element, period; title element, period; publisher, period; note, which is its own parenthetical sentence. Five beats, five periods, and the entry is done.

### When does an APA in-text citation for Gatsby need a page number?

An APA in-text citation needs a page number whenever the student quotes the novel's words directly, and APA encourages but does not require page numbers for paraphrases. The form is (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93) for a single page. Use pp. for a quotation spanning two or more pages.

The page number rule is one of the few places where APA gives the writer discretion, and the discretion has a logic. Direct quotation always needs a page because the reader must be able to verify the exact words. Paraphrase carries the writer's own wording, so the citation's job is only to name the source, and the page is optional. In practice, markers of Gatsby essays like to see page numbers on paraphrases too, because a specific page anchors the claim and shows the student is working from the text rather than from memory of the plot. A student who writes "Nick's narration distances him from the events he describes (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 45)" has done more than cite; the page number tells the reader exactly where the distancing happens, and the essay reads as grounded in the novel rather than floating above it.

Block quotations, which APA requires for quotations of forty or more words, keep the same citation logic with a changed layout. The quotation is set as an indented block without quotation marks, and the parenthetical citation follows the final punctuation of the block. A forty-word passage from Gatsby is roughly four to five lines of the novel, so block quotation arises when a student quotes a full descriptive paragraph, such as a party scene or a landscape passage, at length. The forty-word threshold is counted on the quoted words, and students who are unsure should count rather than guess, because a block quotation used for a short quote looks as wrong as a short quote used where a block was required.

The page number in the citation refers to the edition actually used, which is another reason the version year matters. Page 93 of the 2004 Scribner paperback is not page 93 of a classroom edition with an introduction or a digital text without stable pagination. The reference entry's version year and the in-text page number work together: the year tells the reader which printing the page number belongs to. A student who cites (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93) while holding a different edition has a citation that points at the wrong page, and the careful version is to check the page in the edition the entry names. This is the practical payoff of the two-date convention; it is not abstract honesty but a working locator system.

Digital texts complicate the page number, because many e-book editions have no stable page numbers at all. APA's answer is to cite whatever locator the digital text provides: a chapter number, a section heading, or a paragraph number. A citation to a digital text might read (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, Chapter 3) or (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, Chapter 3, para. 4). The principle is unchanged: give the reader the most precise location the source allows. A student citing a free digital text of the novel, where the entry names the digital publisher, should never invent a print page number for it; the chapter locator is the honest substitute, and markers accept it because APA explicitly provides for it.

### How do you alphabetize Fitzgerald entries in the reference list?

Fitzgerald entries alphabetize by surname, then by given initials, then by year, lettered a, b, c when one author has multiple works in the same year. The novel stands alone under F unless the student cites more Fitzgerald. Sort by what the in-text citation shows, since the list exists to match it.

Alphabetizing sounds trivial until a reference list contains the novel, a journal article about the novel by another author, and the APA manual itself. The ordering rule is letter by letter on the surname, ignoring the punctuation: American Psychological Association under A, Fitzgerald under F, and any critic's surname in its own place. "Nothing before something" is the old cataloguer's rule that APA keeps: a surname that is a prefix of another files first. Students rarely need the fine points, but they need the headline habit, which is to sort by what the in-text citation shows, because the reference list exists to let a reader match (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) to its full entry without hunting.

The same-author ordering rule matters more than students expect, because a Gatsby essay that cites the novel will often cite it alongside criticism, and some students also cite Fitzgerald's other writings. When one author has several entries, they order chronologically by year, earliest first, and entries from the same year take lowercase letters: (2020a), (2020b), with the letters assigned by the alphabetical order of the titles. For the novel's own entry, the ordering year is the version year, the year in the date parentheses, not the original year. An entry dated (2004) with the 1925 note files under 2004. That detail surprises students who feel the novel "belongs" in 1925, but the reference list is a finding tool, and the finding year is the version year.

A reference list with a single Fitzgerald entry needs none of this machinery, and most student Gatsby essays have exactly one: the novel. The machinery is worth knowing anyway, because the moment a student adds a second Fitzgerald work, or a biography of Fitzgerald, the ordering questions arrive, and a student who has seen the rule once handles them without panic. The deeper lesson is that APA's reference list is not a bibliography in the literary sense; it is a lookup table keyed to the in-text citations, and every rule about it serves the lookup.

### What changes if you also cite another work by Fitzgerald?

If the essay cites another work by Fitzgerald, the in-text citations disambiguate by year, and same-year works take letter suffixes. Two Fitzgerald works from different version years cite as (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) and (Fitzgerald, 1925/1995), needing no letters. Shared surnames with a different author add the first initial instead.

The letters enter only when the version years match. Suppose a student cites the novel in the 2004 Scribner reprint and also cites a 2004 collection of Fitzgerald's stories published by the same house. Both entries would carry (2004) in the date position, and the in-text citations would need (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004a) and (Fitzgerald, 2004b) to tell them apart. The letters are assigned by the alphabetical order of the titles in the reference list, and the reference list entries carry the same letters. This is genuinely rare in an undergraduate Gatsby essay, which almost always cites the novel as the sole Fitzgerald entry, but it is the correct answer to the question students ask when they imagine the collision, and knowing it prevents the anxious workaround of citing the second work incorrectly.

A more realistic collision is between Fitzgerald the novelist and a critic who shares the surname. Gatsby criticism is a large field, and a reference list can easily contain Fitzgerald, F. S. for the novel and a different Fitzgerald for a scholarly work. APA resolves this by adding the first initial to the in-text citations: (F. Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) for the novelist and (M. Fitzgerald, 2018) for the hypothetical critic. The reference list needs no change; the initials disambiguate at the point of citation. Students should check the reference list for shared surnames as a final pass, because this is the kind of detail that is easy to miss while drafting and easy to fix once spotted.

The deeper point of these disambiguation rules is that the in-text citation is a key and the reference list is the lock it fits. Every complication in the in-text system exists because some reference list somewhere contained two entries that one simple key could not distinguish. When students see the rules as a key-and-lock system rather than as arbitrary fussiness, the whole apparatus becomes memorable, and the novel's own entry, with its slash date, is just one more key cut to fit one more lock.

The reprint-date error deserves its own sustained treatment, because it is the single most common APA mistake in Gatsby essays and the one this article's central claim names. The error has two mirror forms. In the first, the student cites only the modern reprint date: the reference entry reads Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. with no original-work note, and the in-text citation reads (Fitzgerald, 2004). In the second, the student cites only the original date: the entry reads Fitzgerald, F. S. (1925). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. and the in-text citation reads (Fitzgerald, 1925). Both are wrong, and they are wrong in ways that matter beyond the lost mark.

The reprint-only form is the more common of the two, because it is what a citation generator produces when the student feeds it the reprint's title page. The generator reads the copyright page, finds 2004, and builds an entry for a 2004 book. The entry is internally consistent and looks authoritative, which is why students trust it. Its failure is historical: it presents The Great Gatsby as a book of 2004, and any reader who knows the novel will read the citation as an error, while any reader who does not know the novel will be misled about when it was written. In an essay that discusses the novel as a document of the 1920s, a 2004-only citation quietly contradicts the essay's own argument about the book's historical moment.

The original-only form is less common but more interesting, because it usually comes from a student who knows the novel is from 1925 and reasons that the true date is the honest one. The instinct is understandable and the history is right, but the citation fails as a finding tool: a reader who goes looking for the 1925 first edition will not find the student's 2004 paperback, and the page numbers in the essay will not match the first edition's pagination. APA's version-year rule exists precisely for this situation. The year in the date parentheses names the artifact consulted, not the work's historical moment; the historical moment gets the note at the end. The convention lets the student be historically accurate and bibliographically honest at the same time, which is why it is worth learning rather than approximated.

Before-and-after correction pairs make the difference concrete. Consider a student draft with this reference entry:

Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner.

and this in-text citation: (Fitzgerald, 2004). The corrected version reads:

Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925)

with the in-text citation (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). Two changes, one note and one slash, and the citation now tells the full truth. Consider the mirror draft:

Fitzgerald, F. S. (1925). *The great Gatsby*. Charles Scribner's Sons.

with (Fitzgerald, 1925). The correction keeps the note but changes the date position to the version actually read: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925), with (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). The student who held the 2004 paperback while citing 1925 was closer to the truth than the student who cited only 2004, but both needed the same two-part fix.

A third error pattern involves the note's wording. Students who have seen the convention but not the exact form write things like (First published 1925) or (Originally published in 1925) or place the note before the publisher. APA's wording is fixed: (Original work published 1925). The capitalization, the word "work", and the position at the very end of the entry are all specified. A marker who knows APA will accept the standard form without a second thought and will flag variants as signs of a half-remembered rule. The wording is short enough to memorize as a phrase, and memorizing the phrase is better than reconstructing it from logic each time.

Citation generators deserve a frank assessment. They are fast, they handle the mechanical formatting, and for straightforward books they are usually right. For reprinted classic works they are usually wrong in exactly the way described above, because they read the version's copyright page and stop there. The generator's output is a starting point that the student must audit against the two-date convention, not a finished citation. The audit takes seconds: does the entry have the original-work note, does the in-text citation carry the slash date. A student who runs that two-item check on every generated entry will catch the error every time, and the habit generalizes to every reprinted classic the student will ever cite.

The novel's entry also interacts with edition statements, which are a separate APA element from the original-work note. If the student read a classroom edition that calls itself, say, the second edition or an authorized text with an introduction, the edition information appears in parentheses after the title: *The great Gatsby* (2nd ed.). The original-work note still closes the entry unchanged. The two parentheticals do different jobs: the edition statement describes the version, the note dates the work. An entry for a described edition would read:

Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby* (2nd ed.). Scribner. (Original work published 1925)

Students sometimes merge the two, writing the original publication into the edition parentheses, which produces a malformed entry that satisfies neither rule. Keeping them separate, edition after the title, original-work note at the end, preserves both pieces of information in the positions where APA readers look for them.

Introductions, prefaces, and forewords by other authors, which many classroom editions include, are cited only if the student actually discusses them. An introduction by a scholar is a separate work with its own author, and its entry names that author, not Fitzgerald. A student who quotes the introduction's argument about the novel cites the introducer: the entry would begin with the introducer's name, give the introduction's title or a description of it, and include the book's publication details. A student who merely read the introduction for background and cites only the novel does not list the introduction at all. The reference list contains the works cited, not the works read, and that distinction keeps the list honest and short.

Translators raise a parallel question that barely applies to Gatsby in an English-language essay but completes the picture. A translated work names the translator in parentheses after the title, and the original-work note still records the first publication. Gatsby in translation would carry both the translator credit and the 1925 note. The pattern is consistent: everyone who made the version gets credited in the version's entry, and the work's original date gets the closing note. Once a student sees the pattern across editions, introductions, and translations, the system feels designed rather than arbitrary, because it is.

The five source types a Gatsby essay is likely to cite, each with its correct reference entry and in-text form, are gathered in the table below. It is the article's findable artifact: the one-glance answer to the whole citation question.

|---|---|---|
| Novel, modern reprint | Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925) | (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004); narrative: Fitzgerald (1925/2004) |
| Novel, original 1925 edition consulted | Fitzgerald, F. S. (1925). *The great Gatsby*. Charles Scribner's Sons. | (Fitzgerald, 1925) |

The table's placeholder names in the journal article and book chapter rows stand in for whatever real critic and scholar the student is actually citing; the formatting around them is what matters. The novel rows use the real entry because the novel is the one work every reader of this guide cites. Each row pairs the entry with its in-text form so that the two can be checked against each other, which is the single most useful verification habit in APA: every in-text citation must have a matching reference entry, and every reference entry must be cited in the text.

### How do you cite a journal article about the novel?

A journal article entry names the authors, the year, the article title in sentence case without italics, the journal title in title case with italics, the volume italicized, the issue in parentheses, the page range, and the DOI as a full link.

The journal article is the second most cited source type in a Gatsby essay, after the novel itself, because literary essays lean on published criticism. The entry's architecture repays close reading. The article title is not italicized and uses sentence case, which distinguishes it visually from the journal title, which is italicized in title case. The volume number is italicized but the issue number is not, a small typographic distinction that markers check. The page range uses an en dash in print conventions, but APA entries in plain text use the hyphen that the student's word processor produces, and the house rule of this site, no en dashes anywhere, is satisfied by the ordinary hyphen. The DOI, when the article has one, appears as a complete URL at the end of the entry, and APA's seventh edition guidance is that the DOI is a link, not a code: https://doi.org/10.0000/example, not doi:10.0000/example.

Older articles without a DOI are cited without one; the entry simply ends with the page range. Students sometimes feel the entry is incomplete without a link, but APA does not require a URL for a print journal article that the student read in print or through a library database. A database name or a library proxy URL is never included. The entry describes the article as published, not the route the student took to reach it, which keeps the reference list stable regardless of which library the next reader uses.

The in-text citation for a journal article follows the ordinary author-date pair with no slash, because criticism is not a reprinted classic: (Critic, 2018). Quotations from the article take a page number in the usual way: (Critic, 2018, p. 60). The contrast with the novel's slash date is instructive: the slash appears only where the work's original publication predates the version cited. A 2018 article read in 2018 needs no such apparatus. Students who apply the slash to every source have misunderstood the convention's trigger, which is republication, not age.

Author counts change the in-text form but not the entry's logic. Two authors are joined by an ampersand in the entry and in parenthetical citations: (Critic & Collaborator, 2018), while narrative citations use the word "and": Critic and Collaborator (2018) argue. Three or more authors collapse to the first surname plus "et al." in every in-text citation from the first use: (Critic et al., 2018). The reference list, by contrast, names up to twenty authors in full before using an ellipsis. The asymmetry is deliberate: the text stays readable while the list stays complete.

### How do you cite a book chapter on the novel?

A book chapter entry credits the chapter author, dates the chapter, titles the chapter in sentence case without italics, then nests the book's details: the editor, the book title in italics, the page range in parentheses, and the publisher. The chapter author leads because the in-text citation names that person.

The book chapter, often from an essay collection with titles like New Essays on The Great Gatsby, is the source type students mishandle most after the novel itself, because the entry has two authors in two roles and students confuse them. The chapter author comes first and owns the in-text citation: (Scholar, 2016). The editor appears later in the entry, after "In", with the role marked: In E. F. Editor (Ed.), and for multiple editors (Eds.). The book title is italicized in sentence case. The chapter's page range sits in parentheses before the publisher: (pp. 112-138). Each of those elements answers a reader's question: who wrote the chapter, in what book did it appear, who edited the book, and where in the book is the chapter.

The most common chapter error is citing the editor as the author, which produces an in-text citation that points at the wrong person and a reference entry that misattributes the argument. The test is simple: whose name would the student speak when summarizing the argument in a seminar? That name leads the entry. A related error is omitting the page range, which leaves the reader unable to find a chapter inside a three-hundred-page collection. The page range is not optional decoration; it is the locator, and APA requires it for chapters exactly as it requires page numbers for quotations.

Introductions and forewords to the novel's own edition follow the chapter pattern when they are cited, with one adjustment: the "chapter" is the introduction, and the book is the novel's edition. The entry names the introducer as author and describes the piece. A student who cites both the novel and its introduction will have two entries, one led by Fitzgerald and one led by the introducer, and the in-text citations will keep them distinct. This is the correct handling of the situation described earlier: the introduction is a separate work, and APA treats it as one.

### How do you cite a free digital text of the novel?

A free digital text is cited like the version it is: the author and the version's year lead, the title follows in sentence case italics, the digital publisher is named, a stable URL closes the entry, and the original-work note still records 1925.

The digital text row in the table above shows the print reprint for simplicity, but many students read Gatsby in a free digital edition, and the entry for such a text needs its own worked form. The APA pattern for an e-book without a DOI, drawn from the manual's own examples, names the original publisher and the digital provider together when both appear on the title page, then gives the URL. For the novel the shape is Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925), with a URL appended when the student read the text at a stable open-access address. The manual's own illustration of this pattern pairs a historic reprint with its digital host and a URL, then the in-text citation carries the slash date: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). The student's entry follows the same shape with the student's actual digital source. What must not happen is the invention of a print page number for a pageless text; the in-text locator becomes the chapter or paragraph, as shown in the table.

Retrieval dates are the detail students most often add unnecessarily. APA requires a retrieval date only for sources designed to change over time, such as wikis or frequently updated web pages. A novel's text does not change, so no retrieval date appears, even for a digital text. The entry's URL should be a stable link to the full text, not a search page or a library session link. A student who pastes a long session-specific URL has cited a doorway rather than the text; the fix is to use the work's stable address.

The digital text also raises the question of which version year to use when the digital provider gives no clear publication year for its edition. APA's answer is to use the best available date and to mark uncertainty honestly: a copyright year on the digital title page, or "n.d." only when no date can be determined at all. For Gatsby, the digital edition almost always carries a year, and the student's job is to read the title page rather than guess. The original-work note is unaffected by any of this; 1925 is 1925 regardless of the digital provider's dating practices.

A sample reference list walkthrough shows how the pieces assemble in a finished essay. Imagine a student essay that cites the novel in the 2004 Scribner reprint, two journal articles on Gatsby criticism, and the APA manual itself for a formatting point. The reference list, double-spaced with hanging indents in the submitted paper, reads:

American Psychological Association. (2019). *Publication manual of the American Psychological Association* (7th ed.). American Psychological Association.

Critic, A. B. (2018). Reading wealth and waste in *The great Gatsby*. *Journal of Modern Literature*, *41*(3), 55-72. https://doi.org/10.0000/example

Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925)

Scholar, C. D. (2016). Nick Carraway and the ethics of narration. In E. F. Editor (Ed.), *New essays on The great Gatsby* (pp. 112-138). Cambridge University Press.

Four entries, four in-text keys, alphabetical order by surname, and the novel sitting in its proper place under F. The walkthrough also demonstrates the hanging indent: the first line of each entry flush left, subsequent lines indented half an inch, which is the visual signal that separates one entry from the next. Students formatting in a word processor should use the paragraph indent setting rather than tabs or spaces, because tabs break when the document reflows.

The in-text citations that pair with this list would include (American Psychological Association, 2019) for the manual point, (Critic, 2018) for the criticism, (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) for the novel, and (Scholar, 2016) for the chapter. A reader can match each parenthetical to its entry without ambiguity, which is the entire purpose of the system. The student should perform this matching as a final check: read through the essay, collect every parenthetical, and confirm each one has an entry and each entry is cited. Orphan entries and orphan citations are among the most penalized APA errors precisely because they break the key-and-lock contract.

Practice tasks turn the guide from reading material into skill. The first task is transcription with understanding: take the novel's entry and write it out by hand or by typing, then label each element in the margin, author, version year, title, publisher, original-work note, until the five beats are automatic. The second task is conversion: take three deliberately broken entries, one with only the reprint date, one with only the original date, one with the note in the wrong position, and repair each, writing the corrected in-text citation alongside. The third task is assembly: given the four sources in the walkthrough above, build the reference list from scratch in alphabetical order with hanging indents, then write one sentence citing each source, alternating parenthetical and narrative forms. A student who completes all three tasks has not memorized APA; the student has internalized the logic that makes the slash date and the closing note feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.

### How do you build an entry from a book you have never cited before?

Copy the author, year, title, and publisher from the title and copyright pages, arrange them in APA order, and add the original-work note for a reprinted classic. The entry is a transcription of the book's front matter, not a composition from memory, so every element is checked against the physical pages.

The transcription habit generalizes beyond Gatsby and is worth practicing deliberately, because most citation errors are transcription errors rather than rule errors. Students misread the year, guess at the publisher, or reproduce the title from memory with the wrong capitalization. The fix is procedural: open the book, read the title page for the author and publisher, read the copyright page for the year, and write each element down before arranging them. The arrangement is the only part that requires knowing APA; the facts come from the book. A student who separates fact-gathering from formatting will find that the formatting step becomes easy, because it operates on reliable inputs.

The copyright page deserves a second look as a document. It typically shows the original copyright year, renewal years, and the year of the current printing or edition, sometimes in a dense block of small type. The year APA wants is the edition's publication year, which is usually identifiable as the most recent year associated with the edition rather than a mere reprinting. When the page lists only a string of undifferentiated years, the title page's edition statement, or the publisher's cataloging data, usually resolves the ambiguity. The student who cannot resolve it after checking both pages should use the most defensible year and keep the original-work note accurate; an honest best reading beats a confident guess.

Publisher names on title pages follow conventions of their own. Modern reprints often show a shortened imprint, Scribner rather than Charles Scribner's Sons, and APA wants the name as printed, without the corporate suffixes like Inc. or Ltd. but also without antiquarian restoration. The entry describes the artifact, and the artifact's title page is the authority for the publisher's name. Students who feel the shorter name looks informal should resist the urge to expand it; the informality is in the imprint, not in the citation, and APA's stripping rule is designed for exactly this case.

### How do you handle several critics in one literature review paragraph?

Give each critic their own author-date citation and keep the novel's slash-date citations visually distinct from the plain-year criticism citations. The reader should always see at a glance whether a claim is grounded in the novel or in what someone wrote about it.

The literature review paragraph is where student essays most often blur the line between the primary text and the criticism, and the citation system is the tool that keeps the line sharp. A paragraph that moves from a critic's reading of the green light to the student's own reading of the same symbol needs its citations to mark the handoff. The critic's claim carries (Critic, 2018); the student's own textual claim carries (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). The visual difference between the plain year and the slash date does quiet work here: a reader scanning the paragraph can distinguish primary-text claims from secondary claims without parsing the prose. That distinction is one of the understated benefits of the two-date convention; it makes the novel's citations look different from everything else in the essay.

Multiple critics in one paragraph raise the ordering question: whose citation comes first when two critics support the same point? APA has no strict rule for citation order within a single parenthetical beyond alphabetical arrangement, so (Critic, 2018; Scholar, 2016) orders alphabetically by surname with a semicolon between. In practice, students writing about Gatsby rarely need multi-source parentheticals, because the essay's structure usually gives each critic a sentence of their own. The alphabetical multi-citation is worth knowing for the literature review's summary moments, where several scholars are invoked together to establish that a reading is widely shared.

The deeper discipline is attribution hygiene: every claim about what a critic argues must carry that critic's citation, and claims about the novel must carry the novel's. A paragraph that discusses a critic's argument for three sentences but cites the critic only once leaves two sentences unattributed, and a marker cannot tell whether the student is still reporting the critic or has moved to their own analysis. The safe practice is to cite at the point of each distinct claim, which produces the healthy density modeled in the worked paragraphs above. Under-attribution is the more common student failure; over-attribution is rare and easily trimmed.

### How do you audit a citation generator's output?

Run every generated entry through a two-item check: does the reference entry end with the original-work note, and does the in-text citation carry the slash date? Generators read the reprint's copyright page and stop there, so the reprint-date error is their characteristic failure on classic works.

The audit habit matters because generators are genuinely useful for the mechanical parts of citation: the italics, the punctuation, the ordering of elements, the DOI formatting. A student who builds every entry by hand will make more punctuation errors than a student who generates and then audits. The rational workflow is generation plus verification, not abstinence. The verification takes seconds per entry and catches the one error generators make systematically on reprinted classics. For ordinary contemporary sources, the generator's output can usually be accepted after a glance; for anything first published long before its version year, the two-item check is mandatory.

The audit should also cover the generator's metadata choices. Generators sometimes misidentify the author, pulling an editor's name into the author position for edited collections, or misread the title's capitalization and produce title case where APA wants sentence case. These are the same transcription errors a human makes, and the same defense applies: check the entry against the book's front matter rather than against the generator's confidence. A generator that offers a choice of source types should be given the right type; a book chapter entered as a book will produce a malformed entry no audit can fully repair, because the audit checks the output against the wrong template.

Students should also know what generators cannot do. No generator reads the essay, so none can perform the key-and-lock check that matches in-text citations to entries. No generator knows which edition was actually read, so none can choose the version year reliably when the metadata is ambiguous. And no generator understands the essay's argument, so none can decide whether a paraphrase needs a page number for evidentiary weight. The generator is a formatting assistant; the student remains the citing author, and the responsibility for the citation's truth stays with the human.

### How do you integrate short quotations with signal phrases?

Open with a signal phrase naming what the quotation shows, run the quoted words inside your sentence with quotation marks, and close with the parenthetical carrying both dates and the page number. The quotation should arrive prepared and leave cited, never dropped in to speak for itself.

The signal phrase is the most undervalued element of quotation technique. A quotation introduced by "Fitzgerald writes" tells the reader nothing; a quotation introduced by "Nick's unreliability surfaces when he describes the party as" tells the reader what to notice. The signal phrase does double duty in APA essays: it carries the narrative citation naturally, as in Fitzgerald (1925/2004) presents the party as, and it frames the quoted words so the reader knows their function before reading them. Students who master the signal phrase find that their paragraphs need fewer quotations, because each quotation is doing more work.

The grammar of the integrated quotation needs care. The quoted words must fit the sentence's syntax; a quotation that breaks the grammar reads as pasted rather than integrated. Short quotations can be trimmed with ellipses to fit, and APA's ellipsis formatting, three spaced periods, marks the omission honestly. Brackets mark any word the student adds for clarity, such as a pronoun's antecedent. These are small mechanics, but they are the difference between a quotation that reads as the student's sentence containing Fitzgerald's words and a quotation that reads as Fitzgerald's words interrupting the student's sentence.

The closing parenthetical belongs directly after the quotation marks, before the sentence's final punctuation for short quotations: ...gaudiness" (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 99). The page number's position is fixed; it never migrates to the end of the sentence when other material follows the quotation. When the quotation ends the sentence, the period follows the parenthetical. Students who have internalized the rhythm, signal, quotation, citation, period, produce clean integrations without thinking, and the cleanliness reads as competence.

### What does a finished three-source reference list look like?

List every cited work once, alphabetically by surname, double-spaced with hanging indents, under the centered bold heading References. Three sources make a short but complete list: the novel, one journal article, and one book chapter, each entry matched to its in-text citations.

A complete minimal example shows the whole system at small scale. Suppose the essay cites the novel in the 2004 Scribner reprint, a journal article on wealth in the novel, and a chapter on Nick Carraway from an essay collection. The three entries read:

Critic, A. B. (2018). Reading wealth and waste in *The great Gatsby*. *Journal of Modern Literature*, *41*(3), 55-72. https://doi.org/10.0000/example

Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925)

Scholar, C. D. (2016). Nick Carraway and the ethics of narration. In E. F. Editor (Ed.), *New essays on The great Gatsby* (pp. 112-138). Cambridge University Press.

Three entries, alphabetical, each with its own shape, and the novel's entry carrying the note that the other two do not need. The in-text citations that pair with this list, (Critic, 2018), (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004), (Scholar, 2016), are each distinct keys fitting distinct locks. A student who can build this list from scratch, without copying, understands APA's reference system; everything larger is the same operation repeated.

The minimal list also demonstrates what is left out. No entry for the dictionary the student consulted for a word definition, unless the definition is cited. No entry for the lecture where the essay topic was introduced. No entry for the novel's film adaptation, unless the essay discusses it. The list's honesty comes from its exact correspondence with the text's citations, and the final proofreading pass, matching each parenthetical to an entry and each entry to a parenthetical, is what guarantees that correspondence. Three sources or thirty, the pass is the same.

### What if the edition names no publisher clearly?

Use the publisher name as printed on the title page, stripped of corporate suffixes, and never substitute the historic imprint for the modern one. When the title page genuinely names no publisher, describe the situation honestly rather than inventing one, and keep the original-work note unchanged.

Print-on-demand and some digital editions present title pages with minimal publication data, and students encountering them should know the fallback rules. APA allows the publisher element to be omitted only when the author and publisher are identical, which is not the case here, so a missing publisher is a genuine problem to solve rather than a blank to leave. The first recourse is the copyright page, which often names the publisher even when the title page is sparse. The second is the edition's cataloging data or the retailer's product page, used cautiously and only for the publisher's name, not for the year. What the student must not do is promote a distributor or a bookseller into the publisher position; the publisher is the body that produced the edition, and guessing wrongly misattributes the version.

In the rare case where no publisher can be identified after a genuine search, the entry gives the remaining elements accurately and the in-text citation still carries the slash date, because the date convention does not depend on the publisher. The reference list entry remains honest about what is known: author, version year, title, original-work note. An incomplete entry built from verified facts is always preferable to a complete entry built partly from invention, and markers recognize the difference. The principle that runs through this whole guide repeats one last time: transcribe what the source shows, arrange it in APA order, and never invent what the source does not show.

### How do you cite the novel when your essay quotes the same passage twice?

Cite it the same full way every time: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93) on the second use exactly as on the first. APA has no ibid. and no shortened repeat form, so the citation does not shrink no matter how often the passage recurs.

The absence of ibid. surprises students who have met it in other styles, but APA's reasoning is straightforward. The author-date citation is short already, and repeating it in full keeps every citation independently matchable to the reference list. A reader who encounters (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93) on page nine of the essay should not have to remember what was cited on page four to understand it. Each citation is self-contained, which means the writer never has to track citation history while drafting. The cost is mild repetition; the benefit is a text that can be entered at any point and understood.

There is one narrow exception to the repeat-in-full rule, and it concerns citations within a single paragraph rather than across the essay. When a paragraph discusses the novel continuously and the reader cannot mistake which work is being cited, APA permits the year to be omitted from subsequent narrative citations within that paragraph after the first full citation. In practice this means Fitzgerald (1925/2004) on first mention in the paragraph, then Fitzgerald on later mentions within the same paragraph, provided no other source intervenes. The permission applies to narrative citations only; parenthetical citations keep the full form. Students should use this sparingly in Gatsby essays, because criticism citations often intervene and break the chain, restoring the need for the full form.

Quoting two different passages from the same page is handled without any special machinery: each quotation gets its own parenthetical with the same page number. A paragraph that quotes the description of the party and then the description of the guests, both from page 93, carries (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93) twice, once after each quotation. The repetition looks redundant to the drafting student but reads as precision to the marker, because each parenthetical is attached to specific quoted words and the attachment is what matters. Merging the two citations into one, placed after the second quotation, would leave the first quotation's words unattributed at their point of use.

The same logic governs paraphrases of the same passage restated across a paragraph. Each distinct claim gets its citation, even when the claims share a source and a page. The student who worries about over-citing should compare the risk: a few repeated parentheticals cost nothing, while an unattributed restatement of a distinctive reading risks the charge that the idea arrived without a source. APA's design favors the visible citation, and the Gatsby essay, with its heavy reliance on a single primary text, is exactly the kind of essay where the full repeat form earns its keep.

### What do markers check first on the References page?

Markers check the novel's entry first, because the two-date convention is where Gatsby essays most often fail. A correct slash date and original-work note signal competence; their absence signals a citation generator used without audit, and the rest of the list is read with suspicion.

The marker's skim follows a predictable order, and students who know it can self-mark in the same sequence. First, the novel's entry: is the version year in the date position, is the title in sentence case italics, and does the entry close with (Original work published 1925)? Second, the heading and layout: does the page say References, centered and bold, with double spacing and hanging indents throughout? Third, the alphabetization: does the order run cleanly by surname, and are any shared surnames disambiguated? Fourth, the in-text matching: does every parenthetical in the essay have an entry, and does every entry get cited? A list that passes those four checks will survive almost any marking scheme; a list that fails the first will color everything after it.

Error clustering is the reason the first check matters so much. Citation errors rarely travel alone. A student who missed the original-work note usually also missed the sentence case, because both errors come from the same source: an unaudited generator or a copied model from another style. A student who nailed the novel's entry usually nailed the rest, because the care that produces the note produces the punctuation too. Markers learn this pattern quickly, which is why the novel's entry functions as a signal for the whole list. The student's goal is to make that signal a strong one.

The self-marking routine mirrors the marker's skim and takes about five minutes. Read the novel's entry against the model entry in this guide, character by character, checking the author format, the date position, the title case, the publisher, and the note. Then check the page layout: heading, spacing, indent. Then walk the alphabet. Then walk the key-and-lock match between text and list. Each step is mechanical, none requires judgment, and the routine catches the errors that cost marks. Students who run it before every submission will find that APA stops being a source of anxiety and becomes what it is designed to be: a quiet, reliable apparatus that lets the essay's argument take the stage.

### How do you cite a specific chapter of the novel in the reference entry?

You do not: the reference list cites the whole novel once, and the chapter is named in the in-text locator, as in (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, Chapter 3). APA reserves chapter-level reference entries for chapters by different authors in edited books, not for chapters of a single-author novel.

The urge to give each cited chapter its own entry comes from a reasonable instinct, which is that a citation should point as precisely as possible at the words used. APA satisfies that instinct in the in-text citation rather than in the reference list. The reference list answers the question "which work", and the answer is the novel, cited once. The in-text citation answers the question "where in the work", and the chapter number, page number, or paragraph locator does that job. Splitting the novel into nine chapter entries would bloat the list, confuse the alphabetization, and misrepresent the novel's unity as a single work by a single author.

The confusion usually arrives via the edited-collection chapter, which does get its own entry. The difference is authorship. In an essay collection, each chapter has a different author, so each chapter is a different work that happens to share a binding; the entry must name the chapter's author or the attribution fails. In the novel, every chapter has the same author, so one entry attributes everything. The test the student can apply is simple: would the in-text citation's author change from chapter to chapter? If yes, separate entries. If no, one entry. For Gatsby the answer is always no.

Chapter locators in the in-text citation follow the same honesty principle as page numbers: give the most precise location the source allows. For a print reprint, the page number is usually more precise than the chapter, so (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93) is preferred where pages exist. For a digital text without stable pagination, the chapter becomes the primary locator, optionally refined with a paragraph count: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, Chapter 3, para. 4). A student who discusses an entire chapter's arc, rather than a passage within it, cites the chapter alone, and the citation is honest because the claim genuinely spans the chapter.

Quotations that cross a chapter boundary need no special treatment beyond the page range: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, pp. 110-112) locates the words regardless of where the chapter break falls. The reference entry stays silent on all of this, which is exactly its role. Students who grasp the division of labor, the list names the work, the citation locates the words, stop trying to make the reference entry do the citation's job, and their lists become shorter, cleaner, and correct.

## Verdict: the two dates are not optional

The verdict this guide takes is direct. The original-and-reprint convention is not optional polish on a Gatsby citation; it is the mechanism by which APA tells the truth about a 1925 novel read in a modern edition, and citing only the reprint date is the characteristic APA failure of the Gatsby essay. The correct forms are short enough to memorize: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925) in the list, (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) in the text, with a page number whenever words are quoted. Every other rule in this guide, the sentence case, the ampersands, the chapter nesting, the digital locators, is worth learning, but the two-date convention is the one that determines whether the citation is right or wrong in the sense that matters. A student who gets the dates right and misplaces a comma has made a small error; a student who formats every comma perfectly while citing only 2004 has misdated the novel by eighty years. Proportion the effort accordingly: dates first, then everything else. When a citation question arises that this guide does not settle, the student's recourse is the manual itself, cited above, and then the instructor's preference, since some courses modify APA details in their assignment sheets. A course sheet that specifies a particular edition, for instance, settles the version-year question for the whole class, and the student's entry follows the sheet. What the student should not do is split the difference between conflicting advice by inventing a hybrid form; APA's strength is its uniformity, and a consistent entry built on one authority beats a patchwork built on three. The two-date convention survives every such variation unchanged, because no course sheet and no edition changes the facts it records: the novel is from 1925, the copy on the desk is from its own year, and the honest citation says both.

### Reading a title page: where each element of the entry comes from

The reference entry is not invented from memory; it is transcribed from the book in the student's hands, and learning to read the book's front matter is the skill that makes every future entry accurate. Open the novel to the title page. The author's name appears as F. Scott Fitzgerald, and APA inverts and initialises it: Fitzgerald, F. S. Turn to the copyright page. It lists the original 1925 publication and the reprint year of this printing, perhaps 2004, perhaps another year. The reprint year, the year of the version actually held, goes in the date parentheses. The publisher's name on the title page, Scribner for the standard reprint, completes the entry, and the original-work note records the 1925 first publication. Four facts from two pages of front matter, arranged in APA order, and the entry is built.

The most instructive mistake at this stage is reading the wrong year off the copyright page. Copyright pages often list a string of years: the original copyright, renewals, and the latest printing. The student who grabs the earliest year has the original date; the student who grabs the latest printing year may have a print-run date rather than an edition year. The year APA wants is the edition's publication year, which is usually the most prominent recent year on the copyright page and the year the publisher associates with that edition. When the copyright page is genuinely ambiguous, the title page and the edition statement resolve it, and a classroom edition will usually name itself clearly. The habit of checking two pages rather than one eliminates most dating errors before they reach the entry.

The publisher element has its own small discipline. APA gives the publisher's name as it appears on the title page, stripped of corporate suffixes. Charles Scribner's Sons on the 1925 title page becomes the publisher for an entry built from the first edition; Scribner on the modern reprint's title page becomes the publisher for the reprint entry. Students sometimes expand the modern imprint into the historic full name out of a feeling that the older name is more correct, but the entry describes the version held, and the version held names Scribner. The principle from the date convention repeats here: the entry is a description of the artifact consulted, with the historical facts added in their designated places.

### The classroom edition: a complete worked entry with an edition statement

Classroom editions complicate the basic entry in predictable ways, and a full worked example shows how the complications resolve. Suppose the student reads a classroom edition that identifies itself as an authorized text with an introduction and explanatory notes, published by Scribner in 2004. The edition statement belongs in parentheses immediately after the title, in the entry's title position:

Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby* (Authorized text, with introduction and notes). Scribner. (Original work published 1925)

The exact wording of the edition statement follows what the edition calls itself, kept brief. The in-text citation is unchanged: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). The edition statement does not alter the date logic, the note position, or the citation form; it only tells the reader which version of the 2004 reprint was used. A student comparing notes with a classmate who read the plain reprint will have a different entry but an identical in-text citation, and that is correct: the in-text citation identifies the work, while the entry distinguishes the version.

The introduction in such an edition deserves a second look, because it creates the two-entry situation described earlier. If the essay quotes the introducer's argument, the introduction gets its own entry led by the introducer's name, and the novel keeps its Fitzgerald entry. The two entries will sit near each other in the reference list only if the surnames are near each other alphabetically; otherwise they file in their own places, and the in-text citations keep them distinct. A student who cites the introduction three times and the novel thirty times has a reference list of two entries, not one, and the list is complete because it contains exactly the works cited.

Explanatory notes in a classroom edition are cited like the introduction when they are quoted: the note's author, usually the editor, leads the entry. In practice students rarely quote explanatory notes, since the notes gloss words rather than advance arguments, but the rule stands ready for the student who does. The pattern is always the same: the person whose words are used leads the entry, and the container, the novel's edition, follows. That pattern is the whole of APA's treatment of works within works, and the chapter entry, the introduction entry, and the notes entry are all the same shape with different labels.

### Error gallery: eight broken entries and their repairs

A gallery of broken entries, each repaired, teaches faster than rules alone, because each breakage isolates one decision. First, the missing note: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. The repair adds the closing note: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925). Second, the original-only date: Fitzgerald, F. S. (1925). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. The repair moves the version year into the date position and adds the note: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925). Third, the misplaced note: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby* (Original work published 1925). Scribner. The repair moves the note to the end, after the publisher, where APA puts it.

Fourth, the title case title: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925). The repair lowercases the title to sentence case: *The great Gatsby*. Fifth, the full first names: Fitzgerald, Francis Scott (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925). The repair initialises: Fitzgerald, F. S. Sixth, the missing publisher period and the fused elements: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004) *The great Gatsby* Scribner (Original work published 1925). The repair restores the periods that separate the elements, because in APA the punctuation is the structure.

Seventh, the invented note wording: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (First published 1925). The repair uses the fixed phrase: (Original work published 1925). Eighth, the wrong in-text pairing: an entry that is correct, Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925), cited in the text as (Fitzgerald, 2004). The repair corrects the in-text form to (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004), because the entry and the citation are one system and a correct entry with a wrong citation is still wrong. Each of these eight appears in real student drafts every term, and each is fixed by a single deliberate check against the model entry.

### A paragraph with citations: the in-text forms in motion

The in-text forms are best learned inside a paragraph, where the choice between parenthetical and narrative becomes a matter of rhythm. Consider this student paragraph, with the citations doing their work:

Fitzgerald (1925/2004) opens the novel with Nick Carraway's claim to reserve judgment, a claim the rest of the narrative quietly undermines. The unreliability is structural rather than incidental: Nick presents himself as honest while arranging every scene to flatter his own detachment (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). When Nick describes Gatsby's parties as spectacles of "a universe of ineffable gaudiness," the language performs the very enchantment Nick claims to observe from outside (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 99). Criticism has long noted the gap between Nick's self-portrait and his behavior (Scholar, 2016), and the novel's famous closing passages depend on the reader sensing that gap without being told (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004).

The paragraph mixes narrative and parenthetical forms, quotes once with a page number, paraphrases twice without, and brings in a critic for support. The first citation is narrative because the sentence is about Fitzgerald's construction; the second is parenthetical because the sentence is about the narrative effect. The quotation carries (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 99) directly after the quoted words. The critic's citation needs no page because it supports a general claim. Every citation in the paragraph matches an entry in the reference list, and the paragraph reads smoothly because the citations serve the sentences rather than interrupting them.

A second paragraph shows the forms under heavier load, where the same source is cited repeatedly:

The green light organizes the novel's treatment of aspiration (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). It first appears as a distant, barely visible phenomenon, then returns at moments when Gatsby's longing is most exposed, and finally reappears in the closing pages as the novel widens its lens from one man's desire to a national myth (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). Fitzgerald (1925/2004) does not explain the symbol; he lets it accumulate meaning through repetition, which is why the light can carry both Gatsby's private hope and the essay's larger argument about the American dream without splitting apart. A quotation anchors the reading: the light is "minute and far away" when Nick first sees Gatsby reaching toward it (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 25).

Four citations to the same work in one paragraph, and none of them feels redundant, because each one attaches to a distinct claim. The narrative form in the third sentence varies the rhythm. The page number appears only where words are quoted. This is the realistic density of citation in a close-reading essay, and the student who can produce it without thinking has mastered the in-text system.

### The reference list page: formatting beyond the entries

The reference list is a page with its own formatting rules, and entries built perfectly but presented wrongly still lose marks. The page begins with the word References, centered and bold, at the top. Entries follow in alphabetical order, double-spaced throughout, with the hanging indent: first line flush left, subsequent lines indented half an inch. There are no extra blank lines between entries beyond the double spacing, no numbering, no bulleting, and no subdivision by source type. The list is one alphabetical sequence, novels beside journal articles beside manuals, ordered by surname.

Double spacing surprises students who have written the essay's body in whatever spacing their draft used, because the reference list's spacing is specified independently. The rule is simple: the reference list is double-spaced, period, regardless of the essay's body spacing. The hanging indent is applied through the paragraph settings, not with tabs, and it applies to every entry uniformly. A reference list in which some entries hang and others do not reads as unfinished, and the fix is a single format-painter pass.

The "References" heading itself is worth getting right because it is the most visible element on the page. It is the plural "References", not "Reference", not "Bibliography", not "Works Cited". Each of those alternatives belongs to a different system, and using one of them in an APA paper signals that the writer is mixing styles. The heading is centered and bold, matching the formatting of the essay's other major headings. Students who have internalized the heading, the spacing, and the indent have the page's architecture; the entries are the furniture.

### Why the reference list uses sentence case

Sentence case in the reference list is not an aesthetic preference; it is a retrieval convention with a history. APA's reference list descends from the psychology journal literature, where article titles were set in sentence case to distinguish them from the journal titles that contained them, and the convention extended to book titles for consistency. The visual result is a list in which the emphasized elements, the italicized book and journal titles, stand out against the unemphasized article and chapter titles, and a reader scanning the list can tell at a glance which element is the container and which is the contained. The student's entry participates in that visual grammar: *The great Gatsby* in italics and sentence case is instantly recognizable as a book, while a chapter title in plain sentence case is recognizable as a part.

The rule's application has fine points worth stating. Proper nouns keep their capitals inside sentence case, which is why the novel's title would retain a capitalized proper noun if it had one. The first word after a colon in a subtitle is capitalized, because APA treats the subtitle as a new sentence: *The great Gatsby: A new reading* would be the form if the edition carried a subtitle. None of this affects the standard novel entry, which has no subtitle and no internal proper nouns, but the student who cites criticism with subtitled articles will need the colon rule, and it is better learned here than discovered under deadline.

The contrast with the essay's prose is the point students must hold firmly. In the sentence "In *The Great Gatsby*, Fitzgerald...", the title appears in title case because it is a title being named in prose. In the reference list, the same title appears as *The great Gatsby* because it is data in a retrieval system. The two forms are both correct in their places, and the student who writes title case in the list or sentence case in the prose has swapped the registers. A useful test: if the title is doing grammatical work in the student's sentence, it takes title case; if it is sitting in the entry, it takes sentence case.

### Paraphrase, quotation, and the page number decision

The page number decision repays a fuller treatment because it is where APA gives writers the most discretion and where markers form the strongest impressions. The rule, restated: direct quotations always carry page numbers; paraphrases may carry them. The practice: in a Gatsby essay, paraphrases usually should. The reason is evidentiary. A paraphrase without a page number asks the reader to trust the writer's summary of a 180-page novel; a paraphrase with a page number shows the reader where the summary comes from. The page number on a paraphrase is a small courtesy that reads as scholarly care, and its absence, while not an error, reads as vagueness.

There is a limit, and it matters. A paragraph that paraphrases the whole of chapter 3 cannot honestly cite a single page, and a student who writes a sweeping claim about the novel's arc with (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 45) has implied a precision the claim does not have. For broad claims, the citation without a page is the honest form: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). For claims tied to a scene, a passage, or a chapter, the page or chapter locator belongs. The student should ask, for each citation, what the reader would need to check the claim, and provide exactly that. That question is the whole of citation ethics in miniature.

Quotations have their own discipline beyond the page number. Short quotations run inside the sentence with quotation marks; long quotations of forty or more words are set as blocks. The forty-word count is on the quoted words, and the block is indented half an inch from the left margin, without quotation marks, with the citation after the final punctuation. Students sometimes block-quote to avoid integrating a quotation into their syntax, but the block is for length, not for convenience, and a short quotation set as a block looks as wrong as a long quotation run into the text. The integration matters too: a quotation should arrive with a signal phrase that tells the reader what it shows, not dropped into the paragraph to speak for itself.

### Citing Gatsby alongside other works in one essay

Real essays cite the novel alongside criticism, and the citation system must handle the ensemble. Consider an essay that reads Gatsby against another novel of the period. The reference list would contain two reprinted classics, each with its own original-work note and its own slash date, and the in-text citations would keep them distinct by author: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) and (Other, 1920/2003), with the placeholder standing for the real comparison author. The slash convention scales naturally: every reprinted work gets the same treatment, and the reader can see at a glance which works are historical and which are contemporary.

The ensemble also tests the alphabetizing and the key-and-lock check. With six or eight entries, the student should sort carefully, check shared surnames for initial disambiguation, and then walk the essay matching each parenthetical to its entry. The most common ensemble error is the orphan: an entry for a source mentioned in a discarded draft paragraph, or a citation to a source never added to the list. Both are caught by the matching walk, which takes minutes and prevents the errors markers penalize most heavily. The walk is the closest thing APA has to a proofreading ritual, and students should adopt it as the last step before submission.

A final ensemble consideration is the over-citation of the novel. Some students cite (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) after every sentence of a close-reading paragraph, producing a stutter of identical parentheticals. APA permits a cleaner approach: when a paragraph clearly discusses one source throughout, the citation can appear once per distinct claim rather than once per sentence, provided the reader can always tell which claims the citation covers. The paragraph examples above model this: four citations in a paragraph of sustained novel discussion, each attached to a claim, none redundant. Under-citation is an error, but so is citation noise, and the skilled writer calibrates between them.

### What the manual itself models

The APA manual is both the authority for these rules and a citable source in its own right, and its entry is worth giving in full because students cite it when defending a formatting choice:

American Psychological Association. (2019). *Publication manual of the American Psychological Association* (7th ed.). American Psychological Association.

The entry illustrates several rules at once. The author is a corporate body, spelled out in full, which is why the in-text citation reads (American Psychological Association, 2019) on first use. The edition statement, (7th ed.), follows the title, showing the same position the classroom edition's statement occupied in the novel's entry. The author and the publisher are the same body, and APA lists it twice rather than abbreviating, which surprises students who want to shorten the repetition. The in-text citation for a corporate author may be abbreviated after the first use if the abbreviation is introduced, but the reference entry always spells it out.

The manual's own examples for books include the reprint pattern this guide teaches: a version year in the date position, the publisher, and the original-work note at the end, with the in-text citation carrying the slash date. The manual presents this for classic works generally, and the novel is a textbook instance. A student who wants to verify the convention independently can consult the manual's book examples and find the same architecture: version dated, original noted, both years in the text citation. The guide's claims are not interpretations; they are the manual's pattern applied to this novel, and that is why the guide can state them without hedging.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you cite The Great Gatsby in APA?

You cite the novel with the version year in the reference entry's date position, the original 1925 publication in a closing note, and both years in the in-text citation. The reference entry reads: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925). The in-text citation reads (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004), or Fitzgerald (1925/2004) in narrative form. Add a page number when quoting directly: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93). The version year changes with the edition you actually read, while the 1925 note never changes, because the note records the work's first publication and the date position records the artifact consulted.

### Q: What is the APA reference entry for the novel?

The reference entry for a modern reprint is: Fitzgerald, F. S. (2004). *The great Gatsby*. Scribner. (Original work published 1925). Each element does defined work: the inverted author name with initials, the version year in parentheses, the title in italics and sentence case, the publisher, and the parenthetical original-work note at the very end. If you read a different reprint, substitute its year and publisher in the date and publisher positions and keep the 1925 note unchanged. The entry is byte-identical every time it appears in your essay's reference list, and every in-text citation to the novel must match it.

### Q: How does the APA in-text citation work?

The APA in-text citation is an author-date key that points to the matching reference entry. For the novel it carries both years with a slash, original first: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) in parentheses, or Fitzgerald (1925/2004) when the name is part of the sentence. Direct quotations add a page number: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93). Paraphrases may include a page number but are not required to. The in-text citation never includes the title, the publisher, or the original-work note; those live in the reference entry. Its only job is to name the author and date so the reader can find the full entry.

### Q: How does APA handle the original and reprint dates?

APA splits the two dates into two positions. The year of the version actually consulted goes in the date parentheses after the author's name, because the reference list is a finding tool and the reader needs the version year to locate that version. The original publication is recorded in a parenthetical note at the very end of the entry: (Original work published 1925). The in-text citation then carries both years separated by a slash, original first: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004). This division lets the citation be bibliographically honest about which book was read and historically honest about when the work was written, without either fact crowding out the other.

### Q: Why not cite only the modern reprint date?

Citing only the reprint date, as in (Fitzgerald, 2004), presents The Great Gatsby as a twenty-first-century book and misdates the novel by eighty years. In an essay discussing the novel as a document of the 1920s, the citation quietly contradicts the essay's own historical argument. The reprint-only entry is also what citation generators produce by default, since they read the reprint's copyright page and stop there, which is why it is the most common APA error in Gatsby essays. The fix costs one note and one slash: add (Original work published 1925) to the entry and cite (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004) in the text.

### Q: How do you cite editions and sources in APA?

Editions and sources each get the treatment their role requires. The novel's edition is described by the version year in the date position, an edition statement after the title when the edition names one, and the unchanged original-work note at the end. A journal article about the novel is cited as an ordinary article: author, year, sentence-case article title, italic journal title, volume, issue, pages, DOI. A book chapter is nested: chapter author first, then the edited book's details with the page range. A digital text names its digital publisher and URL. Introductions and notes quoted from a classroom edition are cited under their own author's name.

### Q: How do you format a DOI in an APA journal article entry?

The DOI appears at the very end of the reference entry as a complete hyperlink beginning with https://doi.org/, for example https://doi.org/10.0000/example. APA no longer uses the older "doi:" prefix or the "DOI:" label; the link itself is the format. Do not add a period after the DOI, since the link is the final element and trailing punctuation can break it. If the article has no DOI, end the entry with the page range and add nothing else; APA does not require a URL for a print article or one reached through a library database. The DOI is the same in every citation of that article, so copy it carefully once.

### Q: When do you use "et al." in an APA in-text citation?

Use "et al." in every in-text citation of a work with three or more authors, from the first citation onward. The form is the first author's surname followed by a comma and "et al.": (Critic et al., 2018). Works with one or two authors never use "et al."; two authors are always named and joined by an ampersand in parentheses or the word "and" in narrative citations. The reference list, by contrast, names up to twenty authors in full, so "et al." is a text-only abbreviation that keeps sentences readable while the list stays complete. The period after "al." is required because it is an abbreviation.

### Q: How do you cite a quotation of forty or more words in APA?

Quotations of forty or more words are set as block quotations: indented half an inch from the left margin, without quotation marks, with the parenthetical citation after the final punctuation. A forty-word Gatsby passage is roughly four to five lines of the novel, so blocks arise when quoting a full descriptive paragraph at length. Count the quoted words when unsure rather than guessing. The citation inside the block's closing parenthetical follows the normal rules: (Fitzgerald, 1925/2004, p. 93). Do not use the block format for short quotations to avoid integrating them into your syntax; the block is for length.

### Q: How do you format the hanging indent in an APA reference list?

The hanging indent puts the first line of each reference entry flush with the left margin and indents every subsequent line half an inch. Apply it through the word processor's paragraph settings, not with tabs or spaces, so the formatting survives reflowing. Every entry in the list gets the same indent, the list is double-spaced throughout, and there are no extra blank lines between entries. The indent is the visual signal that separates one entry from the next in an unnumbered list. A list with inconsistent indentation reads as unfinished, so apply the setting to the whole list in one pass.

### Q: How do you cite an edited book as a whole in APA?

An edited book cited as a whole leads with the editor in the author position, marked with (Ed.) or (Eds.): Editor, E. F. (Ed.). (2016). *New essays on The great Gatsby*. Cambridge University Press. The in-text citation uses the editor's name: (Editor, 2016). This differs from citing a single chapter, where the chapter author leads and the editor appears later after "In". Cite the whole edited book only when discussing the collection as such, for instance its overall approach; when discussing one essay, cite that chapter under its author's name. The distinction keeps the attribution pointed at whoever wrote the words being used.

### Q: How do you cite a source with no author in APA?

When no author is named, the title moves into the author position. A reference entry for an anonymous article would begin with the article title in sentence case, followed by the year: *Reading Gatsby in the classroom*. (2015). *Teaching Journal*, *12*(2), 30-41. The in-text citation uses a shortened title in quotation marks: ("Reading Gatsby," 2015). Corporate authors, by contrast, are spelled out in full and do not count as "no author"; the APA manual itself is cited under American Psychological Association. True no-author sources are uncommon in Gatsby essays, but the title-first rule keeps the entry sortable and the citation matchable.

### Q: How do you cite a secondary source in APA?

A secondary source arises when you quote a writer whose words you found quoted in another work. APA prefers that you find and cite the original, but when the original is unavailable, cite the secondary source in the reference list and name both in the text: (Fitzgerald, 1925, as cited in Critic, 2018). Only the work you actually read, Critic (2018), appears in the reference list. Use this form sparingly; a reference list built on secondhand quotations reads as thin research. For the novel itself the situation rarely arises, but it is common with criticism quoted inside other criticism, where tracking down the original article is worth the effort.

### Q: How do you order two works by the same author in the reference list?

Multiple works by the same author are ordered chronologically by year, earliest first. When the years match, lowercase letters are appended to the years, assigned by the alphabetical order of the titles: (2018a), (2018b), and the in-text citations carry the same letters. For the novel's entry, the ordering year is the version year in the date parentheses, not the 1925 original date, so the reprint files under its reprint year. A student citing the novel alongside another Fitzgerald work orders them by their version years and letters only the ties. The reference list is a lookup table, and chronological order is its filing logic.

### Q: How do you cite the introduction to a Gatsby edition in APA?

Cite the introduction under the introducer's name, following the book-chapter pattern, when you quote or paraphrase its argument. The entry names the introducer as author, describes the piece, and gives the book's publication details. Merely reading the introduction for background, without citing its claims, requires no entry at all, since the reference list contains works cited rather than works read. A student citing both the novel and its introduction will have two entries, one led by Fitzgerald and one led by the introducer, filing in their separate alphabetical places. The in-text citations keep them distinct without any special marking.

### Q: What is sentence case in APA titles?

Sentence case capitalizes only the first word of a title or subtitle, plus proper nouns and the first word after a colon. In the reference list, book titles, article titles, and chapter titles all use sentence case: *The great Gatsby*, not *The Great Gatsby*. Journal titles are the exception; they use title case and italics. The rule applies to the reference entry only; when the title appears in your essay's prose, normal title capitalization returns. The contrast is deliberate: the list is a retrieval system with its own visual grammar, while the prose follows ordinary capitalization. Check the case as a separate proofreading pass.

### Q: How do you cite a film adaptation of Gatsby in APA?

A film is cited under its director in the author position, with the year of release and a bracketed description of the medium: Director, D. (Director). (2013). *The great Gatsby* [Film]. Warner Bros. The in-text citation is (Director, 2013), with a timestamp replacing the page number for quotations: (Director, 2013, 1:14:00). The novel and the film are different works with different authors and dates, so they never share an entry or a slash date. A comparative essay citing both will have a Fitzgerald entry for the novel and a director entry for the film, and the prose must keep clear which work each claim concerns.

### Q: How do you cite a work with a corporate author in APA?

A corporate author is spelled out in full in the reference entry: American Psychological Association. (2019). *Publication manual of the American Psychological Association* (7th ed.). American Psychological Association. The in-text citation uses the full name on first use: (American Psychological Association, 2019). If the name is long and you will cite it repeatedly, introduce an abbreviation in the first citation, (American Psychological Association [APA], 2019), and use (APA, 2019) thereafter. The reference entry always spells the name out; the abbreviation lives in the text only. When author and publisher are the same body, list the name twice rather than shortening it.

### Q: What belongs in the reference list in APA?

The reference list contains every work cited in the essay and no work that is not cited. It is not a bibliography of background reading; a source you read but never cited stays out. Each entry must match at least one in-text citation, and each in-text citation must match an entry, which is the key-and-lock contract the final proofreading pass verifies. The list is alphabetical by surname, double-spaced, with hanging indents, under the centered bold heading References. Personal communications, such as an email from a teacher, are cited in the text only and never appear in the list, since readers cannot retrieve them.

### Q: How do you cite a translated edition of Gatsby in APA?

A translated edition credits the translator in parentheses after the title and keeps the original-work note at the end. The entry names Fitzgerald as author, gives the translation's publication year in the date position, adds the translator credit, and closes with (Original work published 1925). The in-text citation carries the slash date in the usual way. The translator is credited because the translation's wording is the translator's work, while the story remains Fitzgerald's. An English-language essay rarely needs this form, but the pattern completes the set: everyone who made the version is credited in the entry, and the work's original date is always noted.
