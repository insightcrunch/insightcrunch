## The Statues of Gold: Ten Lines That Settle and Unsettle the Ending

The golden statues of Romeo and Juliet exist for ten lines and are never built. In the Folger text of the final scene, Capulet offers his hand and calls it his daughter's jointure (5.3.296 to 298), Montague answers by promising to raise Juliet's statue in pure gold (5.3.299 to 303), and Capulet matches the promise for Romeo in a single balancing line (5.3.304 to 305). Nothing else in the play has generated a larger interpretive quarrel per line. One camp reads the vows as the feud's true end, grief converted into honour at ruinous cost. The other camp reads them as the feud's last transaction, two rivals outbidding each other in the only currency either house has ever trusted.

![The golden statues vowed over the lovers in the Capulet tomb - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-92.webp)

The verdict defended here holds both camps to the words on the page, and the words refuse to separate. The reconciliation is real: a hand is offered and taken, a legal demand is renounced, and the feud's two heads call each other brother over the bodies that ended their quarrel. The medium, though, is ironic through and through, because the peace is negotiated in comparatives of price, more and as rich and at such rate, so that the fathers stop fighting in the feud's language rather than in any new one. The statues are reconciliation, then, but reconciliation spoken by men who have learned the cost of their enmity without learning another tongue in which to pay respects. Gold is at once the proof of sincerity and the evidence of limit, and the play makes the metal carry both charges in the same ten lines.

That doubleness is a deliberate construction. Shakespeare inherited a reconciled ending from his sources and chose to add the statues to it, and a dramatist does not add a priced monument to an inherited peace by accident. The addition has to be read as commentary on the peace it ornaments. What follows weighs the exchange word by word: the order of the speeches, the hands, the legal term buried in Capulet's offer, the grammar of outbidding inside Montague's vow, the arithmetic of solid gold, the metal's earlier career in the play, the lovers' rival economics of bounty, the critics who have fought over the gesture, and the plain fact that the statues are promised in a churchyard and never seen again. The question is not whether the fathers mean what they say. The question is what their meaning is made of.

## The Exchange Itself: A Hand, a Settlement, and a Bid

The closing exchange is short enough to be quoted nearly whole, and its brevity is part of its meaning. The Prince has finished his audit of the catastrophe, heaven's scourge laid on the houses' hate, the bereaved instructed to look at what their enmity has cost. Into that judicial quiet Capulet steps first. The order matters, because the house that pressed the fatal marriage, set the feast, fixed the wedding day, and threatened a daughter with the street makes the first move toward peace. Capulet does not open with a statue. He opens with a hand and a kinship term: "O brother Montague, give me thy hand" (5.3.296). Brother is the first word of the peace, and it is spoken by the man whose household had spent the play treating the other name as contamination.

### Who offers first at the tomb, and what exactly does he offer?

Capulet offers first: a hand, with a legal renunciation. In the Folger text he calls the handshake his daughter's jointure and adds that no more can he demand (5.3.296 to 298), which makes the gesture a settlement rather than an embrace: the marriage payment a father might claim is cancelled, the hand tendered as the whole of it.

The hand deserves a pause. Hands have done the play's intimate and violent work alike: the lovers' first meeting was a dialogue of palms, pilgrims and holy palmers, and the feud's work was done by hands on hilts in the first scene and the third. A handshake between the fathers is therefore not a neutral stage picture. It returns the hand from weapon and from sacrament to contract, two principals sealing terms. Yet Capulet's own gloss pulls the gesture out of sentiment and into law before Montague can answer. A jointure, as the editors gloss the term, is the marriage settlement, the property provision settled on a bride, bound up with the dowry economy in which Juliet had been an asset under negotiation since Paris first asked for her in the opening act. Capulet takes the instrument that priced his daughter in life and declares it discharged. The hand is the jointure; the claim is closed; the account, on his side, is settled at the cost of everything the account contained.

Read generously, the renunciation is enormous. "For no more can I demand" is the speech of a creditor tearing up a bond, and creditors in this play have not torn up bonds: Capulet had demanded obedience, the Prince had demanded order, Paris had demanded a wedding date. Read coldly, the renunciation never leaves the ledger. Even surrendering the claim, Capulet thinks in claims. The sequence in [the final reconciliation of the golden statues](/2023/12/15/final-reconciliation-golden-statues/) repays attention precisely here, because the scene's own bookkeeping is divided against itself from its first speech: the peace begins as a feeling and is instantly translated into a financial instrument, and the translation is performed by the feeling's own speaker.

Montague's reply completes the pattern and complicates it. He does not simply accept. "But I can give thee more" (5.3.299) is a sentence built on a comparative, and what follows the comparative is the statue: "For I will raise her statue in pure gold, / That whiles Verona by that name is known, / There shall no figure at such rate be set / As that of true and faithful Juliet" (5.3.300 to 303). Every element of the vow points two ways. The recipient of the honour is the other house's child, which is generosity of a high order: Montague spends his gold on the Capulet daughter, not on his own son. The measure of the honour is a superlative of price, which is the feud's native arithmetic wearing mourning dress. The permanence claimed for the statue is tied to the city's fame, whiles Verona by that name is known, which lifts the gift out of private grief and into public competition for memory. And the epithet, true and faithful Juliet, awards the girl in death the exact virtues her father had punished in her life, since her truth and faith ran to Romeo and against the marriage arranged for her.

### Does Capulet ever actually use the word statue for Romeo?

No. Montague names a statue outright for Juliet, in pure gold, with its superlative attached. Capulet's matching line promises only that Romeo's figure shall lie as rich by his lady's side and calls both children poor sacrifices of the enmity (5.3.304 to 305), so Romeo's monument exists by implication and equivalence, never by the noun itself.

That asymmetry is the exchange in miniature. Montague specifies; Capulet equates. The second father's line is moving, and its most quoted phrase, poor sacrifices of our enmity, is the scene's one unambiguous act of self-accusation, the fathers naming their children as offerings consumed by a hatred the fathers owned. But the line's first half is a matching bid, as rich, and a bid answered is a contest acknowledged. Capulet does not say that Romeo shall have a statue of gold; the syntax lets the gold flow across from Montague's sentence, the way value flows across a market when one price sets another. The play leaves Romeo's statue grammatically dependent on Juliet's, a figure priced by association, lying by his lady. Even in the vows' fine print, one child is minted and the other is rated against her.

## The Grammar of Outbidding: More, Rate, and As Rich

Strip the exchange to its comparative skeleton and a strange fact stands out: nearly every content word that carries value in the ten lines is a word of measurement. Capulet's jointure is a sum by another name. Montague's more is an open comparative with no ceiling stated. His rate is the language of appraisal, the word a valuer uses for what a thing fetches or is held at. His no figure at such rate be set is a superlative framed as a market position, the finest object in Verona at the highest valuation in Verona. Capulet's as rich is an equation, value matched to value. The fathers do not say that the children were beloved, or that the feud was madness, or that the houses will live differently. They say what the dead are worth, in ascending and then in matching terms, and the worth is denominated in the metal the play has spent five acts teaching its audience to distrust.

The competitive reading draws its strongest evidence from this grammar, and the evidence is genuinely strong. Competition had been the feud's whole mode. The opening scene's servants quarrel over precedence of insult; the houses measure themselves against each other in retainers, in feasts, in the Prince's court, in the marriage market where Paris's worth is recited like a valuation. A peace conducted in comparatives keeps the measuring habit alive at the very moment of renunciation. Montague's but I can give thee more is, in form, the sentence of a man raising a bid, and it follows a renounced demand the way a higher offer follows a withdrawn one at any bargaining table. The superlative clinches the resemblance: no figure at such rate be set installs Juliet's statue at the top of a league table of monuments, a ranking in which other figures, including any Capulet might raise, are by definition placed lower. Grief that must win is grief still playing the feud's game with the weapons laid aside.

The reconciliation reading answers, with equal textual warrant, that the grammar is the only grammar these men possess. Verona's great houses are merchant-princely households; honour in their world has always been denominated, displayed, and rated, from the gold clasps of a marriage book to the forty ducats that buy poison in Mantua. A vow not expressed in cost would be, for Montague, a vow not expressed at all, wind rather than covenant. The superlative can be read as the idiom of total commitment rather than of rivalry: to say that no figure shall be set at such rate is to promise that the expenditure will be unexampled, that nothing in the city will be allowed to rival the honour done to the child his house destroyed. On this reading the comparatives measure not against Capulet but against Verona itself, against every monument the city holds, and the contest is between memory and oblivion rather than between two old men.

The adjudication turns on a distinction the text itself supplies. A sincerity of feeling and a competitiveness of form can coexist in one utterance, and the lines insist on being read at both levels because Shakespeare built the comparatives into a scene that did not require them. Nothing in the inherited story obliged Montague to outbid, or Capulet to match. A dramatist seeking an unclouded reconciliation had plain words available: forgive, embrace, bury, mourn. The words chosen are the words of the counting house raised to ceremonial pitch. The honest conclusion is that the vows are bids and bonds at once, competitive in their syntax and reconciling in their effect, and that the play wants the audience to hear the competition inside the reconciliation rather than to vote it away. The feud ends. The feud's accent does not.

## Pricing the Gold: What a Statue in Pure Gold Would Actually Cost

Montague's vow sounds like hyperbole until it is priced, and priced it becomes something stranger: a promise no fortune in Verona could keep at the letter. A human figure cast solid, at life size, occupies roughly the volume of a human body, some sixty to seventy litres of metal. Gold is among the densest substances handled in the period, near nineteen and a third kilograms to the litre, so a solid statue of Juliet would run well past a thousand kilograms of metal, toward thirteen hundred. At the mint values of Shakespeare's England, where a gold sovereign of twenty shillings carried about half an ounce troy of high-fine gold, thirteen hundred kilograms prices out in the hundreds of thousands of pounds. The greatest noble incomes of the age ran to a few thousand pounds a year. The vow, taken at the letter, promises several lifetimes of a great house's entire revenue, sunk into a single figure in a churchyard city.

Nobody in the theatre, and nobody in Verona, expects the assay to be run. Period practice points the other way: the gilded monument, the effigy of wood or bronze or stone washed with gold leaf, splendour achieved by surface rather than by mass. Church tombs and civic images gleamed without consuming bullion by the tonne, and a statue described as golden in ordinary speech was understood to shine like gold, not to be weighed as gold. Montague's phrase, though, refuses the ordinary understanding. He says pure gold, and pure is an assayer's word before it is a poet's: it speaks of fineness, of metal unalloyed and unmixed, the standard against which other gold is tested. The father reaches past the vocabulary of gilding to the vocabulary of the mint, and the reach is the meaning. He is not describing a manufacturing process. He is stating a valuation in the most absolute terms his world affords, denominating his remorse at the highest fineness on the scale.

### What does the arithmetic do to the vow: expose it, or crown it?

The arithmetic exposes the vow as unkeepable in the letter while crowning it as a measure of intent. A solid figure would cost hundreds of thousands of pounds at mint values, beyond any Veronese fortune, so the promise operates as a superlative of feeling rather than a contract with a founder, a price named because it cannot be paid.

The unkeepability cuts both ways, which is why the priced reading and the ironic reading keep trading the same evidence. For the ironist, a promise that cannot be performed is a hollow promise, and its hollowness is the point: the fathers purchase absolution with a cheque drawn on infinity, impressive in the speaking and safe from the audit, since no unveiling is ever staged and no founder is ever named. For the reconciler, the excess is the sincerity, because grief that calculates within its means is grief still keeping accounts, whereas grief that promises beyond its means has at least broken the habit of measure in the act of using measure's language. Between these two, the text plants one quiet fact: the play ends before any statue exists. The vow's value is never tested by performance, and the audience is left holding an instrument whose worth depends entirely on whether a promise in gold, from these men, at this tomb, is a bond or a flourish. The Prince, notably, does not stay to underwrite it. He moves the company toward talk.

The word pure carries a second transfer worth weighing. Purity in the play has belonged to the lovers: to Juliet's faith, to the shrine imagery of the first meeting, to a love described in the language of devotion rather than of price. Montague transfers the assayer's term to the metal and then attaches the moral terms to the girl, true and faithful Juliet, as if the statue's fineness and the child's fidelity were one property stated twice. The compliment is real, and so is the displacement it performs. In life, Juliet's fidelity was the offence: faithful to Romeo meant false to her father's arrangements, and Capulet had raged at the disobedience in terms of property and baggage. In death, the same fidelity is struck in gold and set up as the city's treasure. The fathers can honour the virtue once it has been cast into an object they commission, own, and display. A living fidelity answered to itself; a golden one answers to them.

## Gold Before the Tomb: The Metal's Earlier Career in the Play

No audience reaches the statues innocently, because the play has spent five acts building gold's reputation, and the reputation is bad. The metal enters early and under suspicion. Rosaline, in Romeo's account of his first, unrequited fixation, is proof against seduction by wealth: she will not ope her lap to saint-seducing gold (1.1.212 in the Folger numbering), a phrase that makes gold a pander, an agent of purchased desire, and sets the girl who refuses it above the economy it serves. The lovers' true meeting is then staged in deliberate contrast as an exchange that costs nothing and prices nothing: palms touching, a sonnet shared between two voices, a kiss given and returned in kind. Against that weightless economy the play sets the marriage market of the Capulet household, where gold does the talking. Lady Capulet sells Paris to her daughter as a precious book whose glory is that gold clasps lock in the golden story (1.3.92), a sales pitch in which the husband is a binding, the wife the pages, and the whole union an object whose worth is its cover. The girl being priced is thirteen, and the pricing is done by her mother in her father's house, in the idiom of a luxury trade.

The pattern darkens as the play proceeds. Banishment, the sentence that falls on Romeo after the killings, draws from him the cry that the Friar has cut his head off with a golden axe (3.3.22), gold as the gilding on an instrument of execution, splendour and severance in one image. The axe is golden because the blow is dressed as mercy; the metal there is the shine on a lethal thing, beauty conscripted to make a wound presentable. By the final act the critique becomes explicit doctrine in the mouth of a buyer at Mantua. Romeo, pressing forty ducats on the starved apothecary, pronounces the play's most quoted sentence on the subject: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1.80 to 83). The scene is arranged so that the doctrine is enacted as it is spoken. The apothecary's will refuses and his poverty consents; gold overrides conscience in a starving man within a dozen lines, and the poison changes hands. The whole argument of [the case that the tragedy is not really about love](/2022/02/11/why-romeo-and-juliet-is-not-about-love/) finds some of its firmest ground in this scene, because the play's own spokesman for love identifies gold, not fate, as the world's working murderer.

A dramatist who has built that dossier does not reach for gold at the close by reflex. The statues arrive trailing every prior association: seduction refused, a bride priced as a gilded book, an axe made splendid, a poison outsold by the coin that buys it. Set against the dossier, the vows cannot be heard as innocent, and the innocent reading has to explain away not a line but a motif engineered across five acts. Yet the dossier, examined fairly, has a complication of its own. In every earlier instance, gold is what the living use on the living: to seduce, to package, to gild a sentence, to corrupt a starving man's refusal. At the tomb, gold is proposed for the dead, who cannot be seduced, packaged, sentenced, or corrupted. The metal's power over its object is gone; what remains is its power over the givers and the onlookers. The statues are the first gold in the play that buys nothing from their subject, and that change of direction is the strongest card the reconciliation reading holds. The fathers' gold cannot purchase Juliet. It can only publish her.

Whether publication is a noble use or a final appropriation is the question the motif leaves open, and the play has prepared both answers. Juliet in life resisted publication with all her art: a love confessed to the night air, a marriage in a cell, a name she offers to shed, a face she begs the night to hide. The statue is publication perfected and perpetual, her image set at the city's highest rate for as long as the city keeps its name. The girl who asked Romeo to invent no oath and swear by nothing inconstant is to be commemorated by the most constant substance in the fathers' world, fixed, rated, and displayed by the two men whose arrangements she died escaping. A reader does not need to call the vow hollow to feel the draught in that. It is enough to notice that the honour is done in the fathers' element, on the fathers' terms, in a place of the fathers' choosing, for an audience of the fathers' peers, and that the honoured girl's own element, speech exchanged in darkness between equals, is the one substance of which the monument contains nothing at all.

One more exhibit belongs in the metal's dossier, because it shows the pricing habit operating at the level of courtship itself, where the statues' defenders might least expect to find it. Paris's suit for Juliet is conducted from its first mention as an appraisal. Capulet, listing his daughter's position in the opening act, speaks of her as a stranger in the world who has not seen the change of fourteen years, and counsels the suitor to let two more summers wither in their pride before she is thought ripe to be a bride, the language of a crop assessed for market. The Prince's kinsman is himself introduced by his credentials, a man of wax, a properer man of the world's whole volume, and the courtship that follows is a negotiation between houses in which the bride's consent is scheduled last and consulted least. When the match collapses into the forced wedding of the fourth act, the pricing turns naked: a Thursday fixed, a bride threatened with disowning if the goods refuse delivery, a father calling his daughter a baggage for declining the valuation the house has set on her. The girl the fathers will rate above every figure in Verona was rated by her own father, within the week, as merchandise that had failed to move. The statues do not introduce the habit of valuing Juliet. They inherit it, raise it to its highest denomination, and aim it, for the first time, at honour instead of at sale. Whether the raising redeems the habit or merely crowns it is the question the whole debate exists to ask, and the dossier's completeness, seduction, binding, axe, poison, and bride price alike, is what prevents any honest reader from answering too quickly in the habit's favour.

## The Lovers' Counter-Economy: Bounty That Grows by Being Given

The statues debate sharpens further against the play's one fully stated alternative theory of value, which Juliet articulates on the balcony before any gold has been promised to anyone. Her account of love's wealth runs in exact opposition to the mint: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2.133 to 135). In the fathers' economy, value is finite, ranked, and depleted by display: a statue at the highest rate leaves less for every other figure, and a jointure paid is a claim extinguished. In Juliet's economy, giving increases stock, the ledger balances by overflowing, and no rate can be set on a thing whose supply grows with expenditure. The balcony speech is not a metaphor the play forgets. It is the tragedy's economics lecture, delivered by its youngest speaker, and the tomb scene is unintelligible as a verdict on value without it.

Measure the vows against the lecture and the irony locates itself with precision. The fathers commemorate a love whose discoverer announced that it could not be measured, and they commemorate it by measuring, at the highest rate, in the hardest currency. The statues translate the infinite into the finite and call the translation honour. Something is necessarily lost in the conversion, and the play marks the loss by keeping the lovers silent: the two people who could price love correctly are the two people in the tomb, and their silence is filled by an auction of epithets conducted over their bodies. Even the compliments arrive as valuations. True and faithful are moral terms, but in Montague's sentence they function as hallmarks, the stamps that certify the metal's grade, and a hallmark is a thing applied by an authority to an object that cannot stamp itself.

The reconciliation reading has its counter at exactly this point, and it deserves its full force. The lovers' economy, boundless and self-renewing, is an economy of two, and it dies with the two. A city cannot run on bounty; Verona must settle its feud in the currencies Verona holds, law and money and monument, and a peace that waited for the fathers to speak Juliet's language would wait forever. The statues, on this view, are translation with the losses honestly borne: the fathers render into their own tongue a value they finally perceive, and the rendering's clumsiness is the tragedy's measure of how far the generations stood apart, not evidence that the perception is false. A man who can only say value in gold, saying it of his enemy's daughter at the highest rate he knows, may be saying the largest true thing of which he is capable. The criticism that demands he say it in balcony verse demands that he be somebody else, and the play, which killed his son for being somebody else in secret, is in no position to be surprised that he is not.

The verdict between these two is not a coin toss, because the play supplies a tiebreaker in its own final disposition of substances. The last word of the tragedy is not gold and not statues. It is story: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.316 to 317). Narrative, the one medium in which the lovers' own speeches survive entire, is given the closing couplet, the rhyme that seals the play, while the statues are left in the future tense of a promise made eleven lines earlier. Shakespeare assigns the lovers' permanence twice over, once to the fathers' metal and once to the telling, and then ends the play inside the telling. The audience walks out carrying the story, not the statues, and every reader since has done the same. Whatever the statues weigh, the scales of the play itself come down on the side of speech.

## The Statues in the Sources: An Ending the Poem Never Built

One way to isolate Shakespeare's intention is to subtract everything he inherited and look at what remains. The reconciliation is inherited. In Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562, the tale's chief English source, the deaths reconcile the families, the lovers are laid in a single tomb, and the machinery of judgment settles accounts around them: the Nurse banished, the apothecary hanged, the Friar withdrawn to a hermitage, the fathers at peace over the grave. Painter's prose version and the Italian tellings behind it close on the same reconciling chord, the enmity buried with the children, the shared tomb doing the commemorative work. Across the whole chain of sources, the monument the lovers receive is the tomb they share. No teller before Shakespeare gilds it.

The statues are therefore an addition, and additions at the end of a tragedy are arguments. Shakespeare took a closing peace that his sources rendered in the plain materials of burial and repentance and inserted into it a priced, competitive, unbuilt monument in the most morally compromised metal in his play. The insertion can be read as enrichment, the dramatist giving the reconciliation the splendid public form his stage afforded, or as exposure, the dramatist letting the fathers convict themselves out of their own mouths at the moment of their redemption. What the source comparison rules out is accident. A borrowed ending would have closed the play a dozen lines earlier, at the handshake, with the Prince's sentence and the shared grave. The statues exist because Shakespeare wanted the audience to spend its last minutes of judgment not on the peace but on the price tag the peace arrived wearing.

The addition also redistributes the ending's honours in a way the sources do not. A shared tomb honours the lovers jointly and privately, stone over two bodies in one vault. Twin statues at rival rates honour them separately and publicly, each child reclaimed as a single figure, each figure commissioned by the other house yet measured against the city's whole stock of monuments. The pairing that the play spent five acts joining is, in the fathers' monument programme, politely taken apart again and set up as two exhibits. Romeo's figure lies by his lady's, in Capulet's dependent clause, but they stand as two statues, two commissions, two expenditures, in a way the tomb never made them two of anything. Even the reconciliation's sculpture garden keeps the houses' bookkeeping habit: one entry per child, cross-charged.

## The Critics Weighed: Goddard's Scorn, Snyder's Comic Peace, Kahn's Settlement

The quarrel among readers is old, named, and genuinely undecided in print, which makes it a fit subject for adjudication rather than summary. The sentimental reading, the statues as the feud's beautiful full stop, held the stage and the schoolroom for generations, and its most famous prosecutor remains Harold C. Goddard, whose posthumous The Meaning of Shakespeare (1951) refuses the ending's easy tears in terms that still sting. Goddard observes that much superfluous sentiment has been spent on this ending and asks whether it is not folly to suppose that Capulet or Lady Capulet was spiritually transformed by a daughter's death. Of the statue itself his sentence is the irony case in one breath: the statue of Juliet in pure gold that Montague promised to erect is proof in itself, Goddard writes, of how incapable Montague was of understanding her spirit, and of the truth that her spirit alone, and not monuments or gold, can end feuds. On Goddard's reading the vow is a confession disguised as a tribute, the father's incomprehension cast in the very metal that proves it.

### Which critic first refuses the sentimental reading of the statues?

Harold C. Goddard, in The Meaning of Shakespeare, published in 1951 after his death. Goddard calls the sentiment spent on the ending superfluous, denies that either Capulet was spiritually transformed by the catastrophe, and treats Montague's promised statue in pure gold as proof of the father's incapacity to understand his dead daughter in law's spirit at all.

Against Goddard stands the strongest structural defence the reconciliation has, Susan Snyder's account in The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies (1979). Snyder reads the play as a comic pattern intercepted by death: the young lovers, the blocking fathers, the go-betweens and the near escapes belong to comedy's machinery, and comedy's machinery exists to end in social reintegration, the community reknit around the young couple. On Snyder's showing, the closing reconciliation is comedy's ending arriving in the only form the tragedy leaves available, the community reknit around the couple's graves instead of their marriage bed. The statues, within such a reading, are the reintegration made visible and durable, the city's formal admission of the pair it expelled, and their splendour measures the size of the admission rather than the smallness of the fathers. Snyder's framework explains something Goddard's scorn cannot: why the ending, however priced, has satisfied audiences for centuries as an ending, why the handshake lands. A form fulfilled is a form fulfilled, even in mourning dress.

The scholar who reframes both positions is Coppélia Kahn, whose essay "Coming of Age in Verona" (1978, collected in Man's Estate, 1981) reads the feud itself as an extreme and finally self destructive expression of patriarchal society, a system in which fathers dispose of children as the instruments of honour and alliance. Within Kahn's analysis the closing settlement cannot be innocent, because the system that killed the lovers is the system conducting the settlement. The children escaped the fathers' disposal exactly once, in a secret marriage contracted without consent, and the statues are the means by which the escape is retrospectively cancelled: disposed of again at last, assigned their places, their values, and their meanings by the two men whose authority the marriage defied. Kahn does not need to call the fathers hypocrites. Her point is colder. Sincere grief, expressed through the only institution the fathers command, still performs the institution's work, and the institution's work was always the conversion of children into assets. The handshake is a contract; the jointure is a settlement; the statues are the estate, settled.

Adjudicated, the three positions sort themselves by the level at which each is aimed. Goddard is right about the metal and wrong, or at least unanswerable, about the men: a statue in pure gold is a failure of understanding, but failure of understanding is compatible with genuine transformation of conduct, and conduct is what a feud runs on. Snyder is right about the form: the reconciliation completes the play's comic design and must be felt as completion, not as a trick. Kahn is right about the frame: the completion is transacted inside the patriarchal economy that caused the catastrophe, so the completion and the critique arrive in the same gesture. The reading that survives all three is the verdict this article has defended from its opening: the peace is real, its medium is the indictment, and Shakespeare arranged for the audience to receive both in a single ten line dose so that neither could be used to anaesthetise the other.

## The Prince's Frame: Glooming Peace and the Audit That Outlasts the Gold

The fathers do not get the scene's last word, and the distribution of last words is the play's final piece of stage management in the debate. The Prince opens the closing movement with judgment, heaven's scourge and the bereaved instructed to see what hate has made, and he closes it with a sentence that pointedly declines to ratify the statues' splendour. "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" (5.3.306) is the official description of the reconciliation, and glooming does heavy work in it: the peace is real, mornings do follow, and the light is the light of a sun that for sorrow will not show its head. A state that wanted to celebrate the golden settlement had brighter words in stock. The Prince chooses a word from the weather of mourning, and then he does something more decisive than choosing words. He dissolves the ceremony into conversation: go hence, to have more talk of these sad things, some pardoned and some punished (5.3.307 to 308). The monument programme is left behind in the churchyard, uncommissioned, while the living are sent off to the unglamorous work of testimony, judgment, and account.

That redirection is the play's own adjudication of its statues, delivered in action rather than in commentary. Verona's future, in the Prince's disposition, depends on talk: on the Friar's narration examined, on Romeo's letter read, on pardon and punishment sorted name by name. It does not depend on gold. The fathers' medium is superseded within a dozen lines of its proposal, by the magistrate's medium of record and speech, and the supersession is gentle enough to miss at speed. Nobody rebukes Montague. Nobody cancels the vow. The play simply walks away from it, toward the work that might actually prevent a second tomb, and the walking away is the verdict for any reader who trusts what a dramatist does over what his characters say. The whole history of [why the Capulets and Montagues hate each other](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) is a history of a quarrel nobody in the play can explain, an ancient grudge with no stated origin, and a grudge without an origin cannot be answered by a monument. It can only be answered by an account, which is what the Prince orders and the statues are not.

### Does any character see a finished statue before the play ends?

No character sees one, because no statue is begun within the action. The vows are spoken in the future tense over the bodies, the Prince redirects the company to talk and judgment, and the play closes on his couplet about the story, so the monuments belong entirely to a Verona the audience never enters.

The statues' unbuilt state is sometimes treated as a trivial consequence of the curtain falling, and it is worth resisting that shrug. Shakespeare ends plays with futures made visible when he wants them: thrones filled, marriages danced, fleets arrived. Here the visible future is a court of inquiry dispersing into morning light, and the invisible future is a pair of golden figures whose raising is entrusted to the two men least qualified, by the play's own showing, to understand what the figures mean. Entrusting the lovers' memory to the fathers is either the tragedy's mercy, the worst students given the keeping of the lesson, or its final irony, the lesson's meaning placed in the custody of those who failed it while alive to learn it cheaply. The text's glooming weather and its turn to talk suggest the dramatist knew the custody was unsafe. A story can be retold correctly by anyone who has heard it. A statue says only what its commissioners paid for it to say.

## The Weighing Completed: How the Evidence Sorts Before the Verdict

Before the verdict is pronounced, the evidence deserves one plain sorting, because the debate's long life owes less to disagreement about facts than to disagreement about which facts are allowed to govern. On the facts of the text there is no serious dispute. Capulet speaks first and offers a hand named as a jointure with a demand renounced. Montague answers with a comparative and a statue in pure gold rated above every figure in Verona and inscribed to Juliet's truth and faith. Capulet matches with an equation and a confession that names the children sacrifices of the enmity. The Prince frames the exchange with a scourge behind and a glooming peace ahead, and dissolves the ceremony into talk, pardon, and punishment. No statue is built, placed, sited, or seen. Every camp reads the same ten lines; the camps divide over the weight each fact is permitted to bear.

On the facts of the play, the division is equally instructive. The reconciliation camp governs by the plot's largest fact, the feud's end, and by the Prologue's contract, which promised exactly this burial of strife and is honoured to the letter. The irony camp governs by the motif's largest fact, gold's record, and by the sources' silence, which shows the priced monument to be an addition rather than an inheritance. The formalist governs by the pattern, the feminist by the system, the sentimentalist by the handshake, the prosecutor by the superlative. Each government is legitimate, and each becomes false only when it annexes the whole scene and evicts the rival facts. The scene was built to resist annexation. Its ten lines distribute their evidence with an even hand: a sincere act in a compromised medium, a compromised medium performing a sincere act, and a magistrate standing over both with a word, glooming, that refuses to certify either the warmth or the chill as the morning's whole weather. A verdict that honours the construction will therefore be a verdict about proportion, assigning the peace to the event and the irony to the medium and declining to let either assignment swallow the other. That is the verdict the next section states, and it is less a compromise between the camps than a description of the machine Shakespeare actually built, in which reconciliation and irony are not rival readings of the statues but the statues' two working parts, as inseparable as the hand and the gold in the single gesture that offers both.

## The Verdict: A Real Peace Spoken in the Feud's Own Language

The evidence supports neither camp in its pure form, and the impurity is the discovery. Against the pure irony reading: the feud does end. The Prologue promised that the lovers' death would bury their parents' strife, and the closing scene performs the burial with the proper instruments, a hand given and taken, a brotherhood named between enemies, a financial claim formally renounced, a self accusation spoken aloud by the man who had most to accuse himself of. Nothing in the text marks the fathers as lying, and a dramatist who wanted the peace exposed as fraud had obvious devices available, an aside, a servant's comment, a Prince who doubts. The Prince instead accepts the peace and prices it himself, at the cost of his own kin: all are punished, himself among the losers of a brace of kinsmen. The wider question of [whether the ending is hopeful or hopeless](/2031/03/28/is-the-ending-hopeful-or-hopeless/) is settled, so far as the statues can settle it, on the hopeful side of the ledger of fact: Verona's war is over, and it is over because two old men at a tomb finally did the thing their city had needed since the first scene's brawl.

Against the pure reconciliation reading: everything the fathers do, they do in the feud's dialect. The peace is a settlement, the love is a rate, the honour is a superlative, the matching gift is an equation, and the children are converted at the last into the one form of property the play has taught its audience to suspect, precious objects whose value is set by the men who own them. Gold, the metal of the seduction refused, the bride packaged, the axe gilded, and the poison purchased, is asked to carry the tragedy's tenderest meaning, and it cannot shed its record on request. Shakespeare, who added the statues to an ending that lacked them, who wrote the comparatives into speeches that needed none, who placed the vows between a judicial audit and a magistrate's glooming refusal of brightness, built the contamination in. The irony is not an accident of later cynicism. It is part of the design, load bearing and deliberate.

So the statues are both, in a fixed proportion the text controls. The reconciliation is the event; the irony is the medium; and the play's judgment falls on the medium precisely because the event is genuine. A fake peace in golden language would be mere satire, and a true peace in a cleansed language would be mere consolation. The tragedy Shakespeare wrote is harder than either: a true peace that can only be spoken in the language that made the war, by men whose conversion is real and whose vocabulary is unredeemed. The statues will stand in Verona, if they are ever raised, as the feud's last and best work, honouring the lovers in the idiom that killed them. That is why the gesture has outlasted four centuries of readers who cannot agree about it. It is the ending's perfect emblem because it is the ending's perfect problem, a reconciliation the audience must believe and a bill the audience must read, delivered in the same ten lines, in the same breath, in pure gold.

## The Hands of the Scene: From Palms of Pilgrims to a Clasp of Contract

Before the gold is mentioned, the peace is made by a hand, and the play has been preparing that hand since the lovers first met. The shared sonnet at the feast is a dialogue conducted through palms: Romeo casts his hand as unworthy and Juliet's as a shrine, and the wit of the exchange turns on whether palmer and pilgrim may lawfully touch. Touch, there, is theology played as courtship, and the hand is the instrument of a consent negotiated line by line between equals. Nothing is priced. The hand gives itself, and the giving is ratified by the other hand's answering pressure inside the form of the sonnet. Set that hand against the hand of the tomb scene and the play's whole arc of touch stands revealed. Capulet's "give me thy hand" (5.3.296) asks for the same member to do opposite work: not consent exchanged between lovers but contract executed between principals, the palm no longer a shrine but a seal.

The feud's hands had their own curriculum between those two clasps. Hands open the play in threat, servants biting thumbs and drawing steel; hands do the killing in the third act, Mercutio and Tybalt falling to hand held weapons in a quarrel of precedence; hands arrange the marriage in secret, the Friar joining the lovers' hands in his cell in the one lawful clasp the feud never sees; hands prepare the potion, carry the letter that miscarries, raise the torch at the tomb, and close around dagger and cup in the vault. By the final scene the audience has watched hands perform every function the play contains, sacred, violent, bureaucratic, and suicidal. The fathers' handshake gathers that whole history into one gesture and tries to retire it. A hand that has held a sword, a contract, and a corpse is offered open and empty to the enemy, and the openness is the point: nothing in it, nothing hidden, nothing owed beyond the clasp itself.

Against that reading, the gold arrives as a kind of relapse of the hand. A statue is also made by hands, founders' and sculptors' hands, but it is commissioned by money rather than offered in person, and it stands with its hands fixed forever in whatever attitude the commissioners chose. The lovers' hands, which in life reached, clasped, wrote, and finally struck, are to be reproduced in a metal that holds one pose eternally and reaches for nothing. The contrast is not an argument against the fathers' sincerity; it is a measure of what sincerity can and cannot transmit. A handshake happens between the living, in time, and can be renewed or broken by the hands that made it. A statue removes the gesture from time and from risk, and in removing the risk it removes the very quality that made the handshake a peace rather than a picture of one. The peace that needs no renewal is the peace that can no longer be chosen, and the play, which ends with the living sent off to talk, quietly assigns the future to the renewable instrument and the past to the fixed one.

There is a further precision in the hand's economics that the jointure line brings into focus. A hand given in marriage arrangements was a legal unit long before it was a romantic one: to ask for a daughter's hand was to open the negotiation of settlements, portions, and jointures that the Capulet household had been conducting with Paris since the first act. Capulet's offer reverses the customary direction of the phrase. He asks for Montague's hand, man to man, and pays for the taking of it with his own daughter's jointure, the settlement that would ordinarily have flowed with Juliet into a marriage. The marriage that should have carried the jointure happened in secret and ended in the tomb, so the settlement is discharged to the bridegroom's father as damages, the marriage portion converted into a peace indemnity. Every term in the sentence is doing double duty, kinship and accountancy at once, and the doubleness is the fathers' tragedy in grammar: even their most generous gesture arrives dressed as a transaction, because a transaction is the only dressed form their world provides for a promise between houses.

## Statue or Figure: The Words Chosen for the Monument

The exchange uses two different nouns for the monument, and the difference is worth an assay of its own. Montague says statue once, of Juliet, with the raising verb and the material attached: "I will raise her statue in pure gold" (5.3.300). Thereafter the governing word is figure: no figure at such rate be set, and Capulet's Romeo is a figure only by the carrying force of as rich. Statue is the sculptor's word, concrete, commissionable, an object with a maker and a site. Figure is the valuer's and the rhetorician's word at once: a shape, a representation, a sum in a column, a trope in a speech. As the vow proceeds, the concrete noun gives way to the abstract one, and the monument dissolves from an object into a valuation while the audience listens. By the end of Montague's sentence the statue exists less as a thing to be built than as a position to be held, the highest rated figure in the city, and a position can be defended without a single ingot being poured.

Figure carries one more sense that a dramatist of Shakespeare's verbal habits cannot be presumed innocent of: the figure of speech, the fashioned utterance, the trope. Romeo and Juliet are already figures in that sense before any founder is engaged, figures in the Prince's closing sentence, figures in the story the survivors are sent away to tell, figures in the play an audience has just watched. The fathers propose to fix in gold what the theatre has already fixed in language, and the redundancy is the deepest layer of the scene's irony. The lovers do not need the statues to survive; the play is their survival, and every person in the churchyard is about to become their teller. The golden figures would commemorate, in the most expensive medium available, a permanence that the cheapest medium, speech repeated, has already secured. Verona's gold is late to a task that Verona's talk is performing in the same scene, and the Prince's closing couplet, which gives the lovers to story rather than to sculpture, records the priority in the play's own final lines.

The period's material culture sharpens the point rather than blunting it. Commemoration in the world Shakespeare's audience knew ran along a scale from the painted board and the gilded effigy on a tomb chest to the great bronze and marble monuments of princes, and the scale measured rank no less than cost: to be figured in metal at civic expense was to be enrolled among the city's public worthies, its founders and saints and captains. The fathers' vows propose exactly that enrollment for two teenagers who died as fugitives from the city's order, married in defiance of its marriage market, buried in disgrace's posture if not in name. Read generously, the proposal is a revolution in the city's table of honour, the disobedient children raised above every rated figure Verona holds. Read coldly, it is an annexation: the city that could not accommodate the lovers alive accommodates them dead, at the highest rate, on the city's terms, in the city's metal, their rebellion melted down and recast as civic treasure. The two readings divide the critics because the gesture supports both, and it supports both because annexation and honour are the same act viewed from opposite ends of the tomb.

One more word in the vow deserves isolation, because it does the quiet work of both camps: set. No figure shall be set at such rate, Montague says, and set is the verb of the jeweller and the founder, of placing a stone in its mount and a type in its forme, and of fixing a price on a schedule. The lovers are to be set, placed, mounted, and priced in a single verb, their images installed the way a gem is installed in gold, which is also, not incidentally, the way the play's own imagery has always handled Juliet: a jewel in an Ethiope's ear, a pearl, a thing of price hung in the night's cheek. The fathers did not invent the gem language for their daughter. Her lover did, in the orchard and at the feast, as the idiom of wonder. The vow takes the lover's metaphor and executes it in the fathers' medium, and the execution is faithful and fatal at once: faithful, because the wonder is preserved and made public; fatal, because a metaphor set in metal stops being a way of seeing a person and becomes a way of owning her image. Between the orchard's jewel and the churchyard's figure there is exactly the distance this article has been measuring, the distance between a value discovered by love and a value assigned by settlement.

## The Price of Splendour: Gilding, Wages, and the Ducats of Mantua

The arithmetic of the solid statue has a practical counterpart that brings the vow down to the scale of the play's own transactions, and the scale is instructive. Verona's tragedy is full of priced services, and their prices are stated. Romeo gives the Nurse money for her errand and is mocked for his generosity by Mercutio's bawdy commentary on the transaction. He holds out forty ducats to the apothecary (5.1.59), a sum whose size the scene expects the audience to feel: a ducat was a gold coin of roughly three and a half grams, current across Europe, and forty of them, some one hundred and forty grams of gold, represented years of a poor man's income pressed into a starving hand in a moment. The apothecary's shop, with its tortoise shell, its stuffed alligator, and its beggarly accounts of empty boxes, is the play's counting house of last resort, and the price that works there is a fortune precisely because the seller is starving. Gold's eloquence in that scene is the eloquence of disproportion: the coin speaks louder than conscience because the ratio between the offer and the man's poverty is enormous.

Set the statues on the same scale and their disproportion runs the other way. Forty ducats buy a death in Mantua because the buyer meets a seller with nothing. The fathers' hundreds of thousands of pounds, at the letter of the vow, buy a memorial in Verona because the givers have everything, and the gesture's meaning depends on the surplus being visible. A vow within the fathers' means would be a purchase; a vow beyond any means is a performance of the unpayable, grief declaring itself too large for the market by naming a price the market cannot clear. Early modern gilding practice supplied the ordinary middle term between the two: gold leaf, beaten to a thinness that let a few grams of metal cover a figure entire, splendour by surface. A gilded statue of Juliet might carry less gold than Romeo carried to Mantua in his purse, and shine, to every eye in the square, exactly like the solid figure of the vow. The gap between the vow's letter and the craft's practice is therefore not a detail of the goldsmith's trade. It is the space in which the whole debate lives, the distance between what the fathers say and what any founder would actually deliver, between gold as a stated value and gold as a visible skin.

That space has a name in the play's own thinking, because the tragedy is preoccupied from its first act with the difference between seeming and being, surface and substance. Rosaline's imagined beauty, the friar's herbs that heal and kill in one plant, the potion that counterfeits death with death's exact appearance, Juliet's body read as a corpse by every expert eye in the tomb: the play's machinery runs on surfaces that lie and surfaces that tell the truth, and an audience trained by four acts of counterfeits brings a trained suspicion to a gilded surface in the fifth. A statue that shines as pure gold while carrying an ounce of the metal is the potion's cousin in the order of objects, a seeming that works because the eye cannot assay. The fathers, to do them justice, do not propose the counterfeit; they propose the impossible solid, purity all through. The impossibility protects their sincerity from the craft's compromise, since a promise too large to execute can never be caught executing itself cheaply. Whether that protection is innocence or design is, once again, the whole question, and the play leaves the founder's yard unbuilt so that the question stays open.

## Levin's Formality and the Editors' Glosses: Two More Witnesses

Harry Levin's essay "Form and Formality in Romeo and Juliet" (1960) supplies a witness neither Goddard nor Kahn anticipates, because Levin's concern is neither the fathers' souls nor their system but the play's own deep preference for form. Levin reads the tragedy as a work governed by formal patterns, set speeches, balanced antitheses, ritualised encounters, a world in which experience keeps arriving in ceremonious shapes. Within such a reading, the statues are the pattern completing itself at the level of the object: lovers who met in a sonnet, wooed in set forms, and died in a vault arranged like a stage picture are finally converted into the most formal objects a city can make, matched figures in a matched material, symmetry cast in gold. Levin's formality explains the ending's peculiar satisfaction better than any psychology of the fathers can, because the satisfaction is formal before it is emotional. The audience has watched a pattern strive toward balance for five acts, hate against love, Montague against Capulet, figure against figure, and the twin statues are the pattern's last rhyme, the couplet rendered as sculpture. On Levin's showing the vows are neither sincere nor hollow in the first instance; they are inevitable, the shape the play was always going to leave behind it, and the debate about the fathers' intentions is a debate conducted downstream of a formal necessity.

The editors, whose business is the words rather than the meanings, testify to the same doubleness from the dictionary's side. The glosses that modern editions, Brian Gibbons's Arden 2 and René Weis's Arden 3 among them, supply for the exchange run persistently in two directions at once. Jointure is glossed in the language of property settlement, the provision a marriage contract makes, so that Capulet's tenderest word arrives with a conveyance inside it. Rate is glossed as value, worth, estimation, the language of appraisal. Raise is glossed as erect, set up, the founder's and mason's verb. Even the plain narrative words of the vow, followed through an edition's notes, keep splitting into the vocabulary of the heart and the vocabulary of the account book, because the split is in the text and not in the commentators. An editor cannot gloss the passage into innocence: the notes themselves, those driest of witnesses, record a settlement being executed in the forms of property law at the moment of the play's supreme reconciliation. The scholarly apparatus, which exists to settle questions, here certifies that the central question is unsettleable at the level of the word, and must be carried up to the level of the verdict, where this article has carried it: the words are the feud's, the act is peace, and the play declines to launder the first into the second.

## Staging the Vows: Where the Directors Confess Their Reading

Every production of the play must take a position on the statues, because the ten lines cannot be played neutrally. The text gives the directors almost nothing except words: two old men, a magistrate, a friar, attendants, and a stage arranged around corpses, with dawn coming. Within that bareness, the physical choices available amount to an interpretation performed rather than argued. The fathers may clasp hands and hold the clasp through both vows, so that the gold is spoken inside an embrace and the audience watches the peace upheld by touch while the prices are named. Or the hand may be taken and released before Montague speaks, so that the vow steps out of the embrace and into the open air of public declaration, a proclamation to the city rather than a word to a brother. The first staging argues Snyder's case with bodies; the second argues Goddard's, isolating the bid from the bond so that the audience can hear the auction begin the moment the hands part.

The bodies on the stage offer the second confession. A production may leave Romeo and Juliet visible and central throughout the fathers' speeches, the golden words falling on the actual children, whose stillness rebukes or blesses the promises by simple presence. Or the arrangement may push the bodies to the edge of attention as the living principals take the centre, enacting the displacement the colder critics describe, the lovers already becoming background to their own commemoration while the men who own the future negotiate in the foreground. Neither arrangement is in the text; both are in the text's silences. The same freedom governs the vow's address. Montague may speak to Capulet, the gift a word between principals, or over his head to the assembled city and the audience beyond it, the superlative proclaimed to the market it ranks. A superlative spoken privately is devotion; a superlative proclaimed is competition, and the actor's eyeline decides which the house receives. Directors who complain that the ending is hard to land are usually reporting, without naming it, the doubleness this article has weighed: the lines ask to be played as reconciliation and heard as irony in the same breath, and a staging that resolves them wholly into either warmth or chill thins the one effect the writing guarantees, the audience's divided response, moved and suspicious at once, which is the response the Prince's glooming peace exists to name.

## The Silence Around the Vows: Who Hears the Gold and Does Not Answer

A vow is partly defined by its witnesses, and the witnesses of this one are arranged in a silence so complete that it amounts to a commentary. Lady Capulet stands in the scene. She has lost a daughter within the hour, a nephew within the week, and the marriage she brokered lies cancelled in the tomb in front of her, yet the settlement of her house's feud is conducted, in the text, entirely by her husband, and her portion of the catastrophe receives no word in the exchange at all. The Friar stands there too, the man whose cell joined the children and whose potion timed their deaths, fresh from a narration that has laid the whole plot before the Prince, and he offers no gloss on the statues, neither blessing nor misgiving. The watchmen, the Page, Balthasar, the attendants: the churchyard is populated, and the population is mute. Two men speak the peace in ten lines between them, under a magistrate's supervision, and the play's most talkative community, a city of nurses and servants and friars and cousins, has nothing to add.

The silence matters because the play has taught its audience to hear this family's settlements as performances for witnesses. The feast was a settlement performed, the betrothal a settlement announced, the wedding day a settlement enforced by threat in a bedroom full of women. Each arrangement of Juliet's life was transacted aloud, before the household, in the idiom of the house. The tomb settlement repeats the form with the household reduced to its two heads and its audience reduced to officials and corpses. Stripped of the feast's music and the bedroom's crowd, the transaction stands exposed in its minimal shape: offer, counter offer, match, adjournment. The bareness is why the competitive reading finds the scene so legible. With every social cushion removed, the grammar shows through, and the grammar is a negotiation's. It is also why the reconciliation reading finds the scene so stark: with every cushion removed, the renunciation has nowhere to hide, and a man who tears up his claim in a bare churchyard at dawn, before the magistrate and the bodies, is not obviously engaged in theatre for anyone's benefit but the dead.

Above both silences sits the largest one, the lovers' own. Romeo and Juliet are the most eloquent pair in the play, speakers who turned a first conversation into a sonnet and a balcony into a philosophy of value, and the disposition of their images is settled in a scene where their contribution is to be looked at. The fathers' epithets are the last descriptions of them spoken in the play before the Prince's couplet, true and faithful, poor sacrifices, and both descriptions are assignments made by others, the one certifying a character, the other assigning a function in the fathers' moral economy. Sacrifice is the colder word on inspection. A sacrifice is something offered up by somebody for something, and to name the children sacrifices of the enmity is to confess the mechanism and keep its grammar: they were spent, by the hatred, for the peace. The confession is honest, and its honesty is the fathers' best moment in the scene. But a spent thing has been used, and the word quietly aligns the golden statues with the spending, one more disposition of the same assets, first as marriage counters, then as sacrifices, at last as monuments. The sequence of roles assigned to the lovers by their world never varies in kind. Only the tenderness of the assignment improves.

## What the Statues Do Not Buy: The Unreformed City Around the Gold

A settlement can be audited by its omissions, and the closing settlement's omissions are constitutional. The fathers vow statues; they do not vow laws. Nothing in the ten lines touches the arrangements that made the catastrophe: the marriage market that priced Juliet at thirteen, the household authority that answered refusal with threats of the street, the honour code that armed the servants in the first scene and the cousins in the third, the civic order whose Prince had already threatened both houses with death for brawling and then watched the threat fail. The Prince's own closing business acknowledges the gap by reaching past the vows toward process: more talk, testimony sifted, some pardoned and some punished. Punishment and pardon are instruments of a legal order; statues are instruments of a commemorative one; and the play ends with the legal order's work unfinished and the commemorative order's work merely promised. Verona gets its monuments, in intention, before it gets its reckoning, in fact, and the order of those priorities is the city's character stated as a timetable.

This is the ground on which the irony reading is strongest and on which the reconciliation reading must be most carefully defended, because a peace that reforms nothing but the tempers of two old men is a peace whose warranty expires with them. The feud was never only theirs. It ran through servants who could not pass each other in a street without a quarrel, through kinsmen who treated a masked ball as an incursion, through a citizenry that turned out with clubs in the first scene crying the parties down. The play shows the hatred as a system with a thousand small shareholders, and the settlement is executed by the two largest. Nothing binds the small shareholders: no amnesty is proclaimed in the hearing, no restitution is ordered to the families of Mercutio or Paris or Tybalt, no word is spoken for the Nurse, the servants, the watch, the whole apparatus of the feud's daily life. The statues rise, in intention, over a city whose operating rules the ceremony leaves untouched.

The defence, and it is a real one, is that Shakespeare's Verona is a city of persons before it is a system of rules, and its catastrophe has been caused throughout by wills rather than by statutes. The Prince's edicts failed in the first act and the third; law unbacked by the houses' consent was shown to be weather. What changes at the tomb is the will of the two men whose consent the law always needed, and a dramatist who has spent five acts demonstrating the impotence of proclamation is consistent when he ends with conversion rather than legislation. On that showing the statues are not a substitute for reform but the sign of the only reform the play believes in, the reform of the principals, from which any durable civic change would have to descend. The glooming light and the pending punishments keep the sign from swelling into a triumph. The city is not saved in the closing scene; its two chief patients are discharged from the feud's hospital with their monuments unpaid for, their accounts unsettled, and their homework, in the Prince's phrase, still to be talked through. A reader weighing the vows should weigh that timetable with them: the peace is instantaneous and personal, the reckoning deferred and institutional, and the gold is promised in the interval between the two, glittering in the gap.

## Romeo the Spender: The Son's Gold Against the Father's

One figure complicates every simple account of the metal, and he is lying in the tomb while the vows are spoken. Romeo is the play's great spender, the one character who handles gold as if it were nothing, and his spending is the opposite of his father's in everything except the metal. He overpays the Nurse and laughs at the lecture the overpayment earns. He presses forty ducats on the apothecary with a speech denouncing the very coin he is spending, an act of contemptuous expenditure in which the gold is treated as filth that happens to work. His whole erotic economy runs on the same principle Juliet states on the balcony: value spent is value multiplied, the purse emptied into the moment without a rate ever being set. Romeo never appraises. He does not ask what the potion costs him in standing, what the marriage costs him in safety, what the return to Verona costs him in law. He is the tragedy's bankrupt saint of expenditure, and his ruin is the direct product of the refusal to keep accounts.

Montague's vow, heard against the son's practice, becomes a stranger and sadder thing than either camp admits. The father spends at last, and spends hugely, in the son's element of unmeasured giving, and the spending arrives one scene after the son's death has closed his account forever. There is a sense in which the statue is Montague's first act of Romeo like generosity, the first time the old man gives without a settlement in view, and the jointure renunciation that precedes it can be read as the father unlearning the ledger in real time, claim by claim, until the superlative bursts out of him as the sound of a man discovering bounty at the graveside of its inventor. The irony does not vanish in that reading; it changes its temperature. The gold is still gold, the rate is still a rate, and the son who could have taught the father the other language for value is the occasion of the lesson and its one excluded pupil. Verona's peace is funded by an inheritance of style from the dead, spent by the living in the only coin they hold, and the exchange rate between the son's bounty and the father's gold is the last unpriced quantity in a play that prices everything else.

## Raised, Not Buried: The Vertical Politics of the Vow

The Prologue, which spoils the play on purpose, describes the ending's transaction in a single buried metaphor: the lovers' death is the burial of their parents' strife, the fearful passage of their love marked by the grave doing double duty for children and quarrel alike. Against that promised burial, the closing vow performs a striking act of vertical disobedience. The strife goes down, into the earth with the bodies, exactly as promised. The children, in effigy, go up: raised, set, installed at the city's highest rate, lifted out of the vault where they lie and placed in the air of the public square. The fathers' monument programme reverses the Prologue's gravity for everything except the hatred. What the earth receives is the feud; what the sky receives is the feud's victims, gilded. Whether that reversal is the ending's glory or its last evasion depends on what a reader thinks commemoration is for, and the play has spent its whole length training the distinction between the two directions. Everything true in the tragedy happens low and hidden: a cell, an orchard at night, a vault. Everything false happens high and public: a feast, a market place brawl, a balcony watched by the house. The statues propose to move the lovers, at last, from the first register into the second, from the hidden places where their love was real into the public air where their families' honour was transacted, and the move can be mourned as a translation into the enemy's element or celebrated as the public world's surrender, the square finally obliged to honour what the orchard knew.

Height in the play is never innocent. Juliet speaks from above at the balcony and is, for the play's middle acts, the elevated intelligence of the tragedy, looking down on the garden where Romeo stands in the feud's ground level of names and walls. The tomb reverses the balcony: the lovers end below, in the Capulet vault, among the ancestors, in the one architecture the feud's families share with death. A statue raised in the square would complete a third movement, from balcony to vault to monument, the lovers' image climbing out of the family earth into the civic sky. The fathers, who between them controlled the balcony's house and the vault's ownership, propose to control the third location too, and the continuity of their custody is the quiet fact beneath the vow's splendour. At no point in the lovers' existence, before birth of the story or after its end, is their placement in the world out of the fathers' hands. The rebellion consisted of two young people placing themselves, in a cell, in a bed, in a tomb, and the monument programme is the restoration of the placing power to its original holders, exercised with love, with gold, and with the best intentions the play allows anyone. Restoration with love is still restoration, and Kahn's reading, weighed earlier, draws its long strength from exactly this continuity: the system does not need to be cruel to be itself.

Yet the raised figure has one property no vault and no balcony possessed, and the reconciliation reading is entitled to it in full. A statue in a square addresses strangers. It speaks to citizens unborn, to visitors, to the Veronese of the future whom the feud does not concern, and it tells them, in the city's most permanent medium, that these two children are the city's treasure and that their story is the city's shame and glory in one object. The fathers cannot control that audience, and they cannot recall the message when the square fills with people who will read the figures against the families that raised them, as every reader since has done. A monument is the one gift a commissioner cannot supervise forever. It escapes, into interpretation, the way the lovers escaped into marriage, and there is a rough justice in the escape being prepared by the same hands that built the cages. Montague's clause concedes the escape in advance, perhaps without knowing it: the statue's tenure is tied to the city's name, not to the family's, and a thing given to the city's keeping belongs, in the end, to whoever stands in the square and reads it. The audience of the play is the first crowd in that square, and its four centuries of argument over the figures are the vow's real unveiling, still in progress, still divided, exactly as the glooming light prescribed.

## Ten Lines in Two Hours: Proportion as the Play's Quiet Argument

The disproportion between the statues' bulk in criticism and their size in the play is itself a datum. Ten lines, in a tragedy of more than three thousand, spoken in its final minutes by two minor presences, have sustained a major interpretive industry, because the lines stand at the one point where every thread of the play is gathered and none is cut. They are the last transaction in a tragedy of transactions, the last valuation in a tragedy of valuations, the last formality in a tragedy of forms. An audience does not need a seminar to feel their weight; it needs only to have listened for two hours to a play in which gold seduces, packages, gilds, and corrupts, in which hands bless and kill, in which a girl explains that true wealth cannot be rated, and to hear the fathers, at the end of all that instruction, reach for gold, for hands, and for rates in a single breath. The ten lines are a final examination the play sets its audience, with the whole tragedy as the syllabus, and the centuries of divided answers are the examination's expected distribution of marks.

Proportion argues in one more direction worth recording. The lovers receive, between them, hundreds of lines of the most celebrated poetry in the language, and their monument receives ten lines of prose like verse from two old men who have barely spoken in the play. Montague in particular is among the least developed principals in Shakespearean tragedy, a father defined almost entirely by absence, by reports of sighs and by a wife's death announced in a subordinate clause. That the tragedy's supreme act of commemoration is entrusted to its least developed speaker is a choice with consequences for tone. The vow cannot draw on any stored intimacy between the audience and its speaker; no earlier scene has taught the house what Montague's love for his son sounded like, because the play contains no scene of Montague and Romeo together in private speech at all. The gold is asked to supply the missing intimacy, to prove by its cost what no scene has shown by acquaintance, and the substitution of expenditure for acquaintance is either the father's tragedy or his exposure, depending on the reading. A dramatist who wanted the vow received as simple love had the means to prepare it: one scene, one speech, one moment of the old man and the boy. The means were not used. The statue stands where the scene should be, and the audience is left to decide whether the gold fills the gap or marks it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What exactly does Montague promise, word for word, in the Folger text?

Montague promises a statue of Juliet, raised in pure gold, at a valuation no other figure in Verona shall match for as long as the city keeps its name. The Folger wording runs: "For I will raise her statue in pure gold, / That whiles Verona by that name is known, / There shall no figure at such rate be set / As that of true and faithful Juliet" (5.3.300 to 303). Two precisions matter. The promise names one statue, of the other house's child, not a pair; and its tense is the future throughout, a vow spoken over the bodies rather than a monument described as standing.

### Q: Which father's vow is longer, and what does the difference in length do?

Montague's vow runs to five lines, with the material, the city's name, the superlative, and the epithet all spelled out, while Capulet's answer takes two lines and borrows its wealth from the sentence before it. The disproportion performs the exchange's dynamics in miniature. The first bidder specifies the monument as a founder might, itemising metal, setting, and rank, because specification is how his sincerity proves itself. The second father's brevity reads two ways at once: as a man too broken for rhetoric, matching the bid with the plainest equation available, and as the scene's one flash of economy, since his two lines contain its deepest confession, the naming of the children as poor sacrifices of the enmity.

### Q: What does the word rate mean in "no figure at such rate be set"?

Rate, in the usage the editors record for this line, is valuation: the price or worth at which a thing is assessed and held. Montague is saying that no figure in Verona shall be assessed at a worth equal to Juliet's statue, which makes the superlative a statement about appraisal before it is a statement about size or splendour. The word matters because it belongs to the counting house rather than to the church. A father promising that no memorial shall surpass his gift in beauty would speak of fairness or brightness; a father promising that none shall be set at such rate is keeping a league table of worth, and the audience hears the table being drawn up even as the grief is spoken.

### Q: How much gold would a solid, life size statue of Juliet actually require?

Far more than any fortune in the play's world could supply. A life size human figure occupies roughly the volume of a body, sixty to seventy litres, and gold's density runs near nineteen and a third kilograms per litre, so a solid casting would weigh well past a thousand kilograms, toward thirteen hundred. Priced at the mint values of Shakespeare's England, where twenty shillings of gold coin carried about half an ounce troy of high fine metal, the figure prices out in the hundreds of thousands of pounds, against great noble incomes of a few thousand pounds a year. The arithmetic shows the vow operating as a superlative of feeling rather than a founder's contract, since period monuments achieved their gleam by gilding, a skin of gold over wood, bronze, or stone.

### Q: Why is nearly every value word in the closing exchange a comparison?

Because comparison is the feud's native grammar, and the fathers speak no other. Capulet tenders a settlement, a jointure, which is a sum under a legal name. Montague answers with an open comparative, "But I can give thee more", and builds the vow on a superlative of appraisal, no figure at such rate. Capulet closes with an equation, Romeo to lie as rich by his lady. Renunciation, outbidding, and matching are the three moves of a negotiation, and Shakespeare writes the reconciliation as one, whether because these men can honour only by valuing, or because the dramatist wants the audience to hear the bargaining continue inside the peace. The comparatives are the strongest single piece of evidence the irony reading owns, and the reconciliation reading must absorb them rather than explain them away.

### Q: How does the golden axe of the third act prepare the ear for gold at the tomb?

In the Friar's cell, Romeo hears his sentence of banishment as a beheading performed with a beautiful weapon: calling death banished, he tells the Friar, is cutting his head off with a golden axe (3.3.22). The image teaches the audience a rule for the metal that the rest of the play obeys. Gold in this tragedy is what makes a harmful thing presentable, the shine on an instrument of harm, splendid at the surface and lethal at the edge. An audience that has learned the rule cannot hear pure gold at the tomb as a neutral choice of material. The statues arrive pre judged by the axe, and part of the closing debate is simply the struggle between the vow's evident sincerity and the metal's established character, a struggle Shakespeare arranged by planting the image two acts early.

### Q: How does Juliet's boundless bounty speech argue against the statues before they are vowed?

On the balcony, Juliet states the lovers' whole theory of value in three lines: her bounty is as boundless as the sea, her love as deep, and the more she gives the more she has, since both are infinite (2.2.133 to 135). The theory abolishes rate. A thing that grows by being given away cannot be appraised, ranked, or matched, and it cannot run out. The statues are built on the opposite theory, in which honour is finite metal, assigned a highest price and defended against rivals. When the fathers commemorate Juliet by setting her figure at Verona's supreme rate, they honour her in the one vocabulary her most famous speech refutes, and the refutation was placed where every reader meets it first, two acts before the vow is spoken.

### Q: How does Coppélia Kahn's reading change the meaning of the handshake itself?

Kahn reads the feud as an extreme expression of patriarchal society, a system in which fathers dispose of their children as instruments of honour and alliance, so the handshake cannot be read inside her frame as a simple release from that system. A handshake between the two disposers, glossed by the disposer of the bride as his daughter's jointure, is a contract executed inside the system's own forms, between its two surviving principals, over the bodies of the disposed. The gesture still ends the feud, and Kahn's reading does not require it to be insincere. It requires the audience to notice what kind of act a settlement is: the system closing its account of the children, in the children's absence, with the children's images as the settled estate.

### Q: How does Susan Snyder's comic reading change what the statues mean?

Snyder reads the tragedy as a comic pattern intercepted by death, in which the machinery of comedy, young lovers against blocking fathers, drives toward its appointed end of social reintegration. The closing reconciliation, on her account, is that ending arriving after the interception, the community reknit around the couple it expelled, with graves where the marriage bed should stand. The statues then read as the reintegration made permanent and public, the city's formal readmission of the pair, cast in the most durable honour the city can mint. The splendour measures the size of the readmission rather than the vanity of the givers. Snyder's frame also explains the handshake's emotional landing, since a fulfilled form satisfies an audience even when its fulfilment arrives in mourning dress.

### Q: Do the statues end the feud, or does the handshake?

The handshake ends it, and the statues commemorate an ending already performed. The sequence in the Folger text is exact: Capulet offers his hand and renounces his demand, Montague accepts by answering, and only within that acceptance does the statue appear, as the acceptance's ornament and pledge. Strip the statues away and the peace stands entire, a brotherhood named, a claim cancelled, an enmity confessed over its sacrifices. Strip the handshake away and the statues become two rival monuments commissioned by enemies, the feud continued in sculpture. The gold therefore cannot be the peace's substance, only its publication, which is why the debate about the statues is really a debate about whether publication honours a peace or exposes the publishers.

### Q: Why is Lady Montague, whose death is announced in the same scene, given no part in the closing bargain?

Because the bargain is struck between the feud's two principals, and the play is exact about who its principals are. Montague enters the scene reporting that his wife has died that night, her breath stopped by grief at her son's exile (5.3.210 to 211), which makes three fresh corpses chargeable to the enmity before the fathers speak a word of peace. The exchange then proceeds without her: no statue is vowed for the mother, no jointure names her, and Lady Capulet, who stands in the scene, is likewise given no line in the settlement. The silence is evidence for the colder reading of the vows. The reconciliation is transacted by the surviving heads of the system, in the system's forms, and those whom the system spent without consulting are not consulted at its closing either.

### Q: How does "true and faithful Juliet" turn the language of assay into a judgment of character?

An assay certifies metal: it tests fineness and stamps the result, guaranteeing that a thing is what it claims to be. Montague's epithet performs the same operation on the dead girl. True and faithful are the findings of a test, delivered by the head of the house that had every reason to fail her, certifying that the fidelity her own father punished as disobedience was genuine metal all along. The compliment is considerable, and so is its displacement. A certificate is issued by an authority over an object, and Juliet, who in life insisted on being a speaker and a chooser, is in death certified, stamped, and set up by authorities. The epithet honours her character in the grammar of a hallmark, which is the exchange's doubleness compressed into three words.

### Q: Does the text name any place in Verona where the statues are to stand?

No place is named. Montague's vow fixes the statue's rank and its duration, no figure at such rate for as long as Verona keeps its name, but gives it no square, church, gate, or street, and Capulet's matching line adds only a position relative to the other figure, Romeo to lie by his lady's side. The omission is striking in a scene otherwise dense with place, the churchyard, the tomb, the vault where the bodies lie arranged. Monuments without sites are closer to sentiments than to commissions, and the placelessness supports the reading of the vows as measures of feeling rather than as building contracts, splendid valuations pronounced in a graveyard and left for a future the play declines to enter.

### Q: How does "whiles Verona by that name is known" tie the statues to the city's fame rather than to private grief?

The clause makes the statue's tenure depend on Verona's civic identity, not on the families' mourning. As long as the city is Verona, known by that name to the world, the figure is to stand unrivalled, which enrolls the whole citizenry and every future visitor as the monument's audience and guarantors. Private grief ends with the grievers; this honour is designed to outlast both fathers and to trade on the fame of the place itself. The tie cuts two ways. It lifts the gift out of household vanity into civic memory, the lovers adopted by their city at last. It also makes the children into an asset of Verona's reputation, a sight the city is known by, which is a form of ownership wider and more durable than any father's.

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch statues weighing record, entry by entry?

The InsightCrunch statues weighing records the closing exchange as eleven weighed elements, each entered with the Folger wording that supports it and the direction in which it tips the interpretation. Its entries run from the offered hand and the jointure, through the comparative, the material, the superlative, and the epithet, to the matching line, the confession of sacrifices, the unbuilt performance, the absent mothers, and the Prince's glooming frame. The table's discipline is confinement: nothing is weighed that cannot be checked against the ten lines and their documented setting in under a minute, so the debate's two camps argue from a shared set of exhibits rather than from rival impressions of the scene's mood.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch statues weighing to argue that the reconciliation is real and its medium ironic?

An essay should work the table in two passes. The first pass gathers the entries that tip toward reconciliation, the offered hand, the renounced jointure, the honoured epithet, and the confession of sacrifices, and builds from them the case that the peace is an event, performed and binding. The second pass gathers the entries that tip toward irony, the comparative, the pure gold, the equation that prices Romeo, the unbuilt future, and the silent mothers, and builds the case that the event is transacted in the feud's own dialect. The thesis then states the relation between the passes rather than choosing between them: the peace is genuine, the gold is the price of its genuineness being expressed by these men, and Shakespeare designed the expression to be audited.

### Q: Why does each father commission the other child's statue instead of his own?

The crossing of the commissions is the exchange's most generous structural feature. Montague, who lost a son, spends his gold on the Capulet daughter, and Capulet's matching line places the Montague son beside her, so each child is honoured by the house that hounded the match and each father pays his tribute across the feud's old border. In gift terms the crossing converts rivalry into reciprocity, since a monument to a man's own child would be family pride, while a monument to the enemy's child is reparation. The crossing also has its colder face. Each girl and boy is valued by the other house's head, appraised and certified by a stranger's authority, so the children pass through the fathers' hands one final time, as they did in every arrangement of their lives.

### Q: Does the play name a maker, a founder, or a date for the statues?

It names none of the three. No sculptor is commissioned by name, no founder's yard is mentioned, no term is set for the raising, and no unveiling is promised before any witness. The vow supplies a material, a rank, and a duration tied to the city's fame, and omits every practical particular that would turn a sentiment into a contract with a craftsman. That pattern of specification is itself legible. Montague specifies exactly the elements that express value and omits exactly the elements that would express a plan, which is the behaviour of a man stating the size of his feeling in the largest denomination he knows. A reader who wants the statues built must build them in imagination, on credit extended to two debtors whose credit the play has spent five acts examining.

### Q: How does "saint seducing gold" in the first act set the metal's first meaning in the play?

Gold's first appearance in the tragedy is as a would be seducer refused. Romeo, describing the unattainable Rosaline, says she will not open her lap to saint seducing gold (1.1.212), which casts the metal as a pander offering purchased desire and casts the woman who refuses it as proof against the whole economy of bought love. The phrase fixes gold's character before any statue is dreamed of: it is the agent that tries to convert desire into transaction. Every later appearance obeys the casting, the gilded marriage book, the golden axe, the ducats that out argue a starving man's conscience. When the metal returns at the tomb in its purest stated form, the audience has been trained for two hours to ask what the gold is trying to buy, and the question hangs over the vow whether the fathers intend it or not.

### Q: What verdict does the evidence support: sincere reconciliation, hollow irony, or a real peace spoken in the feud's own language?

The evidence supports the third verdict, and the first two survive inside it as partial truths. Sincere reconciliation is the event: a hand taken, a demand renounced, an enmity confessed, a feud ended in fact. Hollow irony fails as a total account because the text gives the peace real instruments and real effects, and no character is licensed to doubt them. But the peace is transacted entirely in the feud's dialect of settlement, comparison, and appraisal, in the play's most compromised metal, over children converted into rated objects, by the two men whose system spent them. Shakespeare added the statues to an inherited reconciliation in order to make that doubleness unavoidable, and the glooming light in which the Prince wraps the morning is the dramatist's own stage direction for reading it.
