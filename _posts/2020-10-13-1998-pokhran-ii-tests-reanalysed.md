On 11 May 1998, at 15:45 hours Indian Standard Time, three nuclear devices detonated beneath the Pokhran test range in the Thar desert of Rajasthan, and the government of Atal Bihari Vajpayee, seven weeks after taking office, announced that India stood as a nuclear weapon state. Two days later, on 13 May, two further devices followed, and Vajpayee declared the planned series complete. The five detonations of Operation Shakti can be read as strategy, as engineering, or as politics, and each reading has its literature. This article defends the third reading as the one that explains the most: the Pokhran II tests operated as the Vajpayee government's architectural-political moment, a single act through which a fragile coalition government fixed its standing at home, restated the cultural grammar of the movement that led it, demonstrated an administrative machine capable of absolute secrecy, accepted a calculated price in sanctions, and opened the corridor that led, a decade later, to the civil nuclear settlement of 2008. The claim is not that strategy and engineering were absent. The claim is that both were carried, timed and framed by domestic political architecture, and that the architecture is where the explanatory weight lies.

## A Government Seven Weeks Old Meets Its Defining Decision

The government that ordered the tests had been sworn in on 19 March 1998, after the general election of February and March returned a hung Lok Sabha in which the Bharatiya Janata Party emerged as the largest single party with 182 seats. Vajpayee's authority rested on the National Democratic Alliance, a coalition assembled before and after the poll from regional parties whose ideological distance from the BJP was considerable, held together by the National Agenda for Governance, the common programme negotiated in March 1998 that deferred the BJP's three most charged commitments: the construction of a Ram temple at Ayodhya, a uniform civil code, and the abrogation of Article 370. The architecture of that settlement, in which a party founded on an explicit civilisational programme agreed to govern on a moderated agenda, is the subject of [the post-1998 NDA institutional-political context](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/), and it frames everything that followed in May. A government that had publicly parked its signature causes required another register in which to demonstrate resolve, and the nuclear question offered one that the coalition's partners could endorse without endorsing the deferred agenda.

The memory that disciplined the new ministers was the humiliation of May 1996, when Vajpayee's first government fell after thirteen days, unable to prove a majority in the Lok Sabha. The thirteen-day episode, treated in the account of [the 1996 thirteen-day government](/2019/05/31/1996-vajpayee-thirteen-days-explained/), and the two United Front governments that followed under H. D. Deve Gowda and I. K. Gujral, covered in [the interregnum governments of 1996 to 1998](/2019/06/06/deve-gowda-gujral-interregnums-india/), had made governmental fragility the defining fact of Indian politics in the late 1990s. Vajpayee returned to office in March 1998 determined, on the testimony of his own officials, that the second government would not be remembered for hesitation. Within days of the swearing-in, according to Raj Chengappa's reconstruction in Weapons of Peace (2000), the Prime Minister sounded out A. P. J. Abdul Kalam, the scientific adviser to the defence minister and secretary of the Defence Research and Development Organisation, on how quickly the test sites could be readied. Kalam's answer, as Chengappa records it, was that the scientific teams could be ready within weeks if the political decision was firm. The decision was taken in April 1998, in a circle that the participants themselves later counted on one hand.

The smallness of the circle was itself a governing choice with a domestic logic. A decision carried through the full Cabinet in the ordinary way would have engaged the alliance's consultative machinery, the coordination committee through which the NDA managed its disagreements, and with it the risk of leak or veto that the 1995 precedent had made concrete. By holding the decision inside the Prime Minister's office, the government presented its partners with an accomplished national act rather than a proposal, and the partners, as their conduct in May showed, preferred the accomplished act: none of the allies complained in public at having been excluded from the taking of the decision, and several claimed a share of its credit. The method had a cost in institutional terms that the opposition named at the time: a decision of this magnitude, Congress speakers charged in the parliamentary debate, had been taken without the Cabinet's collective deliberation and without any mandate beyond a manifesto sentence the allies had diluted in the National Agenda. The government's answer, that national security decisions of this class had always been held tight, Indira Gandhi's in 1974 included, was historically accurate and politically sufficient. The exchange fixed the precedent that the nuclear file in India belongs to the prime ministerial centre, a settlement later governments of both parties preserved without amendment.

### Why did a government seven weeks old choose May 1998 for the tests?

Three clocks converged on May 1998. The manifesto of February 1998 had promised to exercise the option to induct nuclear weapons, and deferral carried a credibility cost. The test ban treaty's entry-into-force conference of 1999 threatened to harden the legal barrier against testing. A new government also held maximum political capital before coalition friction accumulated.

The manifesto point deserves emphasis, because it separates the 1998 decision from a purely reactive security logic. The BJP had demanded an Indian bomb since the Jana Sangh years of the 1950s, and its 1998 manifesto converted that long-standing demand into an electoral commitment phrased with care: the party would re-evaluate India's nuclear policy and exercise the option to induct nuclear weapons. Vajpayee, campaigning as the coalition's moderate face, did not campaign on a test date, and the National Agenda for Governance spoke the softer language of national security review. But the commitment existed in print, the party's cadre expected it to be honoured, and the strategic community that advised the new government, from Brajesh Mishra, the principal secretary who also served as national security adviser, to K. Subrahmanyam, the doyen of Indian strategic writing, regarded the manifesto as a mandate. George Perkovich, in India's Nuclear Bomb (1999), the most rigorous sceptical account, argued that this domestic configuration, a party ideologically committed to the bomb, a scientific lobby eager to test its designs, and a new government hungry for a demonstration of strength, explained the timing better than any deterioration in India's external environment in the spring of 1998. The adjudication among these readings occupies the closing sections of this article; the factual spine is not in dispute. A government seven weeks old chose the most consequential act available to it, and it chose it early, deliberately, and in secret.

## The Inheritance: 1974, the Open Option and the Aborted Test of 1995

India's nuclear inheritance in March 1998 consisted of one test, twenty-four years of restraint, and an option kept deliberately open. On 18 May 1974, the festival of Buddha Purnima, scientists of the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre detonated a plutonium device at Pokhran in an operation codenamed Smiling Buddha. Indira Gandhi's government described the shot as a peaceful nuclear explosion, a formulation that claimed the technical achievement while disavowing weapon intent. The device, designed under Raja Ramanna with R. Chidambaram among its young physicists, yielded, on the official Indian figure, about twelve kilotons, though outside seismic estimates ran lower. The 1974 test brought sanctions of its own, the rupture of Canadian cooperation at the Rajasthan reactors and the creation, in reaction, of the Nuclear Suppliers Group. It also established the pattern that governed the next quarter century: India would demonstrate capability, decline to weaponise openly, and refuse to sign the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, which New Delhi condemned as discriminatory for freezing the status of the five states that had tested before 1967.

The road to 1974 had itself been paved by defeat and by a neighbour's bomb. China's first test at Lop Nor on 16 October 1964, two years after the border war of 1962, converted the Indian nuclear question from a scientific matter into a parliamentary one: the Jana Sangh demanded an Indian bomb outright, sections of the Congress demanded a review of the disarmament posture, and Homi Bhabha, the founder of the atomic energy programme, broadcast the estimate that India could build a device within eighteen months of a political decision. Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri authorised the study of nuclear explosive technology while refusing a weapons programme, the compromise that held the state together. After Shastri's death in January 1966 and Bhabha's death in an air crash the same month, the programme drifted until Indira Gandhi, pressed by the scientists and by the strategic anxieties of the early 1970s, authorised the device that Ramanna's team fired in 1974. The genealogy matters to the politics of 1998 because every element of the later moment was present in embryo: a China shock, a parliamentary argument, a scientific lobby with designs on the shelf, and a prime minister who treated the decision as an instrument of national standing as much as of defence.

The option survived every government. Indira Gandhi authorised test preparations in the early 1980s and drew back. Rajiv Gandhi, in 1988, placed before the United Nations a plan for the time-bound elimination of nuclear weapons, even as the missile programme expanded. The decisive pressure arrived in the 1990s. In 1995, the NPT was extended indefinitely, locking the five recognised weapon states into their status in perpetuity. In 1996, the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty was negotiated at Geneva, where India's ambassador Arundhati Ghose told the Conference on Disarmament that India would never sign a treaty that froze its option while the weapon states kept their arsenals, a sentence that became the shorthand for the Indian position. The treaty text, adopted over Indian objections, named India among forty-four states whose ratification was required for entry into force, a clause New Delhi read as coercion. George Perkovich's account and Chengappa's narrative converge on the consequence: after 1996, Indian policymakers believed the window for a lawful demonstration of capability was closing, and that a government intending to test would have to act before the treaty's machinery hardened.

The keeping of the option had its own political texture, which the governments of 1998 inherited whole. Morarji Desai, prime minister of the Janata government from 1977 to 1979, renounced explosive testing on principle and said so publicly, the only Indian premier to close the door in words, yet even Desai refused to sign the non-proliferation treaty or to surrender the capability. Indira Gandhi, returned to office in 1980, authorised preparations again in the early 1980s before drawing back under American pressure. Rajiv Gandhi paired the missile programme's expansion with his 1988 action plan for a nuclear-weapon-free world, a pairing that stated the Indian settlement in its classic form: capability at home, disarmament as the demand abroad, and no signature on any instrument that separated the two. The Chandra Shekhar and Rao governments of the 1990s kept the designs current and the shafts maintained. By 1998 the option was therefore not a BJP invention but a bipartisan inheritance administered by five Congress and non-Congress governments alike, a fact the Congress opposition of May 1998 relied upon when it praised the scientists and attacked only the theatre. What the BJP supplied was not the capability but the decision to declare it, and the declaration, not the physics, is the political event under analysis.

One government had already tried. In December 1995, the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao prepared a test at Pokhran, and American intelligence, reading satellite photographs of activity at the range, detected the preparations. The United States ambassador, Frank Wisner, confronted New Delhi with the imagery, and Rao, facing the threat of sanctions under the Glenn Amendment and unwilling to spend his government's remaining capital, stood the test down. The episode, reconstructed in detail by Chengappa and confirmed in its essentials by American officials, taught the scientific and security teams two lessons that shaped 1998. The first was technical: preparations at Pokhran were visible from orbit, and any future attempt would need camouflage, night work and satellite-pass discipline of a kind the 1995 attempt had lacked. The second was political: the decision to test could not survive advance discussion, because detection invited pressure, and pressure, applied early, worked. The secrecy architecture of April and May 1998, and the tiny circle that held the secret, were designed against the memory of December 1995 as much as against any adversary.

## The Treaty Order the Tests Answered: Geneva 1996 and the Closing Window

The international architecture against which the decision was taken requires its own statement, because the government's public case rested on it and the opposition's case disputed its weight. The test ban treaty adopted at Geneva in September 1996, after the Conference on Disarmament failed to reach consensus and the text was carried to the United Nations General Assembly by Australia, banned all nuclear explosions and created an organisation at Vienna to monitor compliance. Its entry-into-force clause required ratification by forty-four named states possessing nuclear reactors, India among them, which meant the treaty could not take legal effect without the signature of a state that had voted against it. Indian diplomacy read the clause as an instrument of coercion drafted for one country. Arundhati Ghose, India's ambassador to the Conference on Disarmament, had stated the position through the negotiations of 1996: India would accept a test ban inside a framework of time-bound global disarmament, and would not accept a ban that merely froze the arsenals of the five recognised powers at their accumulated advantage. When the treaty was adopted over India's objection, the foreign ministry in New Delhi declared that India would never sign it in its existing form, a commitment every subsequent government repeated.

The domestic argument over Geneva ran on two tracks that May 1998 joined. The strategic community, Subrahmanyam's writings prominent among them, argued that the treaty's machinery, its monitoring stations, its challenge inspections and its entry-into-force conference scheduled for 1999, would progressively raise the cost of the Indian option until the option existed only on paper, and that a state intending ever to test had a closing window measured in months. The disarmament critics answered that the window argument was manufactured: the treaty bound only its signatories, India had survived the non-proliferation treaty's indefinite extension in 1995 without testing, and the supposed deadline of 1999 was a date the government had chosen to treat as binding for reasons of its own calendar. Both positions were held by named, serious holders, and both recur inside the adjudication. The factual residue is narrow and undisputed: the treaty existed, its conference approached, the American president who had signed it in September 1996 would see his own Senate refuse ratification in October 1999, and the Indian government of 1998 treated the treaty's approach as one of the three clocks, alongside the manifesto and its own newness, that fixed the month of May.

## Two Mornings in the Thar: What Was Detonated on 11 and 13 May

The Pokhran range, administered by the Indian Army in the desert west of Jaisalmer, had been quiet as a test site since 1974, though its shafts and instrumentation had been maintained and, after 1995, watched by foreign satellites. In the last week of April 1998, the teams returned. The scientific leadership was divided between the two great houses of the Indian nuclear programme. R. Chidambaram, chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission and secretary of the Department of Atomic Energy, held overall technical responsibility for the devices, which the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre at Trombay had designed. A. P. J. Abdul Kalam, who combined the offices of scientific adviser to the defence minister, secretary of the DRDO and, for this operation, co-coordinator of the test campaign, managed the range, the instrumentation and the delivery of the devices to the shafts. Anil Kakodkar, director of BARC, led the design group for the thermonuclear device. K. Santhanam, a senior DRDO scientist, directed test site preparations, the role that placed him at the centre of a controversy that erupted a decade later. The Army's engineers, among them the 58 Engineer Regiment, prepared and guarded the shafts. Secrecy governed dress as well as movement: Kalam and Chidambaram travelled to the range in army fatigues under assumed ranks, Kalam as Major General Prithviraj and Chidambaram under the codename Natraj in Chengappa's account, so that the two most recognisable faces of the programme would not be named in any movement log.

### What exactly was detonated at Pokhran on 11 and 13 May 1998?

India fired five devices under Operation Shakti. On 11 May, three detonated together: a thermonuclear device rated by the Department of Atomic Energy at forty-five kilotons, a fission device at fifteen kilotons, and a sub-kiloton device at a fraction of a kiloton. On 13 May, two further sub-kiloton devices completed the series.

The official figures were issued by Chidambaram and Kalam at a press conference in New Delhi on 17 May 1998, and their presentation was precise about design intent. The thermonuclear device of 11 May, they stated, had been deliberately built to a reduced yield of forty-five kilotons, fifteen from the fission trigger and thirty from the fusion stage, against a theoretical design yield of two hundred kilotons, so that the villages around the range, among them Khetolai, a few kilometres from the shafts, would suffer no damage. The fission device of fifteen kilotons represented the weaponised descendant of the 1974 design lineage. The three sub-kiloton devices, fired on 11 and 13 May, were experimental shots of the kind used to validate design parameters and simulation codes at low yield. On 13 May, after the final two detonations, Vajpayee announced that the planned series stood completed, a formulation that closed the testing window on India's own timetable rather than under the pressure that was already building in Washington and at the United Nations. The seismic signature of 11 May registered around magnitude 5.3 on open networks abroad. What that signature meant in kilotons became, almost immediately, the most contested technical question of the episode, and it is treated in full in the yield section below.

The firing day itself was choreographed against observation. In Chengappa's account, the three shafts of 11 May carried codenames drawn from the team's own shorthand, the thermonuclear shaft called the White House, the fission shaft the Taj Mahal and the sub-kiloton shaft Kumbhkaran, and the devices, fabricated at Trombay, had been flown to the desert in military transport aircraft in sealed crates before being lowered by the range teams. The hour of firing, mid-afternoon, was chosen with the desert wind in mind, so that dust raised by the shots would drift away from the inhabited villages, and with the satellite timetable in mind, so that the final emplacement work fell inside a gap in photographic coverage. Khetolai and the neighbouring villages felt the ground move and reported cracked plaster in a handful of houses; the government's statement that no village damage had occurred held, on the official inspections that followed, because the reduced yield of the thermonuclear device had been set with those villages as the binding constraint. The material point for the political reading is the care itself: the operation was engineered, at every stage, to be undeniable as an achievement and unusable as an atrocity, a demonstration calibrated for the audiences, domestic and foreign, who would judge it within the hour.

The designs carried their own genealogy. The fission device of 11 May descended from the plutonium lineage that ran from the CIRUS reactor's fuel through the 1974 device and the weaponisation work of the 1980s and 1990s, when BARC teams under Ramanna and then Chidambaram refined a deliverable design. The thermonuclear device embodied the programme's ambition of two decades: a two-stage design whose fusion secondary BARC's physicists, Kakodkar's group among them, had modelled through the years when testing was politically impossible, validating their codes against the single data point of 1974. The sub-kiloton shots belonged to the programme's future: low-yield experiments generate the data by which designers certify that weapons will work without further full-scale proof, the exact capability a state requires if it intends to accept a testing moratorium from strength. Read as engineering, the five shots were a curriculum: one weapon proven, one ambition declared, and the instruments of a testless future calibrated. The individual devices carried the operation's name in sequence, Shakti I through Shakti V, in the programme's internal usage.

## The Five-Component Map of the Architectural-Political Moment

The reading defended here disassembles the event into five components, each documented in the sections that follow, and the map of those components is the artifact this article asks to be cited by name: the Five-Component Architectural-Political-Moment Map of Pokhran II. The first component is positioning: a seven-week-old coalition government converted a deferred ideological programme into a demonstration of governing resolve on ground its partners could share. The second is cultural articulation: the code phrase of success, the Buddha smiling again, bound the tests to the deepest symbolic register available to the movement in office. The third is coordination: a six-man architecture of political and scientific authority executed the decision in secrecy against the known capabilities of American surveillance. The fourth is the sanctions response: the government priced the Glenn Amendment penalties in advance, absorbed them, and converted endurance under sanctions into further proof of resolve. The fifth is trajectory: the tests set the terms on which India negotiated with the United States from June 1998 to September 2000, and through that corridor reached the civil nuclear settlement of 2008. Read together, the five components state the namable claim: the May 1998 Pokhran II tests operate as the Vajpayee government's architectural-political moment, and the framework captures domestic-political content that strategic and technological readings, each true within its limits, leave unexplained.

One feature of the moment belongs to no single component because it conditions all five: the government's first hundred days supplied the stage. A ministry formed on 19 March, which had spent its first weeks negotiating survival with its allies and its agenda with its own party, reached its hundredth day in late June carrying a transformed reputation, and the transformation's date can be fixed to a single week in May. Political science has a commonplace about honeymoon authority, that new governments may spend on hard decisions the trust that later tenures must earn. The Pokhran decision inverted the commonplace's usual direction: the hard decision manufactured the trust, converting a negotiated ministry of uncertain duration into the government by which the country's capacity for decision would be measured for years. The components that follow are best read as the mechanisms of that manufacture, each doing work the others could not, and the whole constituting the rarest artefact of coalition politics, a founding act performed after the founding.

The map rests on an evidentiary triangle that the scholarship of the episode supplies, and the triangle's geometry is worth stating because the three bodies of evidence were written from three opposed positions. Raj Chengappa's Weapons of Peace (2000), reported from inside the Indian programme's confidence, supplies the operational narrative: the decision's timing, the secrecy tradecraft, the scientists' world. George Perkovich's India's Nuclear Bomb (1999), written from within the American non-proliferation community by a scholar sceptical of the programme, supplies the critical anatomy of motives and the documentary record of the option's long keeping. Strobe Talbott's Engaging India (2004), the memoir of the American negotiator charged with reversing the tests' effects, supplies the diplomatic sequel from the receiving end. A reading that survives all three accounts, the celebrant's, the sceptic's and the adversary-negotiator's, earns a presumption of solidity that no single tradition could confer, and the five components are drawn so as to be visible in each: Chengappa documents the coordination, Perkovich the positioning and the articulation, Talbott the trajectory, and all three record the sanctions and the price. Where the three accounts conflict, on yields above all, this article attributes and declines to settle, as the yield section sets out.

## Component One: Positioning a Coalition on Ground Every Partner Could Share

Coalition governments are usually read through their compromises, and the Vajpayee government of March 1998 had compromised more visibly than most, shelving the three causes that defined its leading party in order to hold the Telugu Desam Party, the Trinamool Congress, the Samata Party, the Shiromani Akali Dal, the Biju Janata Dal and a dozen smaller formations inside one alliance. The nuclear decision reversed the direction of that arithmetic. Where the National Agenda had asked the BJP's cadre to accept deferral, the tests gave every partner an act of state that required no ideological concession from anyone. Support for an Indian deterrent commanded assent across the Indian political spectrum in 1998 in a way that Ayodhya, the civil code and Article 370 never could; even parties that condemned the tests condemned them on grounds of timing, arms racing or diplomacy, not on the proposition that India must remain permanently without the capability its scientists had demonstrated in 1974. The tests therefore functioned as coalition cement of an unusual kind: an assertion of Hindu nationalist resolve, legible to the BJP's base as the fulfilment of a forty-year demand, that simultaneously presented itself to allies and voters as a national act above party. Vajpayee's own rhetoric in May 1998 worked this seam deliberately, speaking of national consensus, of the scientists' achievement and of restraint, and avoiding triumphalist vocabulary in his formal statements even as his party's workers distributed sweets outside its offices.

The positioning extended to the government's internal hierarchy. The decision elevated the figures who embodied continuity and competence: Brajesh Mishra, whose dual office as principal secretary and national security adviser made him the hinge between the Prime Minister's office and the strategic bureaucracy; Lal Krishna Advani, the home minister and the party's organisational anchor, whose presence in the decision circle signalled that the act carried the movement's full weight, and who told interviewers in the days after 11 May that the tests had settled the question of India's status; and George Fernandes, the socialist defence minister whose Samata Party stood far from the BJP ideologically, and whose early May statements naming China as India's principal threat had prepared the security argument in public. That a coalition containing Fernandes, Chandrababu Naidu's Telugu Desam supporting from outside, and Mamata Banerjee's Trinamool could unite behind the tests, while it could unite behind almost nothing else of comparable weight, is the domestic fact the strategic reading cannot digest. The government's standing in the country rose sharply in the weeks after 11 May, in the surveys published by India Today and other magazines, and the rise was recorded across regions and communities that the BJP had never carried. Positioning, in short, was not a by-product of the tests. It was, on the evidence of the manifesto, the agenda settlement and the decision's timing, one of its purposes.

The slogan in which the positioning announced itself survives as the period's shorthand. Lal Bahadur Shastri had given the republic its most durable slogan in 1965, Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan, hail the soldier, hail the farmer. In the days after the tests, Vajpayee amended the inheritance in public, adding a third term: Jai Vigyan, hail science. The amendment was a small act of political craftsmanship. It placed the scientists beside the soldier and the farmer in the national trinity, it bound the BJP government to a slogan authored by a Congress prime minister of unimpeachable standing, and it told the scientific departments that the state would honour in public what it had demanded of them in secret. The government later fixed the anniversary in the calendar, declaring 11 May National Technology Day, first marked in 1999, so that the positioning acquired an annual recurrence. A strategic act does not require a slogan or a commemorative day. A political moment manufactures both, and the manufacture is evidence of intent: the tests were built to be remembered on schedule, by a government that understood commemoration as a form of rule.

The allies' conduct in May 1998 completes the component. J. Jayalalithaa's AIADMK, the coalition's most volatile partner and the author of most of its crises, welcomed the tests without reservation, an assent her party's Dravidian inheritance made inexpensive and her rivalry with the DMK made attractive. Chandrababu Naidu's Telugu Desam Party, supporting the government from outside, endorsed the decision while pressing its federal demands on other fronts, treating the tests as national policy outside the bargaining ledger. The Samata Party supplied the defence minister who had made the China argument in public. Even the coalition's critics inside the alliance confined their complaints to prices, portfolios and procedure. The nuclear consensus held because the decision had been routed, whether by design or by instinct, through the one register of state action on which the alliance's contradictions did not bite. That routing is what the word architecture names in the framework: not the explosion, but the placement of the explosion inside a structure built to receive it.

## Component Two: Buddha Smiles Again and the Cultural Grammar of the Tests

When the firing party at Pokhran confirmed the successful detonation of 11 May, the message passed to the Prime Minister's residence in New Delhi carried, in the accounts published by the participants and repeated in the anniversary literature of the test programme, the words that the Buddha had smiled again. The phrase was a quotation, and every informed listener in 1998 knew its source. The code message of 18 May 1974, flashed to Indira Gandhi after the first Pokhran shot, had announced that the Buddha was smiling: the 1974 test had been fired on Buddha Purnima, the festival of the Buddha's birth and enlightenment, and the codename of that operation, Smiling Buddha, had folded the festival into the state's most secret act. The 1998 team fired its first salvo on 11 May, which in 1998 was again Buddha Purnima, and the renewed phrase announced continuity with a precision no press release could have achieved. A Congress prime minister's secret of 1974 was being cited, completed and surpassed by a BJP prime minister in 1998, and the citation was itself the political act: the tests were presented, in the movement's own symbolic language, as the nation's inheritance redeemed rather than a party's novelty.

The cultural articulation ran wider than one code phrase. The operation's codename, Shakti, named the feminine divine energy of the Hindu tradition, a choice that required no explanation to an Indian audience and that the government neither hid nor glossed. Party workers and affiliated organisations marked 11 May with public celebration, and BJP president Kushabhau Thakre declared, in statements reported in the Congressional Record's survey of the Indian press, that the tests showed a government that would not give in to international pressure. The critics of the tests heard the same grammar and named it as their central objection. Achin Vanaik, writing on the decision in essays later gathered in his work on Indian nuclear politics, argued that the BJP's advocacy of the bomb long predated any Chinese or Pakistani capability and flowed from an ideological project of national virility, the militarisation of Hindu selfhood, which the Jan Sangh had demanded from the 1950s. Praful Bidwai, the most persistent journalistic critic of the programme, charged in his columns of May and June 1998 that the government's language of civilisational assertion exposed the tests as an exercise in majoritarian symbolism dressed as security policy. These charges are part of the record, and the evenhanded statement of them matters to the adjudication below. What neither the government's celebrants nor its critics disputed was the fact of the articulation itself: the tests were announced, celebrated and attacked inside a cultural vocabulary that a purely strategic act would not have required, and the choice of Buddha Purnima, maintained across twenty-four years and two opposed parties, marks the vocabulary as a property of the Indian state's own political grammar, available to the movement in office and legible to its opponents. The pillar framework for separating the strands of Hindutva as ideology, treated in [the tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/) and in the history of [the post-1989 explicit-Hindutva phase](/2019/10/05/1980-founding-bjp-explained/), supplies the categories for that legibility: doctrinal Hindutva claimed the tests as civilisational assertion, while the governing formation presented them, through Vajpayee's restraint of language, as national statecraft. Both presentations operated at once, and the doubleness was the articulation's strength.

The communal label that attached itself to the tests abroad and at home tested the articulation's limits, and the government's handling of it belongs in this component. Commentators in Pakistan and some critics in India described the arsenal of May 1998 as a Hindu bomb, reaching for the governing party's ideology as the explanation of the state act; voices in Pakistan answered in the same register, claiming an Islamic bomb in reply, a phrase with its own genealogy in Zulfikar Ali Bhutto's rhetoric of the 1970s. The Indian government rejected the label in every formal statement, insisting that the programme was national, that its scientists included men and women of every Indian community, Kalam's own public standing as a Muslim scientist-statesman being the standing refutation the government's spokesmen deployed without needing to name it, and that the 1974 precedent under a Congress government proved the programme's ownership by the republic rather than by a movement. The rejection was more than courtesy. The framework's claim requires the articulation to be cultural in the civilisational register the movement claimed, the Buddha phrase, the festival, the Sanskrit codename, while remaining national in ownership, and the government's May statements policed exactly that boundary, celebrating the register and refusing the communal reduction. Critics such as Bidwai held that the boundary could not be policed, that the register was the reduction; celebrants held that the register was the nation's own inheritance. The dispute is recorded here at its strongest on both sides because the adjudication turns on it: an articulation legible to the whole country, contested in its meaning, is the signature of a political moment rather than of a party rally.

The articulation also disciplined the government's enemies in a way its authors understood. Because the 1974 inheritance belonged to a Congress prime minister, the opposition could not attack the tests as an alien import into Indian statecraft; because the symbolic register was civilisational rather than narrowly partisan, the government's supporters could celebrate in a vocabulary the whole country recognised; and because Vajpayee's formal language remained restrained, foreign governments found no triumphalist quotation with which to indict him. The disarmament critics saw the trap and named it: Bidwai argued in his columns that the restraint of the announcement was itself a technique, wrapping a majoritarian assertion in the grammar of national consensus so that opposition could be painted as disloyalty. That charge, too, belongs to the component's record, and it identifies the articulation's real function in the architecture. The Buddha phrase and the festival date did not merely decorate the tests. They assigned the tests a lineage, a mood and a national ownership that raised the price of dissent, which is what cultural articulation, in a political moment, is for.

## Component Three: The Secrecy Architecture and the Six Coordinators

The execution of Operation Shakti is the component where the political and the administrative fuse completely, because the secrecy that protected the tests was not a technical precaution but a governing method, designed in direct response to the failure of 1995. The decision circle, as Chengappa reconstructed it from the participants, consisted of the Prime Minister; Brajesh Mishra, who ran the channel between the Prime Minister's office, the scientific departments and the armed forces; Advani and Fernandes as the senior political witnesses; and the scientific pair, Chidambaram for the atomic energy establishment and Kalam for the defence research establishment and the range. Anil Kakodkar directed the thermonuclear design team inside BARC. Below that circle, knowledge was compartmented by function: the Army engineers who dug and lined the shafts did not know the firing date; the scientists who prepared the devices did not handle the political timetable; the diplomats who would have to defend the tests abroad, including the foreign secretary, K. Raghunath, were informed only at the edge of the event. India's ambassador in Washington, Naresh Chandra, learned of the tests as the world did. The compartmentation was total enough that American intelligence, which had read the 1995 preparations correctly, assessed the activity visible at Pokhran in the spring of 1998 as routine, a failure the United States Senate debated in public within days of the tests.

The American post-mortem on the failure is itself a document of the episode, and its findings belong to the coordination component because they certify, from the adversary's side, what the architecture achieved. Within weeks of the tests, the director of central intelligence, George Tenet, commissioned Admiral David Jeremiah, a former vice chairman of the joint chiefs of staff, to review the intelligence failure. Jeremiah's assessment, delivered in June 1998 and summarised publicly, declined to blame collection alone: the satellites had flown, the analysts had reported the activity they saw, and the system had still concluded that India would not test, because the prevailing assumption in Washington held that the new government in New Delhi would behave as its predecessors had, calculating costs as Washington calculated them. The failure, in Jeremiah's framing, was one of mindset, a mirror-imaging of Indian intentions. Indian commentary seized on the finding with understandable satisfaction, and the satisfaction had a rational core: the secrecy architecture had not merely hidden objects from cameras, it had exploited the observer's theory of Indian behaviour, the expectation that a coalition government of seven weeks would be too weak to act. The government's domestic opponents had traded on the same expectation, and its falsification, certified by the American review, redounded to the government's credit in both capitals at once. Coordination, measured by its effects on the minds it deceived, was the component in which the political and the operational were most completely the same act.

The tradecraft that defeated the satellites has been described by Chengappa and in the programme's own anniversary accounts, and its elements were mundane: work concentrated in the windows between satellite passes, whose timings the Indian side had studied; equipment moved under camouflage netting; cables, the tell-tale signature of a test site, buried and disguised; the scientists travelling in uniform and under codenames; decoy activity maintained at other locations on the range; and the final preparations timed to a sandstorm season in the Thar, when wind-blown sand degraded photographic reconnaissance. None of this was exotic. Its significance lies in the demonstration it performed for the domestic audience that learned of it over the following weeks, as the details leaked into the press: the Indian state, routinely described by its own citizens as leaky, slow and penetrated, had kept the largest secret of the decade against the most capable surveillance apparatus in existence. The demonstration fed directly into the positioning component. A coalition government accused by its opponents of improvisation had, within seven weeks, run a deception operation that fooled the Central Intelligence Agency, and the government's spokesmen did not scruple to let that fact circulate. When the United States Senate's intelligence overseers demanded to know how thirty billion dollars of intelligence spending had missed the preparations, the exchange itself became, in Indian commentary, a certificate of state competence. Coordination, on this reading, was not merely the means of the tests. It was one of their messages.

The firing chain of 11 May shows the architecture at its tightest. The devices, once emplaced, passed under the control of the range organisation: the firing party at the control point, the safety officers holding the interlocks, the diagnostic teams at their recorders, and, at the head of the chain, the two scientific coordinators who alone could certify readiness to the political authority. Chengappa's narrative dwells on the human texture of that afternoon, the heat inside the control facilities, the held breath of the count, the seismic trace rising on the instruments, and the coded confirmation travelling to New Delhi, where the Prime Minister waited with Advani, Fernandes, Yashwant Sinha and Mishra. On 13 May the pattern repeated at smaller scale, and the decision to stop at five, taken because the scientific coordinators certified that the programme's objectives stood met, closed the operation from inside its own logic. A government performing resolve could easily have fired a sixth device for effect. The refusal to do so, the subordination of spectacle to the coordinators' certificate, became part of the state's presentation of itself: India had tested as a bureaucracy, not as a mob, and the distinction was aimed at every chancellery weighing whether the new weapon state could be trusted with the standing it demanded.

## The Announcement, the Letter to Clinton and the Domestic Chorus

Vajpayee announced the tests himself, at a brief press conference at his residence on the afternoon of 11 May, reading a short statement that India had conducted three underground nuclear tests at Pokhran and that the planned programme would be completed with the shots of 13 May. The statement's restraint was calculated: it used no word of triumph, described the devices by type, and repeated India's commitment to global disarmament. The announcement's reception abroad supplied the government's first evidence that the secrecy architecture had worked at the level of perception as well as of fact. Talbott records in his memoir that the State Department learned of the tests from CNN, and that the intelligence bureau's chief announced the news inside the department as a bulletin from television: the most expensive surveillance establishment in history received the decade's largest South Asian event from a news channel, because a government it had discounted had hidden the event from its machines. President Clinton, travelling in Europe, condemned the tests in the language the wire services carried within the day, calling them deeply disappointing and urging Pakistan to refrain from reply. The sequence, Indian announcement first, American reaction second, Pakistani deliberation third, inverted the region's accustomed order, in which Washington's timetable had set the region's clock since 1971. Indian commentary registered the inversion with precision: for the first time in the republic's history, the country's government had created the fact to which the world was obliged to react, and the obligation itself was the standing the tests had been fired to claim. Two days later the government placed its fuller case before Parliament, and on 27 May 1998 Vajpayee addressed the Lok Sabha in the debate on the tests, declaring that India was a nuclear weapon state and that the weapons existed to deter, not to threaten. The parliamentary statement of 27 May, and the paper laid before the House, committed the government to the posture that became doctrine: no first use in the government's declared intent, a moratorium on further explosive testing once the series closed, and readiness to discuss the test ban treaty if the coercive architecture of 1996 was addressed. Each of these commitments was also a domestic signal, addressed to the opponents who had warned that the tests announced an arms race: the government's answer was that weaponisation was a settled, bounded act of state, not an open-ended programme of escalation.

The public presentation of the scientists formed the announcement's second act, and it had consequences beyond the season. On 17 May 1998, Chidambaram and Kalam appeared together before the press in New Delhi, the atomic energy chief and the defence research chief side by side, to state the yields, describe the devices' types and answer questions on the programme's intentions. The choreography put the two establishments on one platform and the two men, a Tamil physicist from the atomic energy tradition and a Muslim engineer from the missile programme, into a single frame that the newspapers carried across the country. Kalam's emergence in that season as a public figure of the first rank dates from the Pokhran podium: the scientific adviser who had worked in disguise in the desert became, within four years, the consensus candidate of the governing alliance and the opposition alike for the presidency of the republic, elected in July 2002 with the support of the BJP-led alliance and the Congress together. The trajectory is the domestic dividend of the tests in biographical form. A programme conducted in secrecy produced, as its most durable public artefact, a scientist-president whose standing rested on the authority the tests had conferred, and whose election the parties contested to claim rather than to prevent.

### What did Vajpayee's letter to Clinton say in May 1998?

The letter, sent on the day of the first tests and published on 13 May 1998, pleaded a deteriorating security environment, naming no state directly. It recalled a nuclear weapon state on India's borders that had committed aggression in 1962, and a neighbour armed through clandestine transfers and used as a conduit for terrorism against India.

The letter's indirection deceived nobody, and its publication, by leak, in the American press converted a private explanation into a public indictment of China, to Beijing's recorded anger. Its text, reproduced in the Indian press from The Economic Times of 14 May 1998 among other papers, is one of the mandatory documents of the episode: the Prime Minister wrote that he had been deeply concerned at the deteriorating security environment, especially the nuclear environment, faced by India for some years past; that an overt nuclear weapon state on India's borders had committed armed aggression against India in 1962; that the same state had materially helped a neighbour build its clandestine programme; and that the neighbour had itself committed three aggressions against India and sustained a decade of terrorism in Punjab and Kashmir. George Fernandes had made the China argument in public days before the tests, naming China as the principal threat in a television interview of early May, and the letter aligned the Prime Minister's private diplomacy with his defence minister's public candour. The Clinton administration, which received the letter while imposing sanctions, treated its publication as a provocation aimed at Beijing, and the episode fixed the diplomatic problem the Talbott dialogue would spend two years unwinding: India had explained itself to Washington in terms that antagonised the second capital whose assent any settlement would require.

One statement of that fortnight showed how quickly the tests migrated into other files. On 18 May 1998, Advani, speaking as home minister with charge of Kashmir affairs, declared that Pakistan should roll back its anti-India policy and take note of the changed geo-strategic situation in the region, language that Islamabad and several foreign capitals read as nuclear-backed pressure on the Kashmir question. The remark drew a formal protest from Pakistan and anxious commentary abroad, and the Prime Minister's office let it be known that the government's Kashmir position stood unchanged in substance. The episode is a small, perfect specimen of the architectural-political moment at work: the tests had altered the background of every Indian statement, so that a home minister's routine hardening of tone acquired a strategic echo nobody had scripted, and the government had to spend a measure of its new capital explaining that the echo was not the message. The parliamentary debate of 27 and 28 May supplied the controlled version of the same exchange. Vajpayee's statement committed the government to restraint, to the moratorium and to disarmament as the declared horizon; opposition speakers, Congress and Left together, pressed the costs, the arms race and the letter to Clinton, whose publication the opposition treated as a diplomatic blunder that had gratuitously antagonised Beijing; and the government's replies held the line that the tests had ended, not begun, an argument about India's status.

The domestic chorus that answered the announcement divided, predictably and instructively, into celebration, partisan reservation and principled opposition. The celebration was led by the governing parties and their affiliated organisations, and its leading note was civilisational confidence; editorial opinion in the major English-language dailies, The Times of India and The Hindu among them, ran strongly in favour in the first days, with The Hindu's editorial line welcoming the demonstration while warning on the arms race. The Congress party, out of office and led since March 1998 by Sonia Gandhi, occupied the most delicate ground: the capability was its own governments' creation, from 1974 through the option-keeping decades, and Congress spokesmen praised the scientists while charging the BJP with converting a national asset into partisan theatre and with mishandling the diplomatic consequences. The Left parties condemned the tests outright: the Communist Party of India (Marxist) Polit Bureau statement of May 1998 held that the tests would trigger a nuclear arms race in the subcontinent, damage India's standing as a voice for disarmament, and serve the BJP's ideological project rather than national security. The disarmament movement, small but articulate, spoke through Bidwai and Vanaik, through the scientists and writers who formed the Coalition for Nuclear Disarmament and Peace in 2000, and through statements by retired officials and officers, among them Admiral L. Ramdas, who had served as chief of naval staff, warning that weaponisation would make India less secure. Against all of this stood the strategic establishment's stated case, argued by K. Subrahmanyam in his columns of May 1998 and by the serving officials in their briefings: that a state facing a nuclear-armed China and a clandestinely armed Pakistan could not rest its security on ambiguity, that the 1996 treaty architecture was an attempt to foreclose India's option by law what pressure had failed to foreclose in fact, and that the tests, once absorbed, would raise rather than lower India's weight in every negotiation that mattered.

### What did the tests do to Vajpayee's standing at home?

The surveys published in May and June 1998 recorded approval of the tests from overwhelming majorities of respondents, and the Prime Minister's personal ratings rose to the highest levels of his tenure. A government that had entered office on a negotiated agenda acquired, within two months, a reputation for decision, and the reputation survived the sanctions debate that followed.

The electoral ledger complicates any simple claim of dividend, and the complication should be entered honestly, because it refines rather than refutes the positioning argument. In the state assembly elections of November 1998, six months after the tests, the BJP lost Delhi to the Congress under Sheila Dikshit, lost Rajasthan to the Congress under Ashok Gehlot, and failed to dislodge Digvijaya Singh's Congress government in Madhya Pradesh, a round of defeats the party's own post-mortems attributed to prices, candidate selection and state-level incumbency rather than to any national question. Commentators who had forecast a Pokhran wave recorded its absence, and the opposition claimed that the country had already priced the tests and moved on to the cost of onions, the issue that dominated the Delhi campaign. The framework absorbs the result without strain, because the positioning the tests achieved was never a promise of votes at every election: it was a settlement of the government's authority, of who could act for the nation and be believed. The November verdicts show the settlement's limits precisely. Authority in the security register did not transfer to the economic register, and a government that had mastered the politics of the arsenal still had to survive the politics of prices, which in April 1999 it very nearly did not.

The durability of that rise can be tested against the record: the government that tested in May 1998 lost its majority in April 1999 by a single vote, on a motion unconnected to nuclear policy, and returned to office in the election of September and October 1999 at the head of an enlarged alliance, an outcome treated in the account of [the 1999 Vajpayee victory](/2019/06/17/1999-vajpayee-victory-reanalysed/). The tests had not made the coalition immortal. They had made the Prime Minister its indispensable centre, and they had fixed the template of the Vajpayee persona, the moderate custodian of immoderate capabilities, that structured Indian politics until 2004 and that later governments inherited as the normal style of Indian statecraft. The persona's architecture, moderation of manner joined to firmness of programme, is the same renegotiation of the founding tensions that the series framework traces in [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), first assembled at Pokhran.

## The Yield Quarrel: Forty-Five Kilotons Claimed and the Record Contested

Every claim in this article about positioning, symbolism and coordination rests on an event that happened; the yield quarrel concerns what, precisely, the event proved, and the quarrel must be stated with the attributions the record carries, because the figures remain genuinely disputed between named holders. The Indian official position, stated by Chidambaram and Kalam on 17 May 1998 and defended by the Department of Atomic Energy and BARC in technical papers over the following years, holds that the three devices of 11 May performed to design: the thermonuclear device yielded forty-five kilotons in total, its fusion stage contributing about thirty; the fission device fifteen; the sub-kiloton device a fraction of one; and that radiochemical analysis of samples drilled from the test site, the measurement of the crater and cavity, and Indian seismic readings together confirmed the figures. The total claimed yield for 11 May stood at about fifty-eight to sixty kilotons across the three devices.

The outside estimates ran lower from the first week. Seismologists working from open international networks, among them Terry Wallace of the University of Arizona, whose analysis circulated in the scientific press in 1998, computed a combined yield for the 11 May salvo nearer twenty-five to thirty kilotons, roughly half the Indian claim, and several specialists concluded that the thermonuclear device's fusion stage had burned only partially. The Indian establishment answered that the Pokhran geology, hard rock against the softer media on which Western calibration rested, distorted the seismic arithmetic, and that the radiochemical evidence, which only the testing state possessed, settled the question. P. K. Iyengar, a former chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission who had worked on the 1974 device, broke with the official position in print, questioning whether the thermonuclear claim was proven and arguing that the fusion stage's contribution looked small on the published data.

The dispute had a prehistory that its participants knew and the public debate largely forgot. The 1974 device had travelled the same road: the official figure of about twelve kilotons, the lower outside seismic estimates, the atomic energy leadership's insistence that its radiochemistry was conclusive, and, across the decades, the private acknowledgements by figures of that generation, Iyengar among them in his later interviews, that the announced numbers of 1974 had been generous readings of ambiguous data. When BARC's scientists published their technical defence of the 1998 figures in the journals, in papers setting out the radiochemical assay method and the cavity measurements, they wrote inside a tradition in which the testing state's numbers were simultaneously scientific claims and instruments of state. The sceptics wrote inside the counter-tradition of open seismology, whose practitioners, Wallace and others, had built their authority on reading other states' tests from public data. Neither tradition could audit the other on its own ground, since the radiochemical samples never left Indian custody and the seismic stations never entered the shafts. The quarrel's persistence across thirty-five years and two test series is therefore structural, and the 2009 eruption was its third act rather than a new play: 1974's argument, repeated in 1998, repeated again when the treaty politics of 2009 gave it a use.

### Why do the yields of the Pokhran II devices remain disputed?

Only the testing state holds the radiochemical samples from the shafts, while outside seismologists hold the open seismic record, and the two evidentiary bases point to different totals. Indian radiochemistry supports the claimed forty-five kilotons for the thermonuclear device; several Western seismic computations place the combined salvo far lower, and no independent inspection can reconcile them.

The quarrel reopened with force in August 2009, when K. Santhanam, who had directed test site preparations in 1998 as the DRDO's representative at the range, stated publicly, first in interviews of 26 and 27 August 2009 and then in an article in The Hindu of 17 September 2009, that the thermonuclear device had underperformed badly, that its yield was much lower than claimed, and that India should therefore refuse to sign the test ban treaty, since further tests remained a scientific necessity. Santhanam's intervention carried weight because he was the first member of the 1998 test leadership to echo the sceptics, and his argument was explicitly political in form: a deterrent whose largest device was unproven, he held, was not credible. The rebuttals came from the whole leadership of 1998. Kalam stated that the devices had performed as designed and that the deterrent was credible. Chidambaram, by then principal scientific adviser to the government, told The Hindu that those questioning the yield had an obligation to back the charge with scientific evidence, and pointed to the published radiochemical record. Kakodkar restated the BARC position in interviews of the same season. The government of the day declined to reopen the question. The measurement problem at the quarrel's heart can be stated plainly, and stating it shows why neither side could simply win. A seismic station measures ground motion, from which yield is inferred through formulas calibrated on the geology of the test sites where the calibrating power conducted its own shots, principally the Nevada desert's volcanic tuff and the Kazakh steppe. Pokhran's hard rock couples an explosion's energy into the ground differently, and Indian seismologists argued from 1974 onward that applying Nevada calibrations to Thar geology systematically understates Indian yields. Radiochemistry inverts the problem: samples drilled back into the cavity carry the fission products and fusion signatures of the shot itself, a direct record no outsider can inspect, which makes the method conclusive for its holder and unverifiable for everyone else. Chidambaram's published defence rested on the sodium-22 and manganese-54 activity in the samples, isotopes he identified as fingerprints of the fusion stage; Santhanam's rejoinder, carried in his 2009 article, disputed what those traces proved and pointed to the physical state of the shaft after the shot. A lay reader cannot settle the exchange, and this article does not pretend to. What can be settled is the sociology of it: the state held the samples, the sceptics held the seismograms, and each side's evidence was strongest exactly where the other side could not look. Disputes of that structure do not end; they are managed, and the management of this one, by rebuttal, by silence and finally by the passage of the treaty argument that had animated it, is part of the political history of the tests.

The honest summary, and the one the stance charter requires, is that the thermonuclear yield of 11 May 1998 is a disputed point between named experts holding different classes of evidence, and that the dispute matters for deterrence doctrine without touching the political analysis defended here: the architectural-political moment was constituted by the decision, the announcement and the world's reception of India as a declared weapon state, all of which occurred whatever figure the fusion stage finally reached.

Two features of the quarrel deserve record because they show the dispute's own politics. The first is the timing of Santhanam's intervention. He spoke in August 2009, as the administration of President Barack Obama pressed for the test ban treaty's ratification in Washington and as Indian commentators debated whether the moratorium of 1998 would harden into a permanent legal bar. Santhanam framed his charge accordingly: not as memoir, but as a warning against signature, with the deterrent's credibility as the stake. His critics answered in kind, treating the charge itself as a manoeuvre in the treaty argument, and the exchange demonstrated that the yield question had become, by 2009, a proxy in the argument over whether India's 1998 settlement should be reopened. The second feature is the position taken by the strategic analysts who defended the tests in 1998. K. Subrahmanyam, the most authoritative of them, argued across the controversy that deterrence did not stand or fall on the thermonuclear figure: a proven fission arsenal, deliverable by aircraft and missile, sufficed for the credible minimum the doctrine proposed, and the thermonuclear design was an option on the future rather than the foundation of the present. The defence is instructive for the adjudication below, because it concedes, from within the strategic reading itself, that the political and deterrent effects of May 1998 never depended on the largest number in the yield table.

## Component Four: Sanctions Priced in Advance and Absorbed

The Glenn Amendment to the Arms Export Control Act, enacted in 1994, left President Clinton no discretion: on 13 May 1998 the United States imposed the mandatory sanctions, terminating development assistance other than humanitarian aid, ending military sales and financing, barring credits and guarantees from American government agencies, prohibiting loans by American banks to the Indian government, restricting dual-use technology exports, and committing Washington to oppose loans to India in the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund. Japan, India's largest bilateral donor, froze new yen loans and grant aid. The G-8, meeting at Birmingham on 15 to 17 May 1998, condemned the tests and agreed to postpone consideration of lending to India in the international financial institutions. The United Nations Security Council, in Resolution 1172 of 6 June 1998, condemned the Indian and Pakistani tests and demanded that both states join the non-proliferation treaty as non-weapon states, a demand New Delhi rejected as addressed to the wrong party: India had signed no treaty that the tests violated. Pakistan's answer to Pokhran, six devices at Chagai on 28 and 30 May 1998 on Islamabad's count, completed the symmetry of condemnation and placed both states under the same sanctions regime.

The sanctions coalition was never complete, and its gaps carried a message the government read carefully. France, alone among the Western powers, declined to impose penalties, contenting itself with statements of regret and preserving its commercial and strategic dialogue with New Delhi. Russia criticised the tests in form and continued business in substance, signing the supplementary protocol for the Kudankulam nuclear power station with India on 21 June 1998, six weeks after the detonations, as if to demonstrate that the condemnation market had more than one price. China condemned the tests and the letter that had justified them, and conducted no economic retaliation of consequence. The pattern taught the lesson the government had predicted in its pre-test assessments: the mandatory American penalties were the hard core of the price, the Japanese freeze a real second element, and the rest a diplomacy of statement that endurance and counter-diplomacy could outlast. Ministers said as much in the parliamentary debate, distinguishing between the sanctions that bit and the communiqués that merely sounded, and the distinction held up well enough against the record of the following eighteen months.

The government's domestic handling of the sanctions is the component's core, and it must be kept within its boundary: the economic management of the sanctions years, the Resurgent India Bonds of August 1998 and the stabilisation that followed, belongs to the sanctions-response treatment elsewhere in this series, and enters here only as political fact. The political fact is that the sanctions had been priced before they were imposed. The 1995 precedent had shown the government exactly which penalties the Glenn Amendment carried; the finance ministry under Yashwant Sinha had assessed the exposure, modest for an economy with low dependence on American assistance but real in multilateral lending; and the Cabinet proceeded in the knowledge that the bill would arrive. When it arrived, the government converted absorption into argument. Ministers stated that a nation which could not withstand sanctions for its security had mortgaged that security in advance; the rupee's slide and the postponement of World Bank lending were presented as the cost of sovereignty rather than the wages of error; and the partial waivers enacted by the United States Congress in November 1998, under the Brownback Amendment carried by Senator Sam Brownback of Kansas, were received in New Delhi as evidence that the endurance strategy was working. The sanctions regime eroded thereafter by stages, through further waivers in 1999 and effective termination by the Bush administration in September 2001.

The erosion had a domestic lobby on the American side, and its existence shaped the component's politics in New Delhi. American business organisations, grouped around the United States India Business Council and the farm-state exporters who sold wheat and lentils into the Indian market, pressed Congress through 1998 for the waiver authority the Brownback Amendment supplied, arguing that mandatory sanctions punished American exporters while leaving India's security policy untouched. Indian industry bodies, the Confederation of Indian Industry and the Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry among them, carried the mirror argument in Washington and at home: their public statements backed the government's decision while lobbying for the penalties' removal, a combination that let New Delhi present the sanctions as an American domestic problem rather than a verdict of the world. Japan's position, the hardest among the sanctioning states in the first months, softened on the same schedule. The government in New Delhi read the waivers, correctly, as the market's verdict overtaking the legislatures' verdict, and banked the reading as further proof that endurance had been the right price. None of this diminishes the real costs recorded in the economic treatment of the sanctions years; it establishes that the costs were politically survivable, which is the only property the framework requires of them. The component's contribution to the framework is the demonstration of resolve by absorption: the tests positioned the government at home partly through the spectacle of a price accepted without flinching, a spectacle whose audience was domestic before it was foreign.

The multilateral diplomacy of the same months ran on a parallel track that the government worked with equal deliberation. The five recognised weapon states, meeting as foreign ministers at Geneva on 4 June 1998, issued a joint communiqué condemning the tests and setting conditions for the two testing states, and Resolution 1172 carried the same demands into the Security Council two days later. India's reply, delivered by its permanent representative at the United Nations, rejected the demands as ultra vires in substance: the Council, New Delhi argued, could not legislate the surrender of rights by states that had accepted no such obligation, and the resolution's silence on the arsenals of its own permanent members disqualified its moral premise. India then carried its case to the Non-Aligned Movement, whose twelfth summit at Durban in September 1998 declined to condemn India by name and repeated the movement's call for time-bound disarmament, an outcome the foreign ministry banked as proof that the condemnation of May had been a great-power chorus rather than a world verdict. Vajpayee's address to the General Assembly that September completed the sequence, restating the voluntary moratorium, offering the no-first-use posture, and presenting India as a weapon state that had accepted, unilaterally, the disciplines the treaty order had failed to negotiate with it. The diplomatic campaign did not lift a single sanction. Its function was positional in the framework's sense: it held India's standing among the states whose assent the eventual settlement would not require but whose acquiescence it would, and it kept the argument about 1998 framed as an argument about the order's fairness, the ground on which India has never lost a debate it was allowed to join.

## Component Five: The Long Corridor from the Talbott Dialogue to the Settlement of 2008

The fifth component carries the longest fuse. Within weeks of the tests, the Clinton administration appointed Deputy Secretary of State Strobe Talbott as its interlocutor, and the Vajpayee government answered with Jaswant Singh, first as the Prime Minister's special envoy and from December 1998 as minister of external affairs. Between June 1998 and September 2000 the two men met fourteen times in seven countries on three continents, the most extensive dialogue the two states had ever conducted, reconstructed from the American side in Talbott's Engaging India (2004) and from the Indian side in Jaswant Singh's own writings. The American mandate was restraint: signature of the test ban treaty, a fissile material cap, export controls, and restraint in deployment. The Indian mandate was acceptance: recognition that India was a nuclear weapon state in fact, that its security concerns were legitimate, and that the relationship should be rebuilt on that recognition rather than on India's capitulation. Talbott's own verdict, printed in his memoir, grants that Jaswant Singh came closer to his objective than Talbott came to his. India signed nothing, deployed cautiously, announced its voluntary moratorium, published a draft nuclear doctrine through the National Security Advisory Board on 17 August 1999, proposing credible minimum deterrence, no first use and civilian command, adopted the doctrine's essentials in the Cabinet Committee on Security's statement of 4 January 2003, and received President Clinton in March 2000 in the first visit by an American president since 1978. Readers who keep the records of that diplomatic season in presentation form will find the [online presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) a convenient instrument for holding the sequence of statements, communiqués and doctrine papers in one view.

The dialogue's agenda shows the corridor's gradient. Washington arrived with the benchmarks the weapon states had set after the tests: signature of the test ban treaty, a halt to fissile material production for weapons pending a cutoff treaty, restraint in the deployment of missiles and nuclear-capable aircraft, export controls aligned with the supplier regimes, and, after Kargil, respect for the Line of Control as the region's operative fact. New Delhi's method across the fourteen meetings was to concede the periphery and hold the centre: the moratorium on testing was declared and kept; export controls were tightened by Indian law and practice; the doctrine draft of 1999 advertised restraint in deployment; and on the centre, signature and the fissile cap, India conceded nothing, offering instead the argument that a state which had accepted every discipline in substance could not be required to accept the forms that denied its status. The method's domestic management was as careful as its diplomacy. Jaswant Singh reported the dialogue's progress to Parliament and to the press in formulations that committed the government to nothing it had not already declared, and the opposition, which had attacked the tests, found the defence of the dialogue's centre ground, no signature under pressure, to be a position it could not attack without attacking the sovereignty claim on which its own nuclear inheritance rested. The corridor thus ran through the Lok Sabha as surely as through the seven capitals, and its bipartisan floor in New Delhi was one of the assets Jaswant Singh carried into every room, as Talbott's memoir acknowledges in its account of the Indian negotiator's patience.

The corridor was load-tested in war before it reached its destination, and the test of 1999 is part of the component. In May 1999, Indian forces discovered Pakistani regulars and militants holding the heights above Kargil, and the two declared weapon states fought a limited mountain war under the nuclear shadow the tests had cast. The Vajpayee government held its forces on the Indian side of the Line of Control, accepting tactical cost for strategic effect, and the effect arrived on 4 July 1999, when President Clinton received Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif in Washington and required Pakistan's withdrawal to the Line of Control as the price of any American engagement. Talbott's memoir treats the Kargil crisis as the dialogue's vindication: the channel built to manage the consequences of the tests became the channel through which the United States pressed India's adversary, because India's conduct, restraint in war joined to patience in negotiation, had made it the partner Washington could reward. The war also completed the tests' domestic circuit. The government that had detonated in May 1998 fought in May 1999 as a declared nuclear power exercising conspicuous restraint, and the combination, capability with discipline, became the settled Indian self-description that the 2005 negotiators carried into the settlement talks.

The corridor also built the command architecture that a declared arsenal required, and the building was domestic politics in institutional form. The National Security Council, promised in the National Agenda and demanded by strategic writers for decades, was constituted in November 1998, with Mishra as the first national security adviser, converting the improvised channel of the test decision into a standing office. The doctrine process of 1999, the advisory board's draft released for public argument, was itself a political instrument without precedent in Indian security practice: a state accustomed to silence about its arsenal invited its citizens to debate the terms on which the arsenal would be used, and the debate, in the press and the journals, normalised the arsenal as an object of democratic discussion rather than of official mystery. The Cabinet Committee on Security's statement of 4 January 2003 then closed the founding sequence, announcing the Nuclear Command Authority, the political council chaired by the prime minister alone empowered to authorise use, and the Strategic Forces Command to administer the assets. Each element answered a question the tests had posed in May 1998 about the kind of weapon state India would be, and each answer, civilian command, declared doctrine, public argument, was chosen with the domestic audience in view as surely as with the foreign one.

The corridor also had a middle station that belongs to the Vajpayee government itself, and it is often skipped in accounts that jump from 1998 to 2005. In January 2004, in the last months of Vajpayee's tenure, New Delhi and Washington announced the Next Steps in Strategic Partnership, an agreed framework for expanding cooperation in civilian nuclear activity, civilian space programmes and high-technology trade, the three files the sanctions era had frozen. The partnership document conceded in practice what the Talbott dialogue had argued in principle: that the United States would build its India policy around the fact of Indian capability rather than around the demand for its surrender, and that the remaining quarrels would be managed as licensing and safeguards questions inside a partnership rather than as conditions imposed from outside it. The government that signed the framework was the government that had tested, and its negotiators, Mishra's successors in the national security apparatus among them, presented the outcome at home as the corridor's arrival at its first platform. The settlement of 2005 to 2008, negotiated by a Congress-led government that had opposed the tests' theatre in 1998, extended the platform into the full structure, and the continuity across the change of government is the framework's final evidence: the architectural-political moment of 1998 had built something its authors' opponents chose to inhabit rather than demolish.

The corridor's far end was the settlement negotiated by the next governments: the joint statement of 18 July 2005 between President George W. Bush and Prime Minister Manmohan Singh, which promised civil nuclear cooperation; the Hyde Act of December 2006 in the United States Congress; the safeguards agreement with the International Atomic Energy Agency in August 2008; the Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver of 6 September 2008; and the congressional approval of October 2008 that completed the architecture. The domestic convulsion that settlement caused in India, the Left's withdrawal of support and the confidence vote of 22 July 2008, is treated in the account of [the 2008 nuclear deal rupture](/2019/06/28/2008-indo-us-nuclear-deal-rupture/), and its detail belongs there. What belongs here is the line of causation the fifth component asserts: none of the 2005 to 2008 instruments would have been negotiable with a state that had never declared its capability, and the negotiating posture India carried into those rooms, patience, refusal of the treaty's terms as offered, insistence on recognition as the price of cooperation, was assembled in the Talbott corridor between 1998 and 2000. The tests created the fact; the dialogue converted the fact into standing; the settlement cashed the standing. That conversion is unintelligible as strategy alone, because the strategy was static between 1998 and 2008 while the standing moved; it is intelligible as politics, the slow renegotiation of what other states were prepared to say about India in public. The general theory of that renegotiation, foreign policy conducted as an extension of domestic settlement, is the framework of [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/), of which Pokhran II is the hardest test case, and the comparison with the earlier consolidation that followed a victorious war, in [the domestic-consolidation-architecture framework](/2020/10/02/1971-bangladesh-war-indira-moment/), shows the template's range: Indira Gandhi converted 1971 into a decade of personal authority, while Vajpayee converted 1998 into a settlement his successors, of the opposite party, carried to completion.

## Adjudicating the Three Readings: Strategy, Technology and the Political Moment

Three readings of May 1998 stand in the literature, and the charter of this series requires the disagreement to be adjudicated on the evidence rather than surveyed. The strategic reading, held by the Indian strategic establishment and argued in print by K. Subrahmanyam and by the official briefings of May 1998, holds that the tests answered a deteriorating environment: a nuclear-armed China that had transferred missile and warhead technology to Pakistan, a Pakistan whose Ghauri missile test of April 1998 advertised its reach, and a treaty order moving to foreclose India's answer by law. Its strongest evidence is the documentary chain of the letter to Clinton, Fernandes's statements and the treaty's entry-into-force clause. Its weakness is timing: the environment of April 1998 differed in degree, not in kind, from that of 1988 or 1993, and no new threat of those weeks explains why a government of seven weeks acted when stronger governments had waited.

The technological reading, held in different registers by the scientific establishment's celebrants and by its sceptics, holds that the tests were the scientific programme's own culmination: designs validated, a thermonuclear device fired at last, simulation capability secured against the coming ban. Chengappa's narrative gives this reading its richest form, following the BARC and DRDO teams across decades. Its weakness is the yield record itself: on the disputed figures, the technological culmination is the least secure element of the whole event, and the reading cannot explain why a programme ready in essence by 1995 waited for a particular party, a particular manifesto and a particular month.

The architectural-political-moment reading, defended here, holds that the tests were the act by which a specific government, at a specific point in its own construction, converted a national capability into political architecture: coalition positioning, cultural articulation, demonstrated administrative competence, priced defiance and a decade-long negotiating corridor. Perkovich's sceptical scholarship supports the reading's core from outside it, since his explanation of the timing is domestic: the configuration of party, lobby and ambition in the spring of 1998. A fourth voice deserves entry before the adjudication closes, because it rebuilt the strategic reading in institutional form. Ashley Tellis, in his RAND study India's Emerging Nuclear Posture (2001), argued that the significance of 1998 lay less in the devices than in the force posture India would assemble around them, and he pressed New Delhi toward a ready, survivable arsenal on the reasoning that declared status without operational readiness invited the pressures the tests were meant to end. The Indian debate answered him from both directions: official writing held to the recessed, de-mated posture as a virtue of the Indian model, while serving officers and analysts of Tellis's persuasion treated readiness as the unfinished business of Pokhran. The exchange matters here because it shows the strategic reading, in its strongest form, also migrating onto political ground: arguments about posture were arguments about what kind of state India intended to be, conducted before domestic and foreign audiences, with the 1998 moment as their shared premise. Even deterrence, pursued rigorously, terminated in the politics of self-description.

The strongest counterargument, stated fairly, is that the political reading underweights deterrence: whatever the domestic dividends, the tests also built the physical basis of a survivable Indian deterrent, and the deterrent's existence has structured every crisis since, from Kargil in 1999 onward. The answer given by the framework is that the counterargument concedes the framework's point. The deterrent's strategic effects ran through domestic and diplomatic channels, the doctrine debates of 1999 and 2003, the Talbott corridor, the 2008 settlement, each of them political processes conducted by named actors before domestic audiences; strategy supplied the hardware, and politics decided what the hardware meant. The comparison of adjudicative scale is the one this series applies to its constitutional subjects as well, in [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/): an act is architectural when its consequences are produced less by its immediate content than by the settlements it makes possible. Judged by that standard, the five devices of May 1998 were the Vajpayee government's architectural-political moment, and the framework that names its five components captures what the event, in fact, operationalised.

The counterfactual disciplines the claim, and it should be faced directly. Would another government have tested in 1998? The record supplies the materials for an answer. A Congress government had prepared a test in 1995 and drawn back under pressure, which shows the capability and the temptation crossing party lines; the United Front governments of 1996 to 1998, dependent on Left support and committed to the disarmament rhetoric of the Gujral years, showed no movement toward a test in their twenty-two months; and the BJP's manifesto, its forty-year ideological lineage on the question and its new government's need for a founding act were properties of one party at one moment. The fair conclusion is that a test sometime in the closing window was possible under more than one government, while the test of May 1998, declared openly, framed culturally, defended as civilisational inheritance and converted into a decade of negotiation, was the act of this government alone. The architectural-political-moment reading claims exactly that specificity and no more. It does not argue that strategy was absent or that technology was incidental. It argues that the event's consequences, the coalition's consolidation, the persona of its prime minister, the doctrine debate, the corridor to 2008, flowed from the political form the government gave the explosion, and that the form, not the yield, is what a history of Indian politics is required to explain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When did India conduct Pokhran II tests?

India conducted the Pokhran II tests on 11 and 13 May 1998 at the Pokhran test range in the Thar desert of Rajasthan, under the codename Operation Shakti. Three devices were detonated together on 11 May, a thermonuclear device, a fission device and a sub-kiloton device, and two further sub-kiloton devices followed on 13 May, after which Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee declared the planned series complete. The first salvo fell on Buddha Purnima, the same festival day on which India had fired its first device at Pokhran on 18 May 1974, twenty-four years earlier almost to the day.

### Q: Who was R. Chidambaram in 1998?

Rajagopala Chidambaram was chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission and secretary of the Department of Atomic Energy in 1998, the senior scientist of the Indian nuclear programme and the official who held overall technical responsibility for the Pokhran II devices designed at the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre. A crystallographer by training, he had worked on the 1974 Pokhran device as a young physicist, and in 1998 he co-coordinated the test campaign with A. P. J. Abdul Kalam of the DRDO. He presented the official yield figures with Kalam at the New Delhi press conference of 17 May 1998 and defended them against critics for decades afterward.

### Q: What is the Buddha Smiles Again reference?

The reference is to the code message by which success at Pokhran was reported to the Prime Minister. The message sent after the first Indian test of 18 May 1974, fired on the festival of Buddha Purnima, announced that the Buddha was smiling, and the 1974 operation took the codename Smiling Buddha. When the first salvo of Operation Shakti detonated on 11 May 1998, again Buddha Purnima, the confirmation passed to Vajpayee's residence carried the renewed form of the phrase, that the Buddha had smiled again. The words announced continuity: the programme Indira Gandhi's government had opened in secrecy in 1974 stood completed, in the open, by the government of 1998.

### Q: Who coordinated the Pokhran II tests?

Coordination ran through a small circle joining political authority to the two scientific establishments. Brajesh Mishra, principal secretary to the Prime Minister and national security adviser, ran the channel between the Prime Minister's office, the departments and the armed forces. R. Chidambaram, chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission, held technical responsibility for the devices, with Anil Kakodkar, director of the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, leading the thermonuclear design team. A. P. J. Abdul Kalam, scientific adviser to the defence minister and secretary of the DRDO, co-coordinated the campaign and managed the range and instrumentation, while K. Santhanam directed test site preparations and the Army's engineers prepared the shafts.

### Q: How did sanctions affect India after Pokhran II?

The United States imposed the mandatory Glenn Amendment sanctions on 13 May 1998: development assistance ended outside humanitarian aid, military sales and financing stopped, American government credits and guarantees were barred, American banks could not lend to the Indian government, dual-use technology exports were restricted, and Washington voted against Indian loans in the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund. Japan froze new yen loans and grant aid, and the G-8 postponed lending decisions. The effect on India was real but bounded, because American assistance was small and the economy's dependence on official credit was limited. Congress began waiving the penalties in November 1998, and the regime eroded until its effective termination in September 2001.

### Q: What was the post-1998 Jaswant Singh Strobe Talbott dialogue?

The dialogue was the extended negotiation through which India and the United States rebuilt their relationship after the tests. Deputy Secretary of State Strobe Talbott, appointed by President Clinton as his interlocutor, met Jaswant Singh, first the Prime Minister's special envoy and from December 1998 the minister of external affairs, fourteen times between June 1998 and September 2000, in seven countries on three continents. Washington sought Indian signature of the test ban treaty, fissile restraint and deployment restraint. New Delhi sought acceptance of India as a nuclear weapon state in fact. India signed nothing and conceded no cap, while declaring a voluntary testing moratorium, and Talbott's memoir, Engaging India (2004), grants that Jaswant Singh came closer to his objective.

### Q: How did Pokhran II shape the 2008 nuclear deal?

The tests created the fact on which the deal of 2008 was negotiated. Because India had declared and demonstrated its capability in 1998, and had then spent a decade conducting itself as a responsible possessor, the United States moved from punishing the programme to accommodating it: the Bush-Singh joint statement of 18 July 2005 promised civil nuclear cooperation, the Hyde Act of December 2006 permitted it in American law, India accepted a separation plan and an IAEA safeguards agreement, and the Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver of 6 September 2008 opened nuclear commerce to a state outside the non-proliferation treaty. Without the declared arsenal of 1998 there would have been nothing to accommodate.

### Q: What did the BJP manifesto of 1998 promise on nuclear weapons?

The Bharatiya Janata Party's manifesto for the general election of February and March 1998 promised that a BJP government would re-evaluate India's nuclear policy and exercise the option to induct nuclear weapons. The formulation was deliberate: it committed the party to ending the posture of ambiguity that every government since 1974 had maintained, without naming a test or a date. The demand itself was old, reaching back to the Bharatiya Jana Sangh's calls for an Indian bomb in the 1950s and 1960s, but the manifesto converted it into an electoral commitment by a party on the edge of power. The softer National Agenda for Governance, negotiated with the allies in March 1998, spoke of security review, and the tests of May were read against both documents.

### Q: What happened when India prepared to test in December 1995?

The government of P. V. Narasimha Rao prepared a nuclear test at Pokhran in December 1995, and American intelligence detected the preparations in satellite photographs of activity at the range. The United States ambassador in New Delhi, Frank Wisner, confronted the Indian government with the evidence, and Washington made clear that a test would trigger the Glenn Amendment sanctions. Rao, whose government was in its final phase and unwilling to spend its remaining authority on the confrontation, stood the preparations down. The episode, reconstructed by Raj Chengappa in Weapons of Peace (2000) and confirmed in its essentials by American officials, taught the teams of 1998 their governing lessons: preparations were visible from orbit, and a decision discussed in advance could be pressured into reversal.

### Q: What did George Fernandes say about China in May 1998?

George Fernandes, the defence minister in the Vajpayee government and leader of the Samata Party, stated in a television interview in early May 1998, days before the tests, that China was India's principal threat, in his phrase the potential threat number one. He pointed to Chinese assistance to Pakistan's nuclear and missile programmes, to Chinese military infrastructure in Tibet, and to Chinese activity in Myanmar's Coco Islands as the grounds of his assessment. The statement broke the diplomatic convention by which Indian ministers avoided naming China in public, and it prepared the security argument that Vajpayee's letter to President Clinton then made in private. Beijing protested the characterisation formally, and the exchange shadowed the diplomacy that followed the tests.

### Q: What did the draft nuclear doctrine of August 1999 propose?

The draft doctrine, released by the National Security Advisory Board on 17 August 1999 under the convenorship of K. Subrahmanyam, proposed that India's nuclear forces rest on credible minimum deterrence: a posture of no first use, retaliation only after a nuclear attack on India or its forces, a triad of delivery systems based on aircraft, mobile land missiles and sea-based assets, civilian political command through the prime minister, and a commitment to global disarmament as the doctrine's stated horizon. The Vajpayee government released the draft without adopting it formally, inviting public debate, and the Cabinet Committee on Security adopted its essentials, with modifications on retaliation, in the official statement of 4 January 2003.

### Q: What did K. Santhanam claim about the thermonuclear device in 2009?

K. Santhanam, who had directed test site preparations at Pokhran as the DRDO's representative in 1998, stated in interviews of August 2009 and in an article in The Hindu of 17 September 2009 that the thermonuclear device of 11 May 1998 had underperformed badly, its yield much lower than the forty-five kilotons officially claimed, and that India therefore lacked a proven thermonuclear deterrent and must refuse to sign the test ban treaty, since further tests remained necessary. His was the first such statement from inside the 1998 test leadership. Kalam, Chidambaram and Kakodkar each rebutted it, holding that the device had performed to design and that radiochemical measurements from the site confirmed the claimed yield.

### Q: What did Security Council Resolution 1172 demand in June 1998?

Resolution 1172, adopted by the United Nations Security Council on 6 June 1998, condemned the nuclear tests conducted by India in May and by Pakistan in reply, demanded that both states refrain from further tests, and called on both to join the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty without delay and without conditions, as non-nuclear-weapon states under the treaty's definitions. It further demanded that both halt their weapon and missile programmes and refrain from deploying nuclear weapons. India rejected the resolution's operative demands, holding that the Council could not require a state to surrender, by resolution, rights it had never surrendered by treaty, and that the tests violated no obligation India had accepted.

### Q: Why did the scientists at Pokhran work in disguise and under codenames?

The disguises answered the failure of 1995, when satellite photographs of preparations at the range had exposed the Rao government's intention and allowed Washington to pressure it into standing down. In 1998 the teams worked in the windows between satellite passes, moved equipment under camouflage, buried the diagnostic cables that betray a test site, and kept the most recognisable figures anonymous in every log: A. P. J. Abdul Kalam travelled in army uniform as Major General Prithviraj, and R. Chidambaram under the codename Natraj, in Raj Chengappa's reconstruction. The compartmentation worked. American intelligence assessed the spring activity at Pokhran as routine, and the Senate in Washington debated the failure in public within days of the announcement.

### Q: What did Pakistan do at Chagai in response in May 1998?

Pakistan answered Pokhran II with its own test series at the Chagai hills in Balochistan, detonating five devices on 28 May 1998 and a sixth on 30 May, on the count announced by Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif's government. Sharif, addressing his country, presented the tests as compelled by India's, and Pakistan marked 28 May as Youm-e-Takbir, a day of national commemoration. The United States applied the same Glenn Amendment sanctions to Pakistan that it had applied to India. The paired detonations ended the era of ambiguity in South Asia: both states stood declared possessors of nuclear weapons, and every subsequent crisis between them, beginning with Kargil in the summer of 1999, was conducted in the shadow the two Mays had cast.

### Q: What did Vajpayee announce about further testing after 13 May 1998?

After the two detonations of 13 May 1998, Vajpayee announced that the planned series of tests stood completed, closing the programme on the government's own declaration rather than under foreign instruction. In the weeks that followed, the government converted completion into policy: India, having conducted the tests it judged necessary, declared a voluntary moratorium on further underground nuclear test explosions, a commitment Jaswant Singh carried into the dialogue with Strobe Talbott and Vajpayee restated at the United Nations General Assembly in September 1998, where he added that India had accepted, in substance, the basic obligation of the test ban treaty as a voluntary act. The moratorium held as a unilateral commitment of policy, never as a treaty signature, and every Indian government since has maintained it on those terms.

### Q: How did the Congress party respond to the tests in May 1998?

The Congress party, in opposition under Sonia Gandhi's presidency from March 1998, took a position of guarded reservation. Its spokesmen praised the scientists of the atomic energy and defence research establishments, whose achievement Congress claimed as the product of capability built by Congress governments from 1974 onward, while questioning the timing of the tests, the government's handling of the diplomatic consequences, and what the party characterised as the conversion of a national asset into partisan celebration. Congress criticism sharpened as sanctions bit and as Pakistan's answering tests at Chagai completed the regional sequence the party had warned against. The party did not demand reversal of weaponisation, and its parliamentary interventions in the debate of late May 1998 pressed the government on economy and diplomacy rather than on the fact of the arsenal.

### Q: What did George Perkovich argue about the reasons for the tests?

George Perkovich, in India's Nuclear Bomb (1999), the most searching sceptical study of the decision, argued that the tests of May 1998 are explained less by India's external security environment than by its domestic configuration: a governing party ideologically committed to the bomb since its Jana Sangh origins, a scientific establishment eager to validate its designs before a test ban closed the door, and a new government seeking a demonstration of strength in its first weeks. On his reading, the security rationales offered afterward, China, Pakistan and the treaty order, were genuine beliefs of the participants but functioned as justifications assembled around a decision taken on other grounds. His account remains the strongest statement of the domestic-politics explanation, against which official Indian writing continues to argue.

### Q: Who prepared the test shafts at Pokhran in 1998?

The shafts at the Pokhran range were prepared by engineers of the Indian Army working under the direction of the test teams, with the 58 Engineer Regiment among the units charged with the excavation, lining and guarding of the emplacement sites, as it had served the programme in earlier preparations. The work was compartmented: the soldiers who dug and secured the shafts were not told the firing date, and the sites were restored to an appearance of routine between periods of activity so that satellite observation would read nothing unusual. Anil Kakodkar's design group at BARC specified the emplacement requirements for the thermonuclear device, whose shaft had to accommodate the device's dimensions and the diagnostic cabling run from it, while K. Santhanam's site-preparation organisation supervised the whole field programme.

### Q: How did Indian newspaper editorials react to the tests in May 1998?

Editorial opinion in the major Indian newspapers ran strongly in favour of the tests in the first days after 11 May 1998, with qualifications that grew as sanctions and Pakistan's reply arrived. The Times of India welcomed the demonstration as the overdue assertion of a capability the country had possessed since 1974. The Hindu's editorial line supported the achievement of the scientists while warning that an arms race in the subcontinent would tax both economies and that the government owed the country a doctrine and a diplomacy equal to the act. The Indian Express pressed the government on the economic management of the sanctions. The pattern, celebration of the capability joined to scrutiny of its handling, set the terms in which the Indian press covered the nuclear question through the doctrine debates that followed.
