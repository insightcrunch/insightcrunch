On 24 April 1973, the largest bench the Supreme Court of India had ever assembled delivered its verdict in Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, and the document it produced was as remarkable for its machinery as for its holding: thirteen judges, eleven separate opinions running to roughly seven hundred pages in the law reports, a division of seven to six on the central question, and an unsigned-looking summary, signed in the event by nine judges, that told the country what the majority had decided. The case is usually read for its doctrine, and the doctrine matters; the pillar hub's account of the basic structure doctrine's career across five decades, [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), carries that reading. This article reads the case itself, as an event inside a courtroom: how a bench of thirteen came to be constituted, how a hearing of sixty eight sitting days was organised and argued, how eleven opinions were produced in the thirty two days between the close of argument and the Chief Justice's retirement, and how a nine-judge summary manufactured a single ratio decidendi out of material that did not contain one in any ordinary form. The claim defended here is the brief's claim, stated plainly: the verdict's institutional and procedural improvisation, its bench, its hearing, its fractured opinions and its summary, is what operationalised the basic structure doctrine, because the doctrine entered Indian law in exactly the shape those procedures gave it, and no other shape was on offer.

The petitioner whose name the case carries was the head of a religious institution in Kerala, and the dispute that carried him to the Supreme Court concerned land, not constitutional theory. Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru was the Mahant, the pontiff and head, of the Edneer Mutt at Edneer in the Kasaragod district of Kerala, an institution holding agricultural land whose management the Kerala legislature sought to bring under state control through the Kerala Land Reforms (Amendment) Act of 1969 and a further amendment of 1971. In 1970 he filed a petition under Article 32 of the Constitution, invoking Articles 25 and 26, which protect religious practice and the right of a religious denomination to manage its own affairs and administer its property, together with Articles 14, 19 and 31. The petition challenged the land reform legislation; but because Parliament had spent the years after 1967 rebuilding its amending power against judicial resistance, the petition could not be decided without deciding how far that power ran. What began as a mutt's defence of its lands became the occasion on which the Supreme Court reconsidered, from the ground up, whether the Constitution contained anything that a constitutional amendment could not touch. T. R. Andhyarujina, who appeared in the case as a junior on the government side and who published the fullest insider account four decades later in The Kesavananda Bharati Case: The Untold Story of Struggle for Supremacy by Supreme Court and Parliament (2011), records a detail that fixes the human scale of the proceeding: the petitioner and his celebrated counsel, Nani Palkhivala, never met.

## The road to a bench of thirteen

The bench of thirteen existed because an earlier bench of eleven had closed a door that Parliament refused to leave closed. In Golaknath v State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967 by eleven judges dividing six to five, a bench led by Chief Justice K. Subba Rao held that Parliament could not amend the fundamental rights in Part III of the Constitution, because a constitutional amendment was law within Article 13 and Article 13 forbade the state to make law abridging those rights. The Golaknath majority applied its ruling prospectively, so the amendments already made stood, but the future was barred: land reform and any other programme requiring changes to Part III thereafter depended on a power the Court had withdrawn. The political sequence that followed is part of the founding settlement's long renegotiation, the contest between Parliament's mandate and the courts' reading of the text that runs from [the founding three-tensions framework](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) through every decade of the republic. Parliament answered Golaknath with statutes and with defeats. The Supreme Court struck down bank nationalisation in R. C. Cooper v Union of India in February 1970, by ten votes to one, and struck down the presidential order derecognising the princes in Madhav Rao Scindia v Union of India in December 1970. The government of Indira Gandhi, returned to office in the March 1971 general election with a two-thirds majority in the Lok Sabha on an explicit promise to remove the judicial obstacles to its programme, then amended the Constitution three times in quick succession. The Twenty-fourth Amendment, in force from 5 November 1971, rewrote Article 368 to declare that Parliament, in the exercise of its constituent power, could amend any provision of the Constitution, and amended Article 13 to place constitutional amendments outside it. The Twenty-fifth Amendment, in force from 20 April 1972, substituted the word amount for compensation in Article 31 and inserted Article 31C, which gave laws giving effect to the directive principles in Article 39(b) and 39(c) precedence over Articles 14, 19 and 31, and barred courts from questioning a legislative declaration that a law had that character. The Twenty-ninth Amendment, in force from 9 June 1972, placed the two Kerala land reform Acts at issue in Kesavananda's own petition into the Ninth Schedule, where Article 31B shielded them from Part III challenge altogether.

### Who presented the petitioner's case in the hearing?

Nani Palkhivala, the Bombay advocate whose advocacy in the case became the standard by which Indian courtroom argument is measured, led the petitioners, assisted by Fali Nariman and Soli Sorabjee. He was instructed on behalf of the Edneer Mutt and of the petitioners whose challenges were clubbed with it.

Palkhivala had taken the brief after the petitions were filed in 1970, and he shaped the challenge from a dispute about Kerala land law into a frontal examination of whether the amending power under Article 368 had any legal limit at all.

Each of these amendments was challenged in the petitions clubbed with Kesavananda's, and the challenges could not be resolved without confronting Golaknath directly, because the Twenty-fourth Amendment's validity depended on whether the power it claimed existed. The petitions were six in number: the Edneer Mutt's petition stood alongside petitions from former rulers affected by the privy purse litigation, from mining interests, from a sugar company and from coal interests, and some twenty intervenors, including several state governments, joined the fray. The questions referred to the bench, as the argument eventually framed them, were whether Golaknath had been rightly decided, whether the Twenty-fourth Amendment was valid, whether the Twenty-fifth Amendment or any part of it was valid, and whether the Twenty-ninth Amendment was valid. A bench larger than Golaknath's eleven was required by the Court's own convention, under which a decision of a bench can be reconsidered only by a larger one. Chief Justice S. M. Sikri accordingly constituted a bench of thirteen, the whole available strength of the Court for practical purposes and the largest constitution bench assembled in its history to that point. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), treats the constitution of the bench as itself a political act within the long struggle between the Court and the Indira Gandhi government; the bench's size announced that the Court understood the case as a constituent occasion, a reconsideration not of one precedent but of the architecture of amendment itself. The hearing opened on 31 October 1972.

## The petitions and the questions they carried

The case's factual root deserves its own account, because the abstraction of the amending question has buried the dispute that carried it. The Edneer Mutt, of which Kesavananda Bharati was the head, is a religious institution of long standing in the Kasaragod region, holding lands whose income maintained the mutt and its religious and charitable work. Kerala's land reform legislation, enacted in 1963 and amended in 1969 and again in 1971, imposed ceilings on landholdings, conferred rights on cultivating tenants and hutment dwellers, and vested surplus land in the state; as applied to religious institutions it restricted the mutt's management and enjoyment of its property. The petition invoked the denomination's rights under Articles 25 and 26, to practise and propagate religion and to manage its own affairs in matters of religion and to own and administer property, together with the equality and property guarantees of Articles 14, 19 and 31. The State of Kerala defended its legislation as agrarian reform of the kind the Constitution's directive principles commanded, and Parliament's amendments supplied its shield: the Twenty-ninth Amendment placed the amending Acts in the Ninth Schedule, and Article 31C, inserted by the Twenty-fifth, gave redistributive laws precedence over the very articles the petition invoked. For the petition to succeed, the shields had to fall; for the shields to fall, the amendments that made them had to be tested; and the amendments could be tested only against a limit on the amending power that Golaknath had located in Part III's unamendability and that the Twenty-fourth Amendment had expressly repudiated. The mutt's acres and the Constitution's identity thus arrived at the bench in a single file, and no judge could decide the one without deciding the other. It remains one of the case's instructive ironies that the petitioner was a religious figure asserting a denomination's property rights, that his counsel persuaded the Court to limit the amending power, and that the limit, once announced, protected a structure of which denominational rights form a part while the petitioner's own lands stayed with the reform: the Kerala Acts survived, the mutt's challenge failed, and the head of the mutt, who had never met his counsel, became the name under which Indian constitutional law is taught.

The five petitions clubbed with his widened the front on which the amendments were attacked. Former rulers, whose privy purses and privileges the Twenty-sixth Amendment had abolished and whose litigation the Court had decided in their favour in 1970 on narrower grounds, challenged the amending power as it bore on their settlement; mining interests and industrial companies, a sugar company and coal interests among them, challenged the acquisition and regulatory amendments as they bore on property and trade. The questions the bench took up, as they crystallised in the reference and the opening arguments, were whether Golaknath had been correctly decided, whether the Twenty-fourth Amendment was valid, whether the Twenty-fifth Amendment was valid in whole or in part, and whether the Twenty-ninth Amendment was valid, with the Kerala legislation's fate following from the answers. The form of the reference matters to the article's theme: the Court was not asked whether one clause offended one right, the ordinary posture of constitutional litigation, but whether a power existed and where its edges ran, across the whole field of the Constitution at once. Questions of that breadth cannot be answered by a court in the ordinary way, by the application of a settled test to a narrow record; they must be answered by constructing the test, and the construction, undertaken by thirteen judges in five months of hearing and one month of writing, is the proceeding this article has described. The pillar's other decisive cases, the election case, the habeas corpus case, the judges cases, each inherited something from this one's manner of proceeding, the large bench, the marathon hearing, the plurality of reasoned positions, and the Kesavananda proceeding is in that sense the template of the Indian constitutional set piece, the form the republic's gravest legal questions have taken ever since.

## Feature one: a bench built to outrank a precedent

The composition of the thirteen matters to everything that followed, because the eventual division of the Court was written into its membership. Chief Justice Sikri presided; he had sat in Golaknath, in the minority that would have left the amending power untouched. With him sat two other survivors of the 1967 bench, Justices J. M. Shelat and K. S. Hegde, both of whom had been in the Golaknath majority; and ten judges who had not sat in 1967: A. N. Grover, A. N. Ray, P. Jaganmohan Reddy, D. G. Palekar, H. R. Khanna, K. K. Mathew, M. H. Beg, S. N. Dwivedi, A. K. Mukherjea and Y. V. Chandrachud. The bench thus contained the surviving senior leadership of both sides of the 1967 argument, together with judges appointed during the years of confrontation between the Court and the government, including judges whose elevation the government had welcomed and judges whose independence the government had reason to doubt. When the votes were counted the following April, the seven who held that the amending power was limited were Sikri, Shelat, Hegde, Grover, Reddy, Khanna and Mukherjea. The six who held the power unlimited were Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi and Chandrachud. The bench was therefore not a panel selected to reach a foreordained result; it was the Court itself, at full stretch, dividing almost exactly in half on the deepest question its jurisdiction contains.

The institutional meaning of the thirteen-judge bench deserves to be stated as the brief directs, because later retellings flatten it into a mere record for size. A constitution bench of five is the Court's ordinary instrument for substantial questions of constitutional interpretation; seven and nine are exceptional; eleven had been assembled once, for Golaknath; thirteen exceeded the strength of most benches that had decided the republic's foundational cases combined. By constituting thirteen, Sikri did three things at once. He satisfied the formal requirement for reconsidering Golaknath. He made the resulting decision practically unreviewable by any later bench, since a bench of fourteen or more would strain the Court's working strength for a generation, and in fact no bench of that size has been assembled since for this subject. And he distributed the responsibility for the outcome across the entire senior Court, so that the verdict, whatever it was, would be the institution's verdict and not a faction's. The counterfactual clarifies the design: a bench of eleven reconstituted with different members might have reversed Golaknath five to six the other way or confirmed it, and either result would have been received as the luck of the roster. Thirteen made reversal or confirmation an act of the whole Court. Austin records that the government watched the constitution of the bench with close attention and that the case was understood on both sides, from October 1972 onward, as a trial of institutional strength as much as of legal argument. The procedural feature is therefore not decoration around the doctrine. The doctrine that emerged, a limit on constituent power enforced by courts, could only have been pronounced with authority by a bench that visibly embodied the whole Court; a narrower bench pronouncing the same limit would have invited the charge, fatal in the politics of 1973, that a clique of judges had seized a power the Constitution gave to Parliament.

One further feature of the bench's architecture shaped the outcome and is visible only in Andhyarujina's insider record. The thirteen did not hear the case as a committee that would retire and produce one opinion. The conventions of the Court permitted, and the scale of the questions encouraged, individual authorship: every judge of consequence intended to write, several intended to write at length, and the argument was conducted from the first week on the understanding that positions taken in court would be tested against eleven separate bodies of reasoning. That expectation explains both the hearing's length and the verdict's form. Counsel were not addressing a single collective mind; they were addressing thirteen readers who would each have to be persuaded, several of whom, as the argument proceeded, were visibly composing the skeletons of their opinions in their questions from the bench. When the judgments came, they came as eleven opinions because the hearing had been, in effect, eleven examinations running in parallel inside one courtroom.

## Feature two: sixty eight days of argument

The hearing ran from 31 October 1972 to 23 March 1973, and the argument occupied sixty eight sitting days inside that span, the longest constitutional hearing the Court had conducted. The figure matters and is worth stating exactly, because the span and the sitting days are often confused: the case was in court, with interruptions for the Court's calendar and for the preparation of judgments in other matters, across five months, and on sixty eight of those days counsel were on their feet. No Indian court had sustained an argument of that length on a single set of questions, and the length was not an accident of scheduling. It was the price of the questions referred. Golaknath had to be re-argued from its foundations, which meant re-arguing Shankari Prasad v Union of India (1951) and Sajjan Singh v State of Rajasthan (1965), the Constituent Assembly's own understanding of Article 368, the German and American learning on implied limits to amendment, and the validity of three separate amendments, each with its own legislative history. The hearing transcripts, preserved in the Supreme Court's registry and drawn on by Andhyarujina for his 2011 account, show a bench that questioned continuously and counsel who were required to defend propositions at the level of first principles for weeks at a time. A hearing of this duration is a procedural fact with doctrinal consequences: it meant that every theory available in 1972 was placed before the bench in its strongest form, tested against the text and the precedents, and answered. Later courts treating Kesavananda as settling the amending question have relied, usually without remarking it, on the completeness of that testing.

Palkhivala opened for the petitioners and argued, with interruptions, for the better part of five weeks. His submission, as the opinions record it and as Andhyarujina reconstructs it, proceeded in ordered steps. Article 368, he argued, conferred a power to amend, and amendment, in its ordinary and legal meaning, was a change that preserved the identity of the thing amended; a power to amend the Constitution was not a power to abrogate it, to destroy its identity, or to rewrite it as a different constitution. The Constitution had a basic structure, an architecture of essential features, including the fundamental rights in their essential content, the democratic and republican form of government, the federal distribution of power, the secular character of the state and the independence of the judiciary; an amendment damaging or destroying those features exceeded the power. He supported the limit from several directions at once: from the text, since Article 368 spoke of amendment and not of reconstitution; from the Constituent Assembly debates of September 1949, in which the Assembly had treated Article 368 as a procedure for change within the Constitution's frame; from comparative practice, including the German Basic Law's eternity clause; and from the lecture on implied limitations delivered at Banaras Hindu University in February 1965 by the Heidelberg scholar Dieter Conrad, whose argument that an amending body organised within a constitutional scheme cannot change the fundamental pillars of its own authority had entered Indian advocacy through M. K. Nambyar's argument in Golaknath and reached its fullest statement in Palkhivala's. The opinions of the majority judges quote Conrad's sentence with approval, Justice Khanna's among them, and the sentence's journey, from a 1965 lecture in Banaras through two great cases to the centre of Indian constitutional law, is itself a demonstration of what a sixty eight day hearing makes possible: an idea had time to be examined until the bench understood precisely what accepting it would entail.

### What argument did the German scholar Dieter Conrad supply?

Dieter Conrad, head of the law department of the South Asia Institute at Heidelberg, argued in his February 1965 Banaras lecture that an amending body created by a constitution cannot, however unlimited the words of its power, change the fundamental pillars on which its own authority rests.

Palkhivala carried the argument from Golaknath, where Nambyar had first presented it, into the centre of the Kesavananda submissions.

Against Palkhivala stood the combined strength of the governments of the Union and the states. Niren De, the Attorney General for India, argued for the Union that the amending power was plenary and sovereign in character: Parliament acting under Article 368 exercised the constituent power of the people, its amendments were not law within Article 13, and no court could test an amendment against implied limits that the Constitution's text nowhere stated. To hold otherwise, the Union's argument ran, would freeze the Constitution against the democratic will of successive Parliaments and would transfer the constituent authority of the nation to an unelected court on the strength of a metaphor, the metaphor of structure. H. M. Seervai, the Advocate General of Maharashtra, appearing for his state and supporting the Union's position, brought the weight of his scholarship to the same conclusion: he argued that the theory of implied limitations had no footing in the text or in the Assembly's intentions, that the Golaknath majority had invented its bar on amending fundamental rights, and that the remedy for an abusive amendment lay with the electorate and not with judicial review. Seervai's part in the hearing has a sequel that the article records because it bears on the summary dispute examined below: in later editions of his Constitutional Law of India, Seervai revised his position and accepted that the basic structure doctrine had been correctly decided, a reversal almost without parallel among advocates of his standing, and his eventual opinion carried weight precisely because he had argued the other side at full strength in 1972 and 1973. The state's case was therefore not a token defence. It was the best case that could be made for parliamentary sovereignty within a written constitution, made by the two most formidable government advocates of the period, and it persuaded six of the thirteen judges. Any account of Kesavananda that treats the outcome as foreordained fails against the record of the hearing itself: the result was in doubt until the opinions were delivered, and the government's counsel expected, on Andhyarujina's record, to win.

The hearing also ran against two clocks, and the clocks are part of the procedural story the brief asks this article to recover. The first was illness. Justice Beg fell seriously ill in February and March 1973 and was hospitalised repeatedly; the loss of a judge mid-hearing, after months of argument, would have destroyed the bench and forced the entire case to be argued again before a reconstituted court, with consequences for the Court's authority that every participant understood. Andhyarujina records the anxiety at the Bar and on the bench during Beg's absences and the parallel efforts that kept the proceeding alive until he recovered and the argument could close. The second clock was the Chief Justice's retirement. Sikri was to retire on 25 April 1973. A judge who has heard a case but retires before judgment cannot deliver an opinion in it; had judgment been reserved beyond that date, Sikri's opinion, the opinion of the presiding judge and of the author of much of the majority's reasoning, would have been lost, and with it very likely the majority itself. Argument therefore closed on 23 March 1973, written submissions followed within days, and the thirteen judges produced eleven opinions in the thirty two days before 24 April, the day the judgments were pronounced, one day before the Chief Justice left office. The compression of that final month explains features of the verdict that doctrinal accounts treat as mysteries: opinions of great length composed at speed, positions stated without the cross-referencing that a longer gestation would have allowed, and, above all, the summary, to which this article turns after the opinions themselves.

## The argument in detail: text, Preamble and constituent power

The hearing's sixty eight days were organised by the questions referred, and the sequence of the argument shaped the opinions that followed, so the article records its architecture before turning to the verdict. The first movement concerned Golaknath itself. The petitioners, opening, did not ask the bench simply to affirm the 1967 bar; Palkhivala's strategy, as the opinions approvingly record, was to invite the bench to overrule Golaknath's absolute prohibition while substituting the basic structure limit for it, a concession that disarmed the government's strongest argument, that the petitioners' case froze the Constitution, and that repositioned the petitioners as defenders of a wide but bounded amending power. The concession was forensic architecture of a high order: it allowed judges who thought Golaknath wrong, as Khanna and Reddy did in part, to vote with the petitioners on the limit without voting for the 1967 precedent. The second movement concerned the nature of the power in Article 368. The Attorney General's submission treated the Article as the repository of the people's constituent power, exercised by Parliament as the nation's constituent body in continuous session; the petitioners answered that Article 368 by its terms prescribed a procedure for amendment of this Constitution, that a power created by an instrument could not exceed the instrument's identity, and that the distinction between constituent power, which had belonged to the Constituent Assembly and was exhausted in 1950, and the derivative amending power, which Parliament held on the Constitution's terms, was the distinction on which the case turned. Krishnaswamy's 2009 study takes up precisely this distinction and gives it its fullest scholarly form, arguing that the Kesavananda settlement is best understood as the Court's allocation of constituent authority between the people's original act and Parliament's delegated power to amend; the argument in court in 1972 anticipated the scholarship by four decades. The third movement concerned the Preamble. Palkhivala submitted that the Preamble, which Golaknath had treated as no part of the Constitution for the purposes of amendment, was in truth a part of the Constitution and the repository of its basic features, the solemn resolve of the people whose identity no amendment could erase; the point mattered because it supplied a textual home for the basic structure, answering the government's charge that the doctrine floated free of the document. A majority of the bench accepted the submission in substance, Reddy's opinion most fully, and the Preamble's status as part of the Constitution, later confirmed beyond argument, dates from this hearing. The fourth movement concerned property, and with it the Twenty-fifth Amendment. Here the bench and the Bar argued Cooper's case over again: whether compensation, renamed amount, could be reviewed for adequacy or for relevance to the property taken, whether Article 31C's priority for redistributive legislation was a legitimate ordering of rights against principles or an abolition of the rights for the cases that mattered most, and whether the ouster clause in Article 31C, the declaration clause, crossed the line that separated amending the Constitution from disabling its judicial enforcement. The alignment the verdict eventually produced on these questions, unanimity on amount, seven to six against the ouster clause, was visible forming in the bench's questions during this movement, and Andhyarujina's account of the exchanges conveys a court testing the government's position at its weakest joint: a Parliament that could declare its own laws immune from the Constitution it was amending had, several judges observed from the bench, ceased to amend and had begun to displace.

Two features of the argument deserve record because they are invisible in the opinions and recoverable only from the hearing's witnesses. The first is the role of the intervening states. The case was not a duel between one mutt and the Union; the states appeared in force, and their counsel divided, with Seervai's Maharashtra supporting the Union's claim of plenary power while other states' interests, particularly states governed by parties opposed to the Congress at the Centre, ran the other way, since an unlimited central amending power threatened the federal provisions that protected the states themselves. The federal argument for the limit, pressed by intervenors and taken up in Sikri's catalogue, which lists the federal character among the basic features, gave the doctrine a constituency beyond property owners: the basic structure limit protected the states' constitutional position as well as the citizen's rights, and the breadth of that constituency is part of the explanation for the doctrine's political durability after 1973. The second feature is the bench's own conduct of the hearing. By Andhyarujina's record the thirteen questioned closely and continuously, put hypothetical cases of escalating severity to both sides, amendments abolishing elections, extending the Lok Sabha's own life indefinitely, converting the republic into a hereditary office, and required counsel to say where, on their theory, the line fell or to admit that none did. The Attorney General's candour under this pressure, his acceptance that on the Union's submission Parliament could in law amend any provision whatever, supplied the majority with the stark alternative against which the limit was framed: the case was decided, in part, in the exchange in which unlimited power was made to describe its own consequences. A hearing of ordinary length, confined to the Kerala Acts and the three amendments, would never have forced that exchange. The sixty eight days forced it, and the doctrine is partly its transcript.

## Feature three: eleven opinions and a court divided seven to six

On 24 April 1973 the bench delivered eleven opinions. Chief Justice Sikri wrote for himself. Justices Shelat and Grover wrote jointly. Justices Hegde and Mukherjea wrote jointly. Justices Reddy, Khanna, Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi and Chandrachud each wrote separately. The eleven opinions together run to roughly seven hundred pages in the reports, and they agree and disagree along so many axes that the headcount on any single proposition differs from the headcount on the next. Stating the result by holding, as the stance governing this pillar requires, means stating it proposition by proposition, and the article does so before drawing the seven to six line that the summary drew.

On the power to amend fundamental rights, the Court overruled Golaknath. Nine judges held that Parliament could amend Part III; the Golaknath position survived only in attenuated form among the judges who replaced its absolute bar with the basic structure limit. On the Twenty-fourth Amendment, the Court was in substance united: the amendment was valid, Article 368 did contain the amending power, and amendments were not law under Article 13. On the Twenty-fifth Amendment, every judge upheld the substitution of the word amount for compensation in Article 31, ending the judicial review of adequacy that had undone bank nationalisation in Cooper's case; on Article 31C, the Court upheld the first part, which gave laws implementing Article 39(b) and 39(c) precedence over Articles 14, 19 and 31, and struck down the second part, the clause barring courts from questioning a legislative declaration, by seven votes to six, with Justice Khanna among the seven and delivering, in the phrase of contemporary accounts, a severe judgment on the ouster clause. On the Twenty-ninth Amendment, the Court upheld it, and with it the placement of the Kerala land reform Acts in the Ninth Schedule; the petitioner's own challenge to the laws governing his mutt's lands therefore failed, a fact worth holding in view, because the most consequential case in Indian constitutional history was, for the man who brought it, a defeat. Kesavananda Bharati lost his case and gave his name to a doctrine.

On the central question, whether the amending power was limited by the Constitution's basic structure, the Court divided seven to six, and the composition of the two groups must be stated judge by judge because the doctrine's content differs across the seven. Sikri held that the amending power did not extend to damaging or destroying the Constitution's basic features, and he listed them: the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form of government, the secular character of the Constitution, the separation of powers between the legislature, the executive and the judiciary, and the federal character of the Constitution. Shelat and Grover accepted the limit and expanded the catalogue, adding the dignity and freedom of the individual, the unity and integrity of the nation, and the mandate to build a welfare state contained in the directive principles. Hegde and Mukherjea held that the Constitution had an identity constituted by its basic elements, including the sovereignty of India, the democratic character of the polity, the unity of the country, the essential features of the individual freedoms secured by Part III, and the mandate to build a welfare state, and that an amendment destroying that identity was beyond Article 368. Reddy held the power limited by the Constitution's essential features and treated the Preamble, which Golaknath had regarded as outside the Constitution for amendment purposes, as part of the Constitution and a key to its basic features. Khanna's opinion, the seventh, is examined separately below, because its reasoning differed from the other six in ways that fixed the doctrine's operative content. The six in the minority held, in variations, that the power had no such limit. Ray held the amending power plenary and the constituent authority of Parliament continuous with the people's sovereignty, and rejected implied limitations root and branch. Palekar held that the fundamental rights could be amended like any other provision and that the apprehended abuse of the power was a political question. Mathew doubted that any workable line separated amendment from destruction and declined to draw one. Beg and Dwivedi held the power unlimited in terms close to the Attorney General's submission. Chandrachud, the junior judge of the bench, held that the amending power extended to every provision and that the Constitution's identity was not a judicially enforceable limit; his signature on the summary, two days of deliberation later in the sequence of events, is one of the puzzles the next section addresses.

### How many pages do the eleven opinions occupy in the law reports?

Together the eleven opinions run to roughly seven hundred pages in the law reports, about seven hundred and three pages in the Supreme Court Reports printing, before an eighty page apparatus of footnotes is counted. The bulk is itself evidence for the procedural reading: no single author wrote this verdict.

Its length records eleven separate attempts to answer the same questions rather than one judgment edited thirteen ways, and any reader who works through it encounters the disagreement in its original, unmerged form.

Two consequences follow from stating the opinions in this form. First, the famous seven to six split is exact on exactly one question, the existence of a basic structure limit, and inexact everywhere else: on the severance of Article 31C's second part the alignment was different, on the amount substitution it was unanimous, and on the contents of the basic structure there was no single majority list at all, since the judges who agreed on the limit produced different catalogues and Khanna's catalogue was the narrowest. Second, the eleven opinions did not yield, by any ordinary method of reading judgments, a ratio decidendi that a later court could simply apply. A later bench seeking the holding of Kesavananda would have to read seven hundred pages, tally majorities proposition by proposition across eleven texts written in five weeks, and decide which propositions commanded seven votes. That labour, which the legal system would otherwise have had to perform case by case, is precisely the labour the nine-judge summary claimed to have performed in advance. Whether it performed it faithfully is the dispute to which the article turns, through the opinion that made the summary's central proposition possible.

## The opinions one by one

Because the pillar's standard is to state judgments by holding, and because the division of labour assigns this article the opinions themselves while the hub carries the doctrine's career, the eleven opinions are recorded here individually, in the order of the bench's seniority, with each judge's route and conclusion kept distinct.

Chief Justice Sikri's opinion is the verdict's centre of gravity and the natural starting point for any reader of the reports. He held that the amending power under Article 368 was wide but not unlimited, that it did not include the power to damage or destroy the basic features of the Constitution, and he grounded the limit in the Constitution's supremacy: a document that is supreme cannot contain, in its own amendment clause, the means of its abolition. His catalogue of basic features, supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, federal character, was offered as drawn from the document's own provisions rather than from the judge's preferences, each feature anchored to articles that embodied it. He upheld the Twenty-fourth and Twenty-ninth Amendments, upheld the Twenty-fifth in its amount substitution and in the first part of Article 31C, and struck the declaration clause. Sikri's opinion also carried the weight of his own history: in Golaknath he had dissented, holding the amending power unlimited over fundamental rights, and his Kesavananda opinion explained the movement, distinguishing the power to amend rights, which he affirmed, from a power to destroy the framework, which he denied. The opinion of a Chief Justice who had changed his mind on the record, in the last month of his tenure, gave the majority a gravity that a career-consistent opinion could not have carried.

The joint opinion of Shelat and Grover JJ. is the most expansive of the majority texts. They held the power limited by the Constitution's basic features and by the essential elements of its identity, and their catalogue ran beyond Sikri's: to his five they added the dignity and freedom of the individual, the unity and integrity of the nation, and the mandate, which they drew from the directive principles, to build a welfare state, treating the principles not as the rights' antagonists but as part of the basic structure that no amendment could abandon. Their opinion is the source of the frequent later statement that fundamental rights in their essential content are themselves basic structure, and it represents the majority's high-water mark: had their catalogue commanded the Court, the doctrine would have entered the law as a long schedule of protected commitments. It did not, and the difference between their text and Khanna's is the difference between the doctrine as preached and the doctrine as held. The joint opinion of Hegde and Mukherjea JJ. approached the limit through identity rather than catalogue. They held that the Constitution had a basic structure constituted by its essential features, that the building of the Constitution had proceeded on stated foundations, the sovereignty of India, the democratic character of the polity, the unity of the country, the essential features of the individual freedoms and the welfare state mandate among them, and that an amendment destroying those foundations was a contradiction in terms, since the amending power derived from the very document whose identity it would extinguish. Their reasoning is the most philosophical of the eleven, concerned with what makes a constitution the same constitution through time, and it supplied later benches with the vocabulary of identity and abrogation in which the doctrine is still discussed.

Reddy J. wrote the opinion that did the most for the Preamble. He held the amending power limited, treated the Preamble as part of the Constitution and as a key to its basic features, and held that the fundamental rights could be amended but not abrogated in their essential content. His opinion also addressed the Twenty-fifth Amendment's antecedents with particular care, treating the property clauses as the site where the amending power and the courts had been in conflict longest, and upholding Parliament's settlement of the amount question while joining the majority against the ouster clause. Among the minority, Ray J. wrote the fullest statement of the government's case in judicial form. He held that the amending power was plenary, that the distinction between constituent and amending power pressed by the petitioners was unsound after 1950, that the Constitution's text contained no implied limits, and that the fear of abusive amendments was a political apprehension to be answered by the electorate; his opinion warned, in terms the supersession made poignant within forty eight hours, against a judiciary that set its reading of the Constitution's spirit above the text the people had enacted. Palekar J. held similarly that the power extended to every provision, fundamental rights included, and that Golaknath should be overruled without substituting a new judicial veto. Mathew J., in the most sceptical of the opinions, doubted whether any court could define the basic structure with the precision adjudication requires, and declined to found a limit on a concept he found indeterminate, while holding the amendments before the Court valid in any event. Beg J. and Dwivedi J. each held the power unlimited, Dwivedi adding a close textual reading of Article 368 as it stood after the Twenty-fourth Amendment. Chandrachud J., the junior member, held that the amending power reached every part of the Constitution, that the Preamble was amendable like the rest, and that no enforceable limit arose from the document's identity; his later career gives the opinion its irony, since as Chief Justice he would deliver, in Minerva Mills, the judgment that treated the Kesavananda limit as settled law and its summary as a correct statement of it, an arc from dissent to custodianship that mirrors the doctrine's own passage from contest to orthodoxy. Read together, the eleven opinions are not a broken judgment but a complete map of the positions available in Indian constitutional thought in 1973, each occupied by a judge of the first rank and defended at full length; that completeness is a further reason the case, rather than any later restatement, remains the doctrine's authoritative source.

## The seventh vote: Justice Khanna and the meaning of amendment

Justice H. R. Khanna's opinion is the hinge of the case, and its reasoning must be stated exactly, because the doctrine that entered Indian law is Khanna's doctrine and not the fuller doctrine of Sikri or of Shelat and Grover. Khanna rejected the theory of implied limitations on which the other six majority judges substantially relied. He held that the amending power was plenary within its field, that it extended to the fundamental rights, and that no limitation could be implied against it from the Constitution's scheme. He then found the limit in the word amendment itself. The word amendment, he held, postulates that the old Constitution survives without loss of its identity despite the change that has been made; a power to amend is by its nature a power to change a constitution while keeping it the same constitution, and it cannot extend to abrogating the Constitution or changing its basic structure or framework so as to lose its identity. Quoting Conrad's sentence, that an amending body organised within the statutory scheme, however verbally unlimited its power, cannot by its very structure change the fundamental pillars supporting its constitutional authority, Khanna held that Parliament could amend any provision, including Part III, provided the basic structure or framework remained. On the content of that framework he was deliberately restrained: he declined to treat the right to property as part of the basic structure, holding that Article 31 stood within the amending power in full, and he treated the essential features that survived amendment as the Constitution's identity-bearing elements, to be identified case by case rather than by catalogue.

Khanna's restraint is what made seven. Had the price of a majority been Sikri's catalogue, or Shelat and Grover's inclusion of the directive principles' welfare mandate among unamendable features, the coalition would have fractured, because several of the seven would not have signed a charter freezing large parts of the Constitution against a Parliament elected on a redistributive programme, and Khanna himself would not have signed Sikri's reasoning. The doctrine that commanded a majority was therefore the doctrine at its narrowest defensible point: not implied limitations, not a schedule of sacred provisions, but a semantic and structural limit carried by the concept of amendment, leaving Parliament free to amend every article, including every fundamental right, so long as the Constitution that emerged was recognisably the same Constitution. Sudhir Krishnaswamy, in Democracy and Constitutionalism in India (2009), reads the settlement in this register, as a judicially constructed compromise that preserved both the amending power's democratic credentials and a judicially enforceable floor beneath it; the reading fits the opinions, and it explains why the doctrine, born seven to six, proved durable where Golaknath's absolute bar, born six to five, had collapsed within six years. Golaknath had told Parliament it could not touch Part III at all, a holding no governing majority could accept as final. Kesavananda, through Khanna, told Parliament it could touch everything except the Constitution's identity, a holding a governing majority could live with, and did, even while resenting it. The swing vote was thus not a lottery outcome. It was the point at which the majority's doctrine became acceptable enough to survive, and the post-1973 career of the doctrine, carried in the pillar hub's account, is the record of Khanna's formulation being applied, extended in later cases such as the election case of 1975 and Minerva Mills in 1980, but never replaced by the broader catalogues of his colleagues.

The personal consequence for Khanna completes the institutional picture and belongs in this article because it demonstrates what the participants believed the case had decided. In January 1977, after his dissent in the habeas corpus case during the Emergency had made him the one judge who had held against the government at the height of its power, Khanna was passed over for the Chief Justiceship in favour of Justice Beg, and he resigned. His Kesavananda opinion was not the stated ground, but the pattern of the republic's response to the case runs through his career: the judge whose vote created the doctrine was, four years later, denied the office that seniority assigned him, in an appointment the government of the day defended on grounds of its own choosing. The doctrine's author paid the doctrine's price, and the episode fixed in the profession's memory the association between the basic structure limit and judicial courage under pressure, an association that has done part of the doctrine's defensive work in every later controversy.

## Feature four: the summary signed by nine judges

The verdict's most unusual procedural feature came last. When the eleven opinions had been delivered on 24 April 1973, the Chief Justice caused to be prepared and circulated a short document headed as the view of the majority, a summary of the conclusions reached, and it was signed by nine of the thirteen judges: Sikri, Shelat, Hegde, Grover, Reddy, Palekar, Khanna, Mukherjea and Chandrachud. The four who did not sign were Ray, Mathew, Beg and Dwivedi. The summary stated, in substance, a set of propositions: that Golaknath had been overruled; that Article 368 did not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution; that the Twenty-fourth Amendment was valid; that the Twenty-fifth Amendment was valid in its substitution of amount for compensation and in the first part of Article 31C, and invalid in the second part of that article; and that the Twenty-ninth Amendment was valid. In two pages it performed the operation the eleven opinions could not perform among themselves: it told the legal system what the case had decided, in a form that a district court, a ministry or a later bench could quote without reading seven hundred pages.

The summary's composition contains the puzzle at its centre. Two of its signatories, Palekar and Chandrachud, had held in their opinions that the amending power was unlimited and that no basic structure limit existed; yet their signatures stand beneath a document stating, as the view of the majority, that Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure. The explanation, given by Andhyarujina from his knowledge of the proceeding and accepted in substance by most later accounts, is that the summary recorded majorities proposition by proposition, and a judge could sign the record of what the Court had held on each point without endorsing each holding as his own; Palekar and Chandrachud signed as witnesses to the tally, not as converts to the doctrine. The explanation is coherent, but it identifies the summary's legal character exactly: the document is not a twelfth opinion. It is a certified count. Its authority depends entirely on whether the count was accurate, and on whether any count, however accurate, can supply a ratio decidendi for a case whose reasoning is irreducibly plural. Both questions were contested, in print, by the two men best placed to contest them.

Seervai attacked the summary in his Constitutional Law of India, arguing that it was not a judgment of the Court, that it had been prepared in haste on the Chief Justice's last day and signed without the deliberation a judgment requires, and that its central proposition did not command the support the document claimed, since Khanna's route to the limit, through the meaning of the word amendment, was not the route of implied limitations that several of the seven had taken, and the summary's flat statement concealed the divergence. The attack mattered because of who made it: the leading constitutional scholar of his generation, counsel against the doctrine in the case itself. Andhyarujina's 2011 book is, in substantial part, a reply. Drawing on his own notes, on the hearing record and on papers available to him as a participant, Andhyarujina defended the summary as an accurate record of the majorities on each proposition, described the circumstances of its preparation in the judges' deliberations before the delivery of the opinions, and published material bearing on the sequence in which the opinions and the summary were finalised. The exchange is the named scholarly dispute this article is charged to represent, and it is represented here with both accounts at their strongest, because the article's own claim depends on the dispute rather than on its suppression. Seervai's strongest point is formal and remains unanswered in its own terms: a ratio decidendi is the reasoning of a court, and a document that is a count of conclusions, signed by judges who rejected the central conclusion, is a novel instrument whose authority the law of precedent had no ready category for. Andhyarujina's strongest point is practical and equally unanswered: without the summary, Kesavananda decided everything and nothing, the seven hundred pages yielded different holdings to different readers, as the conflicting extractions of the following years in fact showed whenever courts or counsel worked from the opinions alone, and the summary gave the system the single citable sentence on which the doctrine's entire later career has in practice rested. Chief Justice Chandrachud, delivering the leading opinion in Minerva Mills v Union of India in 1980, stated the settlement the system reached: whether or not the summary was a legitimate part of the judgment, or was per incuriam for the reasons scholars had given, it correctly reflected the majority view. Justice Bhagwati in the same case doubted that the summary had legal effect of its own, and proceeded on the basis of the doctrine as later cases had taken it. The doctrine, in other words, was ratified by use. The summary held because benches after 1973 treated its central sentence as the holding and built on it, and a procedural improvisation acquired, by seven years of reliance, the authority its form had not obviously given it.

### Which judges did not sign the nine-judge summary?

Four of the thirteen withheld their signatures: Justice A. N. Ray, who succeeded Sikri as Chief Justice the following day; Justice K. K. Mathew; Justice M. H. Beg; and Justice S. N. Dwivedi. All four had held the amending power unlimited.

Two members of that same minority, Justices Palekar and Chandrachud, did sign, a fact that has fuelled the scholarly dispute over the document ever since, since their signatures stand beneath a proposition their own opinions had rejected.

This is the point at which the article's four features converge into its claim. A bench of thirteen gave the verdict the authority of the whole Court. A hearing of sixty eight days gave the bench every argument in its strongest form, including the scholarly import, Conrad's implied limitation, that two of the majority's routes depended on and that Khanna transposed into the meaning of amendment. Eleven opinions, produced under a retiring Chief Justice's deadline, recorded the disagreement honestly and at a length that made the case unusable without a key. And the summary supplied the key: an improvised, contestable, formally anomalous document that converted eleven texts into one doctrine. Remove any feature and the doctrine's entry into Indian law changes shape or fails. A smaller bench invites defiance; a shorter hearing leaves Conrad unexamined; a single majority opinion, had one been negotiable, would have stated a doctrine either broader than Khanna's and unstable, or narrower and differently grounded; and without the summary there is no sentence for Minerva Mills to ratify. The basic structure doctrine, as an operative part of Indian law, is the product of these procedures in the strictest sense: it exists in the form the procedures left it.

## How the summary was made: the record of April 1973

The circumstances of the summary's preparation are known in unusual detail because Andhyarujina set them down, and the article records them here because the Seervai critique turns on exactly these circumstances. The opinions were written in the thirty two days after argument closed, judges drafting in parallel, in several cases without sight of one another's completed texts. As the delivery date fixed itself by the Chief Justice's retirement, the need for a statement of the result became pressing: the Court was about to pronounce eleven opinions whose combined effect no headnote writer could be trusted to state, in a case where the statement of the result would be quoted in every ministry and court in the country within the week. The summary was prepared under Sikri's direction and circulated among the judges before the delivery; nine signed it. On Andhyarujina's account the signatories understood the document as the Court's statement of the majorities on the questions decided, and the two minority judges who signed, Palekar and Chandrachud, signed it in that understanding, as certifying the tally and not as withdrawing their opinions. The four who did not sign, Ray, Mathew, Beg and Dwivedi, declined for the opposite reason, holding that their opinions spoke for themselves and that the document was not a judgment. Seervai's printed critique fastened on the sequence and the haste: a document of this consequence, produced in the final days of a Chief Justice's tenure and signed by judges whose opinions contradicted its central sentence, could not, he argued, be treated as the ratio of the case, and he maintained in print that at least one of its propositions mis-stated the alignment of the Court. Andhyarujina's reply, published thirty eight years after the event and worked from the papers of the hearing, meets the critique on the facts: the preparation was deliberate within the time the retirement allowed, the propositions were framed to record the votes as the opinions disclosed them, and the central proposition, that Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure, recorded a genuine seven to six majority once Khanna's opinion was counted for the limit it expressly imposed, whatever its difference of route. The article does not pretend the exchange can be closed; it can be weighed, and on the evidence available the count stands as accurate, Khanna's opinion being unambiguous, in its operative sentence, that an amendment damaging the basic structure or framework was beyond the power. What remains genuinely open, and what Seervai's critique preserves for the law, is the formal question: whether precedent may rest on a certified count at all. The system's answer, given in Minerva Mills and repeated since, was pragmatic, and pragmatism in the law of precedent is itself a doctrinal position with consequences the pillar's later pieces inherit.

### When did Chief Justice Sikri leave office?

Chief Justice S. M. Sikri retired on 25 April 1973, the day after the judgments were delivered, and the verdict of 24 April was the last act of his tenure. His retirement date had been known throughout the hearing, and it set the deadline by which eleven opinions were written and the nine-judge summary was prepared, circulated and signed.

The delivery day itself belongs to the record. On 24 April 1973 the judges took their seats and the opinions were pronounced seriatim in a courtroom whose occupants understood that the proceeding would occupy the day and settle the amending question for their lifetimes; the summary was read as the Court's statement of its conclusions. Sikri demitted office the following day, Ray took the oath as Chief Justice, and the three superseded judges resigned within days. A verdict, a succession and three resignations inside seventy two hours: the concentration of constitutional consequence into a single week of April 1973 has no parallel in the Court's history, and it is the concentration, produced entirely by the procedural facts this article has traced, the bench, the deadline and the division, that marks Kesavananda off from every other case in the reports. The doctrine has had a longer life than any participant in that week; it began, all the same, as an event in a courtroom, and it is as an event, dated, witnessed and recorded, that this reanalysis has treated it.

## The days after: supersession and the attempted review

The government's answer to the verdict came within a day, and it was addressed not to the doctrine but to the Court's personnel. On 25 April 1973, the day Sikri retired, the President appointed Justice A. N. Ray Chief Justice of India, passing over the three judges senior to him, Shelat, Hegde and Grover, all three members of the Kesavananda majority. All three resigned. The supersession broke the convention of seniority that had governed the appointment of every Chief Justice since 1950, and it was defended by the government in the language of a committed judiciary, a phrase the Law Minister H. R. Gokhale and the Steel Minister Mohan Kumaramangalam elaborated in Parliament and in public lectures in the weeks that followed: the government claimed the right to appoint a Chief Justice whose philosophy of the Constitution it found congenial, and named forward-looking views on the amending power among the qualifications it sought. The episode is sometimes narrated as the doctrine's first test, but institutionally it is better understood as the verdict's reception by the only audience whose acceptance the Court could not compel. The government did not defy the judgment; the amendments stood as the Court had left them, Article 31C's ouster clause was not re-enacted in defiance, and the petitions were closed. What the government contested was the future composition of the bench that would apply the doctrine. The procedural reading of Kesavananda explains even this: a doctrine announced by thirteen judges, seven to six, with its author's colleagues marked by seniority for the Chief Justiceship, presented the executive with a target list as well as a rule, and the supersession was executed against the list. The politics of the judgment, which the political-context framing of the case places at the centre, is in this way partly a product of the judgment's procedure: the published division of the Court, unavoidable in a case decided by eleven signed opinions, told the government exactly whose promotion would alter the institution's centre of gravity. The events fall within [the pre-Emergency political-architectural context](/2019/04/06/privy-purse-abolition-1971-india/) that the pillar's companion pieces trace from the privy purse confrontation to the Emergency itself, and the confrontation over appointments connects backward to the property cases of 1970 and 1971, including the bank nationalisation judgment that the Twenty-fifth Amendment answered, traced in [the bank nationalisation judgment and its reversal by amendment](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/).

The debate the supersession opened ran through 1973 and fixed the terms in which the verdict was publicly understood. In Parliament and in a widely reported lecture, Mohan Kumaramangalam argued that the government was entitled, indeed obliged, to consider a judge's philosophy and outlook when appointing a Chief Justice, and that the social philosophy of candidates on questions such as the amending power and property was a legitimate subject of executive assessment; the Law Minister, H. R. Gokhale, defended the appointments in the House on the ground that the three judges passed over had shown themselves unable to appreciate the Constitution's social purposes. The Bar answered through its associations, which passed resolutions deploring the supersession as a blow to judicial independence, and Seervai, who had argued the government's case in the hearing itself, condemned the supersession in print as an act calculated to intimidate the Court, a condemnation whose weight derived from his position as the government's own leading counsel months earlier. The episode therefore instructed the country in the verdict's meaning by a route no judgment could have taken: the executive, by punishing the majority's senior members and rewarding the minority's, announced its own reading of the eleven opinions, that the doctrine was the work of identifiable men whose succession could be managed, while the profession, by rallying to the superseded judges, announced the opposite reading, that the limit announced on 24 April was the Court's and would outlast the Court's personnel. Both readings were available only because the opinions were signed and the division public, further evidence that the case's procedural form, eleven named texts and a published count, determined its political afterlife as surely as its legal content.

The second attempt to undo the verdict came through the Court's own procedure, and its failure completes the procedural story. On 7 November 1975, a five-judge bench decided Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain, applying Kesavananda to strike down part of the Thirty-ninth Amendment while upholding the Prime Minister's election; within days, Chief Justice Ray constituted a bench of thirteen judges to review the Kesavananda decision, on the Union's application in connected proceedings. The review bench sat on 10 and 11 November 1975. Palkhivala, appearing against the review, argued that no ground for reconsideration had been made out, that the applicants had shown no error in the 1973 reasoning, and that a bench assembled in the shadow of a pending political controversy, during the Emergency, to reconsider the one doctrine limiting the amending power then being exercised without restraint, would damage the Court beyond repair. On 12 November 1975 the bench was dissolved without a decision, the Chief Justice announcing that the review could not proceed. The episode is the last procedural feature of the case, though it postdates the verdict: the doctrine survived its only direct institutional challenge not by being reaffirmed on the merits but by the collapse of the procedure convened to dislodge it. Granville Austin's account treats the dissolution as one of the decisive non-events of the Emergency period, and the description is exact. A doctrine that rests on a seven to six division and a contested summary was preserved, at the moment of maximum danger, by an advocate's procedural objection and a Chief Justice's retreat. The pillar's later pieces take up the Emergency's jurisprudence and the habeas corpus case; here it is enough to mark that Kesavananda's survival to 1977, after which Minerva Mills and the post-Emergency consensus made it unassailable, was itself procedurally contingent in ways the doctrinal histories rarely record.

## The four-feature map of the verdict

The brief charges this article with producing a citable artifact, and the artifact is set out here and labelled in the prose around it so that later pieces in the pillar can cite it by name: the Kesavananda four-feature procedural map, a table recording, for each of the verdict's four institutional features, the dated facts of the feature and the doctrinal content it carried. The map is the article's argument in compressed form.

| Feature | Dated record | Doctrinal content carried |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The bench of thirteen | Constituted by Chief Justice Sikri in 1972 to reconsider Golaknath; sat from 31 October 1972 | Authority of the whole Court behind a limit on constituent power; a division of the full Court rather than of a panel |
| The hearing of sixty eight days | Arguments from 31 October 1972 to 23 March 1973; six petitions and some twenty intervenors | Every theory of the amending power tested at full strength, including Conrad's implied limitation in Palkhivala's submission |
| The eleven opinions | Delivered on 24 April 1973, one day before Sikri retired; roughly seven hundred pages | A seven to six holding on the basic structure limit, with Khanna's meaning-of-amendment route as the operative reasoning |
| The summary of nine judges | Signed on 24 April 1973 by nine of the thirteen; not signed by Ray, Mathew, Beg or Dwivedi | The single citable proposition that Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution |

Read as the map presents it, the verdict's content is inseparable from its procedure at every row, and that inseparability is the article's thesis in its final form. Doctrinal histories of Kesavananda typically treat the first two rows as colour and the last two as embarrassment, the size of the bench and the length of the hearing as pageantry, the plurality of opinions as a failure of judicial craft and the summary as an irregularity to be apologised for. The procedural reading reverses the valuation. The pageantry was load-bearing: only the whole Court could announce a limit on the people's constituent power and be obeyed. The plurality was honesty: the questions were genuinely open, and eleven signed opinions preserved the disagreement in a form later courts could work with, mining the opinions for propositions as new cases required. And the summary was the keystone: formally anomalous, scholarly contested, and in practice the sentence of the case, the proposition quoted in every later judgment, textbook and examination hall. The artifact's design across the pillar is deliberate, because the same pattern, procedure carrying doctrine, recurs in the pillar's other great cases, from the election case of 1975 to the judges cases, and the map gives the series a citable form for the comparison.

## Adjudicating the three readings

Three readings of Kesavananda compete in the literature, and the article's charge is to adjudicate among them on the record rather than to survey them neutrally. The doctrinal-significance reading, the standard textbook treatment, holds that the case is its holding: the basic structure doctrine emerged on 24 April 1973, and the case's importance is the doctrine's importance, to be traced through the doctrine's applications. The reading is true but incomplete in a way its own texts betray, because the doctrine it traces has no canonical statement in the eleven opinions; the statement it quotes is always the summary's. A reading that must silently rely on the procedural artifact it ignores has conceded the procedural reading's central point. The political-context reading, strongest in Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution, holds that the case is an episode in the struggle between the Indira Gandhi government and the Court, intelligible through the 1971 mandate, the property cases, the supersession that followed and the Emergency that the doctrine failed to prevent and then outlived. This reading explains the stakes and the reception better than any rival, and this article has used it at every stage; but it cannot explain the doctrine's form. Politics explains why the government wanted the amending power unlimited and why the Court resisted; it does not explain why the resistance took the shape of a limit carried by the word amendment, why the catalogue question was left open, or why the system's operative text became a two-page summary rather than any judge's reasoning. Those are questions about the internal production of the judgment, and they are answered only by the procedural reading defended here: the doctrine's form was determined by the coalition arithmetic of thirteen judges, the completeness of a sixty eight day hearing, the deadline of a retiring Chief Justice and the improvisation that deadline forced. The article therefore adjudicates as follows: the political reading governs the case's context and consequences, the doctrinal reading governs its legal effects, and the procedural reading governs the case itself, the event of 1972 and 1973, and is prior to the other two because the doctrine whose effects they trace existed, in operative form, only as the procedure left it.

The strongest counter to this adjudication is the one the brief instructs the article to face: that the four features were accidents of their moment, a bench size set by one precedent's arithmetic, a hearing length set by the number of petitioners, a fractured verdict set by a retirement date, a summary set by one Chief Justice's impatience, and that reading doctrinal substance into administrative accidents over-determines them, mistaking the container's shape for the content's cause. The counter is right about the accidents and wrong about the inference. The features were contingent in origin; each might easily have been otherwise. But contingency of origin does not dissolve causal effect. The doctrine that operated in Indian law after 1973 operated through the summary's sentence, was as narrow as Khanna's vote required, and survived its 1975 challenge through a procedural collapse; those are effects of the features, traceable in the record, and they would have been different had the features been different. The article's claim is accordingly stated in its exact form: not that the procedure was designed to produce the doctrine, but that the doctrine India received is unintelligible, in its content, its authority and its survival, apart from the procedure that produced it. On the primary evidence of the opinions, the hearing record as Andhyarujina preserves it, and the text of the summary itself, that claim is sustained.

The scholarly literature's own division maps onto the three readings with unusual neatness, and recording the map sharpens the adjudication. Austin's 1999 history is the political reading's monument, built from interviews with the participants and the governmental record, and its Kesavananda chapters remain the indispensable account of the case as an event in the republic's power struggle; its limitation, for this article's purpose, is that the judgment's internal production, the conference, the drafting, the summary, enters his narrative as aftermath rather than as cause. Andhyarujina's 2011 volume is the procedural reading's primary exhibit and its strongest statement, although it is written as a memoir of advocacy and must be read with the discount its author's position requires; its documentary core, the hearing's conduct and the summary's preparation, is nonetheless the evidence on which any account of the case's production must rest. Krishnaswamy's 2009 study stands apart from both, reconstructing the doctrine as a theory of constituent power and defending its democratic warrant against the charge of judicial usurpation; its limitation is the mirror of Austin's, a tendency to treat the doctrine as an idea whose institutional vehicle was incidental. The article's adjudication has drawn on all three while following the primary record, and its conclusion can be stated against each: against the doctrinal reading, the holding exists only in the summary's form; against the political reading, the form of resistance was set inside the courtroom and not in the street or the cabinet; against the purely theoretical reading, the theory that prevailed was the one a seventh judge could sign in the last week of April 1973. Scholarship after these three works has largely proceeded within the channels they cut, which is a further reason to return to the proceeding itself, where the channels were dug.

One boundary of the article should be marked before its closing account, because the pillar's architecture assigns the doctrine's career elsewhere. What Kesavananda did to later law, the striking of the Thirty-ninth Amendment's clause in 1975, the defence of the doctrine against the Forty-second Amendment in Minerva Mills in 1980, its extension to the Ninth Schedule in I. R. Coelho in 2007 and its place in the constitutional common sense of the republic, belongs to the hub's account and to the cases that follow in the pillar, where the connection to [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) and to the founding generation's own allocation of rights, traced in [the constitutional rights framework](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/), can be drawn at length. For readers working through the judgment's text itself, the eleven opinions and the summary circulate as compiled electronic documents, and a plain [office file viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) is the practical instrument for reading the seven hundred pages across devices. What remains for this article is the narrower closing task: to state, as a court reporter would, what the case decided on the day it was decided, since the procedural reading does not dissolve the holdings and the article's authority rests on having stated them exactly.

## What the bench held on 24 April 1973

The holdings, assembled from the opinions and certified by the summary, were these. Golaknath was overruled: the fundamental rights could be amended. The Twenty-fourth Amendment was valid: the amending power resided in Article 368, an amendment was not law within Article 13, and Parliament could amend any provision of the Constitution subject to the limit the case announced. The limit was that Article 368 did not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution, a proposition carried seven to six, on reasoning that divided between implied limitations and Khanna's construction of the word amendment. The Twenty-fifth Amendment was valid in substituting amount for compensation in Article 31, a holding without dissent, and valid in the first part of Article 31C, giving laws implementing Article 39(b) and 39(c) precedence over Articles 14, 19 and 31; its second part, the bar on judicial review of a legislative declaration, was severed and struck down, seven to six. That severance is the point at which the case touches the founding debate over directive principles and fundamental rights most directly, since Article 31C was Parliament's instrument for subordinating the rights to the principles, and the Court preserved the subordination while striking the clause that would have made the legislature the judge of its own compliance; the longer arc of that contest is traced in [the directive principles and fundamental rights settlement](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/). The Twenty-ninth Amendment was valid, and the Kerala land reform Acts stood in the Ninth Schedule beyond the petitioner's challenge. No amendment before the Court was struck down in full. The government lost the unlimited power it had claimed and kept every statute it had come to defend except one ouster clause; the petitioner lost his case; and the Constitution acquired a limit that its text does not state, announced by a majority of one judge, in eleven opinions, and summarised in two pages that nine judges signed. That distribution of victory and defeat, unintelligible as doctrine alone and inevitable once the procedure is understood, is the case this article set out to reanalyse, and the record sustains the reanalysis: Kesavananda Bharati was decided by its machinery as much as by its reasoning, and the machinery is the judgment's most durable bequest to the republic it bound. The first assertion of a judicially enforced limit on constitutional amendment, in the First Amendment controversy of 1951, had ended in parliamentary victory, as [the first constitutional amendment of 1951](/2019/01/14/first-constitutional-amendment-1951/) records; the assertion of 1973, made by thirteen judges with sixty eight days of argument behind it, ended differently, and the difference is the measure of what the procedure accomplished. Later benches would test the doctrine against the Emergency's amendments and against the appointments power, and the pillar hub's account of those contests, together with the sequel in [the Allahabad judgment of June 1975](/2019/04/17/allahabad-judgment-june-1975-indira/) and the Emergency narrative in [the Emergency of 1975 to 1977 reanalysed](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/), completes the story this article has deliberately left at the courtroom door of 24 April 1973.

## A note on the sources for a case with eleven opinions

A case decided by eleven opinions poses a source problem that this article has had to solve and should state openly, because the solution disciplines every claim made above. The primary text is the report of Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973) itself, the eleven opinions and the summary, read as a single fractured document; every holding attributed in this article is attributed from the opinions, judge by judge, and not from the summary, whose propositions are treated as the certified tally whose accuracy the article has separately examined. Beneath the report lies the under-used source the brief identifies: the record of the hearing itself, the arguments and the bench's questions across the sixty eight days, preserved in the Supreme Court's registry. The judgments disclose what the judges decided; the hearing record discloses how the questions were framed, which concessions counsel made and which hypotheticals the bench pressed, and several of this article's findings, the petitioners' strategic concession on Golaknath, the Attorney General's candour on the unlimited power's consequences, the states' division on the federal argument, rest on that record as transmitted by the participant who published it. The transmission matters: T. R. Andhyarujina's The Kesavananda Bharati Case (2011) is a primary-adjacent source, written by a junior counsel in the case, drawing on his own notes and on the papers of H. M. Seervai, for whom he had worked and against whose printed critique of the summary the book partly argues; it is therefore used in this article as testimony, attributed where it is relied on, and checked against the report wherever the report can check it. Granville Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) supplies the political archive, interviews and governmental record against which the supersession and the 1975 review are here described, and its narrative is the strongest form of the political-context reading this article has adjudicated against. Sudhir Krishnaswamy's Democracy and Constitutionalism in India (2009) supplies the analytic vocabulary, constituent power against amending power, in which the doctrinal dispute is most precisely stated. The three secondary authorities disagree with one another in emphasis and partly in their conclusions, and the article's adjudication has proceeded by testing each against the primary record rather than by averaging them. Where the brief that commissioned this article conflicted with the record, the record has been followed and the correction flagged here: the hearing occupied sixty eight sitting days rather than the seventy five sometimes stated, and the Attorney General who led for the Union was Niren De, with Seervai appearing for Maharashtra, a distinction loose accounts of the case sometimes reverse. Both corrections are of the kind the series' audit discipline exists to catch, and neither touches the article's thesis, which the corrected record, if anything, strengthens.

## The questions the verdict left open

A complete account of the case must record what it did not decide, because the openings were as consequential as the holdings and were themselves products of the procedure. The verdict fixed no catalogue. Sikri's five features, Shelat and Grover's longer list, Hegde and Mukherjea's foundations and Khanna's refusal to enumerate left later benches with a principle and no schedule, and the identification of basic features proceeded case by case for decades, adding the rule of law, judicial review, free and fair elections, the balance between Parts III and IV, and other features as particular amendments forced the question. The openness was a coalition artefact: a shared catalogue at the level of Shelat and Grover would have cost the majority its seventh vote, and Khanna's case-by-case method was the price of the holding itself. The verdict likewise fixed no test of damage. The opinions speak of damaging, destroying, abrogating and altering the structure, in varying formulations, and the calibration of how much change a feature can bear before an amendment crosses the line was left to later adjudication, which is why the doctrine's applications have the character of successive approximations rather than deductions. The position of laws protected by the first part of Article 31C was left in a deliberate half-light: the Court upheld the article's precedence for laws giving effect to Article 39(b) and 39(c) while striking the clause that barred review of the legislature's declaration, leaving open the precise extent to which a protected law's nexus to those principles could be examined, a question later benches had to answer. And the verdict left the petitioner, and every petitioner after him, with a doctrine that protected the Constitution's identity rather than his property: the fundamental right to property emerged from Kesavananda amendable in full, Khanna expressly declining to place it within the protected structure, so that the right whose defence had driven twenty years of conflict between Parliament and the Court was, by the case that limited Parliament, surrendered to Parliament's power, and was in due course removed from Part III by the Forty-fourth Amendment. These openings are not blemishes on the verdict. They are the shape a seven to six court, writing eleven opinions in a month, was bound to leave, and the pillar's later pieces trace how each opening was worked by the benches that inherited it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Kesavananda Bharati case?

Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala is the Supreme Court of India's judgment of 24 April 1973 on the limits of Parliament's power to amend the Constitution. A bench of thirteen judges, the largest assembled to that date, heard a Kerala religious institution's challenge to land reform legislation together with challenges to the Twenty-fourth, Twenty-fifth and Twenty-ninth Amendments, and held by seven votes to six that Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution, including the fundamental rights, but may not alter its basic structure or framework. The case overruled the 1967 Golaknath bar on amending fundamental rights while replacing it with the basic structure limit, and it remains the foundation of Indian amendment law, the source from which every later judgment on the amending power proceeds.

### Q: Who is Kesavananda Bharati?

Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru was the Mahant, the pontiff and head, of the Edneer Mutt, a Hindu religious institution at Edneer in the Kasaragod district of Kerala. When the Kerala legislature's land reform amendments of 1969 and 1971 subjected the mutt's agricultural lands to ceiling and tenancy provisions, he petitioned the Supreme Court under Article 32 in 1970, invoking the religious denomination's rights under Articles 25 and 26 and the property guarantees of Articles 14, 19 and 31. His petition became the lead petition among six, and his name attached permanently to the case, although the challenge to the Kerala laws themselves failed, the laws were upheld through the Twenty-ninth Amendment, and on the record preserved by counsel in the case he never met Nani Palkhivala, the advocate who argued his petition into constitutional history.

### Q: Why was a 13-judge bench constituted?

A bench of thirteen was constituted because the case required the Supreme Court to reconsider its own decision in Golaknath v State of Punjab (1967), which had been delivered by a bench of eleven judges, and the Court's convention permits a precedent to be reconsidered only by a bench larger than the one that decided it. Chief Justice S. M. Sikri accordingly assembled thirteen judges, the entire working strength of the senior Court and the largest constitution bench in its history to that point, to decide whether Golaknath was right, whether the Twenty-fourth Amendment validly restored Parliament's amending power over fundamental rights, and whether the Twenty-fifth and Twenty-ninth Amendments were valid. The size also carried institutional meaning: a question touching the Constitution's identity was answered by the whole Court, so that the verdict, whichever way it went, would bind as the institution's act and not as the preference of a smaller panel.

### Q: What was the 7-6 split?

The seven to six split was the division of the thirteen judges on the case's central question, whether Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 was limited by the Constitution's basic structure. Seven judges, Chief Justice Sikri and Justices Shelat, Hegde, Grover, Reddy, Khanna and Mukherjea, held that the power was limited and that an amendment damaging or destroying the basic structure was void. Six judges, Justices Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi and Chandrachud, held that the power was unlimited and extended to every provision. The split was exact only on that question: on other issues the alignments differed, the substitution of amount for compensation in Article 31 being upheld unanimously, and the judges in the majority reached the limit by different reasoning, Justice Khanna's construction of the word amendment supplying the seventh and decisive vote.

### Q: Who delivered the lead opinion in Kesavananda 1973?

No single opinion speaks for the Court in Kesavananda, because the thirteen judges produced eleven separate opinions, but the lead opinion in order, seniority and influence is that of Chief Justice S. M. Sikri, who presided over the bench and wrote for himself. Sikri held that the amending power did not extend to damaging or destroying the Constitution's basic features, which he listed as the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form of government, the secular character of the Constitution, the separation of powers and the federal character of the Constitution. His opinion is conventionally read first and frames the majority's position, although the operative reasoning on which the seven to six majority actually rested was Justice Khanna's narrower opinion, and the Court's conclusions were formally stated in the summary signed by nine judges rather than in any one opinion.

### Q: What is the summary signed by nine judges?

The summary is the short document, headed as the view of the majority, in which the conclusions of the Kesavananda bench were stated as a set of propositions and which was signed, on the day the opinions were delivered, 24 April 1973, by nine of the thirteen judges: Chief Justice Sikri and Justices Shelat, Hegde, Grover, Reddy, Palekar, Khanna, Mukherjea and Chandrachud. Justices Ray, Mathew, Beg and Dwivedi did not sign. The summary recorded that Golaknath had been overruled, that Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution, and that the Twenty-fourth, Twenty-fifth and Twenty-ninth Amendments were valid subject to the striking of the second part of Article 31C. Because the eleven opinions yielded no single ratio, the summary became the citable statement of the holding, and its authority has been debated by scholars ever since.

### Q: How long did the Kesavananda hearing last?

The hearing opened on 31 October 1972 and the arguments closed on 23 March 1973, occupying sixty eight sitting days of argument spread across that span of nearly five months, the longest constitutional hearing the Supreme Court had conducted. Six petitions and some twenty intervenors were heard; Nani Palkhivala's opening for the petitioners ran for weeks, the Attorney General Niren De and H. M. Seervai answered for the governments, and the bench questioned counsel throughout on the text of Article 368, the Constituent Assembly debates and the comparative law of amendment. The length was driven by the questions referred, which required Golaknath and the whole line of amendment cases to be re-argued from first principles, and it was compressed at the end by two pressures, Justice Beg's illness during February and March 1973 and Chief Justice Sikri's retirement on 25 April 1973, the day after judgment.

### Q: Who were the thirteen judges on the Kesavananda bench?

The bench consisted of Chief Justice S. M. Sikri and Justices J. M. Shelat, K. S. Hegde, A. N. Grover, A. N. Ray, P. Jaganmohan Reddy, D. G. Palekar, H. R. Khanna, K. K. Mathew, M. H. Beg, S. N. Dwivedi, A. K. Mukherjea and Y. V. Chandrachud. Three of them, Sikri, Shelat and Hegde, had sat in Golaknath in 1967, Sikri in the minority and the other two in the majority. In Kesavananda the seven who held the amending power limited were Sikri, Shelat, Hegde, Grover, Reddy, Khanna and Mukherjea, and the six who held it unlimited were Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi and Chandrachud. The eleven opinions comprised Sikri's, two joint opinions, Shelat with Grover and Hegde with Mukherjea, and separate opinions by each of the remaining eight judges.

### Q: What did the bench hold on the validity of the 24th Amendment?

The bench upheld the Twenty-fourth Amendment. All thirteen judges accepted, in substance, that the amendment was a valid exercise of the amending power: Article 368 itself contained the power to amend, the amendment had clarified that a constitutional amendment is not law within Article 13, and Parliament could amend the fundamental rights in Part III, so that Golaknath, which had forbidden such amendment, was overruled. The division of the Court was not about the Twenty-fourth Amendment's existence as law but about the reach of the power it declared: the seven judges of the majority held that the power so restored remained subject to the basic structure limit, while the six of the minority held that the power, once acknowledged, was plenary and subject to no judicially enforceable limit at all.

### Q: What did the bench decide about the 29th Amendment and the Kerala land reform laws?

The bench upheld the Twenty-ninth Amendment, which had placed the Kerala Land Reforms (Amendment) Act of 1969 and the further amendment of 1971 into the Ninth Schedule of the Constitution, where Article 31B protects listed laws from challenge under Part III. The consequence for the lead petitioner was defeat on his own ground: Kesavananda Bharati's challenge to the land reform legislation governing the Edneer Mutt's lands failed, and the mutt's lands remained subject to the ceiling and tenancy provisions he had petitioned against. The outcome captures the case's characteristic distribution of results, in which the petitioner lost his dispute while winning the constitutional principle he had advanced, the government's statutes survived while its claim to unlimited amending power was rejected, and the only provision struck down anywhere in the case was the declaration clause of Article 31C in the Twenty-fifth Amendment.

### Q: What had Golaknath decided in 1967, and which part of it did Kesavananda overrule?

In Golaknath v State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967 by eleven judges dividing six to five under Chief Justice K. Subba Rao, the Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend the fundamental rights in Part III, because a constitutional amendment was law for the purposes of Article 13 and Article 13 prohibited laws abridging fundamental rights; the ruling was applied prospectively, so amendments already enacted were left undisturbed. Kesavananda overruled that holding: nine of the thirteen judges held that Parliament could amend Part III, and the fundamental rights ceased to be unamendable. What Kesavananda preserved from the impulse behind Golaknath was its anxiety, not its rule, substituting for the absolute bar on amending rights a limit of a different kind, the basic structure limit, which left every right amendable while placing the Constitution's framework beyond the amending power's reach.

### Q: How did Justice Khanna's reasoning differ from the other judges who upheld a limit on the amending power?

Justice Khanna reached the limit by a different road from the six judges who sat with him in the majority. Sikri, Shelat, Grover, Hegde, Mukherjea and Reddy relied substantially on limitations implied from the Constitution's scheme and from the essential character of its features. Khanna expressly rejected the theory of implied limitations and held the amending power plenary within its field, extending to the fundamental rights themselves; his limit came from the meaning of the word amendment, which in his reading postulates that the old Constitution survives without loss of its identity despite the change made, so that the power to amend cannot extend to abrogating the Constitution or destroying its basic structure or framework. He also declined to treat the right to property as part of the basic structure, which several of his majority colleagues' catalogues implied, and his narrower route is the reasoning on which the seven to six holding operatively rests.

### Q: What happened to the three senior judges of the majority when the Chief Justice retired in April 1973?

When Chief Justice Sikri retired on 25 April 1973, the government appointed Justice A. N. Ray, a member of the Kesavananda minority who had held the amending power unlimited, as Chief Justice of India, passing over the three judges senior to him in the Court, Justices Shelat, Hegde and Grover, all three of whom had been members of the Kesavananda majority. All three resigned from the Court in protest within days. The supersession broke the seniority convention observed in every appointment of a Chief Justice since 1950, and the government defended it publicly in the language of a committed judiciary, maintaining through its Law Minister H. R. Gokhale and others that the executive was entitled to choose a Chief Justice whose philosophy of the Constitution, including his views on the amending power, it found acceptable.

### Q: What did Attorney General Niren De argue for the Union of India?

Niren De, the Attorney General for India, argued that the amending power under Article 368 was plenary and in the nature of a sovereign constituent power: Parliament amending the Constitution exercised the constituent authority of the people, a constitutional amendment was not law within Article 13, and no limitation on the power could be implied by a court from the Constitution's structure or spirit. On the Union's submission, as he candidly maintained under questioning from the bench, Parliament could in law amend any provision of the Constitution, including the fundamental rights and the democratic form itself, and the remedy for an abusive amendment lay with the electorate rather than with judicial review. The argument was the strongest available statement of parliamentary sovereignty within a written constitution, it was supported for the states by H. M. Seervai, and it persuaded six of the thirteen judges, failing by a single vote.

### Q: What does T. R. Andhyarujina's account add to the reported judgments?

T. R. Andhyarujina's The Kesavananda Bharati Case: The Untold Story of Struggle for Supremacy by Supreme Court and Parliament (2011) adds the proceeding as the judgments do not record it. Andhyarujina had appeared in the case as a junior counsel, and his book draws on his own notes, on the hearing record and on the papers of H. M. Seervai to reconstruct the sixty eight days of argument, the bench's questioning, the anxiety during Justice Beg's illness in February and March 1973, the writing of the eleven opinions against Chief Justice Sikri's retirement, and the preparation and signing of the nine-judge summary, whose accuracy as a record of the majorities the book defends against Seervai's printed critique. It also preserves human detail the reports omit, including the fact that the petitioner never met his counsel, Nani Palkhivala, and it remains the standard insider source for the case's institutional history.

### Q: Why did the Union government seek a review of Kesavananda in 1975, and why was the review bench dissolved?

In November 1975, days after a five-judge bench had applied Kesavananda in Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain to strike down part of the Thirty-ninth Amendment, Chief Justice A. N. Ray constituted a bench of thirteen judges to review the Kesavananda decision on the Union's application, in the middle of the Emergency, when the amending power the doctrine limited was being exercised extensively. The review bench sat on 10 and 11 November 1975, and Nani Palkhivala, arguing against the review, submitted that no ground for reconsideration had been shown, that the applicants had identified no error in the 1973 reasoning, and that a bench assembled in those circumstances to remove the one judicial limit on amendment would gravely damage the Court. On 12 November 1975 the bench was dissolved without any decision, and Kesavananda survived its only direct institutional challenge by the collapse of the proceeding convened to dislodge it.

### Q: Why do scholars dispute the authority of the nine-judge summary?

Scholars dispute the summary because of its form and its signatures. Formally, a ratio decidendi is supposed to be found in a court's reasoning, and the summary is not a twelfth opinion but a certified statement of conclusions, prepared under the Chief Justice's direction in the final days of his tenure; H. M. Seervai argued in his Constitutional Law of India that it was not a judgment of the Court and that its central proposition concealed divergent reasoning among the seven judges of the majority. The signatures deepen the puzzle, since two signatories, Justices Palekar and Chandrachud, had held in their opinions that no basic structure limit existed. T. R. Andhyarujina's reply defends the summary as an accurate record of the proposition-by-proposition majorities, signed by those two judges as a certification of the count, and Chief Justice Chandrachud stated in Minerva Mills in 1980 that, legitimate or not, the summary correctly reflected the majority view.

### Q: How did the Kerala land reform legislation bring the Edneer Mutt's case before the Court?

The Kerala Land Reforms (Amendment) Act of 1969 and the further amendment of 1971 imposed ceilings on landholdings, conferred rights on cultivating tenants and vested surplus land in the state, and as applied to the Edneer Mutt they restricted the mutt's management and enjoyment of the agricultural lands whose income sustained its religious and charitable work. Its head, Kesavananda Bharati, petitioned the Supreme Court under Article 32 in 1970, contending that the legislation violated the religious denomination's rights under Articles 25 and 26 and the guarantees of Articles 14, 19 and 31. Because Parliament had shielded the legislation with the Twenty-ninth Amendment's placement of the Acts in the Ninth Schedule, and had rebuilt the amending power through the Twenty-fourth and Twenty-fifth Amendments, the mutt's property dispute could not be decided without deciding the reach of the amending power itself, which carried the petition from Kasaragod to a bench of thirteen judges.

### Q: What did the six judges in the minority hold about the amending power under Article 368?

The six judges in the minority, Justices Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi and Chandrachud, held that the amending power under Article 368 was unlimited: Parliament could amend every provision of the Constitution, including the fundamental rights, and no basic structure or other implied limit restricted the power. They differed in emphasis. Justice Ray's opinion gave the position its fullest statement, treating the amending power as the people's constituent authority exercised through Parliament and warning against judicial limits drawn from the Constitution's supposed spirit. Justice Mathew doubted that the basic structure could be defined with the precision a legal limit requires. Justice Chandrachud held that even the Preamble was amendable and that the Constitution's identity was not a judicially enforceable restraint. In their common conclusion, the apprehended abuse of the amending power was a matter for the electorate, not for the courts.

### Q: What distinction did Justice Khanna draw between amending the Constitution and abrogating it?

Justice Khanna drew the distinction from the word amendment itself. An amendment, he held, postulates that the old Constitution survives without loss of its identity despite the change that has been made: the power conferred by Article 368 is a power to change the Constitution while it remains the same Constitution, and it therefore cannot extend to abrogating the Constitution, destroying its basic structure or framework, or so altering it that its identity is lost and a different constitution stands in its place. Quoting Dieter Conrad's proposition that an amending body organised within a statutory scheme cannot, however unlimited the words of its power, change the fundamental pillars supporting its own authority, Khanna held every article amendable, the fundamental rights included, and the right to property unprotected by the limit, while the framework within which all amendment occurs remained beyond the power's reach.
