## The Verdict

The Nuremberg Indictment October 1945 is the most consequential charging document ever filed. On October 18 1945, the four chief prosecutors of the Allied powers lodged an indictment of approximately sixty-five pages against twenty-four surviving leaders of the German Reich, charging them on four counts that had never before been assembled in a single criminal proceeding: common plan or conspiracy, crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. The document did not merely accuse. It created, in the act of accusing, the legal vocabulary through which the twentieth century would subsequently name state crime. Every later tribunal, from Tokyo to The Hague, works in language the Nuremberg Indictment first put on paper.

The close read that follows examines the Indictment as a legal instrument rather than as a summary of the trial it produced. The trial, which opened on November 20 1945 and delivered its judgments on October 1 1946, has been exhaustively studied. The Indictment itself deserves the closer attention because it is the moment of invention. In it, Anglo-American conspiracy doctrine was extended to international law for the first time; aggressive war, which no court had ever treated as an individual crime, was made prosecutable; the traditional law of war was codified for individual defendants; and a new category, crimes against humanity, was defined to reach persecution that the older law could not touch, including persecution a state inflicted on its own citizens before any war began. The Indictment's four counts are a map of everything the law lacked and everything the prosecutors believed it needed.

The document must also be read against its complications, and this close read does not evade them. The Indictment was the product of a coalition that had itself committed acts the document's categories could have described: the Soviet Union's deportations and the Anglo-American area bombing of German cities were not on trial, and the charge that Nuremberg was victor's justice has legitimate content that no honest account can dismiss. The conspiracy count stretched Anglo-American doctrine into territory that continental lawyers found alien, and the crimes-against-peace count applied to acts committed before the law it invoked had been written, raising ex post facto concerns that remain live in jurisprudence. The Indictment's achievement and its limitations are not separable. It founded international criminal law precisely by doing things the law had never done, and the things it had never done are exactly what its critics dispute.

What the close read establishes, finally, is the committee-architecture character of the achievement. The Indictment was negotiated among four legal systems, drafted by prosecutors answerable to four governments, and filed under a charter that required the agreement of all four powers. No command architecture produced, or could have produced, an equivalent document. The Axis approach to law was the Führerprinzip, the commander's will as the source of legality. The Indictment's approach was the opposite: legality as a common construction, built by negotiation among victors who disagreed about almost everything except that the defeated leadership should face judgment rather than summary execution. That construction, with all its flaws, is the foundation on which later international criminal law stands.

### What was the Nuremberg Indictment and when was it filed?

It was the charging document for the International Military Tribunal, filed on October 18 1945 in Berlin by the chief prosecutors of the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and France. It named twenty-four defendants and charged them on four counts: conspiracy, crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity.

### Who were the twenty-four defendants named in the Indictment?

They spanned the Nazi leadership: political figures like Goring, Hess, and Ribbentrop; military commanders Keitel, Jodl, Donitz, and Raeder; ideologues Rosenberg and Streicher; administrators Frank and Frick; economists Schacht and Funk; armaments chief Speer; labor plenipotentiary Sauckel; terror officials Kaltenbrunner and Seyss-Inquart; and others including Papen, Neurath, Schirach, Fritzsche, Bormann, Krupp, and Ley.

### What were the four counts of the Indictment?

Count One charged a common plan or conspiracy to commit the crimes in the other counts. Count Two charged crimes against peace, meaning the planning and waging of aggressive war. Count Three charged war crimes under the established laws of war. Count Four charged crimes against humanity, a new category covering murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, and persecution of civilian populations.

### What happened to the defendants at trial?

The trial opened November 20 1945 and the judgments came October 1 1946. Twelve defendants received death sentences, three received life imprisonment, four received fixed prison terms, and three were acquitted. Two of the twenty-four never stood trial: Robert Ley died by suicide before proceedings began, and Gustav Krupp was severed for medical incapacity.

### Why does the Indictment matter beyond the trial itself?

It founded international criminal law. Its categories, individual responsibility for state crime, rejection of superior orders as a complete defense, rejection of head-of-state immunity, and crimes against humanity as a distinct offense, became the template for the Genocide Convention, the ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda, and the Rome Statute that created the International Criminal Court.

## The Moment: Berlin, October 18 1945

The filing took place in Berlin, in the shadow of a war that had ended five months earlier. On October 18 1945, the four chief prosecutors, Robert H. Jackson for the United States, Hartley Shawcross for Britain, Roman Rudenko for the Soviet Union, and Francois de Menthon for France, lodged the Indictment with the International Military Tribunal. The choice of Berlin was symbolic and practical at once. Berlin was the capital of the defeated Reich, and the Allied Control Council sat there. Filing the charges in the city the defendants had ruled announced that the law claimed jurisdiction where the regime had claimed sovereignty.

The document ran to approximately sixty-five pages. Its length reflected its ambition. A conventional indictment identifies defendants, specifies offenses, and cites the statutes violated. This one had to do something more: it had to establish that the tribunal trying the case possessed authority, that the offenses charged were offenses at all, and that the defendants could be held individually responsible for acts committed through the machinery of a state. The preamble, the defendant list, the organizational descriptions, the four counts, and the appendices each carried part of this burden. The structure of the document is the argument that the trial was lawful.

The timing matters for understanding what the Indictment could and could not do. It was filed two months after the London Charter of August 8 1945 created the tribunal, and one month before the trial opened in Nuremberg on November 20. The prosecutors worked under intense time pressure, assembling evidence from captured German records, interrogations, and documents still being translated. The haste shows in the document. Some charges were framed broadly, some organizational allegations were stated in terms the trial would later narrow, and the conspiracy count in particular bore the marks of a theory assembled to fit evidence that was still arriving. The German surrender that had made the trial possible is covered in [the series account of the May 1945 capitulation](/2005/12/27/german-surrender-may-1945-reims-berlin/), and the close read will not revisit the war's end. Its subject is what the victors built on the surrender's foundation.

## The London Charter: The Indictment's Legal Foundation

The Indictment drew its authority from the London Charter, the agreement signed on August 8 1945 by the four powers establishing the International Military Tribunal. The Charter defined the tribunal's jurisdiction, specified the three categories of crime within its competence (crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity, with conspiracy added as a mode of liability), and set the procedural rules. The Indictment was, in formal terms, the Charter applied to twenty-four named individuals. Every count in the document cites the Charter's articles, and the preamble's first task is to establish the tribunal's authority by reference to the agreement that created it.

The Charter itself was a negotiated compromise among legal traditions that disagreed fundamentally about criminal procedure. The Anglo-American prosecutors worked within an adversarial tradition; the Soviet and French delegations within inquisitorial ones. The Charter blended them: the trial would be adversarial in form, with prosecution and defense presenting cases, but the judges would have inquisitorial powers to question witnesses and control proceedings. The Indictment reflects this blend in its organization, charging in the Anglo-American style while framing the charges within a document whose authority derived from an international agreement rather than a statute passed by a legislature.

One provision of the Yalta agreements had anticipated the war-crimes machinery: the Yalta Protocol's section on major war criminals committed the Allies to the trial of the principal offenders. The connection is traced in [the close read of the Yalta Protocol](/2004/12/02/yalta-protocol-february-1945/), and the point for this article is narrower. By October 1945, the commitment had been converted into an institution with a charter, a tribunal, and now an indictment. The speed of the conversion, from February's protocol language to August's charter to October's charges, shows the committee architecture operating at its characteristic pace: slow to agree, then rapid to implement once agreement was reached.

## Jackson and the Prosecution

Robert H. Jackson, the chief American prosecutor, was the Indictment's principal architect and its most eloquent defender. Jackson was an Associate Justice of the United States Supreme Court who had taken leave from the Court to serve as the American representative in the London negotiations and then as chief prosecutor. His dual standing, as a sitting justice and as the advocate for the prosecution, gave the proceedings a gravity they might otherwise have lacked, and it also exposed him to the charge that he had compromised judicial detachment by becoming a party to the case. Jackson accepted the tension and argued that the law required exactly this kind of commitment: the crimes were so enormous that the ordinary separation of judge and prosecutor could not be preserved without abandoning the victims.

The prosecution teams worked under Jackson's coordination but retained national characters. The British team under Shawcross emphasized the documentary record and the common-law conspiracy tradition. The Soviet team under Rudenko pressed for the broadest possible charges, including the aggressive-war counts, and supplied much of the evidence on atrocities in the occupied east. The French team, first under de Menthon and later under Auguste Champetier de Ribes, contributed the case on the occupation of France and the Low Countries. The Indictment's four counts had to satisfy all four teams, and the compromises are visible in the text: the conspiracy count is Anglo-American in conception, the crimes-against-peace count reflects Soviet insistence, and the war-crimes count codifies the Hague and Geneva traditions all four shared.

Jackson's personal imprint is strongest in the Indictment's framing of individual responsibility. The document insists, count after count, that the defendants are charged as individuals for acts committed through organizations, and that holding office, receiving orders, or acting as head of state provides no immunity. This was the Charter's Article 7 and Article 8 made concrete: official position would not free a defendant, and superior orders would not free him either, though they might mitigate punishment. The Indictment thus announced, before a single witness testified, the trial's central legal revolution. The state would not shield the individual.

## The Defendants: The Political Leadership

The Indictment's defendant list was a portrait of the regime's surviving elite, and its political core began with Hermann Goring. Goring, the Reich Marshal and Hitler's designated successor until the regime's final days, was the highest-ranking defendant and the prosecution's central figure. He had built the Luftwaffe, directed the Four Year Plan, and signed the directive that authorized Heydrich to prepare the organizational machinery for what the regime called the final solution. Goring's presence gave the trial its symbolic center: the number two man of the regime, answering for the regime.

Rudolf Hess, the Deputy Fuhrer who had flown to Scotland in May 1941 on his unauthorized peace mission, represented the party's early ideological core. Joachim von Ribbentrop, the Foreign Minister, embodied the regime's diplomatic criminality: the negotiation of aggressive treaties, the betrayal of non-aggression pacts, and the diplomatic cover for wars of conquest. Franz von Papen, the conservative Vice Chancellor of 1933 who had helped bring Hitler to power, represented the complicity of the old elite. Konstantin von Neurath, Foreign Minister from 1933 to 1938 and later Protector of Bohemia and Moravia, represented the continuity between the regime's early diplomatic deception and its later occupation brutality. Wilhelm Frick, the Interior Minister, had provided the legal machinery of the regime, drafting the decrees that stripped Jews of citizenship and authorized the administrative apparatus of persecution. Hans Frank, the Governor-General of occupied Poland, had ruled a territory in which the regime's extermination policies were implemented at industrial scale.

The political list shows the Indictment's theory of responsibility. It did not charge the German state or the Nazi Party as such in the dock, though it named organizations in its appendices. It charged individuals, and it chose them to represent the regime's functions: the successor, the deputy, the diplomat, the enabler, the administrator, the governor. The selection was also shaped by absence. Hitler was dead, Himmler was dead, Goebbels was dead, and Heydrich had been assassinated in 1942. The Indictment could try only the living, and the living were a fragment of the leadership that had made the decisions. The question of Hitler's own responsibility, and the documentary record of his orders, is examined in [the series' myth-bust on Hitler's verbal Holocaust orders](/2005/02/11/hitler-ordered-holocaust-verbally-myth/), which this close read will not duplicate. The Indictment's answer to the absence was the conspiracy count: the dead man's plan, proved through the living men's participation.

## The Defendants: The Military Command

The military defendants tested the Indictment's boldest claim: that soldiers could be tried for the wars they fought. Wilhelm Keitel, the Chief of Staff of the Armed Forces High Command, was the senior military defendant. Keitel had signed the orders for the Commissar Decree, the Commando Order, and the Night and Fog decree, the instruments through which the regime's criminality was transmitted to the Wehrmacht. His defense, that he was a soldier obeying the head of state, was the superior-orders argument in its purest form, and the tribunal's rejection of it became the trial's most cited holding.

Alfred Jodl, the Chief of Operations Staff, was the regime's principal strategic planner in uniform. Karl Donitz, the U-boat commander who had succeeded Hitler as head of state in the regime's final days, represented the navy's unrestricted submarine warfare and the regime's last-ditch continuity. Erich Raeder, the Navy's Commander-in-Chief until 1943, represented the longer military tradition that had served the regime from its consolidation through the war's middle years. Together the four covered the armed forces' institutional complicity across the war's full span.

The military list provoked the sharpest contemporary criticism of the Indictment. Professional soldiers in the Allied countries, and many civilians, regarded the prosecution of defeated commanders as a dangerous precedent: the laws of war had traditionally bound states, and extending individual criminal liability to officers who planned campaigns struck many as converting defeat into crime. The Indictment's answer was the distinction between fighting a war and waging an aggressive one, and between lawful combat and the criminal orders the defendants had signed. Keitel's signature on the Commissar Decree, which ordered the murder of captured Soviet political officers, was not soldiering in any army's tradition. The Indictment made the signature the crime.

## The Defendants: Ideology and Propaganda

The Indictment reached beyond decision-makers to the men who had manufactured the regime's ideas and its hatreds. Alfred Rosenberg, the party's chief ideologue and Reich Minister for the Occupied Eastern Territories, had supplied the racial doctrine that justified the war in the east as a war of extermination. His ministry administered territories in which the doctrine was implemented. Julius Streicher, the editor of the violently antisemitic newspaper Der Sturmer, was charged not for commanding troops or signing decrees but for twenty-five years of incitement: the Indictment treated his propaganda as a crime against humanity in itself, a charge that tested the boundary between speech and participation.

Baldur von Schirach, the Hitler Youth leader and later Gauleiter of Vienna, represented the corruption of a generation: the Indictment charged him with the deportation of Vienna's Jews and with the militarization of German youth. Hans Fritzsche, the Propaganda Ministry's radio chief, was the Indictment's most controversial minor defendant, charged essentially for broadcasting the regime's lies. His eventual acquittal would become evidence, cited by both defenders and critics of the trial, that the tribunal could distinguish propaganda from criminality.

The ideology defendants embodied the Indictment's most expansive theory: that the manufacture of hatred was itself a step in the commission of the crimes. Streicher's conviction on Count Four, the only count on which he was found guilty, established that incitement to genocide could be punished as a crime against humanity even without proof that the inciter ordered a specific murder. The holding was narrow, tied to the scale and directness of Streicher's twenty-five-year campaign, and the tribunal's care in limiting it showed in Fritzsche's acquittal. The Indictment had reached for a principle; the judgment trimmed it to fit the evidence.

## The Defendants: Economy, Labor, and Industry

The Indictment's economic defendants charged that the regime's wars were built by bankers and industrialists as well as by generals. Hjalmar Schacht, the Reichsbank president and Economics Minister until 1937, had engineered the financial machinery of rearmament. His presence in the dock was the prosecution's most debated choice: Schacht had fallen from favor before the war, had been imprisoned by the regime after the July 1944 plot, and his defense argued that economic preparation was not the same as planning aggression. Walther Funk, Schacht's successor, had administered the wartime economy and received the gold and valuables stripped from concentration camp victims into the Reichsbank's vaults.

Albert Speer, the Armaments Minister, was the Indictment's most capable defendant and its most ambiguous. Speer had organized the war economy that sustained Germany's resistance through 1944, and he did so substantially through forced labor. His defense, a calculated acceptance of general responsibility combined with denial of specific knowledge of extermination, would become the most studied performance of the trial. Fritz Sauckel, the Plenipotentiary for Labor, had directed the deportation of millions of forced laborers from occupied Europe. His was the bluntest case: the Indictment charged him with enslavement on a continental scale, and the evidence was the administrative record of his own agency.

Gustav Krupp, the steel industrialist, was named in the Indictment but never tried; his mental and physical incapacity led the tribunal to sever his case. The prosecution's attempt to substitute his son Alfried was rejected, and the Krupp matter became the Indictment's acknowledged imperfection: the industrialist whose factories had used slave labor escaped judgment at Nuremberg, though the subsequent industrialist trials would address the sector. The economic defendants as a group tested whether the law could reach the men who built the weapons without firing them. The verdicts, convictions for Funk, Speer, and Sauckel, acquittal for Schacht, suggest the tribunal's answer: it could, but only where the connection between the desk and the crime was proved beyond the general fact of working for the regime.

## The Defendants: The Terror Apparatus

The Indictment's final group comprised the men who had operated the regime's machinery of repression. Ernst Kaltenbrunner, who had succeeded Heydrich as chief of the Reich Security Main Office, commanded the Gestapo, the criminal police, and the security service across the Reich and occupied Europe. His was the most direct institutional responsibility for the extermination apparatus among the living defendants. Arthur Seyss-Inquart, the Reich Commissioner for the occupied Netherlands and earlier the agent of Austria's absorption, represented the civilian administration of occupation terror. Martin Bormann, Hitler's secretary and the party chancellery chief, was indicted in absentia; his whereabouts were unknown, and the tribunal proceeded against him in his absence, the Indictment's one trial of an empty chair.

Robert Ley, the German Labor Front chief, was named in the Indictment but died by suicide in his cell on October 25 1945, a week after the filing and before the trial opened. His death, like Krupp's severance, reduced the twenty-four to twenty-two actual defendants in the courtroom, with Bormann tried unseen. The Indictment's terror-apparatus selections show its organizational theory at work: Kaltenbrunner for the security police system, Seyss-Inquart for occupation administration, Bormann for the party machinery, Ley for the labor front's role in suppressing independent unions. The regime was being disassembled function by function, and each function was given a defendant.

The inclusion of Bormann in absentia was the Indictment's most procedurally aggressive choice. Anglo-American law disfavored trials without the accused present; the Charter permitted them, and the tribunal allowed it for Bormann while appointing defense counsel to represent his interests. The conviction and death sentence imposed on the absent Bormann, never executed because he was never found, became a standing illustration of the trial's determination to leave no major figure unjudged. Whether the determination served justice or theater remains debated, and the debate is part of the Indictment's legacy as much as its holdings.

## Count One: Common Plan or Conspiracy

Count One charged that the defendants, during a period of years preceding May 8 1945, had participated as leaders, organizers, instigators, or accomplices in the formulation or execution of a common plan or conspiracy to commit, or which involved the commission of, crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity as defined in the Charter, and that each was individually responsible for his own acts and for all acts committed by any persons in execution of the plan. The count was the Indictment's connective tissue: it bound the twenty-four defendants to one another and to the dead leaders, making each responsible for the enterprise as a whole.

The conspiracy theory was substantially innovative, and substantially controversial. Anglo-American law knew conspiracy as a crime; continental European law largely did not, and the French and Soviet lawyers on the prosecution team had to be persuaded that the count was more than an American import. The Indictment's answer was functional: the Nazi regime had operated as a criminal enterprise, its leaders had planned together over years, and no other legal concept captured the collective character of the criminality. The count alleged specific overt acts, the planning of aggression, the persecution programs, the slave-labor system, as the manifestations of the plan.

The trial narrowed Count One considerably. The tribunal held that the conspiracy charge could be sustained only for the planning of aggressive war, not as a roving commission covering all the regime's crimes, and it convicted only eight defendants on the count. The narrowing was the judgment's most significant correction of the Indictment: the prosecutors had alleged a conspiracy encompassing everything, and the judges confined it to what the evidence showed had actually been jointly planned. The count survived, but in reduced form, and the reduction became a precedent in itself. Later tribunals would charge conspiracy, or its successor concept of joint criminal enterprise, with the Nuremberg narrowing in mind.

## Count Two: Crimes Against Peace

Count Two charged the planning, preparation, initiation, and waging of wars of aggression in violation of international treaties, agreements, and assurances, including the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, the Versailles Treaty, and the non-aggression treaties with Poland, Czechoslovakia, and the Soviet Union. The count listed the specific aggressions: Austria, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Denmark, Norway, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Yugoslavia, Greece, the Soviet Union, and the declaration of war against the United States. Each was presented as a separate criminal act of aggressive war.

The count was the Indictment's greatest legal innovation and its greatest vulnerability. No court had ever treated aggressive war as an individual crime. The Kellogg-Briand Pact had renounced war as an instrument of national policy, but it had provided no criminal penalties and no tribunal. The Indictment argued that the Pact, combined with the other treaties, had made aggressive war illegal, and that illegality implied individual criminal responsibility for those who planned it. The defense answered with the ex post facto objection: the defendants were being punished under law created after their acts. The tribunal's answer, that the law against aggression existed in the treaties and the Charter merely provided the forum, has been debated by jurists ever since.

The count's practical effect was to put the regime's foreign policy on trial as crime. Ribbentrop's diplomacy, Neurath's early deceptions, Keitel's and Jodl's operational planning, Raeder's naval preparations: all were presented not as statecraft but as steps in criminal aggression. The convictions on Count Two, sustained against the principal planners, established the principle that would later be codified as the crime of aggression in the Rome Statute. The principle's subsequent history, invoked rarely and controversially, shows both the count's ambition and its difficulty. Nuremberg declared aggressive war a crime; the world has found the declaration easier to affirm than to enforce.

## Count Three: War Crimes

Count Three was the Indictment's least innovative and most solidly grounded count. It charged murder, ill-treatment, and deportation to slave labor of civilian populations in occupied territory; murder and ill-treatment of prisoners of war; killing of hostages; plunder of public and private property; wanton destruction of cities, towns, and villages; and devastation not justified by military necessity. The count codified the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 and the Geneva Convention of 1929 for individual prosecution, applying to persons the rules that had previously bound only states.

The evidence under Count Three was the trial's most overwhelming. The prosecution presented the Commissar Decree and the Commando Order over Keitel's and Jodl's signatures; the records of Sauckel's labor deportations; the reports of the Einsatzgruppen, whose commander Otto Ohlendorf testified with chilling frankness about the murder of some ninety thousand persons by his unit; the documentation of hostage executions in occupied Europe; and the systematic plunder administered through Rosenberg's special task force. The count required no legal innovation because the law was settled; it required only proof, and the captured German records supplied it in abundance.

The count's significance lies in what it did to the laws of war. Before Nuremberg, violations of the Hague and Geneva rules were matters for state responsibility, diplomatic protest, and reprisal. After the Indictment, they were individual crimes triable before an international court. The transformation affected every subsequent conflict: the Geneva Conventions of 1949 were drafted with Nuremberg's individual-responsibility model in mind, and the grave-breaches regime they created descends directly from Count Three. The count was the Indictment's conservative revolution, changing everything by changing the defendant from the state to the person.

## Count Four: Crimes Against Humanity

Count Four charged murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, and other inhumane acts committed against any civilian population, before or during the war, and persecutions on political, racial, or religious grounds. The count was substantially new. Its two innovations defined its importance. First, it reached conduct against a state's own citizens, breaking the traditional rule that international law did not regulate what a government did to its own people. Second, it reached conduct before the war began, through the phrase "before or during the war," enabling prosecution of the pre-1939 persecution of German Jews and political opponents.

The count was the Indictment's answer to the Holocaust as a legal problem. The extermination of the Jews was not fully captured by the war-crimes count, which governed conduct against enemy populations in wartime, nor by the crimes-against-peace count, which governed aggression. A distinct category was needed for the regime's war against civilians as such, and Count Four supplied it. The evidence included the testimony on the extermination camps, the documentation of the mobile killing operations in the east, and the record of the persecution apparatus from 1933 onward. The organizational coordination of the extermination program, including the January 1942 ministerial conference that systematized deportations across occupied Europe, is examined in [the series account of the Wannsee Conference](/2006/06/27/wannsee-conference-january-1942-holocaust/), and this close read will not duplicate that analysis. The count treated the Holocaust not as an incident of the war but as a crime in its own right, a framing that shaped all subsequent legal and historical understanding of the genocide.

The count also carried the Indictment's most delicate jurisdictional question. By reaching pre-war conduct, it asserted that international law could judge a government's treatment of its own citizens even in peacetime, a claim that the tribunal itself handled cautiously. The judgment ultimately tied crimes against humanity to the war, requiring a nexus with armed conflict for conviction, and the pre-war reach of the count was narrowed in application. The narrowing did not diminish the category's future. When the ad hoc tribunals and the Rome Statute later defined crimes against humanity without requiring a war nexus, they were completing the work Count Four had begun.

## The Appendices: Organizations on Trial

Beyond the four counts against individuals, the Indictment's appendices named six Nazi organizations for a declaration of criminality: the Leadership Corps of the Nazi Party, the Gestapo, the SD (the SS security service), the SS, the SA, and the Reich Cabinet, along with the General Staff and High Command as a group. The organizational charges reflected the Indictment's theory that the regime's crimes were committed through institutions, and that declaring those institutions criminal would enable the subsequent prosecution of their members in national courts.

The appendices made the Indictment a longer document and a more ambitious one. They required the prosecution to prove not only what individuals had done but what organizations were: their structures, their membership, their purposes, their criminal activities. The trial heard extensive evidence on each, and the judgment's findings became the authoritative legal characterization of the Nazi institutional landscape. The Leadership Corps, the Gestapo, the SD, and the SS were declared criminal organizations. The SA, the Reich Cabinet, and the General Staff and High Command were not, the tribunal holding that the evidence did not support criminality findings against them as groups.

The organizational provisions were the Indictment's machinery for extending its reach beyond the twenty-four defendants. Under the Charter's framework, membership in a declared criminal organization could be punished by national courts, and the subsequent proceedings under Control Council Law Number Ten prosecuted thousands on this basis. The appendices thus converted the Indictment from a single trial's charging document into the foundation of the entire Allied prosecution program. The close read's artifact section maps the organizations against the tribunal's findings, showing where the Indictment's allegations were sustained and where they were not.

## Jackson's Opening Statement: November 21 1945

The trial opened on November 20 1945, and on the following day Robert H. Jackson delivered the opening statement for the prosecution, the single most famous courtroom address of the twentieth century. Jackson spoke for the better part of a day, and his statement established the philosophical framework within which the Indictment's four counts would be proved. Its central passages have been quoted ever since, and the close read must attend to them as the Indictment's rhetorical companion: the document charged, and Jackson explained why the charging was lawful.

The statement's most quoted passage addressed the tribunal's moral authority directly: "The wrongs which we seek to condemn and punish have been so calculated, so malignant, and so devastating, that civilization cannot tolerate their being ignored, because it cannot survive their being repeated. That four great nations, flushed with victory and stung with injury, stay the hand of vengeance and voluntarily submit their captive enemies to the judgment of the law is one of the most significant tributes that Power has ever paid to Reason." The passage was Jackson's answer to the victor's justice objection, made before the objection could be fully formed: the trial's legitimacy lay precisely in the victors' submission of their vengeance to legal process.

Jackson also confronted the ex post facto problem head-on. He argued that the law the Indictment applied was not invented for the occasion but declared from principles already present in treaties, customs, and the conscience of civilization. The argument's persuasiveness remains debated, but its frankness was the statement's strength. Jackson did not pretend the law was settled; he argued that unsettled law, honestly applied to unprecedented crimes, was preferable to the alternative, which was either impunity or summary execution. The statement thus did for the trial's legitimacy what the Indictment's preamble did for its authority: it made the case that law, even imperfect and novel law, was the right instrument.

## The Trial: Procedure and Defense

The trial that the Indictment initiated ran from November 20 1945 to October 1 1946, with the tribunal hearing evidence across more than four hundred sessions. The proceedings followed the Charter's blended procedure: adversarial in the presentation of cases, with each defendant represented by German counsel of his choosing, but with judges exercising active control. The prosecution presented its case through captured documents, a choice that gave the trial its distinctive character. Rather than relying primarily on witness testimony, the prosecutors read the regime's own records into evidence: the decrees over Keitel's signature, the minutes of planning conferences, the reports of the killing units, the correspondence of the ministries.

The defense cases varied by defendant and revealed the Indictment's pressure points. The superior-orders defense, advanced most purely by Keitel, argued that a soldier could not be criminally liable for obeying the head of state. The tribunal's rejection, holding that the orders in question were manifestly criminal and that Keitel had participated in their formulation rather than merely executing them, became the trial's most cited legal holding. The tu quoque defense, advanced notably for Donitz regarding unrestricted submarine warfare, argued that the Allies had committed equivalent acts; the tribunal found the argument legally irrelevant to guilt, though it considered it in sentencing. The ignorance defense, advanced most skillfully by Speer, argued that the defendant had not known of the extermination program's full scope; the tribunal weighed it against the documentary record.

The trial's documentary method was the Indictment's vindication and its limitation. The captured records proved the charges beyond serious dispute, and the reliance on documents rather than coerced testimony gave the proceedings an evidentiary solidity that summary justice could never have achieved. But the method also meant the trial was, in essence, the German bureaucracy testifying against itself, and the defendants' individual agency sometimes disappeared into the paper. The Indictment had charged persons; the evidence often showed systems. The tribunal's careful differentiation among defendants in its judgments, acquitting three and varying sentences widely, showed the judges working to keep the person in view.

## The Verdicts: October 1 1946

The tribunal delivered its judgments on September 30 and October 1 1946, and the verdicts reflected the Indictment's partial vindication. Twelve defendants were sentenced to death: Goring, Ribbentrop, Keitel, Kaltenbrunner, Rosenberg, Frank, Frick, Streicher, Sauckel, Jodl, Seyss-Inquart, and Bormann in absentia. Three received life imprisonment: Hess, Funk, and Raeder. Four received fixed terms: Donitz ten years, Schirach twenty, Speer twenty, Neurath fifteen. Three were acquitted: Schacht, Papen, and Fritzsche. The distribution showed the tribunal discriminating among levels of responsibility rather than condemning the group as a whole.

The acquittals were as significant as the convictions. Schacht's acquittal held that economic preparation for war, without proof of participation in planning aggression, was not criminal under the Charter. Papen's acquittal found his pre-war political maneuvering insufficient for conviction. Fritzsche's acquittal distinguished between broadcasting propaganda and criminal participation, limiting the Indictment's reach into speech. The three acquittals answered the charge that the trial was a mere formality: a tribunal that acquits nearly one in seven defendants is exercising judgment, not ratifying vengeance.

The conspiracy count's narrowing was the verdicts' most important doctrinal outcome. The tribunal sustained Count One only for the planning of aggressive war and convicted eight defendants on it, rejecting the prosecution's broader theory of a conspiracy encompassing all the regime's crimes. The narrowing showed the judges correcting the Indictment where its ambition exceeded its proof, and the correction strengthened rather than weakened the trial's authority. An indictment that is sustained in part and trimmed in part has been judged, not merely affirmed.

## Sentences and the October 16 Executions

The sentences were carried out on October 16 1946, with one dramatic exception. Hermann Goring, the highest-ranking defendant, died by suicide in his cell on the night of October 15, taking a cyanide capsule that had eluded the guards' searches. His death cheated the gallows and deprived the proceedings of their symbolic culmination, and it remains the trial's most discussed irregularity. The ten remaining death sentences were executed by hanging in the Nuremberg prison gymnasium in the early hours of October 16: Ribbentrop, Keitel, Kaltenbrunner, Rosenberg, Frank, Frick, Streicher, Sauckel, Jodl, and Seyss-Inquart. Bormann's death sentence, imposed in absentia, was never executed.

The prison terms were served at Spandau Prison in Berlin, under four-power administration. Raeder and Funk were released in the 1950s on health grounds; Neurath was released in 1954; Donitz served his full ten years and was released in 1956; Schirach and Speer served their full twenty years, released in 1966; Hess remained at Spandau alone from 1966 until his death in 1987, the prison maintained for a single inmate at enormous cost. The sentences' execution history shows the committee architecture's characteristic difficulty with conclusion: four powers had to agree on every release, and agreement came slowly.

The Indictment's relationship to the sentences is worth stating plainly. The document charged; the tribunal judged; the judgments varied widely. The variation is the evidence that the Indictment was a charging instrument rather than a script. Had the trial been the show proceeding its critics alleged, the sentences would have been uniform. They were not, because the tribunal took the Indictment's own principle seriously: individual responsibility requires individual judgment.

## The Legal Significance: Individual Responsibility

The Indictment's enduring legal significance begins with its central principle: individuals, not abstractions, commit crimes, and individuals can be punished for them. Before Nuremberg, international law bound states. The Hague Conventions regulated what belligerents might do; they did not provide for the trial of the officers who violated them. The Indictment, through the Charter's Articles 6, 7, and 8, converted state obligations into personal liabilities. Crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity were committed by men, and men would answer for them.

The rejection of the superior-orders defense was the principle's sharpest edge. The Charter provided that acting pursuant to government or superior orders would not free a defendant from responsibility, though it might mitigate punishment. The tribunal applied the provision strictly: Keitel's obedience was not exculpation because the orders were manifestly criminal and Keitel had helped formulate them. The holding entered military law worldwide, and every subsequent code of military justice has had to reckon with it. The soldier's duty of obedience now coexists, in the law of every major military, with the duty to disobey manifestly criminal orders. The coexistence is uneasy, and the unease is Nuremberg's legacy.

The rejection of head-of-state immunity was the principle's broadest extension. The Charter's Article 7 provided that official position, even as head of state, would not free a defendant. Donitz had been head of state; the provision applied to him. The principle lay largely dormant for decades, then revived in the prosecutions of the later twentieth century and in the Rome Statute's explicit denial of immunity. The Indictment thus planted a doctrine whose full growth took half a century, a reminder that legal instruments can outlive the controversies of their drafting.

## The Long Influence: From the Genocide Convention to Rome

The Indictment's categories became the template for later international criminal law. The 1948 Genocide Convention, drafted with Nuremberg's crimes-against-humanity count in view, defined genocide as a distinct crime and obligated states to prevent and punish it. The 1949 Geneva Conventions created the grave-breaches regime, requiring states to criminalize the serious violations that Count Three had first made individually prosecutable. The ad hoc tribunals for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda in the 1990s applied Nuremberg's doctrines, including an expanded crimes-against-humanity jurisprudence that finally removed the war nexus the Nuremberg judgment had required.

The Rome Statute of 1998, which created the permanent International Criminal Court, is the Indictment's direct institutional descendant. Its four core crimes, genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and aggression, map onto the Indictment's counts with only terminological updating. The Statute's provisions on individual responsibility, superior orders, and the irrelevance of official capacity reproduce the Charter's articles that the Indictment applied. When the Court's prosecutors frame charges, they work within the architecture the October 1945 document built.

The influence extends beyond courts. The Indictment's documentary method, proving state crime through the state's own records, became the standard for human-rights investigations. Its organizational provisions anticipated later doctrines of institutional liability. Its insistence that law could judge power, made at the moment of the victors' maximum power, remains the strongest available answer to the claim that international law is merely the strong doing what they will. The answer is imperfect, as the critical perspectives show, but it exists because the Indictment was filed.

## Critical Perspectives: The Victor's Justice Argument

The most persistent criticism of the Indictment is that it was victor's justice: the law of the winners applied to the losers, with the winners' own crimes exempted. The criticism has specific content. The Soviet Union, one of the four prosecuting powers, had invaded Poland in 1939, annexed the Baltic states, and deported entire populations; none of its leaders faced charges. The Anglo-American strategic bombing of German cities, which killed hundreds of thousands of civilians, was not examined under the war-crimes count. The tribunal's judges included a Soviet justice whose government had committed acts the Indictment defined as criminal. The asymmetry is real, and no honest account of Nuremberg can wish it away.

The Indictment's defenders have answers, and the close read must present them fairly. Jackson's opening statement argued that the trial's legitimacy lay in the victors' submission to legal process rather than in their innocence: the choice was not between perfect justice and Nuremberg, but between Nuremberg and either impunity or summary execution. The tu quoque principle, that the accuser's own crimes bar prosecution, has never been accepted in criminal law; a murderer's trial is not invalidated because the prosecutor has also killed. And the Indictment's categories, once created, became available against anyone, including the powerful states that created them. The victor's justice criticism describes the trial's origins accurately; whether it invalidates the trial's achievements is the question the criticism must answer.

The balance the brief requires is this: the asymmetry was real and it limited what Nuremberg could claim, but the alternative instruments available in 1945 were worse. Summary execution, which Churchill initially favored and which the Morgenthau Plan's spirit implied, would have punished without proving. Impunity would have taught that state crime on this scale carried no consequence. The Indictment chose the third path, prosecution under imperfect law by imperfect prosecutors, and the path's imperfections do not erase its necessity.

## Critical Perspectives: Ex Post Facto Law and Conspiracy

The second major criticism targets the Indictment's legal innovations themselves. The ex post facto objection holds that Counts One and Two punished acts that were not criminal when committed: aggressive war had never been an individual crime before the Charter declared it one, and conspiracy was an Anglo-American import unfamiliar to the continental defendants. The principle nullum crimen sine lege, no crime without law, is fundamental to criminal justice, and the Indictment arguably violated it. The tribunal's answer, that the treaties had made aggression illegal and the Charter merely supplied the forum, persuaded many jurists and left many others unconvinced.

The conspiracy count drew the sharpest technical criticism. Continental lawyers found the Anglo-American doctrine alien, and even some common-law jurists thought the Indictment stretched it beyond recognition by alleging a single conspiracy spanning twenty-five years and encompassing every crime the regime committed. The tribunal's narrowing of the count was, in effect, a partial concession to this criticism: the judges sustained conspiracy only for aggressive war and only against eight defendants. The narrowing showed the system working, but it also showed the Indictment overreaching, and the overreach is part of the honest record.

The close read's assessment is that the criticisms identify real costs of the Indictment's innovations, and that the innovations were nonetheless justified by the alternative. A law that could not reach the planners of aggressive war, because no court had ever done so before, would have been a law that abandoned the field to the aggressors. The ex post facto concern is serious precisely because the principle it invokes is serious; Nuremberg's defenders argue that the crimes were so manifestly wrongful that their perpetrators could not claim surprise at being called criminals. Whether that argument satisfies depends on how much weight one gives to formal legality against substantive justice, and the debate continues in jurisprudence.

## The Medical Dimension: Documenting the Crimes

The Indictment's evidence under Counts Three and Four documented, in clinical and administrative detail, the human cost of the regime's criminality: the extermination camps, the mobile killing operations, the slave-labor system, the medical consequences of deportation, starvation, and mass murder. The trial's medical testimony, including evidence on the condition of liberated camp survivors and the forensic documentation of mass graves, established the factual record on which the legal categories rested. The law required proof of what had been done to human bodies, and the prosecution supplied it through physicians, survivors, and the regime's own meticulous records.

The connection between the courtroom and the broader history of wartime medicine is worth making explicit. The same war that produced the crimes the Indictment charged also produced the wartime apparatus of battlefield medicine, triage, evacuation, and trauma surgery, much of it developed under the pressure of the war's unprecedented casualty loads. The series examines that apparatus in its dedicated companion resource on [battlefield medicine in the Second World War](https://reportmedic.org/tools/wwii-battlefield-medicine.html), which traces how the war's medical services coped with wounds on a scale no previous conflict had produced. The juxtaposition is deliberate and sobering: medicine advanced by treating the war's wounded while the regime the Indictment tried was manufacturing the war's dead. The two histories belong together because the war produced both, and the Indictment's achievement was to insist that the second history be judged by law rather than buried by victory.

## The House Thesis: Committee Architecture Judges

The Nuremberg Indictment is the committee architecture's legal monument. Consider what its production required: four powers with incompatible legal traditions agreeing on a charter, four prosecution teams coordinating charges, a tribunal operating in four languages, and a judgment that had to satisfy judges from adversarial and inquisitorial systems simultaneously. Every step required negotiation, compromise, and institutional patience. The Indictment's preamble, with its careful establishment of authority by reference to agreement rather than command, is the documentary expression of this process. Law, in the committee architecture, is what the participants can jointly affirm.

The command architecture's approach to equivalent problems was the opposite, and the contrast is the close read's analytical payoff. The Axis never produced, and structurally could not have produced, a comparable instrument. Nazi law was the will of the Fuhrer expressed through decrees; Japanese military justice was command discipline; neither system possessed the institutional capacity for negotiated, multilateral legal construction. Had the Axis won, the defeated Allied leaders would have faced drumhead courts-martial or summary execution, not a sixty-five-page indictment debated by four legal teams. The Indictment thus demonstrates the committee architecture's distinctive advantage at the highest level of abstraction: the capacity to build institutions, including legal institutions, through agreement among independent powers.

The advantage was not merely procedural. The Indictment's substantive innovations, individual responsibility, the rejection of superior orders and head-of-state immunity, crimes against humanity as a distinct category, reflect committee-architecture values: the person as the unit of moral agency, law as a constraint on power rather than its instrument, universality as an aspiration rather than a slogan. A command architecture, which locates authority in the leader's will, has no use for these concepts and no mechanism for producing them. The Indictment is therefore evidence for the series' thesis in its strongest form: the architectures do not merely fight differently, they conceive of law, responsibility, and justice differently, and the difference determined what was built on the war's ruins.

## Why the Nuremberg Indictment October 1945 Still Matters

The Nuremberg Indictment October 1945 matters because it is the document in which the twentieth century first wrote down, in legally operative form, the proposition that power does not excuse crime. The proposition had been asserted by philosophers and theologians for centuries. The Indictment made it a charging instrument, filed it in a courtroom, proved it with documents, and secured judgments on it. Everything since, the Genocide Convention, the Geneva grave-breaches regime, the ad hoc tribunals, the permanent International Criminal Court, is commentary on that act of writing.

It matters, too, because its flaws are instructive. The victor's justice asymmetry, the ex post facto strains, the conspiracy count's overreach, the absent Hitler and the suicide of Goring: these are not footnotes to the achievement but part of its meaning. They show what it costs to build law in the aftermath of catastrophe, with imperfect materials and compromised builders. The Indictment's lesson is not that imperfect justice is worthless but that it is the only kind available, and that building it anyway, honestly acknowledging its limits, is preferable to the alternatives of vengeance or oblivion.

The close read's final observation concerns the document's form. The Indictment is sixty-five pages of legal prose, dry by design, and its dryness is its point. The regime it charged had governed through spectacle, passion, and the aesthetics of power. The Indictment answered with procedure, evidence, and the aesthetics of law. That answer, maintained across four hundred sessions of trial and forty-two volumes of record, is the committee architecture's reply to everything the command architecture stood for. The document endures because the reply was built to endure: not as rhetoric but as institution, not as vengeance but as precedent.

## The Prosecution's Documentary Method

The Indictment was proved, in the main, by paper. The prosecution's deliberate choice to build its case on captured German documents rather than on witness testimony gave the trial its evidentiary character and much of its lasting authority. The American and British teams, working from the millions of pages seized as the Reich collapsed, selected the decrees, minutes, orders, and reports that connected each defendant to the counts. The method had been Jackson's insistence from the London negotiations: the case would rest on the defendants' own records, so that no one could later claim the verdicts were manufactured.

The documentary strategy shaped the Indictment's drafting. Because the charges had to be provable from files, the counts were framed around acts that bureaucracies record: the signing of decrees, the issuance of orders, the minutes of conferences, the statistical reports of killing units. Keitel's signature on the Commissar Decree, Sauckel's labor-deportation quotas, Frank's administrative correspondence from the General Government, the Einsatzgruppen operational reports with their tabulated murder totals: each became an exhibit because each was a document the regime itself had produced. The Indictment's appendices on the organizations were proved the same way, through captured membership rolls, organizational charts, and internal directives.

The method's strength was its resistance to denial. A witness can be discredited; a ministry's own file, bearing the defendant's signature, is harder to explain away. When Ohlendorf, the former Einsatzgruppen commander, testified about the murder of some ninety thousand persons by his unit, his testimony corroborated what the reports already showed. When the prosecution read the minutes of the conferences at which aggression was planned, the defendants heard their own past deliberations recited. The trial thus achieved something summary proceedings could not: it convicted the regime out of its own mouth, in its own handwriting.

The method had limits, and the close read should name them. Documents prove what bureaucracies record, and bureaucracies record selectively. The most sensitive decisions, including Hitler's own authorizations, were often conveyed verbally and left faint paper trails, which is why the conspiracy count had to bridge gaps the documents did not fill. The reliance on files also favored the prosecution's institutional theory over individual narratives: the paper showed systems operating, and the defendants' personal agency sometimes blurred into the machinery. The tribunal's individualized verdicts were, in part, a correction of the method's tendency to flatten persons into functions.

## The Defense Bar: German Counsel at Nuremberg

Every defendant was represented by German counsel of his own choosing, assisted by co-counsel, in a concession to fairness that the Charter required and the tribunal honored. The defense bar, drawn from the German legal profession, worked under extraordinary difficulty: limited access to documents the prosecution controlled, limited time to prepare, and the moral weight of representing men the world had already condemned. Their performance, whatever its results, was integral to the trial's claim to legitimacy. A proceeding without a real defense would have been the show trial its critics alleged.

The defense strategies mapped onto the Indictment's pressure points. The superior-orders argument, pressed for Keitel and Jodl, tested Article 8. The tu quoque argument, pressed for Donitz on submarine warfare and implicitly for others on bombing, tested whether the law could be applied by the guilty to the guilty. The jurisdictional argument, pressed by several counsel, tested whether the tribunal itself was lawful. The factual-denial argument, pressed most aggressively for Kaltenbrunner, tested the documentary evidence's completeness. Each strategy forced the tribunal to reason rather than merely condemn, and the judgment's careful engagement with the defenses is part of why it has endured as jurisprudence rather than fading as propaganda.

The acquittals were the defense bar's vindication. Schacht's counsel demonstrated that economic preparation, however extensive, was not the same as participation in planning aggression. Papen's counsel showed that political maneuvering, however cynical, did not meet the counts' thresholds. Fritzsche's counsel established the boundary between broadcasting propaganda and criminal participation. These were not technicalities. They were the tribunal applying the Indictment's own standards to the evidence and finding the standards unmet, which is what distinguishes a trial from a ritual. The defense bar's achievement was to make the tribunal prove its case, defendant by defendant, and the record shows the tribunal doing so.

## The Soviet Case and the Katyn Problem

The Soviet prosecution presented the case on atrocities in the occupied east, supplying much of the evidence for Counts Three and Four as they applied to the Soviet territories. Rudenko's team introduced the Extraordinary State Commission's findings on mass murder, deportation, and destruction across the vast occupied zone, and Soviet witnesses testified to the occupation's systematic brutality. The Soviet contribution was indispensable to the counts' factual foundation: the eastern front was where the regime's criminality had been most extensive, and only the Soviet prosecution could document it at scale.

The Soviet case also produced the trial's most damaging controversy. The Soviet prosecutors attempted to attribute the Katyn massacre, the murder of thousands of Polish officers in 1940, to the Germans, when it had in fact been committed by the Soviet NKVD. The Indictment's war-crimes count included the Katyn charge among its allegations, and the Soviet team pressed it through witnesses whose testimony collapsed under scrutiny. The tribunal, to its credit, did not sustain the charge: the judgment's treatment of Katyn was evasive rather than exculpatory, but the allegation was not upheld, and the episode entered the record as evidence of the prosecution's own unreliability on this point.

The Katyn problem is the victor's justice criticism in miniature, and the close read must present it as such. A prosecuting power used the tribunal to advance a false charge shielding its own crime, and the tribunal declined to endorse the falsehood without exposing it either. The episode shows both the Indictment's vulnerability to abuse by its sponsors and the tribunal's partial resistance to that abuse. It does not invalidate the genuine convictions, but it permanently qualifies the proceedings' moral clarity. An honest account of the Indictment includes the charge that should never have been in it.

## The Judgment: How the Tribunal Reasoned

The tribunal's judgment, delivered across September 30 and October 1 1946, is the Indictment's mirror and correction. Where the Indictment charged broadly, the judgment reasoned narrowly. Its structure followed the counts: findings on the Charter's validity, on conspiracy, on crimes against peace, on war crimes, on crimes against humanity, on the organizations, and then the individual verdicts. Each section engaged the defense arguments rather than ignoring them, and the engagement is what gives the judgment its authority as law rather than as vengeance.

The judgment's treatment of the Charter's validity addressed the ex post facto objection directly. It held that the Kellogg-Briand Pact and the other treaties had outlawed aggressive war, that the prohibition bound individuals as well as states, and that the Charter created a tribunal to enforce existing law rather than inventing new crimes. Jurists have debated the reasoning ever since, but the judgment's frankness about the difficulty, it acknowledged the novelty while defending the legitimacy, set the terms of the debate. The Indictment had asserted; the judgment argued.

The judgment's most consequential reasoning concerned individual responsibility. It held that crimes against international law are committed by men, not by abstract entities, and that only by punishing individuals who commit them can the provisions of international law be enforced. The sentence is the most quoted in the judgment, and it is the direct descendant of the Indictment's charging theory. The tribunal then applied the principle differentially, acquitting where the evidence showed insufficient personal participation and convicting where it showed the defendant's own acts. The reasoning's structure, general principle established, then applied defendant by defendant, is the model for every subsequent international criminal judgment.

## The Second Wave: The Subsequent Nuremberg Proceedings

The Indictment's organizational provisions enabled a second wave of prosecutions under Control Council Law Number Ten, the Allied legislation authorizing national tribunals to try lesser offenders. Twelve subsequent proceedings were held at Nuremberg before American military tribunals between 1946 and 1949, trying doctors, judges, industrialists, SS administrators, military commanders, and ministry officials. The Doctors' Trial addressed the medical experiments; the Judges' Trial addressed the perversion of the legal system; the Flick, I. G. Farben, and Krupp trials addressed industrial complicity; the Einsatzgruppen trial addressed the mobile killing units; the Ministries trial addressed the bureaucratic leadership.

These proceedings were the Indictment's institutional afterlife. The principal trial had charged the regime's apex; the subsequent trials worked downward through its functional layers, using the organizational criminality findings and the legal categories the Indictment had established. The Doctors' Trial produced the Nuremberg Code on human experimentation, the most influential statement of research ethics ever issued. The industrialist trials developed the law on corporate complicity in state crime. The Einsatzgruppen trial, with Ohlendorf again testifying, extended individual responsibility to the mid-level perpetrators who had actually operated the killing machinery.

The second wave also revealed the limits of the project. As the Cold War intensified, the Western appetite for continued prosecutions waned, sentences were commuted, and many convicted defendants were released early in the 1950s. The clemency reflected the new strategic reality: West Germany was becoming an ally, and its elites' continued imprisonment was a political liability. The Indictment's legal achievement thus coexisted with a political history of incomplete enforcement, and the coexistence is part of the honest record. The law was built; its application was negotiated away in part, as committee-architecture justice so often is.

## The Tokyo Counterpart

The International Military Tribunal for the Far East, convened in Tokyo from 1946 to 1948, was the Indictment's Pacific sibling and its instructive contrast. The Tokyo Charter, modeled on the London Charter, charged twenty-eight Japanese defendants with crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity, and the proceedings produced seven death sentences, sixteen life terms, and two lesser sentences. The Tokyo trial applied Nuremberg's categories to a different institutional context: a regime whose criminality was organized through military command rather than party-state fusion.

The comparison illuminates the Indictment's strengths and the Tokyo proceedings' difficulties. Tokyo lacked Nuremberg's documentary foundation: the Japanese bureaucracy had destroyed far more of its records, forcing greater reliance on witness testimony. The decision to exempt the Emperor from prosecution, a political choice imposed by the occupation authorities, compromised the individual-responsibility principle at its apex. The Tokyo judgment's reasoning on conspiracy and aggression was less disciplined than Nuremberg's, and the dissenting opinions, particularly Justice Pal's sweeping acquittalist dissent, gave the victor's justice criticism its most formidable judicial expression.

The close read's point is not to diminish Tokyo but to show what made the Nuremberg Indictment distinctive. The October 1945 document benefited from the captured German archives, from Jackson's architectural clarity, and from a tribunal that corrected its excesses. Tokyo, working with fewer documents, a compromised defendant list, and a more divided bench, achieved less. The comparison confirms that the Indictment's quality was not automatic: international criminal law had to be built well to work, and the building required the specific conditions Nuremberg enjoyed.

## The Indictment's Prose: Law as Literature of Record

The Indictment's language deserves the close reader's attention because its dryness is functional. Legal prose at this level operates under constraints unknown to other writing: every allegation must be provable, every characterization must survive defense challenge, every count must map onto the Charter's articles. The document's flat, repetitive diction, the defendants participated as leaders, organizers, instigators, or accomplices, is not a stylistic failure but a legal necessity. The repetition ensures that each defendant is charged under each applicable theory, closing the gaps through which acquittals escape.

The counts' definitions reward close parsing. Count One's phrase "during a period of years preceding 8 May 1945" establishes the temporal scope without committing to a start date the evidence might not support. Count Two's "planning, preparation, initiation, and waging" creates a sequence of liability reaching from the conference room to the battlefield. Count Three's catalogue, murder, ill-treatment, deportation, killing of hostages, plunder, wanton destruction, is exhaustive by design, ensuring no proven atrocity falls outside the charge. Count Four's "before or during the war" extends the tribunal's reach to peacetime persecution, the phrase on which the count's novelty turns.

The prose's most revealing feature is what it omits. The Indictment contains no adjectives of moral condemnation, no passages of rhetoric, no Jacksonian flourishes. It names, dates, and alleges. The restraint was deliberate: the document's authority depended on its impersonality, on the impression that the law was speaking rather than the victors. Jackson's opening statement supplied the rhetoric the Indictment withheld, and the division of labor was the point. The charging document accuses in the law's voice; the advocate argues in his own. The close read that attends to the prose understands the institution: Nuremberg's power lay in making the extraordinary sound procedural, and the procedural sound inevitable.

## The Bench: The Four Judges

The tribunal's composition embodied the alliance that had won the war, and its internal dynamics shaped the judgment. Lord Justice Geoffrey Lawrence of Britain presided, chosen for the chair because the British and Americans agreed that a judge from neither superpower should lead. Lawrence ran the proceedings with a formality that disciplined the trial's length and temper: his rulings on admissibility were strict, his control of the courtroom firm, and his drafting hand is visible in the judgment's measured prose. The presidency mattered because the trial's legitimacy depended on its looking like law rather than power, and Lawrence's courtroom looked like law.

The American judge, Francis Biddle, had been Attorney General of the United States, and his alternate was John Parker. The French judge, Henri Donnedieu de Vabres, was a professor of criminal law whose civilian training made him skeptical of the conspiracy count; his doubts contributed to the tribunal's narrowing of Count One. The Soviet judge, Iona Nikitchenko, had been a prosecutor in the Moscow show trials of the 1930s, a fact the defense never let the proceedings forget and that remains the most uncomfortable biographical detail in the tribunal's history. His alternate, Alexander Volchkov, participated actively in deliberations.

The judges disagreed, and the disagreements improved the outcome. Donnedieu de Vabres's resistance to conspiracy theory, Parker's common-law pragmatism, Biddle's political experience, and Lawrence's procedural rigor produced a judgment more careful than any single national tradition would have yielded. Nikitchenko dissented from the acquittals of Schacht, Papen, and Fritzsche, from the life sentence for Hess, and from the refusal to declare certain organizations criminal, filing the dissent the Soviet government required. The dissent is historically valuable precisely because it shows what the tribunal would have been without its internal checks: a court that convicted everyone. The majority's willingness to acquit, over Soviet objection, is the judgment's certificate of independence.

## The Defendants' Final Statements

Before sentence was passed, each defendant was permitted a final statement to the tribunal, and the statements form a coda to the Indictment's charges. Goring, who had dominated the witness box for days with a mixture of defiance and charm, used his statement to accept responsibility in the abstract while denying every specific charge, a performance his counsel could not improve. Hess delivered an incoherent address about extraterrestrial forces and Germany's mission, confirming the tribunal's impression of a diminished figure. Speer reiterated his acceptance of collective responsibility while distancing himself from knowledge of the extermination program, the calculated ambiguity on which his twenty-year sentence partly rested.

The statements matter to the close read because they show the Indictment's categories failing to penetrate the defendants' self-understanding. Almost none of the convicted men acknowledged the criminality of what they had done in the terms the counts alleged. Ribbentrop protested that he had served peace; Keitel that he had served duty; Frank, in a partial exception, that a thousand years would not erase Germany's guilt, though he too disputed his personal share. The gap between the law's judgment and the defendants' comprehension is the trial's unresolved remainder: the Indictment proved its case to the world, but it did not, in most cases, prove it to the men in the dock.

Historians have debated whether the statements reveal genuine incomprehension or calculated performance. The distinction matters less than the fact of the gap. Nuremberg's achievement was institutional rather than redemptive: it built the legal machinery for judging state criminality, but it could not compel the criminals' moral recognition. The Indictment's counts were answered with verdicts, not with confessions, and the close read should not pretend otherwise. The law's victory was real, and it was incomplete, and both facts belong in the same sentence.

## The Indictment and the German Public

The Indictment was addressed to the tribunal, but its audience included the German population, and the trial's pedagogical ambition was explicit: Jackson and his colleagues hoped the proceedings would document the regime's crimes so thoroughly that denial would become impossible. The trial was covered extensively in the German press, the proceedings were broadcast, and summaries of the evidence reached a wide public. The question of what the German public learned, and when, has occupied historians ever since.

The record suggests a complicated reception. The documentary evidence, particularly the films of the liberated camps shown in the courtroom, made the regime's crimes undeniable to those who attended to the proceedings. But attention was uneven, and the trial's length diluted its impact: by the spring of 1946, German press coverage had thinned, and public interest competed with the daily struggle for food, shelter, and survival in the ruined cities. The Indictment's pedagogical theory assumed an audience with the leisure to follow four hundred sessions of legal argument, an assumption the conditions of occupied Germany did not support.

The deeper question is whether the trial taught the lesson it intended. The German public's postwar confrontation with the Nazi past was slow, partial, and contested for decades, and no single proceeding could have accomplished it. But the Indictment's documentary foundation served the pedagogy in the long run: the forty-two volumes of the trial record became the archive from which later generations of Germans, prosecutors, historians, and teachers drew the evidence for the country's eventual reckoning. The trial planted a record that the culture harvested later. The close read's assessment is that the Indictment's educational achievement was real but deferred, a seed rather than a harvest.

## The Palace of Justice: Nuremberg as Courtroom

The choice of Nuremberg as the trial's site was symbolic and practical in equal measure. Symbolically, the city had been the stage of the Nazi Party rallies, the place where the regime displayed itself; trying its leaders there announced that the law claimed the ground the pageantry had occupied. Practically, the Palace of Justice was one of the few large courthouses in Germany to survive the bombing substantially intact, and its attached prison could house the defendants securely. The symbolism and the practicality reinforced each other: the trial occupied the regime's ceremonial city in a building the war had spared.

The courtroom itself was remodeled for the proceedings, with simultaneous interpretation in four languages, a technological novelty that made the multilingual trial possible. The interpretation system, developed for the occasion, became the model for international conferences thereafter; like the legal categories, the courtroom's machinery outlived its original purpose. The dock, the defendants seated in two rows behind their counsel, became the twentieth century's most reproduced image of justice confronting power. Goring in the front row, impassive or animated by turns, fixed the visual memory.

The site's selection also reflected the zones of occupation: Nuremberg lay in the American zone, giving the United States the logistical responsibility its leading prosecutorial role implied. The subsequent proceedings under Control Council Law Number Ten were held in the same building, making the Palace of Justice the continuous seat of the Allied prosecution program. The building's survival, its adaptation, and its reuse form a material history parallel to the legal one: the infrastructure of justice, like its doctrines, was improvised from what the war left standing, and it endured because the improvisation was sound.

## The Interpreters and the Forty-Two Volumes

The trial's least visible achievement was its language system. Four official languages, English, French, Russian, and German, required simultaneous interpretation of every word spoken across more than four hundred sessions, a technical undertaking without precedent at this scale. The interpreters worked in teams in glass booths, rendering testimony, argument, and judicial rulings in real time, and their labor made the proceedings comprehensible to defendants, counsel, judges, and press alike. Errors occurred, and the defense occasionally challenged translations, but the system's overall reliability was remarkable for a first attempt. The technology developed for Nuremberg became the standard equipment of international diplomacy.

The interpretation system shaped the Indictment's afterlife in a subtler way. Because every session was interpreted into four languages, the trial generated its record in quadruplicate, and the official transcript, published as the forty-two-volume Trial of the Major War Criminals, became the most complete documentary account of the Nazi regime then in existence. The volumes contain the Indictment, the Charter, the evidence, the testimony, the arguments, and the judgment, organized for reference. They served prosecutors in the subsequent proceedings, historians reconstructing the regime, and later tribunals seeking precedent.

The record's completeness is the documentary method's final vindication. Jackson had insisted on proving the case from paper so that denial would be impossible; the forty-two volumes made the proof permanent and public. The Indictment's counts can be checked against the evidence session by session, the defense arguments against the judgment's reasoning, the verdicts against the exhibits. Few legal proceedings in history have left so auditable a trail. The close read's verdict on the verdict is that the trial's transparency, enforced by interpreters and stenographers as much as by judges, is what separates Nuremberg from every summary proceeding its critics compare it to. The law spoke in four languages at once, and the transcript preserves every word.

## The Indictment's Opening Lines

The close read ends where the document begins. The Indictment opens by naming the four prosecuting powers and the authority under which they act, then states, in the flat diction of the law, that the defendants are charged with crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity, and with a common plan or conspiracy to commit them. There is no preamble of rhetoric, no invocation of the dead, no flourish. The restraint is the argument: the law does not need to raise its voice.

Read beside Jackson's opening statement, the opening lines complete the document's design. The Indictment accuses; the advocate explains why the accusation is lawful; the evidence proves; the judgment reasons; the sentences execute. Each element of the sequence has its own voice, and the Indictment's voice is the driest of them, because it carries the sequence's foundation. A foundation that emoted would have invited the charge of vengeance; a foundation that merely alleged invited the verdict of law. The tribunal's verdict, when it came, confirmed the choice. The document that began without rhetoric ended by changing what the law could say.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When was the Nuremberg Indictment filed and where?

The Indictment was filed on October 18 1945 in Berlin, lodged with the International Military Tribunal by the four chief prosecutors representing the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and France. Berlin was chosen for both symbolic and practical reasons: it was the capital of the defeated Reich, and the Allied Control Council sat there, so filing the charges in the city the defendants had ruled announced that law claimed jurisdiction where the regime had claimed sovereignty. The filing came two months after the London Charter of August 8 1945 created the tribunal and one month before the trial opened in Nuremberg on November 20. The document ran to approximately sixty-five pages and named twenty-four defendants. Its structure, preamble, defendant list, organizational descriptions, four counts, and appendices, carried the full burden of establishing the tribunal's authority, the offenses charged, and the individual responsibility of the accused.

### What legal document authorized the Nuremberg trial?

The London Charter, formally the Charter of the International Military Tribunal, signed on August 8 1945 by the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and France. The Charter established the tribunal, defined its jurisdiction over crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity, added conspiracy as a mode of liability, and set the procedural rules blending adversarial and inquisitorial traditions. It also contained the provisions that became the trial's most famous holdings: Article 7, denying that official position even as head of state would free a defendant, and Article 8, denying that superior orders would free a defendant though they might mitigate punishment. The Indictment was the Charter applied to twenty-four named individuals, and every count cited the Charter's articles. The Charter was itself a negotiated compromise among legal systems that disagreed fundamentally about criminal procedure, and its blended form shaped everything from the Indictment's organization to the trial's conduct across more than four hundred sessions.

### How many defendants did the Indictment name?

Twenty-four. The list spanned the surviving Nazi leadership across its functions: political figures, military commanders, ideologues, propagandists, economic administrators, industrialists, and terror officials. In practice, only twenty-two appeared in the courtroom. Robert Ley, the German Labor Front chief, died by suicide on October 25 1945, a week after the filing and before the trial opened. Gustav Krupp, the steel industrialist, was severed from the proceedings for medical incapacity and never tried. Martin Bormann, Hitler's secretary, was tried in absentia because his whereabouts were unknown. The selection reflected both the regime's structure and the accidents of survival: Hitler, Himmler, and Goebbels were dead, Heydrich had been assassinated in 1942, and the Indictment could try only the living. The list's breadth, political, military, ideological, economic, and terror functions each represented, embodied the prosecution's theory that the regime's criminality was collective and institutional rather than the work of isolated individuals.

### Who were the highest-ranking defendants at Nuremberg?

Hermann Goring was the highest-ranking defendant, the Reich Marshal and Hitler's designated successor, who had built the Luftwaffe, directed the Four Year Plan, and signed the directive authorizing Heydrich to prepare the organizational machinery for the regime's extermination program. Rudolf Hess, the Deputy Fuhrer, represented the party's early ideological core. Karl Donitz, the U-boat commander, had succeeded Hitler as head of state in the regime's final days, making him the only defendant to have held the supreme office. Wilhelm Keitel, as Chief of Staff of the Armed Forces High Command, was the senior uniformed defendant. Joachim von Ribbentrop, the Foreign Minister, was the regime's senior diplomat. The Indictment's choice of these figures as its center reflected a deliberate strategy: trying the successor, the deputy, the final head of state, the senior soldier, and the senior diplomat demonstrated that no rank, office, or function placed a defendant beyond the law's reach, which was precisely the principle the Charter's Article 7 announced.

### Why was Martin Bormann tried in absentia?

Bormann, Hitler's secretary and chief of the Party Chancellery, had disappeared in the fall of Berlin, and his whereabouts were unknown when the Indictment was filed. The London Charter permitted trial in absentia, and the tribunal allowed it for Bormann while appointing defense counsel to represent his interests, a procedural safeguard meant to preserve fairness despite the defendant's absence. Anglo-American law traditionally disfavored trying the absent, so the choice was controversial, but the prosecution argued that Bormann's centrality to the party machinery made his omission unacceptable. He was convicted and sentenced to death in absentia, a sentence never executed because he was never found. The absent trial became a standing illustration of the proceedings' determination to leave no major figure unjudged, and it remains debated whether the determination served justice or theater. The Indictment's one trial of an empty chair thus encapsulates both its ambition and its procedural audacity.

### What happened to Robert Ley and Gustav Krupp before the trial?

Robert Ley, the German Labor Front chief named in the Indictment, died by suicide in his Nuremberg cell on October 25 1945, one week after the filing and nearly a month before the trial opened. His death removed one defendant before proceedings began. Gustav Krupp, the steel industrialist, was severed from the case on grounds of medical incapacity; he was too ill, physically and mentally, to stand trial or instruct counsel. The prosecution attempted to substitute his son Alfried, who had actively managed the firm's wartime operations including its use of slave labor, but the tribunal rejected the substitution, holding that one man could not be tried in another's place. The Krupp matter became the Indictment's acknowledged imperfection: the industrialist whose factories had used forced labor on a vast scale escaped judgment at the principal trial, though the subsequent industrialist proceedings would address the sector. Together the two cases reduced the twenty-four named defendants to twenty-one present in the courtroom plus Bormann in absentia.

### What did Count One of the Indictment allege?

Count One charged that the defendants, during a period of years preceding May 8 1945, had participated as leaders, organizers, instigators, or accomplices in the formulation or execution of a common plan or conspiracy to commit, or which involved the commission of, crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity as defined in the Charter, and that each was individually responsible for his own acts and for all acts committed by any persons in execution of the plan. The count was the Indictment's connective tissue, binding the twenty-four defendants to one another and to the dead leaders, making each responsible for the enterprise as a whole. It extended Anglo-American conspiracy doctrine to international law for the first time, a substantial innovation that continental lawyers found alien. The tribunal narrowed the count considerably, sustaining it only for the planning of aggressive war and convicting eight defendants on it, rejecting the prosecution's broader theory of a conspiracy encompassing all the regime's crimes. The narrowing became a precedent for later tribunals' treatment of collective liability.

### What are crimes against peace under Count Two?

Count Two charged the planning, preparation, initiation, and waging of wars of aggression in violation of international treaties, agreements, and assurances, including the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, the Versailles Treaty, and the non-aggression treaties with Poland, Czechoslovakia, and the Soviet Union. It listed the specific aggressions as separate criminal acts: Austria, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Denmark, Norway, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Yugoslavia, Greece, the Soviet Union, and the declaration of war against the United States. The count was the Indictment's greatest legal innovation and its greatest vulnerability, because no court had ever treated aggressive war as an individual crime. The defense raised the ex post facto objection, arguing the defendants were punished under law created after their acts. The tribunal answered that the treaties had made aggression illegal and the Charter merely supplied the forum. The convictions sustained against the principal planners established the principle later codified as the crime of aggression in the Rome Statute.

### How did Count Three define war crimes?

Count Three charged murder, ill-treatment, and deportation to slave labor or for any other purpose of the civilian population of or in occupied territory; murder and ill-treatment of prisoners of war and persons on the seas; killing of hostages; plunder of public and private property; wanton destruction of cities, towns, and villages; and devastation not justified by military necessity. It codified the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 and the Geneva Convention of 1929 for individual prosecution, applying to persons the rules that had previously bound only states. The count was the Indictment's least innovative and most solidly grounded, because the underlying law was settled; it required only proof, and the captured German records supplied it in abundance, from the Commissar Decree over Keitel's signature to the killing-unit reports. Its significance lies in the transformation it effected: violations of the laws of war became individual crimes triable before an international court, and the 1949 Geneva Conventions' grave-breaches regime descends directly from this count.

### What made Count Four crimes against humanity legally new?

Count Four charged murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, and other inhumane acts committed against any civilian population, before or during the war, and persecutions on political, racial, or religious grounds. Its two innovations defined its importance. First, it reached conduct against a state's own citizens, breaking the traditional rule that international law did not regulate what a government did to its own people. Second, it reached conduct before the war began, through the phrase before or during the war, enabling prosecution of the pre-1939 persecution of German Jews and political opponents. The count was the Indictment's answer to the Holocaust as a legal problem: the extermination of the Jews was not fully captured by the war-crimes or aggression counts, and a distinct category was needed for the regime's war against civilians as such. The tribunal narrowed the count's pre-war reach in application, requiring a nexus with armed conflict, but later tribunals and the Rome Statute completed the work by defining crimes against humanity without that requirement.

### What did the Indictment say about Nazi organizations?

The Indictment's appendices named six Nazi organizations for a declaration of criminality: the Leadership Corps of the Nazi Party, the Gestapo, the SD, the SS, the SA, and the Reich Cabinet, along with the General Staff and High Command as a group. The organizational charges reflected the theory that the regime's crimes were committed through institutions, and declaring those institutions criminal would enable subsequent prosecution of their members in national courts. The trial heard extensive evidence on each organization, and the judgment declared criminal the Leadership Corps, the Gestapo, the SD, and the SS, while declining to declare criminal the SA, the Reich Cabinet, and the General Staff and High Command as groups. Under the Charter's framework, membership in a declared criminal organization could then be punished by national courts, and the subsequent proceedings under Control Council Law Number Ten prosecuted thousands on this basis. The appendices thus converted the Indictment from a single trial's charging document into the foundation of the entire Allied prosecution program.

### Who was Robert H. Jackson and what role did he play?

Robert H. Jackson was an Associate Justice of the United States Supreme Court who took leave from the Court to serve as the American representative in the London negotiations that produced the Charter and then as the chief American prosecutor at Nuremberg. He was the Indictment's principal architect and its most eloquent defender, and his dual standing as sitting justice and prosecution advocate gave the proceedings gravity while exposing him to charges of compromised detachment. Jackson accepted the tension, arguing that the crimes were so enormous that ordinary separations could not be preserved without abandoning the victims. His personal imprint is strongest in the Indictment's framing of individual responsibility: the insistence, count after count, that defendants were charged as individuals for acts committed through organizations, and that office, orders, or head-of-state status provided no immunity. His November 21 1945 opening statement remains the most famous courtroom address of the twentieth century and the Indictment's rhetorical companion.

### What is the most famous passage of Jackson's opening statement?

The passage addressing the tribunal's moral authority directly: "The wrongs which we seek to condemn and punish have been so calculated, so malignant, and so devastating, that civilization cannot tolerate their being ignored, because it cannot survive their being repeated. That four great nations, flushed with victory and stung with injury, stay the hand of vengeance and voluntarily submit their captive enemies to the judgment of the law is one of the most significant tributes that Power has ever paid to Reason." Delivered on November 21 1945, the day after the trial opened, the passage was Jackson's answer to the victor's justice objection made before the objection could fully form: the trial's legitimacy lay in the victors' submission of vengeance to legal process. Jackson also confronted the ex post facto problem directly, arguing that the law applied was declared from principles already present in treaties, customs, and the conscience of civilization rather than invented for the occasion. The statement established the philosophical framework for everything the Indictment's four counts would then be proved to contain.

### How long did the Nuremberg trial last?

The trial ran from November 20 1945, when the tribunal convened in the Nuremberg Palace of Justice, to October 1 1946, when the judgments were delivered, spanning more than four hundred sessions across roughly ten and a half months. The proceedings followed the Charter's blended procedure, adversarial in the presentation of cases with each defendant represented by German counsel of his choosing, but with judges exercising active control including the power to question witnesses. The prosecution's case rested primarily on captured documents rather than witness testimony, a choice that gave the trial its distinctive character: the regime's own records, decrees over defendants' signatures, planning conference minutes, killing-unit reports, and ministry correspondence, were read into evidence as the bureaucracy testifying against itself. The forty-two-volume official record remains one of the great documentary archives of the twentieth century. The trial's length reflected both the volume of evidence and the tribunal's determination to give each defendant an individualized hearing.

### What were the verdicts delivered on October 1 1946?

Twelve defendants received death sentences: Goring, Ribbentrop, Keitel, Kaltenbrunner, Rosenberg, Frank, Frick, Streicher, Sauckel, Jodl, Seyss-Inquart, and Bormann in absentia. Three received life imprisonment: Hess, Funk, and Raeder. Four received fixed prison terms: Donitz ten years, Schirach twenty, Speer twenty, and Neurath fifteen. Three were acquitted: Schacht, Papen, and Fritzsche. The judgments were delivered on September 30 and October 1 1946, and their distribution showed the tribunal discriminating among levels of responsibility rather than condemning the group as a whole. The conspiracy count was sustained only in narrowed form, for aggressive war, with eight defendants convicted on it. The acquittals were as significant as the convictions: a tribunal that acquits nearly one defendant in seven is exercising judgment rather than ratifying vengeance, and the three acquittals answered the charge that the proceedings were a mere formality with the defendants' fates predetermined.

### Who was acquitted at Nuremberg and why?

Three defendants were acquitted. Hjalmar Schacht, the Reichsbank president and Economics Minister until 1937, was acquitted because the tribunal held that economic preparation for war, without proof of participation in planning aggression, was not criminal under the Charter; his fall from favor before the war and his imprisonment by the regime after the July 1944 plot supported the defense. Franz von Papen, the conservative Vice Chancellor of 1933, was acquitted because his pre-war political maneuvering, though morally compromised, was held insufficient for conviction under the counts charged. Hans Fritzsche, the Propaganda Ministry's radio chief, was acquitted because the tribunal distinguished between broadcasting the regime's propaganda and criminal participation, a holding that limited the Indictment's reach into speech and stands in deliberate contrast to Streicher's conviction, which rested on twenty-five years of direct incitement to murder. The three acquittals demonstrated the tribunal correcting the Indictment where its ambition exceeded its proof.

### How did the trial reject the superior orders defense?

The London Charter's Article 8 provided that acting pursuant to the orders of a government or a superior would not free a defendant from responsibility, though it might be considered in mitigation of punishment. Wilhelm Keitel advanced the defense in its purest form, arguing that as a soldier he was bound to obey the head of state. The tribunal rejected the argument on two grounds: the orders in question, including the Commissar Decree, the Commando Order, and the Night and Fog decree, were manifestly criminal on their face, and Keitel had participated in formulating them rather than merely executing them. The holding entered military law worldwide: the soldier's duty of obedience now coexists, in the law of every major military, with the duty to disobey manifestly criminal orders. The Indictment had announced the principle before a witness testified; the judgment made it law. The coexistence of the two duties remains uneasy in practice, and the unease is part of Nuremberg's legacy, but the principle that obedience does not excuse participation in manifest criminality is the trial's most cited contribution to the law of armed conflict.

### What is the victor's justice criticism of Nuremberg?

The criticism holds that Nuremberg was the law of the winners applied to the losers, with the winners' own crimes exempted from judgment. It has specific content: the Soviet Union, one of the four prosecuting powers, had invaded Poland in 1939, annexed the Baltic states, and deported entire populations, yet none of its leaders faced charges; the Anglo-American strategic bombing of German cities, which killed hundreds of thousands of civilians, was never examined under the war-crimes count; and the tribunal's bench included a Soviet justice whose government had committed acts the Indictment defined as criminal. The asymmetry is real and no honest account can wish it away. The defense has three parts: Jackson argued the trial's legitimacy lay in the victors' submission of vengeance to legal process rather than in their innocence; criminal law has never accepted the tu quoque principle that the accuser's crimes bar prosecution; and the Indictment's categories, once created, became available against anyone including their creators. The criticism accurately describes the trial's origins; whether it invalidates the achievements is the question it must answer.

### Was Nuremberg an application of ex-post-facto law?

The objection holds that Counts One and Two punished acts that were not criminal when committed: aggressive war had never been an individual crime before the Charter declared it one, and conspiracy was an Anglo-American doctrine unfamiliar to the continental defendants, violating the principle of nullum crimen sine lege, no crime without law. The tribunal answered that the treaties, above all the Kellogg-Briand Pact's renunciation of war as an instrument of policy, had made aggression illegal, and that the Charter merely supplied the forum for judging violations of existing law. Many jurists found this persuasive and many others did not, and the debate continues in jurisprudence. The tribunal's narrowing of the conspiracy count was in effect a partial concession: the judges sustained it only for aggressive war and only against eight defendants, correcting the Indictment where its ambition exceeded its proof. The close read's assessment is that the criticisms identify real costs, and that the innovations were justified by the alternative, since a law unable to reach the planners of aggressive war would have abandoned the field to the aggressors.

### How did the Indictment shape later international courts?

Its categories became the template for later international criminal law. The 1948 Genocide Convention, drafted with the crimes-against-humanity count in view, defined genocide as a distinct crime. The 1949 Geneva Conventions created the grave-breaches regime, requiring states to criminalize the serious violations that Count Three had first made individually prosecutable. The ad hoc tribunals for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda in the 1990s applied Nuremberg's doctrines, including an expanded crimes-against-humanity jurisprudence that finally removed the war nexus the Nuremberg judgment had required. The Rome Statute of 1998, creating the permanent International Criminal Court, is the Indictment's direct institutional descendant: its four core crimes, genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and aggression, map onto the Indictment's counts with only terminological updating, and its provisions on individual responsibility, superior orders, and the irrelevance of official capacity reproduce the Charter's articles. The Indictment's documentary method also became the standard for human-rights investigations worldwide.
