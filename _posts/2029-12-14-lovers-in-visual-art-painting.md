Painting gave Romeo and Juliet their public faces, and the gift came with an editor's pencil. Between the first illustrated editions of the eighteenth century and the last great Victorian canvases, painters performed on the play the most consequential act of selection in its whole afterlife. They chose five stations from a crowded five-act tragedy and promoted them into a visual canon: the meeting at the feast, the conversation at the window, the parting at dawn, the discovery of the apparently dead Juliet, and the tomb. Then, in the portrait galleries of the later nineteenth century, they performed a second selection and discarded the scenes altogether, keeping only a face. The verdict of this article is that painting did not illustrate the tragedy. Painting re-edited it, fixing the lovers' story in an order the text does not have, with the window scene first, though the play never speaks the word balcony, and the causes of the catastrophe, the feud, the plague, the machinery of the failed letter, left unpainted in every generation. For most of the world that has encountered the pair without reading a line, the painters' edit is the play.

The evidence discipline of the survey matters, because art lore divides as readily as theatre lore. Every canvas treated here is carried at the precision of its holding institution's record or a scholarly catalogue: artist, title, date, medium, present home, and the scene depicted. Where the record itself divides, as it does on the size of the Boydell collection and on the present home of Leighton's oil, the division is stated rather than smoothed. Line references to the play follow the Folger Shakespeare edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, the edition this series quotes throughout. The argument's shape follows from the verified corpus. A census of what got painted, repeated across a hundred and fifty years by artists who owed one another nothing, is stronger evidence about the play's public meaning than any single masterpiece, because repetition is the mechanism by which an image becomes a fact. One painter choosing the window scene is a preference. Fifty painters, three national schools, and a century of print sellers choosing the window scene is a verdict delivered by the culture itself, and the business of this article is to read that verdict closely: what it keeps, what it cuts, and what the cutting cost.

## How did the lovers first get their faces?

The illustrated edition came before the exhibition canvas, and it set the terms. Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709, published by Jacob Tonson, was the first illustrated edition of the works: a Sotheby's catalogue essay on the edition counts forty-three plates, one at the beginning of each play, beside the engraved portrait frontispieces. A reader opening the tragedy in 1709 therefore met a picture before a line of verse, a single chosen moment standing at the gate of the text like a frontispiece argument. The University of Michigan's exhibition account of the illustrating tradition notes that these early plates preserve the costumes and staging habits of the contemporary theatre, pictures of performance no less than of poetry. The constraint was structural and it never lifted. An edition could afford one plate per play, so somebody had to decide, before any reader voted, which moment the play would be allowed to mean. Illustration began as criticism under a budget.

Rowe's double office deserves its emphasis, since he held both halves of the canon-making apparatus at once. He was the plays' first modern editor, the man who regularized the texts, divided them tidily, and supplied the dramatis personae lists and stage directions readers still unconsciously expect, and his edition's plates made him the plays' first iconographer as well. The pairing was not accidental and its consequences were structural. An editor decides what the words are; an iconographer decides what the people look like; when one publishing house settles both in a single product, the product issues the play entire, text and faces bound together, each certifying the other. Later editions inherited both settlements together and rarely thought to separate them, so the illustrated Shakespeare descended the century as a single tradition with two departments. The point matters for everything that follows in this article, because the painters of the Boydell generation and after were not the first suppliers of the lovers' faces to a faceless text. They were competitors entering a market the editors had already furnished, and their pictures are best read as rival editions, claims that the frontispiece tradition had fixed the wrong moments or fixed them in the wrong key. Wright's tomb competes with the plate programme; Brown's parting competes with a hundred graceful windows; the competition is the criticism, conducted in the only medium where the editors' plates could be answered in kind.

Sir Thomas Hanmer's Oxford edition of 1743 and 1744 raised the stakes and left the paperwork. The Folger Shakespeare Library holds a unique copy of the edition, its ART Vol. b72, containing all thirty-six original drawings and the contract between editor and artist, dated 28 November 1740. The contract, quoted in the Folger's account of the volume, bound Francis Hayman to design a drawing to be prefixed to each play, taking the subject of each scene as Hanmer himself should direct, at three guineas a drawing. Hayman completed thirty-one of the thirty-six; Hubert Gravelot designed the remaining five and engraved the set. Here, in a legal instrument, is the birth certificate of the play's iconography: an editor directing an artist, scene by scene, three guineas at a time. The Folger's exhibition account calls the results some of the most effective and original illustrations of their date and observes that the edition is remembered for them. The text Hanmer supplied has had no such mercy from posterity. A standard textual companion quoted in the trade calls his edition one of the worst of the eighteenth century, its silent conjectures unmarked. The pattern of the whole afterlife is present in miniature in 1744: the words were botched and forgotten, the pictures endured and were copied.

### Did the contract between Hanmer and Hayman decide what each play would look like?

In substance, yes. The agreement preserved at the Folger obliged Hayman to draw a design for the front of every play on a subject the editor directed, finished in Indian ink fit for an engraver, at three guineas the drawing. Selection of the visual canon began as an editor's contractual right, exercised before the artist lifted a pen.

The plates themselves deserve a closer look, because they established habits that a century of successors inherited. Malcolm C. Salaman, whose survey Shakespeare in Pictorial Art appeared in 1916, records that Hayman began his career as a scene-painter at Drury Lane and painted Shakespeare subjects for the decoration of Vauxhall Gardens before he ever drew for Hanmer. The frontispieces carry the theatre in their bones: figures arranged like actors across a shallow stage, gestures pitched to the back rows. Salaman also notes that Hayman did not escape the convention of contemporary costume. The first canonical Juliet in English print therefore wore the dress of a Georgian lady in a Georgian room, and the first Romeo courted her in a wig's distance from the audience's own world. Nothing in the record suggests embarrassment about this. The eighteenth century pictured the lovers as contemporaries because the pictures were arguments about the present life of the text, not reconstructions of Verona. The archaeological Verona of brown stone and trecento dress, the Verona the world eventually built in masonry as well as paint, was a nineteenth-century invention, and it arrived when the pictures stopped reporting the stage and started reporting a researched past. Between Hayman's Georgian room and Ford Madox Brown's stone balcony lies the whole first act of the iconography's history: the migration of the image from performance to scholarship, from the playhouse's present tense to the museum's past.

The plate deserves its economics understood, because the economics explain the canon better than taste ever will. A frontispiece engraving was the eighteenth century's image network in miniature: drawn once, engraved once, printed ten thousand times, bound into volumes that sat in libraries and parlours for generations, and copied freely by lesser editions whose publishers could not commission originals. A stage performance reached the hundreds who attended and vanished the same night; a plate reached everyone who ever opened the book, in every year the book survived, and it greeted each of them at the same threshold with the same chosen moment. The reader formed by Rowe and Hanmer did not merely know a picture of the play. That reader's Juliet had a face before the first line was read, assembled from a Georgian interior and an engraver's burin, and every subsequent reading decorated itself around the plate's decision about which instant deserved the honour. When Lamb complained, seventy years on, that the pictures had evicted his Shakespeare, the eviction he described had begun in exactly these volumes. The exhibition canvas inherited a public whose inner theatre was already furnished by the frontispiece, and the painters who prospered were the ones whose canvases rhymed with the furniture.

The Hanmer volume's division of labour is itself a document worth reading slowly. Thirty-six designs were wanted, one for each play; Hayman, the scene-painter turned illustrator, completed thirty-one; Gravelot supplied the remaining five, the Folger's record placing his designs in the fourth volume, and engraved the whole set. The edition thus speaks in two accents bound as one: an English theatrical hand, trained on the Drury Lane stage's sense of grouping and gesture, and a French hand formed in the Parisian book trade, and the mixture set the illustrated Shakespeare's tone for decades, polish laid over performance. The five-play gap in Hayman's tally is the kind of detail a survey keeps tripping on, and it matters beyond its size, because it shows the frontispiece system operating as manufacture rather than as inspiration. Designs were quota work under contract, deliverable by substitution, and the plays whose images came from the substitute's hand are indistinguishable in the bound volumes from the rest. Canon formation at this level is not a series of visions. It is a production schedule with a deadline, and the faces that governed a century's reading were settled, in part, by which artist had time in the winter of a given year. The conclusion is not cynical. It is clarifying: an iconography's authority has never depended on the purity of its origins, only on the fidelity of its repetitions.

One more hand in the Hanmer plates went on to shape the whole visual century, and it was not English. Gravelot, born in Paris and settled in London, engraved Hayman's designs and contributed five of his own, bringing to the English book trade the finish of French rococo book illustration, all lightness, elegance, and conversational grace. The style became a kind of visual accent in which the plays were thereafter pronounced. Shakespeare in pictures would spend a hundred years moving between Gravelot's drawing-room polish and the darker sublime the Boydell generation preferred, between the lovers as polite company and the lovers as figures in a storm of fate, and the oscillation is visible in this play's record from first to last: the feast drawn as a ballroom, the tomb drawn as a cavern. Neither register is in the text, which contains both a ballroom and a cavern and declines to choose between them as the story's climate. The pictures had to choose, because a plate has one weather. Every illustrated edition is therefore a climate report on its century's Shakespeare, and the lovers' faces change with the pressure systems of taste far more obediently than they change with anything in the verse. The frontispiece era's lasting bequest was the habit itself: the expectation, installed in readers before painters ever took it up, that a great play comes with a sanctioned set of looks, and that knowing the play includes knowing how its people are supposed to appear.

## What did Boydell's Shakespeare Gallery do to the lovers?

John Boydell, engraver, publisher, and Alderman of London, industrialized the process Hanmer had run by contract. The scheme, launched in 1786, had three arms: an edition of the plays edited by George Steevens and published across the 1790s, a folio Collection of Prints issued in 1803, and a gallery at 52 Pall Mall where the commissioned paintings themselves hung. The gallery opened in 1789 with thirty-four paintings and grew until the dispersal; the Folger counts 173 canvases by thirty-five artists, while accounts following Winifred Friedman's research give 167, and the division is worth keeping because it measures how much of the enterprise survives only as catalogue entries. Well over one hundred thousand pounds went into commissions by one documented reckoning, and the folio and the edition carried the images far beyond the visitors who climbed the Pall Mall stairs. Boydell said the project would foster a British school of history painting, and the claim was sincere. It was also a print business wearing a patriotic cockade, and the lovers were among its hardest-working stock.

The room itself taught the lesson before any print repeated it. A visitor to 52 Pall Mall walked a hang in which every play stood represented by its chosen instant, the whole works reissued as a portrait gallery of moments, and the experience trained a way of knowing Shakespeare that books had never offered: at a glance, comparatively, with the eye moving from canvas to canvas as a reader's memory moves, except that the eye's version was furnished entirely by decisions the visitor had not made. This play's three instants hung there among the collected moments of the histories, comedies, and tragedies, and their meanings were set partly by their neighbours, the tomb darkening the feast across the room, the discovery's grief answering comedies on the far wall. Selection in a hang is never only selection within a play. It is casting within a repertory, and the lovers' pictures took their tone from the company Boydell assembled around them: the national poet arranged as a sequence of great instants, each play reduced to the moment its painter swore it lived by.

The machine's genius was cross-subsidy. The gallery charged the fashionable world the pleasure of standing before the originals; the folio sold collectors the same images at portfolio scale, engraved with a finish meant to advertise what British burins could do; the edition carried smaller reductions of the designs into the ordinary trade, so that a provincial bookseller's Shakespeare arrived with the gallery's choices already installed at its scenes. Each arm marketed the others. A subscriber who had seen Northcote's monument on the Pall Mall wall met it again in the folio at home and a third time, reduced, in the family Shakespeare, and by the third meeting the image had the standing of a fact: this is what the tomb looks like. No earlier moment in the play's history offers the pattern so complete. Hanmer's plates had furnished the reader's inner theatre one volume at a time; Boydell furnished it as an environment, wall, portfolio, and bookshelf agreeing with one another, and agreement among media is the strongest form of persuasion the image trades possess. The play's other eighteenth-century faces, the actors' faces, could not compete with an environment. David Garrick's Romeo had been the century's Romeo for playgoers, but a performance, however legendary, is a rumour beside an engraving, and when the century closed it was the gallery's still, repeatable Juliet who remained in circulation, not the player's remembered one. Painting's victory over the stage in the custody of this play's appearance dates from these rooms, and it was won by logistics before it was won by art.

The gallery's choices for this play can be read off the surviving lists like a table of contents for the next century. Three canvases were commissioned from the tragedy. William Miller painted the meeting at Capulet's feast, the first act's fifth scene, where the pilgrim sonnet passes between the strangers: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine" (1.5). John Opie painted the fourth act's fifth scene, the household's discovery of Juliet on her bed, apparently dead, the lamentation scene in which Capulet cries that death has tied up his tongue. James Northcote painted the fifth act's third scene, and the Folger's catalogue gives his picture a title that is itself a piece of criticism: the monument belonging to the Capulets, Romeo and Paris dead, Juliet and Friar Laurence. Meeting, discovery, tomb. No window scene, no parting, no wedding, no street fight. The Boydell canon, fixed by the market's first great machine, already contains the shape of the finished iconography, with one significant absence the nineteenth century would correct: the lovers alone together occupy only the meeting, and the meeting is a public room. The private emblem, the pair at the window with the world shut out, was left for later painters to invent as the play's centre.

The three pictures repay individual attention, because each founded a line. Miller's feast chose the tragedy's most social instant, the lovers meeting as strangers under the eyes of the whole Veronese world, masks and music around them, the sonnet's exchange of pilgrim and saint conducted in public. Its descendants are every ballroom Romeo since, the play imagined as a party at which doom is a guest. Opie's discovery chose the play's loudest grief, the chamber scene where the household finds the bride on her wedding morning and Capulet's rhetoric breaks into the terrible shorthand of possession undone: "Death is my son-in-law, Death is my heir" (4.5). Its descendants are the painted lamentations, the bed surrounded by a choir of mourners, Juliet's body already being rehearsed as the tomb's centrepiece while she is only sleeping. Northcote's monument, which survives at the Folger Shakespeare Library, chose the aftermath entire: the title's own inventory, Romeo and Paris dead, Juliet and the Friar present, makes the canvas a census of the catastrophe's cost with the rival counted and the churchman left standing to explain. Opie's canvas survives in the record by a thinner thread, its documented trace running to a Christie's appearance in 1892, a reminder that the canon the gallery fixed was preserved by prints more faithfully than by paint. Read as a triptych the three choices already argue the article's thesis. What Boydell's artists kept is the tragedy's frame: desire's first look, grief's first cry, cost's final sum. What they omitted is everything between the look and the cry that makes the sum intelligible: the marriage, the banishment's politics, the plague, the letter. The edit was complete by 1805. The nineteenth century merely illuminated it.

The fourth Boydell picture of the play is the one that never hung. Joseph Wright of Derby had proposed a Juliet waking in the tomb in 1776, a decade before the scheme, and Boydell took the subject up for the gallery. The commission ended in a quarrel over money and standing: Wright learned that his fee of three hundred pounds stood far below the thousand paid to artists he considered his inferiors, and the painting was rejected. Wright exhibited Romeo and Juliet: The Tomb Scene at the Royal Academy in 1790 instead, and wrote to a correspondent in 1791 that the picture had been painted too dark, that he had simplified the background and thrown more light into the tomb. The canvas that resulted, oil, roughly six feet by eight, hangs in Derby Museum and Art Gallery, which acquired it in 1981. It shows the tragedy's most inward instant: Juliet kneeling beside the body, a footstep heard, her hand on the dagger, the moment the text compresses into "Yea, noise? Then I'll be brief. O happy dagger!" (5.3). Wright, the painter of candlelight and furnaces, chose neither the embrace nor the reconciliation. He chose a woman alone deciding, lit like an experiment. The gallery's loss was the iconography's gain, because Wright's tomb is the strongest argument the eighteenth century produced that a Shakespeare picture could think.

### Why did Wright of Derby's tomb picture never hang in the Shakespeare Gallery?

The commission collapsed in a dispute over the fee and the painter's rank. Wright proposed the subject in 1776 and painted it for Boydell's scheme, but on learning that his three hundred pounds fell far short of the thousand paid to others, he quarrelled with the publisher; the canvas was rejected and reached the Royal Academy in 1790.

Charles Lamb delivered the period's own review of the whole enterprise, and his testimony matters because it comes from the defence's side of the courtroom: a reader who loved the play and felt the pictures as a loss. "What injury did not Boydell's Shakespeare Gallery do me with Shakespeare," Lamb wrote in his 1811 essay on the tragedies. "To have Opie's Shakespeare, Northcote's Shakespeare, light headed Fuseli's Shakespeare, wooden-headed West's Shakespeare, deaf-headed Reynolds' Shakespeare, instead of my and everybody's Shakespeare. To be tied down to an authentic face of Juliet! To confine the illimitable!" The complaint is usually quoted as a period curiosity. It is better read as a measurement. Lamb does not say the pictures were weak. He says they were strong enough to evict his own Shakespeare, to replace a face formed in reading with a face supplied by the trade, and for this play his chosen emblem of the injury is Juliet's portrait. No critic of the gallery has ever put the mechanism more exactly: a successful image does not decorate a reading, it forecloses one. The nineteenth century would industrialize Lamb's injury and call the product the play's visual tradition.

The machine broke on money and war. The continental market for prints, on which the accounts depended, closed as the French wars spread, subscriptions failed, and in December 1803 Boydell obtained an act of Parliament for a lottery of the whole collection. Twenty-two thousand tickets at three guineas sold; Boydell died in December 1804, days after the last ticket went; the draw in January 1805 gave the gallery and its contents to William Tassie, a gem engraver, who refused an offer of ten thousand pounds to keep the collection together and sent the pictures to Christie's instead. The May 1805 sale scattered them for a little over six thousand pounds in total. Only a third of the gallery's paintings are known to survive, by the Folger's count, and fourteen of them hung together again in an exhibition at the Folger that opened in October 2025, the first reunion since the dispersal. The dispersal is usually told as a business failure. For the iconography it was a distribution strategy beyond Boydell's imagining. The canvases vanished into country houses and salerooms, but the engravings, the folio, and the edition had already carried the faces into thousands of homes, and an image, once printed, cannot be recalled by any bankruptcy. The gallery died in 1805. The gallery's Juliet is with the culture still.

## How did the Romantics set the lovers free from the stage?

The Boydell pictures, for all their ambition, remained theatre translated into oil: proscenium spacing, rhetorical gesture, the scene held at the distance of a front row. The Romantic generation broke the proscenium. Its two defining treatments of the play, painted within thirteen years of each other in Milan and London, moved in opposite directions at once. One shrank the scene to a single kiss and charged it with the whole tragedy; the other dissolved the lovers into a city's dusk until they became the smallest figures on their own canvas. Between them, the iconography learned its two lasting tricks: intensification and atmosphere. The window and the tomb, the two stations the Romantics inherited, came out of their hands transformed into feelings rather than settings, and a feeling is a far more portable product than a setting. Settings stay in museums. Feelings get copied.

Francesco Hayez painted The Last Kiss of Romeo and Juliet in 1823, on commission from Giovanni Battista Sommariva, and exhibited the canvas at the Brera annual exhibition in Milan that year to popular success. The holding institution, Villa Carlotta on Lake Como, catalogues the picture as an oil on canvas; other catalogues divide on the medium and the dimensions, a small lesson in the hygiene this survey keeps repeating. The scene is the farewell, Romeo's last kiss before the exile tears the marriage night into the dawn parting, and Hayez floods it with the physical evidence of the whole plot: the warmth of the chamber against the danger outside, an embrace that reads as both consummation and theft. A documented caveat governs the picture's pedigree. Hayez drew as much on Luigi Da Porto's novella, the prose source the play itself descends from, as on Shakespeare's text, and his later Marriage of Romeo and Juliet of 1830 likewise follows Da Porto. The most famous painted kiss of the century is therefore only partly a picture of the play. It is a picture of the story the play shares with its source, the lovers as the Italian tradition held them before an English company ever reached Verona. The iconography's loyalties, the Hayez career proves, were never to the text. They were to the couple.

The success made Hayez a repeat offender against single images. He returned to the farewell in further versions in 1825 and twice in 1830, each an author's replica feeding a market that wanted the kiss again at new sizes and prices, and the Villa Carlotta account traces his celebrated Il bacio of 1859 back into this series, the kiss re-composed until the nameless medieval pair of 1859 could stand for every parted couple in the new Italy. Painting here performs the operation the whole article tracks: detach the emblem from the plot, polish it, and set it circulating as common property. Nothing in the play's text authorizes the migration. The kiss leaves the tragedy the way a chorus leaves a song, and once detached it owes the tragedy nothing. Romeo and Juliet became, in the Romantic decades, the brand under which European painting sold young love itself, and the brand's logo was an embrace at the point of separation. The meeting had offered courtship, the window courtship continued by other means. The parting offered the product in its purest form, love and loss in a single frame, and the market's preference among the three is the verdict the census in the next section records.

The Da Porto share in Hayez deserves its full weight, because it quietly changes what the iconography is an iconography of. A painter faithful to the play must answer to the play: to its scenes, its sequence, its words. A painter painting the story answers to a tradition in which the play is merely the most famous tenant. Hayez's versions slide between the two allegiances without friction, the farewell kiss checkable against the third act and against the novella at once, the Marriage of 1830 owing the novella outright. Once the story, rather than the text, becomes the warrant, the pictures begin citing one another as authority. A painter of 1860 who wanted the parting consulted Hayez's construction of it as readily as Shakespeare's, and a painter of 1880 consulted the accumulations of both, which is how traditions acquire their characteristic drift: each generation paints the previous generation's painting at one remove, and the text's share in the image thins with every transfer. The balcony's career is the drift's purest case, an architectural feature with no textual warrant whatsoever, sustained across two centuries entirely by picture citing picture. Hayez's kiss is the respectable end of the same process, a scene the text does contain, polished by repetition until its polish, not its source, is what successors copied. The Romantics did not set out to loosen the iconography from the play. They set out to feel the story more strongly than the stage allowed, and the loosening followed from the method: a feeling, once perfected in paint, belongs to whoever can reproduce the feeling, and the text's permission is neither sought nor required.

Turner's Juliet and Her Nurse, exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1836, executed the opposite manoeuvre. The picture, oil on canvas, some three feet by four, sets the figures on a balcony at the lower right of a Venetian vista at dusk: St Mark's Square, fireworks, the lagoon's traffic, a city performing its evening. The Metropolitan Museum of Art's Heilbrunn Timeline, in its account of Turner and the Romantic landscape, identifies the distant couple as Romeo and Juliet and reads the gorgeous dusk as the doomed appointment kept in the wrong city. For Verona's heroine stands in Venice, and the title's own geography is the picture's provocation. Turner had painted Venice from his visit of 1833, and the canvas is a Venetian capriccio wearing a Shakespearean title as its passport into the Academy's rooms. The reviewers noticed. The Reverend John Eagles, writing in Blackwood's Magazine, attacked the picture as a strange jumble, and John Ruskin's later account in Praeterita remembers how the ribaldry aimed at the freak of placing Juliet at Venice instead of Verona raised him to black anger. Ruskin, seventeen and unknown, wrote a defence of the picture, his first venture in art criticism, and a career in judgement began as a footnote to a Shakespeare painting. The canvas itself settled into a patron's collection, passing to Hugh Munro of Novar, and reached the wider public by the usual ferry: George Hollis's engraving of 1842, issued under the title St Mark's Place, Venice. The retitling is the census's favourite kind of evidence, a trade document confessing a trade truth. The print seller calculated, correctly, that the picture would sell as a view of Venice with the lovers as garnish, and the calculation records how completely the Romantic manoeuvre had succeeded. Boydell's prints had sold scenes of a play. Turner's print sold a place, a light, and an hour, with the play retained as a prestige watermark in the corner. The iconography had learned to survive the loss of its subject, which is the skill that would carry it through the portrait phase and into the souvenir century.

The quarrel is worth pausing over because it isolates what Romantic painting had done to the iconography. Eagles demanded fidelity to a place the play names; Ruskin defended the painter's liberty to treat the lovers as a chord to be struck in any key. Both were right about their own premises, and the picture survives the quarrel because it had made a decision the Boydell generation never attempted: the subject is not the scene but the mood the scene licenses. A balcony survives the transplant, notice. Even in Venice, even reduced to staffage at the edge of a fireworks display, Juliet is placed on a balcony, the prop the text never gave her, assembled by the pictorial tradition and carried from canvas to canvas like a piece of touring scenery. The iconography had become self-quoting. Painters no longer consulted the play for the lovers' furniture. They consulted earlier paintings, and the balcony crossed the Alps and the lagoon without a passport from the Folger text, which offers a window and nothing more: "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2). The word in the line is window. The architecture in the pictures is a balcony. That gap, repeated until it hardened, is the single most influential misreading in the play's visual history, and the Romantics, for all their liberty, never questioned it.

### Why did Turner put Juliet in Venice instead of Verona?

Because the picture is a Venetian evening that borrowed the lovers as its excuse and its accent. Turner had painted the city from his 1833 visit, and the canvas subordinates the pair to fireworks, water, and dusk over St Mark's Square. Verona supplies the title and the balcony; Venice supplies everything the painter actually cared to show.

## Which scenes did the painters choose, and which did they refuse?

The census can be stated plainly once the verified corpus is laid in a row. Painters chose thresholds and states; they refused machinery and argument. The chosen scenes share a geometry and a grammar. The meeting is a look exchanged across a room, desire at first sight with the plot held offstage. The window scene is a conversation the painters converted into architecture, two figures on a vertical axis, she above, he below, the house between them like the feud made stone. The parting repeats the axis at dawn with the embrace added and the lark as executioner: "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5). The discovery is a household arranged around a bed, grief in formation. The tomb is grief arranged around two bodies, the vertical axis collapsed at last into the horizontal. What unites the five is that each can be understood by a viewer who knows no Italian, no English, and no plot, because each is a universal posture wearing Veronese dress: courtship, longing, farewell, mourning, death. The pictures are legible in the way proverbs are legible. That is their genius and their fraudulence in a single property, since a tragedy whose meaning rides on proverb-postures has been stripped of everything that makes it this tragedy rather than any other.

The refused scenes are the tragedy's engine room. The street brawls of the first and third acts, the Queen Mab speech, the plague that quarantines Friar John, the Apothecary's shop, the Friar's long reasoning with the frantic girl, and above all the wedding in the cell: the verified corpus offers no major canvas for any of them. The wedding's absence is the loudest, because the wedding is the plot's hinge, the act that converts courtship into catastrophe, and its only significant painted treatment, Hayez's Marriage of 1830, illustrates Da Porto's novella rather than the play's cell scene. Even the one speech the play sets at the ceremony is a warning the painters quoted with their selections while skipping its occasion: "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6). The potion scene tells the same story from inside Juliet's chamber. Her soliloquy over the vial, "I have a faint cold fear thrills through my veins" (4.3), is the play's supreme study of a mind arguing with terror, and the corpus leaves it almost untouched, preferring the potion's results, the bed discovered at dawn, to its decision. Painters wanted Juliet's body in postures. Her mind at work, talking for forty lines to an empty room, defeated the medium's habits, and the defeat is itself an iconographic fact: the tradition that claims to picture the play pictures everything except the thinking.

The survey grades each scene of the play by its pictorial standing across the documented corpus, from the scenes painters returned to until the returns became the public's play, down to the scenes the tradition refused. The grades rest on the works named in the article; they measure survival and repetition, not quality, and the distinction is the argument. Wright's tomb may be the finest single picture in the corpus and still grade below Dicksee's farewell as a force in the culture, because force is counted in copies, and the survey counts what the culture copied.

Read down the standing column, the survey shows the edit completing itself. The tradition begins with scenes the play actually stages and ends with a face that needs no scene at all, and at every step the criterion of selection is the same: what can be felt at a glance survives, what must be understood by following an argument does not. The balcony's supremacy within the grades is the survey's headline, and it deserves its emphasis, because the balcony is the tradition's own contribution, absent from the text, present in the pictures from the window conversation's pictorial youth, enthroned by Dicksee, rebuilt in Verona's stone, and finally so completely naturalized that correcting it requires an argument where the error required none. The following sections take the canon's three most instructive cases in turn: the great exception, Leighton's ending that nobody copied; the great correction, the Pre-Raphaelite parting and tomb of 1870; and the great fixation, Dicksee's farewell and the portrait phase that dissolved the scenes into a face.

The favoured grades have their own instructive mechanics, and the meeting's is the neatest. The feast scene hands painters a problem in pure form: the play's most famous courtship is a sonnet, fourteen lines traded line about by two strangers, a verbal artefact with almost no action in it beyond an approach, a dance declined, and a touch. Painters solved the problem by seizing the sonnet's own central conceit. The shared poem is built on pilgrims and saints and the joining of hands, "palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss," and the handclasp it licenses becomes the scene's visual signature, the one gesture a canvas can hold still while the verse that justifies it flies past. Miller's Boydell feast stands at the head of a long line of ballroom treatments in which the lovers are discoverable, amid the masks and the music, by their joined hands and locked attention, the crowd arranged as a frame for a privacy performed in public. The meeting grades favoured because it alone among the stations contains its own excuse for beauty: a feast justifies costume, candlelight, and company, and a first look justifies the suspension of everything else the play contains. It is the tragedy's least tragic scene, the one moment the catastrophe has not yet begun to tax, and the tradition's fondness for it is the fondness of an art that preferred its lovers untaxed. Between the feast's untaxed look and the tomb's final accounting, the survey's whole argument is contained: painting kept the look and the accounting, and invoiced the middle of the play, where the tax falls due, to nobody.

Two grades in the survey reward a second look, because they locate the tradition's blind spots with unusual precision. The discovery's merely occasional standing surprises anyone who knows the scene's power in the theatre, where the household's lament over the bed is the play's first full rehearsal of its ending. On canvas the scene labours under a structural defect the painters could not design away: it is a duet play photographed with one of the pair absent and the other unconscious. The discovery offers grief without reciprocity, a chorus around a sleeper, and the market that bought the lovers together, vertical at the window or locked in the farewell, declined a composition in which neither lover can return a look. The potion's refusal cuts deeper still. The vial itself migrates happily through the corpus as a prop: Lucy Madox Brown's tomb watercolour sets it beside the bodies, where it testifies mutely to the mechanism. What no major painter took was the vial's scene, the forty lines in which a fourteen-year-old argues herself past terror into swallowing it. The tradition's Juliet is looked at in every station of her story. She is never shown thinking, and the survey's most eloquent row is the empty one, because it records the exact point where painting's idea of the heroine and the play's idea of her part company beyond repair.

## What did painters do with Verona itself?

A census of scenes conceals a second census, of place, and its returns are stranger than the first. The play names Verona on its opening line of setting and keeps the city present as walls, watch, law, and exile's geography; the banishment works as a death sentence because Mantua is far. Painters, who needed Verona in every frame, painted it hardly at all. What the corpus shows, scene after scene, is Italianate atmosphere in place of a city: a loggia that could stand in any peninsula town, a palace stair, a cypress, a depth of golden air. Turner made the substitution literal by moving the heroine to Venice and was scolded for the one honest version of a universal practice. The others kept the caption and generalized the place, which is the same liberty with better manners. Verona in the painted tradition is not a location. It is a licence, a warrant that the architecture may be old, the stone warm, and the light southern, after which the painter invents as freely as the scene requires.

The invention had consequences no canvas intended. A city repeatedly pictured as a generic beautiful backdrop becomes, in the public's possession, a stage set awaiting its furniture, and the furniture arrived by the logic traced in this article: first the balcony, supplied by the window scene's needs; then, in the fullness of the tourist century, the tomb, the courtyard, and the house, assembled in Verona itself to match the pictures the visitors carried in their heads. The direction of influence is the point. A working city did not teach painters how the play looked. Painters taught a working city how it was expected to look, and the city, accommodating trade with the fidelity of a good host, rearranged its stones until the expectation could be photographed. The surveyed scenes grade the window supreme; the place census explains its supremacy's final proof. An iconography that can rebuild its setting in masonry has passed from representation into planning permission, and the painted Verona, invented in London, Milan, and Paris studios by men working from other paintings, stands in the province of Verona receiving visitors who find it authentic because it matches the pictures. Nothing in this history diminishes the canvases. It measures their power at the only scale that settles arguments about images: they altered the physical world their admirers walk through, and they did it while misquoting the text that licensed them.

The place census also acquits the painters of a charge the scene census invites. Judged by scenes, the tradition looks incurious, returning to five stations while a five-act play burned around them. Judged by place, the same painters were explorers of a kind, testing how far the lovers could be carried from their text before the image broke: into Georgian drawing rooms in Hayman's plates, into Venetian dusk in Turner's vista, into Pre-Raphaelite archaeology in Brown's researched stone, into a portrait's placeless blue in Waterhouse's last reduction. The image never broke. It held in every climate, which is itself a finding about the play's visual core: what survives translation across rooms, cities, and centuries is not Verona, not the feud, not even the plot, but the vertical diagram of the pair, she in the light above, he in the dark below, desire drawn as architecture. Verona was the licence. The balcony was the cargo. The painters who seem, by the scene census, to have narrowed the play were, by the place census, stress-testing its emblem against the whole map of Italy, and the emblem passed every test they set it. A tradition can be an editor and an explorer at once, and the survey's two censuses, read together, convict and acquit it in the same breath.

## Why did Leighton paint the ending nobody else wanted?

Frederic Leighton's The Reconciliation of the Montagues and the Capulets over the Dead Bodies of Romeo and Juliet is the one great canvas in the corpus whose subject is the play's verdict rather than its passion. The work survives in two documented states. The Yale Center for British Art holds the signed and dated watercolour of 1854, watercolour with gouache over graphite, a sheet of some sixteen by twenty-one inches. The large oil, dated 1855 and measuring just over five feet ten by seven feet seven, was exhibited at the Paris exhibition of that year; a Philadelphia sale catalogue of the oil printed beneath the entry the passage the picture illustrates, Capulet's outstretched submission and Montague's answer, closing on "O brother Montague, give me thy hand" (5.3). Listings divide on the oil's present home, and the survey records the division rather than choosing: the watercolour at Yale is the version a student can stand in front of, and it carries the design entire.

The design is an argument, and its audacity is easy to miss because the picture is beautiful. Every other major treatment in the corpus arranges the lovers as the centre and lets the world attend them. Leighton demotes them. The bodies lie in the foreground as evidence, horizontal, finished, while the picture's action happens standing up and further back: two old men, the feud's proprietors, meeting over what their quarrel purchased. The protagonist of the canvas is the peace, arriving at the price the play itself states, and the composition enforces the play's own bookkeeping by making the eye travel from the cost to the settlement. A young painter in his middle twenties, formed in Rome and Frankfurt rather than in the London schools, produced the one picture in the tradition that takes the tragedy's final audit as its subject. Nothing in the market rewarded the choice, which is precisely the diagnostic value of the picture. The reconciliation grades as singular in the survey not because it failed as art but because it succeeded as criticism, and the tradition's refusal to repeat it maps the boundary between the pictures the culture wanted and the pictures the play deserved.

Why did the ending go uncopied, when the tomb a yard to its left in the same scene was painted again and again? The prints market supplies the mechanism. An engraving of the reconciliation offers a buyer a handshake and two corpses; an engraving of the parting offers an embrace; an engraving of Wright's tomb offers a heroine at the pitch of decision. Each of the repeated scenes gives its owner a feeling to live with. The reconciliation gives its owner a judgement to live under, and domestic walls have never been a growth market for judgements. Leighton's canvas also refuses the vertical consolation the tradition loved. The window scene promises that love rises; the tomb, for all its horror, lets love lie down in state, mourned by architecture. The reconciliation insists that the story's last word belongs neither to love nor to death but to the men who made both necessary, standing in the wreckage with their hands out. Painters after Leighton who wanted the play's seriousness took the tomb and skipped its sequel, keeping the corpses and dropping the handshake. The survey's grade for the scene, singular, is the tradition's confession: it wanted the tragedy priced in feeling, and Leighton priced it in blame.

The picture's maker's later career sharpens the irony rather than softening it. Leighton became the Victorian art establishment's most complete embodiment, president of the Royal Academy, the first British artist raised to the peerage, a man whose own enormous history paintings defined official taste for a generation. The Reconciliation is the young outsider's work, designed in the watercolour of 1854, when its painter was in his middle twenties and formed by study in Rome and Frankfurt rather than by the London schools, and it argues like an outsider: no star part, no embrace, the play's least marketable minutes promoted to the subject. The establishment painter he became never repeated the experiment at this pitch, and neither did anyone else, which leaves the canvas in its survey grade as a permanent might-have-been for the whole tradition. Had the Reconciliation multiplied as the parting multiplied, the public's play would carry its verdict inside its favourite image, and the feud's owners would stand in the iconography beside the lovers they ruined. The tradition declined the offer. It is worth adding that the design survives complete in the Yale watercolour, gouache heightening a graphite drawing, so that the picture's argument can be studied in the very state in which it was first made, before the oil's scale and Paris showing dressed it for the Salon world. Arguments in art usually survive by reproduction. This one survives by a sheet of paper in Connecticut, unreproduced into fame, which suits it.

## What did the Pre-Raphaelites correct in the painted lovers?

Ford Madox Brown's Romeo and Juliet, Delaware Art Museum, oil on canvas signed and dated 1870, is the Victorian era's most consequential treatment of the play, and its first correction is a correction of identity. The picture shows the dawn parting of the third act's fifth scene, the lovers' farewell on the balcony with Romeo descending by the rope ladder toward exile, not the first window conversation of the second act with which it is endlessly confused. The Delaware label says as much in plain terms, calling the scene the poignant moment in the early dawn when Romeo parts from his love for what will be the final time. The confusion the label has to correct is the tradition's own doing: paint the pair at a balcony often enough, at whatever hour and in whatever act, and the public files every version under the one scene it knows. Brown's biographer, his grandson Ford Madox Hueffer, dates the oil's execution from October 1867 to completion in July 1870, a long labour; a watercolour version is at the Whitworth, and the Bancroft provenance carried the oil through the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893 to its Delaware home. Brown stood godfather to the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood without ever belonging to it, the mentor at one remove, and the picture carries the movement's doctrine into the play's most inherited scene.

### Is Ford Madox Brown's celebrated canvas a picture of the balcony scene?

No. The Delaware Art Museum's label places the moment in the third act's fifth scene: the early dawn on the balcony, Romeo parting from Juliet for what will be the final time, the rope ladder over the rail. The picture is a farewell misremembered as a courtship: in the public mind the balcony files every embrace under one scene.

The doctrine shows in the painting's famous ungainliness. The Delaware label names it directly, observing that the dramatic awkwardness of Romeo's climb down the rope ladder gives the subjects a sense of realistic immediacy. Where the Boydell generation posed its lovers like actors conscious of the house, and where Hayez melted his into a single sculptural knot, Brown paints weight, leverage, and risk: a young man genuinely climbing down from a stone balcony at dawn, a wife genuinely holding the ladder of his escape, early Renaissance dress researched rather than draped. The picture argues, in the medium's own terms, that the parting's pathos is physical before it is lyrical, that the scene's truth is a body halfway down a wall with the watch coming. This is criticism by canvas, the strongest support in the corpus for the claim, examined in a later section, that the best literary painting does not serve the text but cross-examines it. Brown cross-examined the tradition's favourite piece of stage furniture and found the lovers' real scene was not the moonlit conversation but the grey departure the conversation made inevitable. The window made the iconography; the parting made the tragedy; the survey's grades show the public kept the furniture and the painters, at their best, kept the departure.

The same year closes the family's diptych. Lucy Madox Brown, the painter's daughter, exhibited her own Romeo and Juliet in 1870, a watercolour of the tomb scene, the fifth act's third, in which Romeo bends over Juliet's apparently lifeless face with the spent vial beside the pair. The work hangs at Wightwick Manor under the National Trust. Father and daughter, in a single year, divided the play between them at its two poles: he took the parting, the lovers still warm and the catastrophe still avoidable; she took the tomb, the parting's invoice come due. The Watts Gallery's exhibition of Lucy Madox Brown's work took its title, Uncommon Power, from the words of Tom Taylor in The Times on this picture, and the phrase catches the watercolour's refusal of the tomb scene's usual consolations. No reconciliation softens her frame, no fathers arrive; the picture stays inside the minutes when the catastrophe is complete and nobody in Verona knows it yet. The tradition had painted the tomb as a public arrangement of grief since Northcote. Lucy Madox Brown painted it as a private one, a man reading a face for a life already misread once by everyone who loved her, and the economy of the watercolour, its small scale and close crop, enforces the privacy. Between the father's ladder and the daughter's vial, 1870 contains the whole corrected iconography: bodies under strain, architecture researched, feeling earned by fact. The Pre-Raphaelite correction was never mainly about jewelled colour. It was about weight.

Brown's parting then behaved the way successful images in this history always behave: it multiplied. The Delaware oil is the prime version, and the record around it is a small industry: the watercolour version at the Whitworth, a smaller watercolour dated across 1868 to 1871 in the saleroom literature, and a monochrome version of 1876 associated with Bradford's collection. Replica culture is usually discussed as a commercial footnote, painters feeding demand. In this survey it counts as evidence of the first rank, because a version exists only where a buyer wanted the image again, and Brown's ladder was wanted again and again by buyers who might have had any graceful moonlit alternative. The correction, in other words, sold. The market that had enthroned the window as courtship accepted the same furniture recast as an escape route at dawn, which suggests the public's loyalty was never to grace as such. It was to the vertical composition itself, the girl above and the boy below with the house between them, a diagram so strong that it survived being filled with opposite meanings: first the promise that the lovers would meet, then the knowledge that they were parting. Form outlasted content inside the tradition's favourite shape, and the diagram's durability is the deepest reason the balcony, in all its variants, grades supreme in the survey. Painters argued about what happened on it. Nobody argued about whether the play's emblem should be played out on it, because by 1870 the question had been answered by sixty years of walls.

## How did Dicksee's farewell become the play's public portrait?

Frank Dicksee's Romeo and Juliet of 1884, oil on canvas at Southampton City Art Gallery, is the picture the culture chose when it finally voted with its walls, and the gallery's own record explains the choice's mechanics. The gallery states that in the 1880s Dicksee provided illustrations for a luxury edition of the play, one of which became the basis of this painting, and that the canvas depicts the end of the famous balcony scene and Romeo's line, "Farewell, farewell, one kiss and I'll descend" (2.2). The picture therefore began life inside a book, as one plate among many in an expensive object for prosperous shelves, and graduated to the wall without changing its nature: it is an illustration that achieved the scale and finish of a history painting while keeping an illustration's contract with its viewer, which is to deliver the story's most desired instant, unobstructed, at the highest polish. The canvas reached Southampton by bequest in 1941, from J. J. Crossfield, and the gallery records that a poll voted it Britain's most romantic artwork, a title whose significance is less the ranking than the category. By the measure of that vote, the play's public portrait is not its beginning, its wedding, or its catastrophe. It is a goodbye kiss at a window, framed by the architecture the text never supplied.

Describe the picture's decision and the fixation explains itself. Dicksee paints the farewell at the window as a vertical sacrament: the lovers locked at the top of the composition where the light is, the drop below them carrying the eye down the wall toward the garden and the exile waiting in it. The line the gallery cites is the scene's hinge, the moment courtship turns into departure, and Dicksee fuses into one frame what the tradition usually separated: the window conversation's architecture, the parting's emotion, the tomb's foreknowledge, since no viewer of 1884 or since can look at the kiss without knowing its price. The canvas is the iconography's summa, every station folded into the most reproducible one. Its supremacy in the survey's grades is not a judgement that it is the corpus's best painting; Wright's tomb argues harder, Brown's parting sees more, Leighton's ending judges more. Dicksee wins on transmission. A picture engineered inside a luxury book, finished like a jewel, attached to the play's most quoted farewell, and ratified by a popular vote is not an artwork competing with rivals. It is infrastructure. When the souvenir trade, the greeting card, and the classroom poster needed the lovers, the image that presented itself was this one, and the balcony it enthrones had by then spent a century being built by hands that never opened the text to check whether the play contained one. It does not. The play contains a window, an orchard wall, and a girl leaning out to talk to the dark. The balcony is painting's gift to the play, and Dicksee's canvas is the gift's presentation copy.

The luxury edition that fathered the canvas deserves a word of its own, because it marks the moment the iconography's two economies, the wall and the page, finally merged into a single product. The illustrated gift book of the 1880s was engineered for prosperous domestic display: large pages, fine plates, a binding that furnished a room, a text that furnished a conscience. To commission a play's illustrations for such an object was to accept, in advance, the census's whole discipline, since a gift book does not sell the plague scene to a Christmas parlour. Dicksee delivered the discipline's masterpiece, a farewell engineered to glow. When the design was then promoted to a full-scale oil, the transaction reversed the usual direction of reproduction: prints ordinarily shrink paintings for the page, but this painting enlarged a page's design for the wall, carrying the illustration's obligations with it, legibility at a glance, sentiment unobstructed, the story's best second framed. The popular vote the gallery records, naming the canvas the most romantic artwork in Britain, is therefore less a surprise than a ratification of design intent across a century and a half of distribution. Romantic is the tradition's own word for what it had been selecting since Boydell: the feeling at the expense of the machinery. Dicksee's Juliet, leaning from her painted balcony into her painted night, is the feeling perfected, and the poll merely certified the perfection in the vocabulary the market had always used among itself.

## When did Juliet stop needing Romeo in the picture?

The final phase of the painted tradition drops the scene and sells the face. Its conditions were set early. In the 1830s Charles Heath's Shakspeare Gallery issued the heroines as collectible plates, designed by Kenny Meadows among others, and the Folger's account of the beauties tradition describes the code those plates enforced: rosebud lips, sweet faces, the heroines rendered as daydreaming girls of the viewer's own decade. Juliet under this code is not a Veronese of the trecento arguing her father down in blank verse. She is an ideal of girlhood with a famous name, and the name, not the play, does the commercial work. The format had a pedigree the trade understood perfectly. Hanmer's system allotted one plate to a play; the heroine industry allotted one plate to a woman, and the second allotment simply completed the first's logic at a smaller unit, the way a later century would complete Boydell's folio with the postage stamp. Each reduction preserved the selling unit and discarded context in a fixed ratio: the play lost its acts to the scene, then the scene lost its partner, its room, and its occasion to the face. Nothing in the sequence required a villain. It required only publishers solving, at each step, the same problem Hanmer's contract posed: what single image will stand for the whole, at this size, at this price, for this buyer. The answers differ by century. The problem is the constant, and its constancy is why the survey reads as one tradition rather than as a series of fashions.

The single-figure portrait detaches the character from every scene that gives her meaning and re-attaches her to a market in feminine types. Ophelia gets her flowers and her stream, Cleopatra her asp and her barge; Juliet, whose play is a duet, was reissued as a solo, and the reissue is the tradition's most candid admission of what it had been selling all along. Not the tragedy. A heroine, with the tragedy as her certificate of depth.

The Graphic magazine industrialized the solo in 1888. The paper commissioned twenty-one paintings of Shakespeare's heroines, displayed them in a London gallery, and reproduced them as double-page centrefolds and in gravure portfolios as The Graphic Gallery of Shakespeare's Heroines, with commentary by W. E. Henley; the Folger's catalogue of the series adds that the pictures toured further than London. Philip Hermogenes Calderon's Juliet was this play's contribution: the heroine alone, signed and dated 1888, offered to subscribers as one panel in a complete set of Shakespearean womanhood. A Sotheby's catalogue essay on a companion picture in the series records that the set drew the attention of Queen Victoria, who had the pictures brought for her inspection; the anecdote's market meaning needs no inflation. The painted Juliet had become a parlour furnishing with a royal viewing in its provenance, a collectible in a series whose completeness was the point. Collect the heroines as a set and the plays survive as captions. The tradition that began with an editor choosing one scene per play ended with a magazine choosing one face per play, and the selection logic is identical. Only the unit shrank, from the scene to the sitter.

The set's completeness was its sales pitch, and completeness performs a quiet violence on the material that scene selection never attempted. A scene can be argued with; a set can only be completed. The subscriber assembling the twenty-one heroines was not invited to ask whether Juliet belongs alone, or whether a duet play survives portraiture, but whether the Ophelia had arrived and whether the Cleopatra matched. Henley's accompanying texts, retelling each heroine's story beside her plate, supplied the minimum of narrative the format allowed, a paragraph of plot to license a page of face. Under this dispensation the play becomes documentation for its own cover portrait. It is worth pausing on the royal inspection the Sotheby's account records, because it captures the portrait phase's social completion: the heroines, engraved for a magazine's subscribers, carried to a palace to be looked at by the sovereign in whose reign the type of the sweet daydreaming girl had become the national template for young womanhood. The painted Juliet's journey from Hanmer's frontispiece ends there, in the most respectable room in the kingdom, as one panel in a set. Nothing in the journey required anyone to read the tragedy, and almost everything in it rewarded not reading it too closely: a close reader knows what the solo portrait omits, which is the entire world that made the sitter interesting.

John William Waterhouse's Juliet of 1898 completes the drainage. The canvas, exhibited at the New Gallery in London that year and held in a private collection, shows Juliet alone in a landscape by water, and the artist's catalogues record its later circulation under a second title drawn from her dress, The Blue Necklace. No balcony attends her, no Romeo, no Friar, no parents, no vial; the picture cites no line and stages no moment a reader could footnote. What remains is the name and a melancholy girl in blue, and the double title is the tradition's epitaph for itself. A painting that can be renamed for a necklace has stopped illustrating a play and started furnishing a mood, and the market received it exactly so, as it had received Calderon's centrefold and Heath's plates. The face survived the story. This is the end-state the survey's grades record: the iconography's last Victorian form is a portrait whose sitter happens to be fictional, requiring of its viewer nothing but the name, and giving back nothing but the feeling the name has been trained, by a century of canvases, to release. Lamb's confinement is complete in it. The authentic face no longer confines the imagination to a scene. It confines the play to a face, and the face to an accessory.

Waterhouse's wider practice shows the Juliet was no accident of one commission but the product of a career-long method. He painted Shakespeare's women across decades, Ophelia above all, returning to the drowned girl in picture after picture while the century closed, and his method was constant: take the name, keep the beauty, release the story's machinery, and let landscape and dress carry a melancholy the text would have earned by argument. The Juliet of 1898 is the method applied to the heroine whose story resists it hardest, since Juliet's meaning is relational to the bone: a daughter against a father, a wife against a city's law, a mind against terror in the potion speech. Strip the relations and the figure that remains is beautiful and vacant in exact proportion, which the picture does not hide and the market did not mind. Exhibited at the New Gallery in 1898, the canvas belongs to the last season of the portrait phase's confidence, and its afterlife confirms the grade the survey assigns. It hangs in no public collection to anchor a pilgrimage, as Dicksee's canvas anchors Southampton; it circulates as an image among images, its sitter's necklace promoted to a title, the play's name doing the work the scene no longer does. The tradition that began by choosing the play's moments ends by choosing its faces, and the faces, at the last, are interchangeable with any beautiful melancholy the market cared to label. Juliet alone by the water is the iconography's masterpiece of subtraction, and the subtraction is the verdict the next section's critics were already arguing about while the paint was wet.

## Is a Shakespeare painting an illustration, a criticism, or a theft?

The three answers have names, dates, and books, and the corpus assembled here is large enough to judge between them. Lamb's answer came first, in the 1811 essay on the tragedies: the pictured face is a theft from the reader's imagination, the illimitable confined, Opie's Shakespeare displacing everybody's Shakespeare. His chosen emblem, remember, was this play's heroine, the authentic face of Juliet. The scholarly answer of the late twentieth century ran the other way in temperature if not entirely in kind. Richard D. Altick's Paintings from Books of 1985, the standard survey of British literary painting, treated the whole field as a branch of the illustration trade, its pictures rising and falling with the fortunes of the illustrated book, subordinate by constitution to the texts they served. On Altick's map, a Romeo and Juliet canvas is a leaf from the book's long appendix, handsome evidence about publishing, minor evidence about painting, and no evidence at all about the play. The artists were tradesmen of other men's meanings.

Stuart Sillars, in Painting Shakespeare: The Artist as Critic, 1720-1820 of 2006, redrew the map. Sillars's artists work from the new scholarly editions rather than from the stage, and their canvases constitute a tradition of critical and interpretive painting: pictures that read the plays, take positions on them, and argue. The Boydell gallery, in his account, amounted to radically new reading practices made visible, and the mechanism of selection supports him. Quoted in the University of Iowa's account of the book, Sillars observes that no record exists of Boydell's having issued instructions to individual artists about the subjects to be represented; the painters proposed their scenes themselves, and some canvases were begun before the scheme existed. Miller's meeting, Opie's discovery, Northcote's monument are therefore not a publisher's table of contents. They are three critics' essays that happen to be in oil, competing readings of where the tragedy's weight lies, hung in one room to be argued with. The national museum culture eventually ratified the revaluation: Jane Martineau's exhibition Shakespeare in Art at Dulwich Picture Gallery in 2003 hung the material as a subject in its own right, three centuries of it, and the crowds treated the pictures as authorities on the plays rather than as ornaments of them.

The field had known its own extent for a long while; what changed across the century was the valuation. Salaman's Shakespeare in Pictorial Art, issued by The Studio as a special number in 1916, already surveyed the territory with a connoisseur's confidence, though its sympathies ran to craftsmanship and its judgements stayed inside the illustration frame. Winifred Friedman's Harvard research on Boydell's gallery, a dissertation of 1973, rebuilt the enterprise's archive, its commissions, its hang, its numbers, from the documents, and every serious account since stands on that recovery: the counts this article cites divide in part because the archive itself is a ruin the documents only partly repair. Sillars wrote with the archive open in a way Altick, a decade earlier, had not needed to, and the sequence matters for the adjudication. The interpretation of Shakespeare painting as criticism was not a fashion applied to passive material. It was what the evidence looked like once the commissions, contracts, and quarrels were read as a record of decisions rather than as a shelf of decorations. Lamb, writing before any archive existed, judged by consequence and judged rightly; the documents, when they came, added mechanism to his consequence and produced Sillars. Altick's survey, the noblest statement of the older frame, stands between them as the record of how the field appeared when the pictures were still shelved under the books they served.

### What exactly did Charles Lamb fear from an authentic face of Juliet?

He feared foreclosure. A pictured Juliet, manufactured by the trade and ratified by an exhibition, would stand between every reader and the heroine the reader's own mind had built, until the supplied face became the only face the play could wear. The illimitable, in his word, would be confined to one painter's afternoon of decisions.

The adjudication the corpus supports splits the verdict by stratum, because the three critics were describing different pictures. On mechanism, Sillars wins outright. The documented behaviour of the painters is the behaviour of critics: Wright quarrelling with his publisher over a tomb scene he had conceived a decade before the commission existed; Turner relocating the heroine to Venice against the text's geography and being defended for the liberty by the young Ruskin; Brown spending three years correcting the tradition's favourite scene into an awkward dawn climb; Leighton demoting the lovers in their own tragedy to make room for its verdict. None of this is the conduct of illustrators executing instructions. These are readings, some of them hostile to the readings that preceded them, and the selection census is their collected works. On consequence, Lamb wins outright, and his victory does not contradict Sillars; it completes him. Precisely because the pictures were strong readings, they overwrote the private text for the millions who met the play through engravings, plates, centrefolds, and posters. The confinement Lamb feared is the survey's central measurement. Altick's hierarchy survives only where the pictures surrender, in the portrait phase, where Heath's plates, The Graphic's set, and Waterhouse's necklace do subordinate painting to a publishing formula, and there the word illustration is exact and damning. The disagreement dissolves once the corpus is sorted by ambition. Shakespeare painting was criticism when it argued with the scene, theft when it merely succeeded, and illustration when it gave up and sold the face.

## Where did the painted play go when painting let it go?

It went, first, deeper into the book. The illustrated edition that opened this history did not close it; the nineteenth century's great Shakespeare editions carried wood-engraved armies of images, Sir John Gilbert's designs among the most prolific, and Sillars's companion history of the illustrated Shakespeare runs its story to 1875, by which date the hand-made image had done its canonical work. After the Victorian peak, major painters largely left the play alone, and the image-making contract passed to the camera: the play's public pictures in the following century are production stills and film frames, the 1996 film's stills among the most remixed, rather than canvases argued over in academies. The migration obeyed the old economics in new materials. A still is a plate for an edition of millions, and the selections the camera made repeat the painters' census with eerie fidelity: the window, the parting, the tomb, the faces. The machinery stayed unfilmed in spirit even when it was filmed in fact, present as plot and absent as image, which is the census's definition of refusal wearing a new technology.

The classroom completed what the camera began. Generations of school editions carried the canonical selections as frontispieces and plates, the window and the parting greeting pupils at the thresholds of acts the pictures had chosen for them, so that the sequence of instruction ran image first, text second, exactly as it had in Rowe's parlours. A pupil formed by such an edition experiences the play as an illustrated story whose pictures happen to be unusually well captioned by verse, and the machinery scenes, unpictured in the schoolbook as in the folio, register as connective tissue between the stations the images sanctified. Pedagogy is the iconography's quietest distribution channel and its most effective, because it operates before taste forms and files its selections under the name of the text itself. Nobody remembers choosing the balcony as the play's centre. It was assigned, in a classroom, by a plate, carrying the authority of the book it prefaced. The survey's grades, which measure repetition across two centuries of adult markets, understate the case by exactly the volume of this juvenile traffic, where the edit was absorbed as fact by readers who had not yet read a scene. Lamb feared a gallery. The deeper confinement was set in type and issued to schools.

It went, second, into matter. The painted balcony, assembled by the pictorial tradition from a window the text does supply and a structure it does not, was rebuilt in Verona as architecture, and [the account of the Casa di Giulietta and its constructed balcony](/2029/06/22/casa-di-giulietta-balcony-myth/) belongs to the building's own strange record; the traffic between canvas and courtyard ran in both directions, each certifying the other's invention. The faces shrank, third, into the state's smallest portraits, the miniature art of [stamps and coins struck with the lovers' profiles](/2029/12/21/romeo-and-juliet-stamps-and-coins/), where the iconography's last reduction is performed by engravers working at the scale of a thumbnail and still choosing, century after century, the same kiss, the same window, the same pair of profiles. Nothing in that persistence is accidental, and none of it is the text's doing. Painters made the shortlist. Every later medium inherited it, the way every later edition inherited Hanmer's plates: as a settled decision about what the play looks like, taken before the audience arrives.

A word is owed to the comfortable claim that images rescue the play from mere text, that painting restores to the tragedy the bodies, rooms, and weather that print strips away. The census supports the claim's letter and refutes its spirit. Painting did restore bodies, and the restoration was genuine gain: Brown's ladder carries a knowledge of the parting's physics that no stage direction holds, and Wright's tomb preserves a reading of Juliet's solitude at the dagger that criticism since has only paraphrased. But the restored iconography congealed, in its turn, into a second confinement, a visual cliché as coercive as any verbal one, under which the play must wear a balcony it never mentions and resolve itself into a kiss it survives by three acts. The text's clichés, the star-crossed tag, the rose by any other name, at least quote the play. The pictures' cliché corrects it, supplying architecture the words refuse and amputating the machinery the words insist on. Rescue and editing were the same act viewed from two sides, and the proof is that the world which received the rescue defends the edit as the original, correcting the text, when the two disagree, in the picture's favour.

One complication in the record deserves its verdict before the verdict, because it is the strongest form of the case against this article's own emphasis. It is often said that the images shaped the popular imagination of the play as much as the text did, and the census assembled here suggests the saying understates the matter for the majority of the play's public. The text shaped the imagination of readers, a company that has always been outnumbered by the company of lookers: the parlour that knew the folio but not the quarto, the subscriber who completed the heroines without opening the histories, the schoolchild whose edition assigned the pictures before the scenes, the visitor who travels to a courtyard in Verona to stand beneath an architecture learned from canvases. For that majority the images did not rival the text's influence; they replaced it, supplying plot, faces, places, and emotional instructions in a form that required no literacy in blank verse and brooked no ambiguity of staging. The text's defenders reply, correctly, that every image in the corpus is parasitic on the words, that no Dicksee hangs without the farewell line beneath it, and that the pictures' authority is borrowed capital. The census grants the premise and denies the conclusion. Borrowed capital, compounded over two centuries of editions, folios, centrefolds, classrooms, and courtyards, becomes the larger fortune, and the play the world actually possesses, the one it can picture unbidden, is the painters' edition of it. Shakespeare wrote the capital. The image trades did the compounding. The verdict follows from the arithmetic.

The verdict, then, stands as the census delivered it. Painting edited Romeo and Juliet into five stations and one face, in an order the play does not have: the balcony first, though the word is absent from every early text; the meeting as courtship's emblem; the parting as love's purest portable form; the discovery and the tomb as grief's two postures; and the wedding, the brawls, the reasoning, the plague, and the failed letter discarded entire, the causes struck from the record while the effects were framed. The edit was performed by critics in oils who believed, most of them, that they were honouring the text, and the honouring was genuine; Wright's tomb and Brown's ladder honour the play more searchingly than a season of respectful productions. But honour is not innocence. The pictures taught the world a shorter, softer, lovelier play than the one Shakespeare's company staged, a play about an embrace rather than about a city that made embraces lethal, and the lesson was learned so completely that the shorter play is the one the world's walls, windows, and souvenirs describe. To know the iconography is to hold the receipt for that transaction: five stations purchased, the engine room left on the shelf, and a balcony that exists everywhere except in the play.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which painting of Romeo and Juliet is the most famous, and where is it kept?

Frank Dicksee's Romeo and Juliet of 1884, at Southampton City Art Gallery, holds that position by the only measures available: reproduction, recognition, and the popular vote the gallery records, which named it Britain's most romantic artwork. The canvas shows the close of the window conversation, the farewell kiss on the line "Farewell, farewell, one kiss and I'll descend," a scene whose full text is treated in [the balcony scene's act and scene analysis](/2023/07/21/balcony-scene-act-2-scene-2-analysis/). Dicksee first designed the image as an illustration for a luxury edition of the play in the 1880s, then realized it at full scale in oils. The picture reached Southampton by bequest in 1941 and remains the single image most of the public would name as the play in paint.

### Q: What was the Boydell Shakespeare Gallery?

It was the print publisher John Boydell's three-part enterprise for turning Shakespeare into British history painting: a gallery at 52 Pall Mall in London that opened in 1789, an edition of the plays edited by George Steevens, and a folio of engravings after the commissioned canvases. The Folger counts 173 paintings by thirty-five artists at the collection's height; accounts following Winifred Friedman give 167. Boydell spent heavily, by one reckoning well over one hundred thousand pounds, and the continental print market's collapse under wartime conditions ruined the scheme. Parliament authorized a lottery in 1803; the draw of January 1805 passed the collection to William Tassie, who dispersed it at Christie's that May for a little over six thousand pounds.

### Q: Which scenes from the play did Boydell's painters choose for their canvases?

Three scenes, and the choice reads as the iconography's first draft. William Miller painted the meeting at Capulet's feast in the first act, the exchanged look and the pilgrim sonnet. John Opie painted the discovery in the fourth act, the household gathered in lamentation around Juliet on her bed, apparently dead from the Friar's potion. James Northcote painted the tomb in the fifth act, catalogued by the Folger under a title naming Romeo and Paris dead with Juliet and Friar Laurence in the Capulets' monument. No canvas took the window conversation, the wedding, or the street fighting. Meeting, discovery, tomb: the gallery's selection already contains the tradition's grammar, with the lovers' private emblem still to be invented by the following century.

### Q: Who painted the tomb scene that Boydell's gallery rejected?

Joseph Wright of Derby. Wright had proposed Juliet waking in the tomb as early as 1776 and painted the subject for Boydell's scheme, but the commission ended in a quarrel when the painter learned his fee stood far below sums paid to artists he rated beneath him, and the canvas was refused. He exhibited Romeo and Juliet: The Tomb Scene at the Royal Academy in 1790, then reworked it, writing in 1791 that it had been painted too dark and that he had simplified the background and thrown more light into the tomb. The finished oil, roughly six feet by eight, shows Juliet kneeling by Romeo's body with her hand on the dagger. Derby Museum and Art Gallery acquired it in 1981, and it remains the eighteenth century's strongest painted reading of the play.

### Q: What does Francesco Hayez's Last Kiss of 1823 actually show?

It shows the farewell: Romeo's last kiss before the exile ends the marriage night, the embrace at the point where the dawn parting begins, the scene examined speech by speech in [the dawn parting's act and scene analysis](/2023/10/06/act-3-scene-5-dawn-parting-analysis/). Hayez painted the canvas on commission from Giovanni Battista Sommariva and exhibited it at the Brera annual exhibition in Milan in 1823 to popular success; Villa Carlotta on Lake Como holds the work and catalogues it as an oil on canvas, though other catalogues divide on medium and dimensions. A documented caveat qualifies the picture's pedigree: Hayez drew as much on Luigi Da Porto's novella, the play's own prose source, as on Shakespeare's text, and his Marriage of Romeo and Juliet of 1830 follows Da Porto likewise. The century's most famous painted kiss is a picture of the shared story before it is a picture of the play.

### Q: Who attacked Turner's Juliet picture, and who defended it?

The Reverend John Eagles attacked it, in Blackwood's Magazine, dismissing the Venetian canvas as a strange jumble and mocking its central liberty, Juliet displayed in Venice instead of Verona. The defence came from John Ruskin, seventeen years old and unknown, whose anger at the review Ruskin himself recorded in Praeterita, remembering how the ribaldry against the picture raised him to black anger and moved him to write an answer. The exchange is a landmark twice over: it marks the moment Romantic painting claimed the right to treat the lovers as a mood rather than a scene, and it launched the century's most influential art critic on a career that began with a Shakespeare painting as its first brief.

### Q: What happened to Turner's Juliet and Her Nurse after its exhibition?

The picture passed into the collection of Hugh Munro of Novar, one of Turner's steadiest patrons, and in 1842 it was engraved by George Hollis under a new title, St Mark's Place, Venice, a retitling that concedes the point its critics had made: the city, not the heroine, is the canvas's true subject. The oil itself crossed the Atlantic in the twentieth century and hangs in the AMALITA Collection in Buenos Aires. Its market history carries a footnote the iconography deserves: at a Sotheby's sale in 1980 the picture made 6.4 million dollars, at that date an auction record, as if the market wished to confirm that the least narrative of the great treatments had become among the most valuable.

### Q: Who else in Ford Madox Brown's family painted the play, and which scene did she choose?

His daughter, Lucy Madox Brown, chose the tomb. Her Romeo and Juliet, a watercolour exhibited in 1870, the year her father's parting canvas was completed, shows Romeo bending over Juliet's apparently lifeless face in the Capulets' monument, the spent vial beside the pair. The work hangs at Wightwick Manor under the National Trust. Tom Taylor, writing in The Times, found in the picture an artistry of uncommon power, a phrase the Watts Gallery later borrowed as the title of its exhibition of her work. The pairing within one household and one year is the corrected iconography in miniature: the father paints the farewell that made the tomb necessary, the daughter paints the tomb with no reconciliation admitted to soften it.

### Q: Why did Leighton choose the play's final minutes when other painters chose its first nights?

Because the ending is where the play keeps its meaning, and Leighton painted meanings. The Reconciliation of the Montagues and the Capulets, designed in a watercolour of 1854 held by the Yale Center for British Art and realized in the oil of 1855 exhibited in Paris, makes the fathers' handshake over the bodies its action and demotes the lovers to evidence. Every rival canvas sells an embrace, a posture of grief, or a beautiful face; Leighton's sells a settlement, the feud's proprietors reading their invoice. The choice explains both the picture's critical standing and its failure to found a line of imitators. A reconciliation offers a wall no embrace to live with, and the prints market that carried the other scenes into thousands of homes had no use for a judgement.

### Q: What is The Graphic Gallery of Shakespeare's Heroines, and who painted its Juliet?

It is the portfolio issue of a publishing venture by The Graphic magazine, which in 1888 commissioned twenty-one paintings of Shakespeare's heroines, exhibited them in a London gallery, and reproduced them as double-page centrefolds and in gravure sets with commentary by W. E. Henley. The Folger's catalogue documents the series and its display; a Sotheby's essay on a companion picture records that the set was carried for Queen Victoria's inspection. Philip Hermogenes Calderon painted the Juliet, signed and dated 1888: the heroine alone, detached from every scene, offered as one panel in a collectible set of Shakespearean womanhood. The venture marks the portrait phase of the iconography in its purest commercial form, the plays surviving as captions to a gallery of faces.

### Q: Why do so many late Victorian pictures show Juliet alone?

Because the market had learned to buy the feeling without the plot. Detached from scene, partner, and catastrophe, Juliet could be sold as an ideal of girlhood to buyers who wanted the name's aura at no narrative cost, a development prepared by the heroine plates of the 1830s, whose Folger-documented code gave the heroines rosebud lips, sweet faces, and the daydreams of contemporary girls. A solo Juliet asks nothing of a parlour wall and risks nothing in a portfolio; she is the play reduced to its most portable asset. The tradition's own logic drove the reduction. Once selection had shrunk the tragedy to five scenes, nothing prevented a further cut to the single figure every scene had really been framing, and the portrait phase simply collected the dividend.

### Q: Which line of the play does Waterhouse's Juliet of 1898 illustrate?

None, and the absence is the picture's meaning. John William Waterhouse exhibited the canvas at the New Gallery in London in 1898; it shows Juliet alone in a landscape by water, cites no speech, stages no identifiable moment, and later circulated under a second title drawn from her dress, The Blue Necklace. Earlier treatments anchor themselves to checkable text: Dicksee's canvas carries a named line, Wright's a named instant at the dagger. Waterhouse's Juliet requires only the name, which by 1898 carried the whole charge a century of canvases had stored in it. The picture is the portrait phase's logical end, a heroine whose play has become optional, legible entirely through the feeling her name had been trained to release.

### Q: Did painters change the ending of the play in their pictures?

Formally, no; effectively, yes, and the difference is the iconography's quietest act of editing. No major canvas in the documented corpus invents a survival, a rescue, or a softened close. What the painters did was refuse the ending's content while keeping its furniture. The tomb was painted repeatedly, but as grief's posture, a heroine with a dagger or a lover reading a face, while the ending's actual business, the fathers' reconciliation, the Prince's audit, the golden statues promised in lieu of children, attracted exactly one great treatment, Leighton's, and no succession. A public formed by the pictures therefore knows the lovers died beautifully and knows almost nothing of what their deaths settled. The ending survives as an image and vanishes as an argument, which is a change no forger would have dared.

### Q: How did engravings carry the paintings into homes that never saw the originals?

Through the same three channels Boydell built and the century extended. The illustrated edition put a plate at the play's gate, beginning with the single engravings of Rowe's 1709 edition and Hanmer's of 1743 and 1744. The portfolio multiplied prestige images at collectible prices, from Boydell's folio Collection of Prints of 1803 to The Graphic's gravure heroines of 1888. The magazine centrefold completed the descent in price and ascent in reach. Each channel repeated the selections of the one above it, because engravers copy what is famous and fame is what the previous channel manufactured. The mechanism explains the census better than taste does: the window, the parting, and the tomb were not merely chosen by painters. They were chosen again by every print seller who decided what would sell, until the choices hardened into the play's appearance.

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch art survey record, scene by scene?

The survey records the painted tradition's selections and refusals in a single table: for each scene of the play, what painters chose to show, the documented treatments that carry the choice, and the scene's pictorial standing across the corpus, graded by survival and repetition rather than by quality. The meeting, the window conversation, and the dawn parting stand favoured, the window supreme; the discovery and the tomb stand favoured with weaker reproduction; the reconciliation stands singular in Leighton's single treatment; the wedding, the street violence, and the plot's machinery stand refused, with no major canvas in the documented corpus; and Juliet alone closes the table as the portrait phase in which narrative drains away. Every entry rests on works verified against holding institutions' records or scholarly catalogues for this article.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch art survey to argue that painting edited the play down to a handful of scenes?

Begin from the grades, not from any single masterpiece. The survey's standing column shows a selection pattern no individual painter intended and no audience voted on directly: thresholds and postures survived, machinery and argument vanished, and the balcony, a word absent from the play, outranks every line in it. An essay can then test the pattern against the corpus's instructive exceptions, Leighton's uncopied reconciliation, Wright's rejected tomb, and the Da Porto share in Hayez's kiss, each of which proves the rule by showing what the tradition declined to multiply. Close on the mechanism rather than on taste: editions, folios, centrefolds, and posters repeated the chosen scenes until repetition became recognition. The survey's value to the argument is that it counts the repetitions, which is where the editing actually happened.

### Q: Why did the play's street fights and Mercutio's death go almost unpainted?

Because the market in Shakespeare pictures was a market in the lovers, and the feud's violence belongs to the play's other subject, the city. The brawls of the first act and the duel of the third contain no embrace, no window, no beautiful corpse arranged for grief; they contain men arguing with swords in public space, the catastrophe's cause shown at working temperature. Painters who wanted the play's seriousness could reach it through the tomb without passing through the street, and painters who wanted its beauty had no errand there at all. The refusal matters because it completes the edit: a visual tradition that keeps every effect of the feud and none of its occasions teaches its public to grieve the outcome and forget the mechanism, which is the tragedy's politics deleted in oil.

### Q: When did painters largely stop treating the play as a major subject?

After the Victorian decades, by the evidence of the record's shape. The documented corpus thickens from the 1740s, crests between the Boydell commissions and the portrait phase that closes with Waterhouse's Juliet of 1898, and thins thereafter into occasional treatments rather than a living tradition; the standard history of the illustrated Shakespeare closes its account in 1875, and the Dulwich exhibition of 2003 already framed three centuries of Shakespeare in art as a completed inheritance to be surveyed. The image-making did not stop. It changed hands, passing to the camera, whose stills repeat the painters' selections, and to the souvenir trades. Painting surrendered the play at the moment the photograph could manufacture the authentic face more cheaply than any academy.

### Q: Is the balcony in the paintings the same object as the balcony in Verona?

It is the same invention at two removes. The play supplies a window and an orchard wall; the pictorial tradition, needing architecture for its vertical staging, supplied the balcony, repeated the supply until the structure felt documentary, and bequeathed the feeling to Verona, where the courtyard of the Casa di Giulietta eventually acquired a balcony of its own for the crowds the pictures had trained. The courtyard's fabric and the canvases' carpentry certify each other in a circle with no text at the centre: visitors trust the balcony because the paintings show one, and the paintings gain authority from a balcony visitors can touch. The survey's window row grades the scene supreme for exactly this reason. Of all the tradition's selections, the balcony is the one that escaped the gallery and got itself built.

### Q: What did painters make of Romeo's rival Paris?

Almost nothing, and the near-erasure is one of the census's sharpest findings. Paris survives in the corpus chiefly as a corpse with a catalogue credit: Northcote's Boydell canvas of the tomb is titled, in the Folger's record, with Romeo and Paris dead, the rival admitted to the picture as part of the monument's inventory rather than as a person with a claim. No major canvas paints his courtship, his flowers at the tomb as a living suitor, or the duel in which the play grants him a death as honourable as its hero's. The tradition that kept five stations of the lovers' story discarded the man whose presence turns a romance into a triangle with a body count. Painting wanted a duet. The play, characteristically, had written a rivalry, and the pictures quietly unwrote it.
