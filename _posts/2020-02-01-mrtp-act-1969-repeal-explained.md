The MRTP Act is usually described as a single event: a monopolies law enacted in 1969 and repealed when the Competition Act arrived. That description compresses two separate events into one and loses the most instructive part of the story. The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 lost the provisions that gave it its purpose in 1991, when Parliament deleted the entire part of the statute that dealt with concentration of economic power. It was not repealed until 2009, eighteen years later, when Section 66 of the Competition Act, 2002 was brought into force. Between those two dates India had a statute named for monopolies that could not examine a merger, could not measure market power in the manner of a modern competition law, and armed its Commission with no power to impose a penalty of its own. This article follows the statute through its three distinct lives: the permission regime of 1970 to 1991, the conduct-only statute of 1991 to 2009, and the repeal that distributed its unfinished business between a tribunal and the consumer fora.

![MRTP Act 1969 and the long road to its repeal - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

For the practitioner the division has a practical edge, because the repeal was drafted so that the forum and the governing law for a legacy matter depend on its subject and on the channel of Section 66 through which it passed. For the researcher and the examination candidate the division is the answer to the standard questions on this statute, which are almost always phase questions in disguise: a question about registration and prior approval is a question about the period ending in 1991, a question about gateways or about contests and advertisements is a question about the conduct regime that ran the whole way through, and a question about repeal is a question about a provision that slept in the Competition Act for six years before it was commenced. Confusing the phases produces confident wrong answers; separating them produces the right ones.

The distinction matters beyond tidiness. Anyone asking what the MRTP Act controlled, or when it was repealed, or why a committee of experts recommended its abolition, is really asking three different questions about three different versions of the same statute. The Act of 1975, with its registration registers and its prior approval requirements, was a materially different instrument from the Act of 1995, which retained the name and the Commission but had shed the machinery that the name promised. The repeal of 2009 closed the second of those instruments, not the first. Accounts that treat the repeal as the end of a forty year permission regime overstate what survived into the final two decades, and accounts that treat 1991 as the end of the Act understate how much work the surviving conduct provisions continued to do. The argument of this article is that the eighteen year interval is the fact to hold on to, because it explains the shape of the successor regime, the fate of pending cases, and the reason the transition is so often misdated.

## Why India Wanted a Monopolies Law

The MRTP Act was the legislative answer to a finding that ran through three official inquiries in the nineteen sixties: that the planned economy, built on industrial licensing and directed credit, was concentrating economic power in a small number of private hands. A committee appointed to examine the distribution of income and levels of living, chaired by P. C. Mahalanobis and reporting in the middle of the decade, recorded sharp inequalities of income and the emergence of large business houses within the planning framework itself. The Monopolies Inquiry Commission, a commission of inquiry chaired by Justice K. C. Das Gupta and reporting in 1965, documented concentration in industry after industry and the prevalence of monopolistic and restrictive practices. A committee on industrial planning and licensing associated with R. K. Hazari reported in 1967 that the licensing system had worked to the advantage of large houses, which had learned to pre-empt licensed capacity and foreclose entry by rivals. A further committee on industrial licensing, the Industrial Licensing Policy Inquiry Committee chaired by Subimal Dutt and appointed in 1967, reported in 1969 with findings to similar effect on the manner in which [the licensing system and the large houses](/2020/01/15/idra-1951-explained/) had grown together.

The constitutional premise stood behind the inquiries. Article 39 of the Constitution directs the State to secure that the ownership and control of the material resources of the community are distributed to subserve the common good, and that the operation of the economic system does not result in the concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment. The preamble of the MRTP Act borrowed that language almost word for word. The statute was enacted, it declared, to provide that the operation of the economic system does not result in the concentration of economic power to the common detriment, for the control of monopolies, for the prohibition of monopolistic and restrictive trade practices and for matters connected with those subjects. Concentration was the target; conduct was the second limb. That ordering shaped everything that followed, because a statute whose first purpose is to prevent concentration will be built around thresholds, registers and permissions rather than around the competitive effects of particular behaviour.

The political setting reinforced the design. Industrial entry was already licensed under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951, so a second permission layer keyed to size fitted the administrative habits of the period. Large industrial houses were objects of public suspicion in a decade that also saw bank nationalisation, and a statute that required the biggest undertakings to seek the Central Government's approval before growing was a natural extension of the control apparatus rather than a departure from it. The MRTP Act should therefore be read as part of the licensing architecture, not as a free-standing competition code of the modern kind. Its concern was capital and structure first, and market behaviour second.

### Which committees produced the case for the Act?

Three inquiries supplied the evidence and a fourth confirmed it. The Mahalanobis Committee examined how income and wealth were distributed after a decade of planning. The Monopolies Inquiry Commission of 1965 mapped concentration of economic power across industries. The Hazari report of 1967 showed licensing favouring large houses, and the Dutt Committee, reporting in 1969, reached similar conclusions on licensing.

The evidence assembled by those inquiries had a specific texture that shaped the remedy. The Monopolies Inquiry Commission counted concentration product by product, finding that in a long list of industries a single producer or a small group controlled the greater part of output, and it documented the practices by which that control was exercised and defended: exclusive dealerships that closed distribution to newcomers, tie-in arrangements, resale price maintenance, and agreements among producers that the Commission treated as restrictive. It also traced the structure of the great houses themselves, networks of companies bound by common directors, family control and managing agency connections, which meant that a law addressed to single companies would miss the unit that actually held the power. The statutory answer to that finding was the definition of inter-connected undertakings, which followed the control wherever it sat inside a group. The Dutt Committee added the dynamic half of the story, showing how houses with established access to the licensing system obtained licences ahead of applicants without that access, held licensed capacity without using it, and thereby converted a system designed to allocate resources into a system that entrenched incumbents. Concentration, on this evidence, was not an accident of the planned economy but one of its products, and a statute addressed to concentration could present itself as planning's own corrective.

The political debate of the late nineteen sixties ran with the evidence. Bank nationalisation in 1969 expressed the same suspicion of concentrated private financial power, and the monopolies bill was debated in an atmosphere in which the largest houses were treated as a problem of political economy rather than merely of market organisation. Opposition came from industry, which argued that India needed scale to industrialise at all, that thresholds would penalise the most efficient producers, and that a permission regime would hand officials a second veto over investment already licensed once. The Government's answer, repeated through the debates, was the constitutional one: Article 39(c) committed the State to preventing concentration to the common detriment, and the statute was the machinery for that commitment. Neither side in 1969 framed the question in the vocabulary of consumer prices or allocative efficiency that a later generation would treat as natural. The Act was born as an instrument for dispersing economic power, and its later critics and defenders alike are most intelligible when that origin is kept in view.

## The MRTP Act as Enacted

The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 is Act 54 of 1969. It received the assent of the President on 27 December 1969 and came into force on 1 June 1970. It extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir. Its structure as enacted fell into four parts. A preliminary part carried the definitions on which the whole statute turned, including the definitions of monopolistic trade practice, restrictive trade practice, dominant undertaking and inter-connected undertakings. A second part established the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Commission under Section 5 and provided for the appointment of the Director of Investigation and the Registrar of Restrictive Trade Agreements. A third part, Chapter III in the enacted arrangement, dealt with concentration of economic power and contained the registration and prior approval machinery. A fourth part dealt with monopolistic, restrictive and, after 1984, unfair trade practices, together with offences, penalties and miscellaneous provisions.

Two features of the enacted design deserve emphasis because they explain the statute's later troubles. The first is that the Act measured its subjects by size and share rather than by conduct alone. An undertaking crossed into the concentration part of the statute by holding assets above a prescribed value, or by being dominant in a line of goods or services, whether or not it had done anything the statute disapproved of. The second is that the Act split its functions between two authorities. The Central Government, not the Commission, held the prior approval powers over expansion, new undertakings, amalgamations and takeovers, and the power to order the division of an undertaking. The Commission inquired into practices and passed orders about conduct. Size was administered by the executive; behaviour was adjudicated by the Commission. When commentators later complained that the Act controlled size and neglected competition, they were describing this original allocation of functions with precision.

### When did the MRTP Act come into force?

The Act received Presidential assent on 27 December 1969 as Act 54 of 1969, and it commenced on 1 June 1970 under a notification published in the Official Gazette at the end of May 1970. It extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, a territorial exception common to central statutes of that period.

## A Statute Among Controls

The MRTP Act did not govern the commanding heights alone, and its boundaries against the neighbouring control statutes explain much of its shape. Industrial licensing under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 decided who could produce what, and in what quantity, at the point of entry and expansion; the MRTP prior approvals then applied a second, size based screen to the largest undertakings on top of the licence. Price and distribution control for essential commodities lived in the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 and the orders under it, not in the MRTP Act, whose unreasonable price language under the monopolistic limb was an adjudicatory standard rather than a price fixing power. Foreign ownership and foreign exchange were the territory of the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1973, which pressed foreign companies towards dilution on its own criteria. Managerial appointments, inter-company loans and the internal governance of companies belonged to the Companies Act, 1956. The MRTP Act's distinct contribution to this crowded field was the proposition that size itself, aggregated across a house, warranted registration and permission, and that trade practices of three defined kinds warranted inquiry by a specialised commission.

The boundaries mattered in practice because a large house lived under all of the regimes at once and experienced them as a single weather system. A proposal to build a new plant could require an industrial licence, an MRTP clearance if the house stood within Section 20, capital issues consent under the capital control legislation of the period, and, where foreign collaboration was involved, approvals under the foreign exchange regime. The MRTP file was rarely the longest of these journeys, but it was the one addressed to the applicant's identity rather than to the project's merits: the question was not whether the plant should exist but whether this house should be allowed to own it. That identity based question is what gave the Act its political charge in 1969 and its anachronistic flavour by 1991, when policy had ceased to regard the identity of the investor as a legitimate ground of refusal. The liberalisation programme dismantled the neighbouring regimes in the same years, delicensing industry, replacing the foreign exchange control statute with a management statute at the end of the decade, and repealing capital issues control, so the MRTP deletion of 1991 was one panel of a general dismantling rather than a solitary judgment on one Act.

## The Definitions That Did the Work

A statute of this design lives in its definitions, and Section 2 of the MRTP Act carried several that determined the reach of everything else. An undertaking was defined broadly, as an enterprise engaged in the production, supply, distribution or control of goods, or the provision of services, which caught factories, trading houses and service providers alike. Goods bore the meaning drawn from the Sale of Goods Act tradition, and trade practice meant any practice relating to the carrying on of trade, widely enough to include a single or isolated action and anything done by a trader in the course of business. The width mattered because the conduct limbs attached to trade practices rather than to firms of any particular size: a small trader could engage in a restrictive or unfair practice even though the concentration part would never look at him.

The most engineered definition was that of inter-connected undertakings in Section 2(g). Two undertakings were inter-connected where one of them was a subsidiary of the other, where the same person or group controlled both, where their managements overlapped in the prescribed manner, or where the other statutory links of common ownership and control were present. The definition converted the evidence of the Monopolies Inquiry Commission into law: the unit of assessment was the house, not the company. Every threshold in Part A of Chapter III was applied to the undertaking together with its inter-connected undertakings, so assets scattered across a group's balance sheets were aggregated before the twenty crore test was applied. Undertakings could apply for a determination of whether they were inter-connected, and disputes about inter-connection generated a steady procedural jurisprudence of their own, since everything downstream, registration, approval, exemption, depended on the answer.

Dominant undertaking, in Section 2(d), has already been described: a market share test of one-third as enacted, revised to one-fourth in 1985, coupled with a small asset floor. Its interest lies in what it was not. It was not a finding that the undertaking had abused anything, and it was not assessed within a defined relevant market arrived at by economic analysis of substitutability; the Act spoke of goods of a description and of substantial parts of India, and the share was computed against production figures of the prescribed kind. A modern dominance inquiry asks whether a firm can behave independently of competitors and customers in a market defined for the purpose. The MRTP definition asked a counting question. The difference between the two questions is the distance between the Act and its successor, and it was one of the Raghavan Committee's central exhibits.

The three practice definitions completed the architecture. Monopolistic trade practice in Section 2(i) and restrictive trade practice in Section 2(o) are examined in their own sections of this article. Unfair trade practice arrived only in 1984, in Section 36A, drafted for the Part it belonged to rather than for the whole Act, a placement that produced its own interpretive disputes. Around these definitions sat the institutions: the Commission in Section 5, the Director of Investigation and, after 1984, the Director General of Investigation and Registration in Section 8, and the Registrar of Restrictive Trade Agreements, whose register of agreements under Section 35 gave the restrictive practices limb its documentary base. The scheme was coherent on its own terms. Its terms were the terms of 1969.

## The Concentration Machinery: Registration and Prior Approval

Part A of Chapter III, running from Section 20 to Section 26, was the heart of the original statute and the part whose deletion in 1991 hollowed the Act out. Section 20 identified the undertakings to which the part applied. One class consisted of undertakings which, together with their inter-connected undertakings, held assets of a prescribed value. As enacted, that threshold stood at twenty crore rupees. A second class consisted of dominant undertakings, defined by market share: an undertaking which by itself or with inter-connected undertakings produced, supplied, distributed or controlled not less than one-third of the total goods of a description in India or a substantial part of India, or provided or controlled not less than one-third of services, and which held assets above a smaller prescribed value, set at one crore rupees as enacted. The definition of inter-connected undertakings did the aggregating work, pulling companies under common control into a single notional house so that the threshold could not be evaded by dispersing assets among group companies.

The thresholds were revised once in the life of the regime. In 1985 the asset threshold for undertakings under the first class was raised from twenty crore rupees to one hundred crore rupees, and the companion changes raised the dominant undertaking's asset floor from one crore rupees to three crore rupees while the share test moved from one-third to one-fourth of the market. The revision was an admission that inflation and industrial growth had pulled ordinary mid-sized companies into a regime designed for the largest houses, but it left the architecture untouched: the trigger remained a balance sheet figure and a market share figure, not any finding of harm.

An undertaking inside Section 20 lived under a permission regime. Section 21 required the prior approval of the Central Government for any proposal to substantially expand an existing undertaking, whether by increasing production, adding plant, or acquiring another unit. Section 22 required approval for establishing a new undertaking. Section 23 required approval for proposals of merger, amalgamation and takeover, and Section 24 attached consequences to a merger carried through in contravention of the approval requirement. Section 25 dealt with the appointment of directors in certain cases arising from inter-connection. Section 26 required every undertaking to which the part applied to register itself with the Central Government and to furnish prescribed particulars. Alongside the approval provisions stood Section 22A, under which the Central Government could exempt classes of undertakings from the part's operation, and the retained power in Sections 27, 27A and 27B to order the division of an undertaking or the severance of inter-connection where concentration so required, provisions that sat outside Part A and, by that accident of drafting, survived 1991.

The practical character of the regime followed from these provisions. A large house planning capacity had to route its investment decisions through a ministry file. Approval could be refused, delayed or conditioned, and the criteria were the broad purposes of the Act rather than any test of competitive effect familiar to modern law. The system duplicated industrial licensing for the largest firms and added a second queue. Defenders of the arrangement pointed to the Dutt Committee's evidence that licensing alone had been gamed by the houses it was meant to discipline, and argued that a size based checkpoint was the only instrument capable of catching growth that arrived through acquisition and inter-connection rather than through fresh licences. Critics answered that a checkpoint keyed to assets caught the honest expander and the predator alike, measured the wrong variable, and consumed administrative effort on proposals that raised no competitive concern at all. Both readings were still being rehearsed when the regime was abolished, and both are taken up in the assessment later in this article.

Registration under Section 26 had a function distinct from the approvals and easily overlooked: it was an instrument of information. The Central Government of 1970 knew remarkably little, in consolidated form, about the structure of the large houses, whose holdings ran through managing agencies, trusts, family arrangements and chains of subsidiaries. The Monopolies Inquiry Commission had reconstructed that structure laboriously for the industries it examined; registration made the reconstruction routine and continuous, obliging every undertaking within Section 20 to disclose its particulars and keep them current. Whatever view is taken of the approval regime, the register was the State's first systematic map of private industrial concentration, and the inter-connection determinations made under the Act remain a documentary record of how the houses were actually held together. The approval files built on the register then recorded, proposal by proposal, the growth plans of the largest sector of Indian industry across two decades. Historians of Indian business use these materials for purposes the draftsman never intended, which is usually the fate of well kept registers.

The administration of the approval provisions produced a particular kind of corporate life for the houses inside them. Expansion proposals carried through the Ministry of Corporate Affairs were examined for their effect on concentration, and applicants learned to present investment as meeting a public purpose, employment, exports, regional dispersal, rather than as meeting a market opportunity. The process interacted with industrial licensing rather than duplicating it cleanly: some proposals needed both clearances, and the two queues moved at different speeds for reasons of their own. Critics inside the system pointed to the distortion that followed, in which firms shaped their investment plans around threshold management, splitting projects, timing applications and restructuring holdings to stay outside Section 20, while the inter-connection definition pursued them. The cost was not only delay but design: industrial organisation under the Act was partly a function of where the statutory lines fell rather than of what production required.

Section 22A offered relief where the regime cut too fine, empowering the Central Government to exempt classes of undertakings from Part A of Chapter III. Exemptions covered fields such as small scale industry and specified categories of export oriented and technology driven enterprise as policy shifted, and the power was exercised with some regularity as the Government recognised the mismatch between the 1969 thresholds and the size of ordinary industrial firms. The exemption history is worth noticing because it shows the regime loosening by executive action before it was abolished by statute: the 1985 threshold revision and the steady use of Section 22A were administrative admissions that the net was catching the wrong fish, even before the 1991 amendment swept the net away.

The surviving concentration power in Sections 27, 27A and 27B deserves a word of its own, since it was the only structural instrument that lived past 1991. Under Section 27, where the Central Government, on the report of the Commission, was of the opinion that an undertaking was operating to the common detriment through concentration, it could order the division of the undertaking or the severance of inter-connection between undertakings. The power was deliberately exceptional: it required a Commission inquiry on the Commission's own knowledge, a complaint, a reference, or an application by the Director General, a report, and a Government order. In the event it was exercised rarely and never as a routine industrial policy tool, and after 1991 it stood as an unused sword, a theoretical answer to a practical question the Government had decided to stop asking. Its survival mattered for a different reason, doctrinal rather than practical: because it stood outside Part A, the 1991 deletion could not touch it, and the formal record continued to show a power against concentration long after the Government's willingness to use such a power had gone.

### What assets brought an undertaking under the concentration part?

As enacted in 1969, an undertaking together with its inter-connected undertakings fell within Part A of Chapter III when its assets reached twenty crore rupees, while a dominant undertaking, holding at least one-third of a market, was caught with assets of one crore rupees. In 1985 the figures became one hundred crore and three crore rupees.

## Monopolistic Trade Practices

The second limb of the Act addressed conduct. Section 2(i) defined a monopolistic trade practice as a trade practice which has, or is likely to have, the effect of maintaining prices at an unreasonable level by limiting, reducing or destroying competition in the production, supply or distribution of goods or services, or of limiting technical development or capital investment to the common detriment, or of allowing the quality of goods produced, supplied or distributed, or services rendered, in India to deteriorate. The definition is worth reading closely. Its organising idea is unreasonableness of price and detriment to the public, judged against competition in the abstract. It contains no concept of a relevant market to be defined before power can be assessed, and no structured inquiry into dominance of the kind the successor statute later adopted. A practice was monopolistic because of what it did to prices, investment or quality, not because of the position of the firm that engaged in it.

Inquiry into monopolistic trade practices lay with the Commission, which could act on a complaint, on a reference from government, on an application by the Director General, or on its own knowledge or information. The Commission's conclusions then fed the Central Government, which held the operative powers under Chapter IV: where a trade practice was found to be a monopolistic trade practice operating against the public interest, the Central Government could pass orders regulating the production, storage, supply, distribution or control of goods, prohibiting practices, or requiring other remedial steps specified in the statute. The Commission's role in this limb was therefore substantially advisory in character, a design that blunted the limb in practice. The number of monopolistic trade practice inquiries that ended in structural relief was small, and the division power in Section 27, exercisable by the Central Government on the Commission's report, was scarcely used. Commentators on the regime were close to unanimous that the monopolistic limb promised more than it delivered, and that the statute's working life was led by its other two conduct limbs, restrictive and unfair trade practices.

Yet the monopolistic limb mattered conceptually, because it stated the theory of the Act in its purest form. The mischief was unreasonable price and restricted development in an economy of scarcity, and the remedy was administrative direction. The theory assumed an economy in which the State could identify the reasonable price and the proper level of investment, assumptions that belonged to the era of administered prices. When those assumptions came under challenge in the nineteen eighties, the limb had no second line of defence, and the Raghavan Committee's later criticism that the Act spoke of monopolistic practices without defining dominance or market was aimed at this part of the text.

In working terms, the monopolistic limb asked the wrong authority to do the wrong task with the wrong tools. The Commission could inquire, but the order lay with the Government; the Government held the order, but no administrative discipline compelled it to reach a conclusion within any horizon that mattered to a market; and the standards of unreasonable price and common detriment invited evidence of a kind that files generate slowly and inconclusively. A practice that maintained prices at an unreasonable level by destroying competition was, in truth, a charge requiring market definition, share calculations and counterfactual reasoning, none of which the statute supplied. The result over four decades was a limb cited in every textbook account of the Act and operative in few reported outcomes of consequence. Where the State wished to act against a dominant producer in this period, it reached more often for price control under other statutes, for import competition, or for licensing decisions, than for Chapter IV of the MRTP Act.

One further weakness followed from the definition itself. Because Section 2(i) attached the label monopolistic to effects on price, investment and quality rather than to the structure of the trade, the limb could in theory be engaged by a practice of a firm without a monopoly, and could not be engaged by a monopoly that merely charged its prices without any practice that could be characterised under one of the heads. The ordinary intuition, that a monopolies law exists to deal with monopolists, was therefore never quite the law. The successor statute corrected the arrangement by making abuse of a dominant position depend first on a finding of dominance in a relevant market and then on enumerated abusive conduct, a two step structure whose absence from the 1969 text the Raghavan Committee put high on its list of defects.

## Restrictive Trade Practices and the Gateways

The third limb was the statute's busiest. Section 2(o) defined a restrictive trade practice as a trade practice which has, or may have, the effect of preventing, distorting or restricting competition in any manner, and in particular any practice which tends to obstruct the flow of capital or resources into the stream of production, or which tends to bring about manipulation of prices or conditions of delivery, or to affect the flow of supplies in the market in a manner that imposes unjustified costs or restrictions on consumers. Section 33 then listed classes of agreement that were deemed to be restrictive trade practices for the purposes of the Act: agreements restricting the persons to whom goods could be sold, tie-in arrangements compelling a buyer to take other goods as a condition of purchase, exclusive dealing agreements, agreements to purchase goods exclusively from a particular seller, agreements restricting resale prices, agreements allocating areas or markets or buyers, agreements restricting output or supply, agreements not to employ or use specified methods or equipment, boycott arrangements, and agreements to withhold goods from the market. Section 35 made agreements within the listed classes registrable, and the Registrar of Restrictive Trade Agreements maintained the register.

The deeming in Section 33 created an apparent per se regime, and the early Commission practice leaned on it heavily: find the clause, find the class, record the practice. The Supreme Court displaced that approach in the two decisions that frame the jurisprudence of the Act. In Tata Engineering and Locomotive Company Limited v Registrar of Restrictive Trade Agreements, decided in 1977 and known as the TELCO case, the Court considered a vehicle manufacturer's dealership agreements, with their territorial allocations and resale conditions. The Court held that a practice does not become a restrictive trade practice merely because it answers a description in Section 33(1); it must also satisfy the definition in Section 2(o), which requires an actual or probable effect on competition judged in the real conditions of the market. In Mahindra and Mahindra Limited v Union of India, decided in January 1979, the Court carried the point further. The question, it held, is not the theoretical possibility that a clause might be used to restrict competition but whether there is a real probability that the clause will have that effect, a question of market effect to be answered from a business and common sense point of view rather than by a doctrinaire reading of the clause.

The balancing clause at the end of Section 38(1) did quiet but decisive work inside the gateway inquiry. Passing through a gateway was not enough: the Commission also had to be satisfied, after balancing the detriment to the public against the gateway circumstance, that the restriction was not unreasonable. The clause converted what might have been a mechanical checklist into an evaluative judgment, and it is the textual seat of the rule of reason flavour that TELCO and Mahindra and Mahindra gave the Act. A restriction that protected employment in a depressed area might still fail if its competitive cost was disproportionate to the employment actually protected; a restriction conferring a benefit on purchasers might still fail if the benefit could have been had without it. The Commission's orders under the clause are studies in proportionality conducted before Indian administrative law had settled that vocabulary, and they show the tribunal weighing, in concrete market settings, the same considerations of benefit, necessity and proportion that effects based statutes later organised into enumerated factors. The gateways are often described as a loophole through which restrictive practices escaped. The balancing clause is the reason that description is only half right: the gate was narrow, guarded, and the guard had instructions to weigh.

Once a practice was found restrictive, Section 38 supplied the escape. A restrictive trade practice was deemed prejudicial to the public interest unless the Commission was satisfied that the case fell within one or more of the gateways in Section 38(1) and, after balancing the competing considerations, that the restriction was not unreasonable. The gateways protected, among other things, restrictions necessary to prevent serious and persistent adverse effects on the general level of unemployment in an area, restrictions necessary to counter measures taken by persons outside India to protect their trade, restrictions needed to hold down prices of goods whose production was controlled or prices fixed under other laws, restrictions required for defence or security, and restrictions that conferred specific and substantial benefits or advantages on the public as purchasers or users of goods. The structure was borrowed from the English restrictive practices legislation, and it structured every inquiry into two stages: first the Section 2(o) finding, then the gateway inquiry under Section 37 and Section 38. The Commission's remedial order at the end of that path was a direction that the practice be discontinued or not repeated, and that the agreement be void or modified in respect of the practice.

The gateway apparatus was the analytical signature of the regime, and it deserves a balanced statement. In its favour, the two stage structure forced attention to justification: a distribution restriction that secured supply in a scarce market, or protected employment in a depressed region, could be defended on stated grounds rather than condemned by classification. TELCO itself illustrates the point, since the Court treated some territorial arrangements in a market of acute scarcity as defensible while striking at others. Against the apparatus, the gateways placed the burden of the competitive analysis on a checklist of excuses drafted in the nineteen fifties for a different economy, and the balancing exercise produced elaborate orders on clauses of modest consequence. The successor statute abandoned gateways altogether in favour of an inquiry into appreciable adverse effect on competition, weighing enumerated factors for and against the practice. The comparison between the two techniques belongs to the comparison article in this series; the point for this article is that the gateways were not a drafting quirk but the working method of the older law for thirty years.

The procedure of a restrictive practices inquiry shaped outcomes as much as the doctrine did. Proceedings commonly began with the Registrar, or after 1984 the Director General, either on a complaint by a trader or association affected by the practice or from the register of agreements itself. A preliminary investigation gathered the agreement and its market setting, and a notice of inquiry framed the clauses complained of. The respondent's route to safety ran through the gateway pleading: the circumstances of Section 38(1) relied on had to be pleaded and proved, and the balancing satisfaction that the restriction was not unreasonable had to follow. An order under Section 37 could declare the agreement void in respect of the restrictive practice, or direct its modification, alongside the direction that the practice be discontinued. Orders could also be made by consent in the course of proceedings, and the volume of consent and assurance based disposals in the Commission's annual work was large, as TELCO's assurance on resale price lists in 1977 itself illustrates. The regime therefore operated much of the time as a negotiation conducted in the shadow of a gateway inquiry rather than as a trial to judgment, and the jurisprudence grew through contested cases at the margins while the middle of the docket settled.

Resale price maintenance and exclusive dealing illustrate the pattern. A supplier fixing the price at which dealers must resell, or forbidding dealers to handle competing goods, fell within the listed classes and displaced dealer competition in ways the Act plainly cared about; TELCO and Mahindra and Mahindra established that the complainant still had to show a real probability of restrictive effect in the actual market, and the supplier could then look to the gateways, pointing to scarcity, to distribution into distant markets, to service networks or to price control elsewhere in law. Exclusive distribution in an economy of shortages could preserve supply lines; the same clause in a market of plenty could close the door to rivals. The two stage test was built to hold both possibilities open, and the Commission's reports are filled with the granular market findings the exercise required: market shares by region, waiting periods for vehicles, numbers of dealers handling competing brands, the presence or absence of inter-brand competition. Read carefully, the late nineteen seventies reports of the Commission contain some of the earliest systematic market inquiries in Indian statutory practice, conducted under a statute whose questions would not satisfy a modern economist but whose method of asking about actual effects anticipated one.

### What was a gateway under Section 38?

A gateway was one of the circumstances in Section 38(1) which, if established, meant that a restrictive trade practice, though proved, was deemed not prejudicial to the public interest. Gateways covered matters such as unemployment in an area, defence, price control under other laws and substantial public benefit, and the Commission then applied a further balancing test of reasonableness.

## Unfair Trade Practices: The 1984 Turn

For its first fourteen years the Act had nothing to say to the consumer who had been misled by an advertisement or sold goods under a false representation. That gap was identified by the High Powered Expert Committee on Companies and MRTP Acts, chaired by Justice Rajinder Sachar and appointed in 1977, which recommended a chapter on unfair trade practices. The recommendation was enacted by the amendment of 1984, in force from 1 August 1984, which inserted Sections 36A to 36E as Part B of Chapter V and recast the investigative office as the Director General of Investigation and Registration. The same amendment inserted Section 12A, empowering the Commission to grant temporary injunctions during an inquiry, and Section 12B, empowering it to award compensation for loss or damage suffered as a result of a monopolistic, restrictive or unfair trade practice, on application by the Central Government, a State Government, a trade association or a consumer.

Section 36A defined an unfair trade practice as a trade practice which, for the purpose of promoting the sale, use or supply of goods or the provision of services, adopts an unfair method or an unfair or deceptive practice, and it listed the practices included. The list ran to false representations that goods or services were of a particular standard, quality, quantity, grade, composition, style or model; representations that re-built, second-hand or renovated goods were new; false claims of sponsorship, approval or affiliation; false or misleading representations about the need for or usefulness of goods or services; warranties or guarantees of performance, efficacy or length of life not based on adequate or proper test; misleading representations about price; bargain price advertisements where the price was not genuinely a bargain or the goods were not intended to be offered at that price; offers of gifts, prizes or free items with the intention of not providing them as offered, or where the cost was covered in the price; contests, lotteries and games of chance or skill run to promote sales; the sale or supply of goods which the seller knew or had reason to believe did not comply with standards prescribed by competent authority for safety related matters such as performance, composition, design, construction or packaging; and the hoarding or destruction of goods, or refusal to sell, where the object or effect was to raise prices. One item in the list carried a significance that only became visible at the repeal: the false statement item included, as clause (x), the giving of false or misleading facts disparaging the goods, services or trade of another person.

The unfair trade practices provisions changed the Commission's docket and its public identity. Restrictive practice inquiries required economists' arguments about agreements between businesses; unfair practice inquiries began with a misleading advertisement, a bogus gift scheme or a false claim, and they could be set in motion by a consumer or a consumer association. The Supreme Court, in Lakhanpal National Limited v MRTP Commission, described the object of the 1984 insertion in terms that the seller must speak the truth while advertising. The provisions also reached the statute book two years before the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 created the consumer fora, so for a period the Commission was the only specialised forum in the country for deceptive marketing claims, and the definition it administered was later carried into the consumer statute itself. When the assessment of the Act is made, the 1984 chapter is the strongest entry on the credit side, and its migration into consumer law is the clearest evidence that a part of the MRTP scheme outlived the philosophy that produced the rest of it.

The unfair practices chapter had one drafting wrinkle that generated real litigation. As inserted in 1984, the definition in Section 36A described a practice which adopted one of the listed methods and thereby caused loss or injury to the consumers of the goods or services, whether by eliminating or restricting competition or otherwise. The closing words raised the question whether proof of consumer loss or injury was an ingredient of every case, including cases about sales promotion contests and lotteries, where the consumers who entered might be said to have suffered nothing beyond disappointment. The Commission's contest jurisprudence of the late nineteen eighties and early nineteen nineties worked around the difficulty, holding contests and lottery-like schemes to be unfair practices, and the 1991 amendment then recast the opening words of the definition, so that an unfair trade practice became a practice which adopts any unfair method or unfair or deceptive practice including the listed practices. The episode is a compact lesson in the hazards of consumer protection drafted inside a competition statute: a phrase meant to locate the mischief in consumer harm became, for a time, a defence for the schemes most plainly aimed at consumers.

The docket that resulted was unlike anything else in the Act. Contests offering prizes by draw, gift schemes attached to fans, refrigerators and washing powder, scratch cards with prizes ascending from detergent to a flat, advertisements claiming qualities that a product did not possess, and representations that misled buyers about the identity or origin of goods: these were the matters on which the Commission's unfair practices benches spent the nineteen nineties. Competing manufacturers were frequent complainants, since a rival's false claim distorted competition as well as deceiving consumers, and the disparagement item, clause (x), was regularly invoked in advertising wars between brands. The Supreme Court's later decisions on the definition, including its insistence in the Philips litigation that Section 36A is concerned with practices connected to the sale or supply of goods to consumers, show the definition being read back towards its consumer protection centre. The fate clause (x) met at the repeal, when its pending cases were sent to the Appellate Tribunal rather than to the consumer commissions, is the residue of the same ambiguity: disparagement was a competitor's complaint wearing a consumer statute's clothes.

From 1986 the Commission shared its unfair practices field with the consumer fora created by the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, and within a few years the consumer statute had taken over the Section 36A definition for its own purposes, so that the same deceptive practice could be pursued in either system. The overlap was productive rather than wasteful. The consumer fora were local, cheap and built for individual redress, with compensation as their natural remedy; the Commission sat in New Delhi but could inquire into a practice as a practice, order it discontinued for the market as a whole, and proceed on the Director General's motion without an individual victim willing to litigate. Traders complained of by a competitor went to the Commission; consumers complaining of their own loss increasingly went to the district fora. The dual jurisdiction also meant that the Commission's advertising jurisprudence and the consumer commissions' case law grew up citing each other, and the definition's centre of gravity shifted steadily towards the consumer end of the field. That shift explains a feature of the repeal that puzzles readers who meet it cold: when Section 66 sent most pending unfair practice cases to the National Commission in 2009, it was not exiling them to a foreign system but returning them to the jurisdiction that had shared the definition for over twenty years and had long since become its principal home.

The 1984 amendment made one further change that shaped the restrictive practices limb. It sharpened the treatment of the practices listed in Section 33, strengthening the presumption that agreements within the listed classes were restrictive, a response to decisions requiring the Commission to establish the Section 2(o) ingredients afresh in every case even for practices of a plainly exclusionary character. The amendment did not displace TELCO and Mahindra and Mahindra on the need for a real probability of competitive harm, but it adjusted the working burden in the classes of case where harm was most familiar. The detail belongs to the case law of the period; what matters to the narrative is that by the middle of the nineteen eighties the Act had settled into the form it would keep for the rest of its life: a dormant concentration part, an advisory monopolistic limb, an active restrictive practices jurisdiction structured by gateways, and a growing unfair practices jurisdiction oriented to consumers.

## The Commission and the Limits of Its Powers

The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Commission was a quasi-judicial body established under Section 5, headed by a Chairman who was, or had been, a judge of a High Court or the Supreme Court, sitting with a small number of members appointed by the Central Government. Section 10 set out the doors through which matters reached it: a complaint from a consumer, a consumer association, a trade association or a registered consumer body; a reference from the Central Government or a State Government; an application by the Director General of Investigation and Registration, the officer who investigated practices and maintained the register of agreements; or the Commission's own knowledge or information. For its inquiries the Commission held powers modelled on a civil court for summoning witnesses, requiring the production of documents and receiving evidence, and its investigative arm could conduct preliminary fact finding before a formal inquiry opened. Files built up in this way contained the commercial records of the undertakings under inquiry, and the handling of such material raised the same concerns about the custody of sensitive business information that attend any investigative office; practitioners who keep evidence and source material in order will recognise the discipline reflected in guidance on [protecting sensitive material held for journalistic work](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), applied in a very different setting.

What the Commission could do at the end of an inquiry defines the regime. Under Section 37 for restrictive practices, and under the corresponding provisions for the other limbs, it could direct that the practice be discontinued and not repeated, and that any agreement stand void or modified in respect of the practice. Under Section 36D it could pass similar directions for unfair trade practices, and under Section 12A it could restrain a practice by temporary injunction while the inquiry ran, a power amplified in 1991 to permit an injunction without prior notice where delay would defeat the purpose. Under Section 12B it could award compensation to an applicant who had suffered loss or damage from a practice found against. Appeals from its orders lay to the Supreme Court under Section 55.

The limit stood in the middle of this list and is the fact most often got wrong in summary accounts: the Commission had no power to impose a penalty, a fine or any monetary exaction of its own for engaging in a prohibited practice. Punishment existed in the statute, but it was routed elsewhere. Offences against the Act, and contravention of the Commission's orders, were triable by the ordinary criminal courts, with imprisonment and fine available on conviction; Section 48C, for example, prescribed punishment for contravention of orders relating to unfair trade practices. The Commission could therefore order a practice to stop and could compensate a victim who applied, but it could not make the practice expensive for the party that had profited from it, and prosecution in a magistrate's court, with its delays and its burden of proof, was an imperfect substitute. The 1991 amendment strengthened compliance at the edges, adding deterrent punishment for contravention of orders of the Commission and of the Central Government and a power for the Commission to punish for contempt of itself, without altering the basic position that the adjudicating body held no penal jurisdiction over the practices it condemned. A regulator that can only say stop, and cannot fine, deters weakly where the gains from the practice are large and the detection rate is low. That structural weakness, more than any individual decision, is what the Raghavan Committee later placed at the centre of its case.

Two further limits completed the picture. The Commission's monopolistic practice findings ran to the Central Government for orders, so the limb with the most dramatic theoretical reach was the limb furthest from the Commission's own hand. And the Act, being addressed to practices in India, had no extraterritorial application of the kind that allows a modern authority to proceed against conduct abroad with effects at home, and no procedure for a cartel participant to purchase immunity by confession, so the international apparatus that hard cartels are investigated with elsewhere was absent. The Commission worked conscientiously within these limits for four decades, and its unfair practices docket in particular produced a body of orders on advertising and marketing that consumer law inherited. The limits were in the statute, not in the institution.

The road into an inquiry ran through investigation, and the design of that road tells its own story about the regime. Section 11 provided for preliminary investigation by the Director General before the Commission committed itself to a formal inquiry in restrictive and unfair practice matters: the Director General examined the complaint or reference, called for the agreements and the trading facts, and reported whether a prima facie case existed for the Commission to proceed. The filter conserved the Commission's scarce bench time and gave respondents an early, less public stage at which weak complaints could be closed, but it also meant that the office which investigated was the office which then prosecuted the inquiry before the Commission, a combination of functions that the successor statute reproduced with its own Director General and that critics in both eras have questioned. Preliminary investigation files, built from the respondent's own records and correspondence, were the evidentiary foundation of the reported orders, and the published decisions quote agreements, price lists and dealer circulars at a length that shows how documentary the practice was. A regime remembered for its thresholds was, at the working level, a regime of paper: registers of agreements, investigation reports, notices of inquiry, gateway pleadings and reasoned orders, layer on layer, in New Delhi.

The Commission's weakness in enforcement should be set against its genuine strengths of access. Standing was wide: a consumer, a registered consumer association, a trade association, a competitor, the Central or a State Government, or the Director General could set the machinery in motion, and the Commission could act of its own motion on its own knowledge or information. Its procedures were less formal than a court's, its costs were not the barrier that civil litigation presented, and for a consumer complaining of a deceptive scheme in the years before the consumer fora matured, the Commission offered the only specialised hearing available in the country. Interim relief under Section 12A allowed a misleading campaign to be halted while the inquiry proceeded, a remedy with immediate commercial value to the honest competitor and immediate protective value to the public. Much of the Commission's output took the form of negotiated discontinuance and assurances, outcomes that a penalty driven regulator might have converted into fines but which ended the practices complained of all the same.

The other side of the ledger is the compliance record. An order to discontinue binds the party before the Commission; it does not, without the slow machinery of prosecution, expose anyone to a fine for the gains already made, and the reports of the period contain the recurring spectacle of practices discontinued by one trader and adopted by another in the same trade, each requiring a fresh inquiry. The Raghavan Committee treated this as the decisive institutional flaw, and officials who served the Commission did not seriously dispute the diagnosis: a tribunal can build doctrine and reputation, but deterrence on an economy wide scale requires the credible threat of monetary consequence. The Competition Act's penalty provisions, which allow percentage of turnover penalties for cartels and other contraventions, were drafted by men and women who had watched the MRTP Commission direct the same practices to stop, year after year, and drew the obvious conclusion. Procedure without penalty had given India four decades of persuasion in a field that rewards defection.

A last institutional feature completes the account. The Commission's composition narrowed its reach. With a Chairman and a handful of members sitting in New Delhi, its benches could hold only a limited number of inquiries, and its effective docket was always a selection from the complaints received, prioritised by the Director General's office. The institution therefore functioned as a tribunal of notable cases rather than as a market wide supervisor, and its jurisprudence, developed in dealer agreements, advertising claims and distribution arrangements, reflects that case by case method. The successor Commission was built on a different scale and with an investigative directorate separated from adjudication, but the habits of episodic, complaint driven inquiry formed under the MRTP Act passed into the new institution's early practice all the same.

## The Act in Three Phases

| Feature | 1970 to 1991 | 1991 to 2009 | Position after repeal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Registration | Undertakings within Section 20, and agreements within Section 33, were registrable | Concentration registration abolished; agreement registration under Section 35 continued | No registration under the Act; the register closed with the repeal |
| Prior approval for expansion and merger | Required from the Central Government under Sections 21 to 23 for undertakings within Part A of Chapter III | Abolished with the deletion of Part A; no approval of any kind under the Act | No approval under the Act; combination review began under the Competition Act on 1 June 2011 |
| Conduct control | Monopolistic, restrictive and, from 1984, unfair trade practices inquired into by the Commission | Continued unchanged; the conduct provisions were the whole of the Act's working content | Anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominance governed by Sections 3 and 4 of the Competition Act from 20 May 2009 |
| Unfair trade practices | Absent until 1984, then governed by Sections 36A to 36E | Continued as the Commission's busiest jurisdiction | Governed by the Consumer Protection Act; pending cases distributed under Section 66 |
| Penalties | None imposable by the Commission; offences triable by criminal courts | Unchanged, with deterrent punishment for contravention of orders added in 1991 | The Competition Commission may impose penalties under the Competition Act |
| Adjudicating body | MRTP Commission, with prior approval powers held by the Central Government | MRTP Commission for conduct alone | Competition Commission of India and the Competition Appellate Tribunal; consumer fora for unfair trade practice cases transferred to them |

Read across the middle column and the namable claim of this article becomes visible in a single view. The middle phase kept the name of the Act, the Commission, the definitions and the conduct jurisdiction, and surrendered the registration, the prior approvals and with them the entire reason the statute had been enacted. The middle column is also the phase that standard accounts skip, moving directly from the permission regime to the repeal as though one followed the other. Eighteen years is longer than the interval between the Act and its first great amendment, and longer than the whole life of many statutes in this series; a phase of that length is not a transition but a settlement, and it deserves to be described as one.

The table also disciplines the vocabulary. It is common to call the whole period from 1970 to 2009 the era of the MRTP Act, as though the name guaranteed a constant content. The phases show the name outliving two different statutes in succession: a concentration statute with conduct provisions attached, and a conduct statute with a concentration statute's name, preamble and institutional memory attached. Legal historians are accustomed to statutes that change by amendment at the margins; the MRTP Act changed at the centre while keeping its margins, which is why its history resists the single paragraph summary and why this article has proceeded phase by phase.

## 1991: The Deletion of the Core

The New Industrial Policy of July 1991 dismantled industrial licensing for most industries and recast the role of the State in investment decisions. The monopolies statute could not survive that settlement in its original form, because its concentration part was a second licensing system for the largest firms. The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices (Amendment) Ordinance, 1991 was issued first, and the MRTP (Amendment) Act, 1991, Act 58 of 1991, replaced it, with the substantive changes deemed to have come into force on 27 September 1991, the date of the Ordinance. The Statement of Objects and Reasons was candid about the reason: pre-entry restrictions on investment decisions had outlived their utility and hindered the speedy implementation of industrial projects, and the Act was to be restructured so that its emphasis fell on controlling monopolistic, restrictive and unfair trade practices. The amendment belongs to the same legislative moment as [the wider movement from the licence raj towards liberalisation](/2020/03/15/licence-raj-to-liberalisation-laws/), and it cannot be understood apart from that movement.

What the amendment removed can be stated exactly. By Section 8 of the Amendment Act, Part A of Chapter III of the principal Act, comprising Sections 20 to 26, was omitted with effect from 27 September 1991. That single omission carried away the identification of undertakings by asset value and dominance, the prior approval requirements for substantial expansion under Section 21, for new undertakings under Section 22 and for merger, amalgamation and takeover under Section 23, the consequences of an unapproved merger under Section 24, the director provisions of Section 25, and the registration requirement of Section 26. Part C of Chapter III and Chapter III-A were omitted at the same time; Chapter III-A, inserted in 1984 to restrict acquisition and transfer of shares likely to concentrate ownership, was not simply discarded but transplanted, its substance moving into the Companies Act, 1956 as Sections 108A to 108C so that the share transfer restrictions continued in company law while the monopolies statute let them go.

What survived is equally exact. The conduct provisions survived entire: the definitions and the inquiry machinery for monopolistic trade practices, restrictive trade practices and unfair trade practices, the Commission, the Director General, the gateways, Sections 12A and 12B on injunctions and compensation. Sections 27, 27A and 27B also survived, with amendments, because they stood outside Part A: Section 27 preserved the power to order the division of an undertaking, Section 27A the power to order severance of inter-connection, and Section 27B the associated procedure. The amendment also brought a change in the opposite direction on the Act's personal reach. Section 3 was amended so that the Act extended to public sector undertakings and government companies, which the original exemptions had largely kept outside, subject to exceptions for undertakings concerned with defence, atomic energy, currency and coinage, and space and similar sovereign fields. A statute that had just ceased to control private size was simultaneously made applicable to public size, a broadening that mattered for the conduct limbs, since state owned suppliers could from 1991 be respondents in restrictive and unfair practice inquiries on the same footing as private firms.

The mechanics of the changeover were as abrupt as the text suggests. The Ordinance route meant that the deletion operated from 27 September 1991 without waiting for parliamentary time, and the Amendment Act then replaced the Ordinance while preserving the retrospective commencement. Proposals that had been moving through the approval process under Sections 21 to 23 lost their legal necessity at a stroke, since there was no longer any approval to obtain, and the registration obligation under Section 26 fell away with the section that created it. No transitional code was provided for the concentration part because none was thought necessary: a permission system does not need a savings clause when the policy is that the permission is no longer required. The contrast with the elaborate savings that accompanied the final repeal in 2009 is instructive. Abolishing a gateway requires a sentence; distributing the unfinished adjudication of thousands of complainants requires a scheme.

The 1991 amendment also made changes that had nothing to do with liberalisation and are easy to miss. It strengthened the Commission's hand within the conduct regime it was retaining: punishment for contravention of orders of the Commission and of the Central Government was made deterrent, the Commission received a power to punish for contempt of itself, and Section 12A was amplified so that a temporary injunction could issue without notice where notice would let the practice achieve its object before the Commission could act. A Parliament abolishing the entry control half of the statute was simultaneously sharpening the enforcement of the other half, and the combination is the best evidence that 1991 was a considered redesign and not an oversight. The conduct statute that emerged was meant to be taken seriously. What was not provided, and what no one in 1991 appears to have regarded as urgent, was a successor regime for the subject matter abandoned: the size and combination questions were left to a market that the Government of the day believed would discipline itself.

The result deserves to be stated without varnish. From 27 September 1991 the MRTP Act contained no provision under which any merger, amalgamation, takeover or expansion required anyone's approval, and no provision under which an undertaking registered because it was large. The Act's name continued to announce the control of monopolies, and its preamble continued to recite the prevention of concentration, while the operative text held neither. Successive governments left the position untouched for eighteen years, not because the position was a considered policy of merger freedom, but because the deletion was the policy and a replacement was nobody's urgent business until the Raghavan process concluded. This article is general legal information on a repealed statute and not advice on any transaction or proceeding.

## The Eighteen Years Between

What did the Act do between 1991 and 2009? The honest answer is: the work of its second and third limbs, in a market that had been transformed around it. The restrictive practices jurisdiction continued, structured by Section 2(o), Section 33 and the gateways, and the unfair practices jurisdiction continued to grow as advertising and consumer markets expanded. The Commission decided cases on misleading claims, prize schemes, disparagement between competitors and distribution arrangements throughout the period. Proceedings under Section 12B for compensation were brought, and the Commission's orders of this period fill the later volumes of the company law reports. Nothing in the repeal narrative should be allowed to suggest that the Commission sat idle for eighteen years; it sat, rather, without the first of its three original subjects.

What the Act did not do in those years is the sharper point. Indian industry consolidated through the liberalisation decades, by merger, acquisition and internal growth, with no competition authority anywhere in the system empowered to review a combination for its effect on competition. The Companies Act provisions that had received the share transfer restrictions in 1991 were disclosure and approval machinery of a different character, addressed to concentration of ownership in individual companies rather than to market effects. Nor could the MRTP Commission fill the gap through its conduct limbs: a merger is not a restrictive trade practice, and the monopolistic limb, which in any event routed its conclusions through the Central Government, had not been built to appraise transactions. The eighteen years therefore contained a genuine institutional vacancy on a subject that the 1969 Parliament had thought important enough to place at the front of its statute. The vacancy ended only on 1 June 2011, when the combination provisions of the successor statute were finally brought into force, twenty years after the deletion that created it.

The interval also had a doctrinal consequence. Because the Act's surviving text was conduct text, its case law developed around agreements, advertisements and representations, and the vocabulary of Indian competition discussion in the period was the vocabulary of practices, gateways and prejudice to the public interest. Concepts that dominate the successor regime, the relevant market, appreciable adverse effect, the single economic entity, had no statutory home. Universities taught the gateways; practitioners pleaded them. The Raghavan Committee's complaint that the old statute measured size rather than power was, by the end of the period, partly out of date as a description of the Act's daily work, since the size provisions had been gone since 1991. It remained exact as a description of what the statute lacked: any apparatus at all for appraising market power where it mattered most, in mergers and in the abuse of a dominant position as such.

The strangest sub-period of the eighteen years ran from 2003 to 2009, when the two regimes coexisted with their roles inverted. The Competition Commission of India existed, staffed and housed, but the substantive provisions it was created to enforce had not been commenced, so its lawful work consisted of competition advocacy, market studies and institution building: it could preach competition and could not police it. The MRTP Commission, condemned by the Raghavan Committee and scheduled for dissolution, retained the only operative conduct jurisdiction in the field and continued to receive complaints, hold inquiries and pass orders under the gateways and Section 36A. Litigants with a grievance about an agreement or an advertisement in, say, 2006 went to the old Commission because there was nowhere else to go, and the new Commission's early annual reports read as the programme notes for a performance that had not begun. The arrangement was defensible as sequencing, since the tribunal question had to be settled by the 2007 amendment before adjudication could start, but it fixed the repeal's timetable to the successor's slowest part, and it is the immediate reason the repeal waited until September 2009 although the decision to repeal had been taken in 2000 and enacted in 2002.

The docket of the period tells the same story from the institution's side. Advertising grew faster than any other part of the consumer economy in the nineteen nineties, and the unfair practices benches carried a correspondingly growing share of the Commission's work, with complaints by competitors featuring prominently because a false claim by one brand was the cheapest injury a rival could prove. The Commission's final phase, after the Competition Act had been passed and the new Commission constituted, was one of acknowledged transience: everyone in the system knew the repeal provision would be commenced, no one knew when, and long term institution building had stopped while the caseload continued. By September 2009 the Chairperson's office stood vacant and two members remained, the facts that forced the Ordinance described in the repeal section of this article. An institution does not have to be abolished to wind down; it is enough that abolition is certain and undated.

Political time explains part of the delay. The Finance Minister's budget speech of February 1999 conceded the position publicly, observing that the Act had become obsolete in certain areas in the light of international economic developments and that the focus needed to shift from curbing monopolies to promoting competition, and announcing a committee to examine the range of issues and propose a modern law. The committee reported in May 2000. A bill followed, was examined by a parliamentary committee, and became the Competition Act at the end of 2002. Even then, commencement was staggered over years, a constitutional challenge by a member of the new Commission's first leadership generation required a further amendment in 2007 to settle the tribunal's structure, and the repeal provision waited until September 2009. Each step is intelligible in itself. Together they produced the series' longest gap between a policy's abandonment and its statute's repeal, a pattern this series has found in shorter forms across many fields of Indian legislation.

## The Raghavan Committee's Verdict

The High Level Committee on Competition Policy and Law was constituted in October 1999 under the chairmanship of S. V. S. Raghavan, and it reported to the Government on 22 May 2000. Its terms required it to advise on a modern competition law in line with international developments and to suggest a legislative framework, whether by new law or by amendment of the existing statute. The Committee chose replacement, and its reasoning is the clearest official statement of what was wrong with the older Act. It recommended that the MRTP Act be repealed and the MRTP Commission wound up, and that a new competition statute create a Competition Commission of India charged with preventing practices harmful to competition, promoting and sustaining competition, protecting consumers and ensuring freedom of trade.

The grounds the Committee gave map closely onto the defects traced in this article. The old Act was addressed to the control of monopolies and to size, where the proper subject of a competition law is the preservation of the competitive process. Its key terms were undefined where definition mattered most: it contained no definition of a relevant market, no working definition of dominance as a threshold for scrutiny, and no treatment of cartels as a distinct and serious category. It gave its Commission no power to impose penalties, so its orders lacked deterrence. It provided no leniency mechanism by which a member of a secret cartel could be induced to disclose it, although cartels are seldom proved without an insider. It had no extraterritorial application, so conduct abroad harming Indian markets lay outside its reach. And its surviving merger position was an empty chair: the 1991 deletion had removed prior control without substituting any review of combinations, which the Committee treated as a subject a modern law must cover. The Committee also urged that the new law apply across the economy, to state monopolies, government procurement and foreign companies alike, and that consumers be protected whoever they were and whatever the purpose of the purchase.

Two features of the Committee's wider programme are worth separating from its critique of the old Act, because both passed into the successor statute and both mark the distance travelled since 1969. The first is the place given to competition advocacy. The Committee treated law as one instrument of competition policy and the authority as its advocate inside government, charged with examining policies, procurement practices and regulations that restricted competition and with saying so publicly. A monopolies commission of the old kind had no such brief; it waited for cases. The second is the insistence that the new law bind the State as market participant. Government enterprises, departmental undertakings selling goods and services, and foreign firms operating in India were all to stand inside the statute, a recommendation that closed the circle opened by the 1991 extension of the MRTP Act to public sector undertakings and carried it to its conclusion. Where the 1969 Parliament had built a law against private concentration that largely exempted public power, the Committee of 2000 proposed a law about competitive conduct that exempted neither. The Competition Act followed the Committee on coverage and on advocacy, and its Commission's published opinions on government policy in its early years are the advocacy recommendation in operation.

The Committee's report is also the point at which the sympathetic reading of the old Act receives its most respectful official hearing and is still set aside. The report did not deny that the 1969 statute had answered the conditions of its time, when entry was licensed, capacity was allocated and a handful of houses dominated private industry. It held, in substance, that those conditions had ended: quantitative restrictions were being dismantled, tariffs were low, and Indian firms from toy makers to steel producers were about to face international competition in their home market, so that a law designed to keep domestic firms small was a handicap to the very economy it served. The verdict was replacement rather than repair because the defect was in the statute's organising idea, and no amendment of detail could convert a concentration law into a competition law. Nine years passed between that verdict and the repeal it recommended, which is the second of the two intervals this article asks the reader to hold separately.

The passage from report to statute consumed the rest of the period this article covers. A draft competition law prepared on the basis of the report was placed before the Government in November 2000, and the Competition Bill was introduced in Parliament in 2001 and referred to the Standing Committee on Finance, whose examination produced changes of detail before the Bill was passed in December 2002. Assent followed on 13 January 2003. The Bill's journey was unhurried by the standards of economic legislation because the subject was new: Parliament was being asked to abolish a familiar institution, create an unfamiliar one, and adopt concepts, relevant market, appreciable adverse effect, dominance, that had no Indian statutory pedigree. The debates returned repeatedly to the fear that a powerful new Commission might become a licence raj of a different kind, a fear the Act's staged commencement and the later creation of the Appellate Tribunal by the 2007 amendment were designed in part to answer.

That 2007 amendment deserves its place in the repeal's chronology. Litigation challenging the structure of the new Commission, on the ground that a body exercising adjudicatory functions was to be headed by a person who was not a judge, produced an assurance recorded by the Supreme Court that the Government would create a separate appellate tribunal and confine the Commission's composition accordingly. The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2007 gave effect to that design, establishing the Competition Appellate Tribunal in the form in which it later received the MRTP caseload, and recasting Section 66 with the two year continuation for the old Commission that events in 2009 were to overtake. The repeal of the MRTP Act was therefore drafted twice, adjusted once by constitutional litigation, and executed once by Ordinance, before it took final effect. Few provisions in the modern statute book have had so managed a birth or so postponed an operation.

## The Successor at Headline Level

The Competition Act, 2002, Act 12 of 2003, received the assent of the President on 13 January 2003. Its preamble states its programme in one sentence: a Commission to prevent practices having an adverse effect on competition, to promote and sustain competition in markets, to protect the interests of consumers and to ensure freedom of trade. Its operative scheme rests on three prohibitions and one review: anti-competitive agreements under Section 3, abuse of a dominant position under Section 4, regulation of combinations under Sections 5 and 6 with inquiry under the following sections, and a Competition Commission of India with a Director General for investigation and a Competition Appellate Tribunal for appeals. The detailed text of that scheme, its definitions of agreement, dominant position and relevant market, and its penalty provisions, belongs to [the article on the successor statute](/2020/02/15/competition-act-2002-explained/) and is carried there; the treatment here is confined to what the repeal required this article to record.

Commencement came in stages, and the staging is part of the story of the repeal. The provisions establishing the Commission came first, and the Commission was constituted in 2003, though its adjudicatory work could not begin until the substantive provisions followed and the constitutional questions about the tribunal were settled by the Competition (Amendment) Act, 2007. Sections 3 and 4, the agreement and dominance prohibitions, were brought into force on 20 May 2009. Section 66, the repeal provision, followed on 1 September 2009. The combination provisions came last, on 1 June 2011, completing a commencement process that had taken more than eight years from assent. A statute can therefore be enacted, partially alive, and yet leave its predecessor in office for most of a decade, which is what happened here: for six of the years between 2003 and 2009 the successor existed on paper while the predecessor continued to decide cases.

### When did merger control begin in India?

Merger control of the modern kind began on 1 June 2011, when the combination provisions of the Competition Act, 2002, Sections 5, 6 and the connected inquiry sections, were brought into force. Between the deletion of the MRTP prior approval regime in 1991 and that date, no authority in India reviewed combinations for their effect on competition.

## Repeal and the Distribution of Pending Cases

Section 66 of the Competition Act is headed repeal and saving, and it was brought into force on 1 September 2009. Its first sub-section repeals the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 and provides that the MRTP Commission shall stand dissolved. As enacted in 2002 and adjusted in 2007, the section had softened that sentence with a proviso under which the Commission would continue for two years after commencement to dispose of cases filed before it, taking no new ones. Events overtook the proviso. The office of Chairperson was vacant on 1 September 2009 and only two of the Commission's members remained in post; those two demitted office on 14 September 2009 and 1 October 2009 respectively on completing their tenures, and the Commission became non-functional with its pending cases stranded. The Competition (Amendment) Ordinance, 2009 was accordingly promulgated on 14 October 2009 to transfer the pending cases at once, and the Competition (Amendment) Act, 2009, Act 39 of 2009, which Parliament passed in December 2009 in replacement of the Ordinance, removed the two year afterlife and completed the transfer scheme. When accounts date the Commission's dissolution to October 2009, it is this sequence they are recording: repeal on 1 September, a stranded institution within weeks, and dissolution in fact when the Ordinance took the cases away on 14 October.

The distribution of pending business under Section 66, as it stands after the 2009 amendment, is precise and repays exact statement. Sub-section (1A) preserves the standard savings for things done under the repealed Act: rights, privileges, obligations and liabilities acquired or incurred continue, penalties incurred may be enforced, and proceedings in respect of them may be instituted, continued or enforced as if the Act had not been repealed. Sub-section (2) deals with people: the Chairman, members, the Director General of Investigation and Registration and the Commission's officers vacated office on dissolution, the Chairman and members being entitled to compensation limited to three months' pay and allowances, officers on deputation reverting to their parent cadres, and regular staff becoming officers and employees of the Competition Commission or the Appellate Tribunal as the Central Government specified, with pension and gratuity rights preserved. Sub-section (10) preserves the operation of Section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 alongside the express savings.

The case transfers are in sub-sections (3) to (9), and they follow the subject matter of the case rather than its age. Under sub-section (3), all cases pertaining to monopolistic trade practices or restrictive trade practices pending before the MRTP Commission, including cases in which an unfair trade practice was also alleged, stand transferred to the Appellate Tribunal, which adjudicates them in accordance with the provisions of the repealed Act as if that Act had not been repealed; an Explanation makes clear that applications for compensation under Section 12B travel with the cases they belong to. Under sub-section (5), cases pertaining to the one clause of the unfair practices definition that concerned competitors rather than consumers, the giving of false or misleading facts disparaging the goods, services or trade of another person under clause (x) of Section 36A(1), likewise stand transferred to the Appellate Tribunal. Under sub-section (4), by contrast, all other cases pertaining to unfair trade practices stand transferred to the National Commission constituted under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, to be disposed of as if they were cases filed under that Act, and the National Commission may transfer any such case onward to the State Commission for the State concerned, which disposes of it as if filed under that Act; the channel by which so much of the old Commission's docket passed into [the consumer fora whose modern working the homebuyer litigation illustrates](/2019/02/15/rera-vs-consumer-protection-homebuyers/) was this sub-section. A further proviso, added in 2009, sent unfair practice cases still pending before the National Commission when the amendment commenced onward to the Appellate Tribunal, to be decided under the repealed Act after all.

Investigations incomplete at the repeal were distributed on the same logic. Under sub-section (6), investigations and proceedings pending before the Director General not relating to unfair trade practices stand transferred to the Competition Commission of India. Under sub-section (7), the Director General's unfair practice investigations, other than those under clause (x), stand transferred to the National Commission, and under sub-section (8) those relating to clause (x) stand transferred to the Competition Commission. Sub-section (9) then closes the scheme with a sentence of real severity: save as provided in sub-sections (3) to (8), all other cases or proceedings pending before the MRTP Commission shall abate. A matter that fitted none of the transfer channels did not migrate; it ended. Residual proceedings under the transferred heads continued for years afterwards in the Tribunal and the consumer commissions, decided under a repealed statute kept alive for them by the saving words, and questions on the transitional position still occasionally reach advisers whose clients were parties in that period. The Tribunal took the transferred work up from the date of the Ordinance, 14 October 2009, and the early returns reported to Parliament during the passage of the Amendment Bill recorded fifty four of the transferred cases disposed of by 11 December 2009, a pace that reflected the light the Ordinance had intended to clear. The National Commission and the State Commissions absorbed the unfair practice transfers into dockets built for consumer complaints, where the cases were re-dressed as consumer disputes and decided under the consumer statute's remedies. A complainant who had filed before the MRTP Commission in, say, 2007 alleging a misleading advertisement might therefore find the complaint decided years later by a consumer commission under a statute the complaint had never mentioned, while the respondent in a restrictive practices inquiry from the same filing season answered before the Appellate Tribunal under the repealed Act itself. Section 66's subject matter logic produced these divergent afterlives deliberately, on the view that each class of case belonged with the institution whose living law most resembled it.

The head-to-head comparison of the two regimes, their tests, their institutions and their remedies, is the subject of [the comparison article in this series](/2020/05/15/mrtp-vs-competition-act-comparison/), which takes up where this account of the older statute ends.

## The Afterlife of a Repealed Statute

A repealed statute does not vanish; it retires into the savings clauses. The MRTP Act's afterlife has three compartments, and practitioners meet all of them. The first is the transferred caseload itself. Cases that reached the Appellate Tribunal under Section 66(3) were governed by the repealed Act as if it had not been repealed, which meant that the Tribunal spent its early years applying Section 2(o), Section 33 and the gateways of Section 38 to conduct that predated the repeal, in a building otherwise devoted to appeals under the new Act. Compensation applications under Section 12B travelled the same road, so findings of loss and damage were made under a dead statute by a living tribunal. The unfair practice cases that went to the consumer commissions were decided under the Consumer Protection Act instead, because Section 66(4) directed that they be disposed of as if filed under that Act, so the same advertisement could be judged under two different statutes depending on the door through which the complaint had entered the old Commission.

The second compartment is doctrinal. The definition of unfair trade practice that the Sachar Committee brought into Section 36A in 1984 had already migrated into Section 2(1)(r) of the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, and the consumer statute that replaced the 1986 Act in 2019 retained a definition of unfair trade practice built on the same list: false representations, misleading advertisements, bargain price offers, gifts and contests, disparagement and hoarding all appear there in recognisable descent. Staff and litigants changed statutes; the list endured. Comparative advertising disputes, which the MRTP Commission had handled under clause (x), continued before the consumer commissions and the courts with the old case law cited as the natural starting point. In this compartment the repeal changed the forum and the remedies, and barely touched the substance.

The third compartment is the one of principle. Section 66(1A) and Section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 preserve accrued rights, incurred liabilities and penalties for the period of the Act's operation, so conduct between 1970 and 2009 that contravened the Act or an order under it did not become lawful on repeal, and proceedings in respect of it could continue. The abatement rule in Section 66(9) drew the boundary on the other side: a proceeding pending before the Commission that fitted none of the transfer channels ended, however meritorious, because Parliament chose a clean institutional break over a universal carry over. Between the savings and the abatement lies the complete theory of the repeal: the past is preserved where a channel exists to receive it, and abandoned where none does. Advisers asked about a matter from the Commission's last years begin with that map, and the occasional question on the transitional position that the brief for this article predicted still arises is answered from Section 66's text and from nothing else.

## Two Readings of the MRTP Act

The critical reading of the Act is formidable and largely made out. The statute defined the problem as size rather than as harm to competition. Its triggers were asset values and market shares, not effects; it restrained the growth of large firms while doing nothing about collusion between small ones; and a cartel of modest firms, the classic subject of competition law, could pass beneath a threshold system aimed at houses. The Commission it created could order a practice to stop and could compensate an applicant, but it could not fine, so its orders carried no deterrent weight against conduct that paid. Its monopolistic limb routed decisions through the Central Government and changed little. Its gateways, borrowed from English legislation of the nineteen fifties, invited elaborate justifications of ordinary distribution arrangements. On this reading the Act was an instrument of the control economy wearing the language of competition, and the wonder is not that it was repealed but that its core survived only twenty one years while its shell survived forty.

The critical reading can be pressed further on the Act's own terms. A registration and approval regime keyed to assets taxes precisely the undertakings that invest, since assets grow by investment, and leaves untouched the undertaking that extracts monopoly profit from a static plant. The thresholds therefore selected for scrutiny the expanding firm and gave the stagnant monopolist a quiet life, an inversion of any defensible priority. The gateways, for their part, invited respondents to dress commercial restrictions in the vocabulary of public benefit, and the Commission's time, scarce in any event, went into appraising the employment effects of dealer territories and the foreign exchange merits of tie-in clauses, questions bearing only a distant relation to the competitive process. Nor did the Act's silences help it: no cartel provision, no leniency, no extraterritorial reach and no penalties reads, at any distance, less like a competition law than like a licensing statute that had wandered into the wrong shelf of the statute book.

The sympathetic reading begins by refusing to judge 1969 by the assumptions of 2000. In the economy for which the Act was written, entry was licensed, capacity was created by permission, foreign exchange was rationed and a small number of houses controlled a large share of organised industry, as the Monopolies Inquiry Commission and the Dutt Committee had documented in detail. In that setting size and market power were not identical, but they were close relations: a house that could pre-empt licences, command credit and speak to government with one voice held power that a pure conduct law, enforced case by case after the event, would have struggled to reach. A prior approval checkpoint was a crude instrument, and crudeness was the price of administering it at all. The sympathetic reading adds the Act's second life to the account: the unfair trade practices chapter of 1984, enacted on the Sachar Committee's recommendation, gave Indian consumers a statutory remedy against false advertising and deceptive schemes before any consumer statute existed, and its definition passed into the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 and from there into the everyday law of consumer redress. A statute whose consumer chapter seeded a permanent branch of Indian law was not a dead letter, whatever is said of its concentration chapters.

The sympathetic reading also has an institutional chapter. The Commission, working without penal powers, still built the first Indian jurisprudence of market inquiry: TELCO's insistence that a restrictive practice be proved by actual or probable effect, and Mahindra and Mahindra's real probability standard, are recognisable ancestors of the effects analysis that the Competition Act later made statutory. A generation of Indian lawyers learned to argue about markets, shares and justification inside the gateway structure, and the successor regime inherited that trained audience along with the Sachar Committee's consumer chapter. Institutions teach as well as decide, and the teaching function of the MRTP Commission, in a country that had never had a competition tribunal before 1970, is part of its record even on the least charitable view of its powers.

Weighing the two, the verdict that the evidence supports is the Raghavan Committee's, with one qualification. Replacement was right: a competition law must define markets, appraise effects and deter by penalty, and the MRTP Act could do none of these things well. The qualification concerns the obituary usually written for the Act as a whole. The concentration regime deserves its reputation; it measured the wrong thing and it taxed growth. The conduct regime deserves a more careful one. Restrictive practices doctrine under TELCO and Mahindra and Mahindra contained, inside the gateway structure, an early Indian statement of the rule of reason, and the unfair practices chapter was a genuine consumer protection achievement inserted into the wrong statute by a committee that saw the gap clearly. The failure was architectural, and architecture is what repeals are for.

## How the Story Is Usually Told Wrong

Because the Act is a fixture of economics and law syllabi, its errors are fixtures too, and four of them recur often enough to deserve correction in one place. The first is dating the repeal to 2002. The Competition Act bears the year 2002 in its short title and the assent date of 13 January 2003 in its history, but the MRTP Act remained in force, deciding cases, until Section 66 commenced on 1 September 2009. A student who writes that the MRTP Act was repealed in 2002 has confused the enactment of the successor with the repeal of the predecessor, an error the staggered commencement of the Competition Act invites and the record does not excuse. The second error is the mirror image: describing the Act as though it stood intact, concentration provisions and all, until 2009. It did not; the operative core was deleted in 1991, and the Act of 2005 would have been unrecognisable to the draftsman of 1969 precisely in the part he cared about most.

The third error concerns the Commission's powers. Summary accounts routinely state or imply that the MRTP Commission could fine companies for restrictive or unfair practices. It could not, at any point in its life; its monetary remedy was compensation on application under Section 12B, and punishment for offences and for contravention of its orders belonged to the criminal courts. The error matters because it conceals the regime's central design weakness and makes the Raghavan Committee's insistence on penalty powers in the successor statute unintelligible. The fourth error is quieter: treating the asset threshold as a curiosity rather than as the regime's trigger. That a firm became an object of statutory control by crossing twenty crore rupees of assets, a figure later raised to one hundred crore, with no inquiry into its conduct or its market, is the single most revealing fact about the Act's theory of harm, and accounts that omit the threshold omit the theory. To these four a fifth may be added from the examination hall: the belief that India has always had merger control of some kind. Between 1991 and 2011 it had none, and the gap is a consequence of dated legislative choices, not a trick of the question paper.

A sixth error concerns the Commission's end and produces contradictory dates in otherwise careful accounts. Some sources give the dissolution as 1 September 2009 and others as 14 October 2009, and both can be defended because they record different events. The repeal and the statutory dissolution took effect on 1 September 2009, when Section 66 commenced. The Commission as a working institution, its members gone and its cases unassigned, was wound up in fact when the Ordinance of 14 October 2009 transferred its pending business and the Amendment Act that followed extinguished the two year afterlife the original section had promised. Accounts that choose October are describing the funeral; accounts that choose September are describing the death. The repeal section of this article sets out the sequence in full, and the discrepancy in the secondary literature is best treated not as an error to be adjudicated but as a sign that the repeal, like the Act's hollowing out eighteen years earlier, happened in stages that a single date cannot hold.

## What the Eighteen Years Explain

The series of which this article forms part has repeatedly found a gap between the moment a policy changes and the moment the statute book follows, and the MRTP Act supplies the longest measured instance of that gap in the file: not the interval between enactment and repeal, which many statutes exceed, but the interval between the removal of a statute's core and the repeal of the statute itself. The deletion of 1991 was a deliberate act of liberalisation, taken in the open and defended in the Amendment Act's own Statement of Objects and Reasons. The eighteen years that followed were not a considered retention of a conduct statute; they were the product of a committee process that began only in 1999, a bill that took three years, a commencement that took six more, and an amendment needed in 2009 to clear away a winding up scheme that events had already falsified. Policy moved in 1991. The statute followed in 2009. The merger control that the deleted provisions had crudely attempted arrived in 2011.

There is a final measure of the gap worth recording. The series counts other statutes in this file whose policy cores were removed, reversed or abandoned while the text endured, and in most of those instances the interval between the substantive change and the formal correction is counted in months or in a small number of years. Eighteen years exceeds the working life of the concentration regime's own first phase, which ran for twenty one years from 1970 to 1991, and it exceeds the entire period during which the unfair trade practices chapter was the Commission's main business before the consumer fora were established. A gap of that size ceases to be a lag and becomes a condition: two generations of practitioners qualified, practised and retired inside it, textbooks were written around it, and the conduct statute that filled it acquired a settled jurisprudence that the repeal then had to preserve by savings clauses. The gap is the biography of the Act, and any account that collapses it into a single date, 1969 to 2009, has discarded the biography and kept only the bookends.

That is the explanation the article set out to give, and it supports the One Test the brief prescribed. The reader who holds the three phases apart, the permission regime, the conduct-only statute and the repeal with its exact distribution of unfinished business, holds the whole subject: why the Act was enacted, what it did, what was taken from it and when, why a committee of the Government's own appointment recommended its abolition, and where its cases went when it ended. The statute that policed the size of Indian business for a generation ended not with a single repeal but with an eighteen year epilogue, and the epilogue is the part of the story that explains the law India has. The dates reward memorising in their proper order: assent in 1969, commencement in 1970, deletion in 1991, verdict in 2000, successor in 2003, repeal in 2009, and merger control in 2011.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the MRTP Act 1969 control?

The Act controlled three things in its original form. It controlled the growth of large undertakings, which required registration and the prior approval of the Central Government for substantial expansion, new undertakings, mergers, amalgamations and takeovers where asset or dominance thresholds were crossed. It controlled monopolistic and restrictive trade practices through inquiries by the MRTP Commission. From 1984 it also controlled unfair trade practices such as false representations and misleading advertisements. The first of these controls was deleted in 1991, leaving only the conduct controls until repeal in 2009.

### Q: Which committees led to the MRTP Act?

Four official inquiries built the case. The Mahalanobis Committee on the distribution of income and levels of living reported in the mid nineteen sixties on inequality and the rise of large business houses under planning. The Monopolies Inquiry Commission, chaired by Justice K. C. Das Gupta, reported in 1965 on concentration of economic power and monopolistic and restrictive practices. The Hazari report of 1967 found that industrial licensing had favoured large houses. The Dutt Committee on industrial licensing, appointed in 1967 and reporting in 1969, confirmed that the licensing system had been turned to the advantage of the biggest groups.

### Q: What did the 1991 amendment delete from the MRTP Act?

The MRTP (Amendment) Act, 1991, Act 58 of 1991, omitted Part A of Chapter III, Sections 20 to 26, with retrospective effect from 27 September 1991. The deletion removed the identification of undertakings by asset value and dominance, the prior approval requirements for expansion, new undertakings, merger, amalgamation and takeover, and the registration of large undertakings. Part C of Chapter III and Chapter III-A were also omitted, the share transfer restrictions moving to the Companies Act. The conduct provisions on monopolistic, restrictive and unfair trade practices, and Sections 27 to 27B on division of undertakings, survived.

### Q: What powers did the MRTP Commission have?

The Commission could inquire into monopolistic, restrictive and unfair trade practices on a complaint, a government reference, an application by the Director General of Investigation and Registration, or its own information. It held civil court powers for summoning witnesses and documents. It could order a practice to be discontinued and not repeated, declare an agreement void or modified, grant temporary injunctions under Section 12A, and award compensation for loss or damage under Section 12B. It could not impose a penalty of its own; offences and contraventions of its orders were punished by the criminal courts.

### Q: Why did the Raghavan Committee recommend repeal?

The High Level Committee on Competition Policy and Law, chaired by S. V. S. Raghavan and reporting on 22 May 2000, held that the Act addressed size rather than market power. It contained no definition of a relevant market, dominance or cartel, gave its Commission no power to penalise, provided no leniency mechanism for uncovering secret cartels, and had no extraterritorial reach. After the 1991 deletion it also contained no merger control. The Committee concluded that the organising idea of the statute was wrong for a liberalised economy, and recommended repeal and replacement by a modern competition law administered by a new Competition Commission.

### Q: When was the MRTP Act actually repealed?

The Act was repealed by Section 66 of the Competition Act, 2002, which was brought into force on 1 September 2009. The MRTP Commission stood dissolved under the same provision. Because the Commission's Chairperson's office was vacant and its last two members left office within weeks, the Competition (Amendment) Ordinance of 14 October 2009 and then the Competition (Amendment) Act, 2009 completed the transfer of its pending cases. The Competition Act itself had been enacted in January 2003, and its combination provisions followed only on 1 June 2011, so enactment, repeal and full commencement were three separate dates.

### Q: What happened to pending MRTP cases?

Section 66 distributed them by subject. Cases on monopolistic or restrictive trade practices, including mixed cases and compensation applications under Section 12B, went to the Competition Appellate Tribunal to be decided under the repealed Act as if it had not been repealed. Cases on disparagement under clause (x) of Section 36A(1) also went to the Tribunal. Other unfair trade practice cases went to the National Commission under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, which could send them on to State Commissions. Pending investigations were divided between the Competition Commission and the National Commission, and anything outside the transfer provisions abated.

### Q: Which conduct counted as an unfair trade practice under the MRTP Act?

Section 36A, inserted in 1984, listed the practices: false representations about the standard, quality, quantity or grade of goods or services; passing off re-built or second-hand goods as new; false claims of sponsorship or approval; misleading claims about the need for or usefulness of goods; warranties not based on adequate test; misleading price representations; bargain price offers not genuinely intended; gifts, prizes and contests offered without intent to provide them, or run to promote sales; selling goods known not to comply with prescribed safety standards; and hoarding, destroying or refusing to sell goods in order to raise prices.

### Q: How were monopolistic trade practices defined under the MRTP Act?

Section 2(i) defined a monopolistic trade practice as one which has, or is likely to have, the effect of maintaining prices at an unreasonable level by limiting, reducing or destroying competition, or of limiting technical development or capital investment to the common detriment, or of allowing the quality of goods or services to deteriorate. The definition turned on unreasonable prices and public detriment rather than on any defined market or any finding of dominance, which is why the Raghavan Committee criticised it. Findings on such practices went to the Central Government, which held the power to pass remedial orders under Chapter IV.

### Q: What made an undertaking a dominant undertaking under the MRTP Act?

An undertaking was dominant when, by itself or together with its inter-connected undertakings, it produced, supplied, distributed or otherwise controlled the prescribed share of the total goods of a description, or of services, in India or a substantial part of India, and held assets above a small prescribed floor. As enacted, the share was not less than one-third and the asset floor was one crore rupees. In 1985 the share test was revised to one-fourth and the floor raised to three crore rupees. Dominance in this sense was a status that attracted registration and prior approval obligations under Part A of Chapter III until 1991.

### Q: What did registration of agreements under Section 35 involve?

Section 35 required agreements falling within the classes listed in Section 33, the classes deemed to be restrictive trade practices, to be registered with the Registrar of Restrictive Trade Agreements. Particulars of the agreement had to be furnished within the prescribed time, and the Registrar maintained the register. Registration did not validate an agreement; it exposed it to scrutiny, since the Commission could inquire into a registered agreement under Section 37 and order the practice behind it to stop. The registration obligation for agreements survived the 1991 deletion of the concentration part and continued until the repeal of the Act in 2009.

### Q: How did the Sachar Committee reshape the MRTP Act?

The High Powered Expert Committee on Companies and MRTP Acts, chaired by Justice Rajinder Sachar and appointed in 1977, recommended that the Act take up unfair trade practices and consumer protection. Its recommendations produced the amendment of 1984, in force from 1 August 1984, which inserted Sections 36A to 36E defining and regulating unfair trade practices, Sections 12A and 12B on injunctions and compensation, and the office of Director General of Investigation and Registration. The amendment gave the Act its second, consumer facing life and supplied the definition of unfair trade practice later adopted by the Consumer Protection Act, 1986.

### Q: Could the MRTP Commission award compensation for loss or damage?

Yes, within limits. Section 12B, inserted by the 1984 amendment, empowered the Commission to award compensation where loss or damage had been suffered as a result of a monopolistic, restrictive or unfair trade practice. The power was exercised on an application by the Central Government, a State Government, a trade association, a consumer association or the person who had suffered the loss, and the Commission first had to determine the nature and extent of the loss through an inquiry. Compensation under Section 12B was the Act's only monetary remedy in the Commission's own hands, and it was compensatory rather than penal. Applications pending at the repeal travelled to the Competition Appellate Tribunal with the cases to which they belonged.

### Q: Why did India have no merger control between 1991 and 2011?

Because the two halves of the change were separated by twenty years. The 1991 amendment deleted the MRTP Act's prior approval provisions for merger, amalgamation and takeover as part of the liberalisation programme, and no replacement review was enacted at the time. The Competition Act, 2002 contained combination provisions in Sections 5 and 6, but the Central Government commenced the Act in stages and brought those sections into force only on 1 June 2011. Between those dates, mergers were governed by company law procedures on schemes of arrangement and by sectoral regulators where they existed, but no authority appraised them for their effect on competition.

### Q: What did Section 66 of the Competition Act provide?

Section 66 is the repeal and saving provision of the Competition Act, 2002, brought into force on 1 September 2009. It repealed the MRTP Act, dissolved the MRTP Commission, preserved rights and liabilities accrued under the repealed Act, dealt with the position of the Commission's Chairman, members and staff, and distributed pending cases and investigations among the Competition Appellate Tribunal, the National Commission under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 and the Competition Commission of India. As first enacted it allowed the old Commission a two year afterlife for pending cases; the Competition (Amendment) Act, 2009 removed that arrangement and made the transfers immediate.

### Q: Which pending cases went to the Competition Appellate Tribunal on repeal?

Under Section 66(3), every case pertaining to a monopolistic trade practice or a restrictive trade practice pending before the MRTP Commission went to the Competition Appellate Tribunal, including a case in which an unfair trade practice was also alleged, and including applications for compensation under Section 12B of the repealed Act. Under Section 66(5), pending cases on disparagement, the giving of false or misleading facts about the goods, services or trade of another person under clause (x) of Section 36A(1), went to the Tribunal as well. The Tribunal decided all of them under the repealed Act as if it had not been repealed. Unfair practice cases of other kinds went to the consumer commissions instead.

### Q: What role did the Director General of Investigation and Registration play?

The Director General of Investigation and Registration, heading the office created by the 1984 amendment from the earlier offices of Director of Investigation and Registrar, was the Commission's investigative and prosecutorial arm. The office conducted preliminary investigations into practices, maintained the register of registrable agreements, and applied to the Commission for inquiries into restrictive and unfair trade practices. Complaints from consumers and references from government commonly reached the Commission through the Director General's findings. At the repeal, the office was abolished with the Commission, and its incomplete investigations were transferred by Section 66 to the Competition Commission or the National Commission according to their subject.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court hold in the TELCO case?

In Tata Engineering and Locomotive Company Limited v Registrar of Restrictive Trade Agreements, decided in 1977, the Supreme Court considered the dealership agreements of a vehicle manufacturer, including territorial allocation and resale terms. The Court held that an agreement does not become a restrictive trade practice merely because it falls within one of the classes listed in Section 33(1); it must also satisfy Section 2(o) by having an actual or probable effect of preventing, distorting or restricting competition in the real conditions of the market. The decision imported a rule of reason into the Act and was carried further in Mahindra and Mahindra Limited v Union of India in 1979, which required a real probability, not a theoretical possibility, of restrictive effect.

### Q: Did the MRTP Act apply to public sector undertakings?

For most of its life, largely not, and then yes. The Act as enacted exempted undertakings owned or controlled by government and similar public bodies from much of its operation. The 1991 amendment altered Section 3 so that the Act extended to public sector undertakings and government companies, subject to exceptions for fields such as defence, atomic energy, currency and coinage, and space. The extension mattered because the concentration provisions were deleted in the same amendment, so public undertakings became answerable mainly under the conduct provisions, in restrictive and unfair trade practice inquiries, on the same footing as private respondents from 27 September 1991.

### Q: What happened to the MRTP Commission's members and staff on dissolution?

Section 66(2) of the Competition Act provided for the human side of the repeal. On the Commission's dissolution, the Chairman, members, the Director General of Investigation and Registration and the subordinate officers vacated their offices. The Chairman and members were entitled to compensation not exceeding three months' pay and allowances for the premature end of their terms. Officers who had come on deputation reverted to their parent cadres. Officers and employees who held regular posts became officers and employees of the Competition Commission of India or the Competition Appellate Tribunal, as the Central Government specified, with their pension and gratuity rights preserved.
