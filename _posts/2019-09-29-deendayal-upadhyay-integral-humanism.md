Between 22 and 25 April 1965, in Mumbai, Deendayal Upadhyay delivered four lectures under the title Ekatma Manav Darshan, the doctrine that English-language publication rendered as Integral Humanism. Upadhyay spoke as general secretary of the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the post he had held since 1953, and the lectures were delivered during the party's national session before an audience of its own delegates and organisers. The four lectures were then issued as a short monograph of roughly fifty printed pages. That slim volume became the most consequential text in the Jana Sangh's doctrinal life: the text the party adopted as its official ideology, the text its successor party retained as a named commitment, and the text through which later governments justified programmes bearing Upadhyay's name. The standard treatments of the monograph divide into two kinds. Hagiographic treatments, multiplied after 2014, celebrate the author and attach his name to welfare programmes, renamed stations and school curricula. Organisational treatments, written by historians of the Jana Sangh, file the lectures as an antecedent, a doctrinal prelude to the Bharatiya Janata Party founded in 1980. Both kinds pass over the object itself. The lectures contain a worked philosophical argument, built from named concepts, arranged in a deliberate sequence, and aimed against named rival doctrines. That argument is the subject here. The claim defended is the brief's claim: Deendayal Upadhyay's Integral Humanism is philosophically distinct from Savarkar's territorial-cultural framework and from Golwalkar's organic-social framework, and the philosophical-substantive reading is what makes the distinction citable.

## The Mumbai Lectures of April 1965 as a Philosophical Event

The circumstances of delivery matter because the lectures were not a campaign speech and not a party resolution. The Jana Sangh's national session at Mumbai in April 1965 gave Upadhyay four consecutive evenings, from 22 to 25 April, and he used them to construct an argument in stages. The first lecture laid a foundation by attacking the adequacy of the two frameworks that dominated Indian political argument in 1965, the liberal individualism inherited through the Indian National Congress and the class collectivism of the Communist Party of India. The second lecture erected the positive anthropology, the account of the human person, on the classical four purusharthas. The third lecture moved from the person to the nation, introducing chiti as the nation's civilisational essence and virat as its awakened collective strength. The fourth lecture descended to economic organisation and specified a decentralised order against both centralised planning and unrestrained capitalism. The sequence is itself evidence. A doctrine assembled for electoral convenience does not require an anthropology before an economics. Upadhyay's order of topics declares that the economic positioning is a deduction from the account of the person, and the account of the nation sits between the two because the nation, in his construction, is the person writ at civilisational scale. The monograph's brevity has invited summary dismissal, yet brevity is a property of the lecture form, and the text's compression is the compression of a speaker who expected his audience to supply the tradition he named. The lectures cite the purusharthas, the doctrine of dharma, and the organic analogies of nation and body without extended defence, because the Jana Sangh delegates of 1965 were formed inside the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's shakha pedagogy, where those references carried settled meanings. Reading the lectures philosophically means reconstructing what the compressed references assert, testing the assertions against the rival frameworks, and only then judging the doctrine's distinctness. That reconstruction also explains the text's afterlife. A party that adopts a philosophy requires a philosophy to adopt, and the Jana Sangh in 1965 possessed manifestos, resolutions and a programme, but no systematic statement of first principles. The Mumbai lectures filled a vacancy that organisational histories register without examining: they record that the party acquired an ideology in 1965, and they move on to vote shares. The content of what was acquired is the vacancy this article occupies. The comparison with the two earlier Hindutva frameworks runs through the whole analysis, because distinctness is a relation, and the relation can be stated exactly only when Savarkar's definition of 1923 and Golwalkar's compilations of 1939 and 1966 are held steady as comparators. The founding generation of the republic supplies a third comparator. The Constituent Assembly's settlement, argued between 1946 and 1949, had already staged [the religion-state and rights-development founding tensions](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) that any indigenous philosophy of 1965 had to answer, and Upadhyay's lectures answer them by refusing the Assembly's terms rather than by amending them.

## A Pracharak's Formation: Nagla Chandrabhan, Kanpur and the RSS, 1916 to 1951

Deendayal Upadhyay was born on 25 September 1916 at Nagla Chandrabhan, a village near Mathura in the United Provinces, where his father Bhagwati Prasad served as a station master on the railways. Orphaned in childhood and raised by relatives, he passed the matriculation examination of the Ajmer Board at the top of his cohort, took a first class degree in mathematics at Sanatan Dharma College, Kanpur, and in 1937, during his Kanpur student years, joined the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the organisation K.B. Hedgewar had founded at Nagpur in 1925. The RSS formed him through its pracharak system, the cadre of full-time organisers who surrendered salaried careers and family households to itinerant organisational work. Upadhyay rose to joint provincial pracharak for the United Provinces, a post that placed him inside the Sangh's provincial command while still in his twenties. The pracharak years explain the lectures' manner nearly as much as their matter. The shakha curriculum prized the short spoken exposition, the single governing idea carried through an hour of address, and the avoidance of scholastic apparatus. Upadhyay's prose keeps that discipline to the end of his life: paragraphs built on one distinction, terms defined once and then worked hard, examples drawn from railway travel, village economy and the household. His formation was also literary and journalistic. At Lucknow he launched the monthly Rashtra Dharma in 1947, the weekly Panchjanya in 1948 and the daily Swadesh in 1949, a publishing cluster that gave the Sangh's Hindi belt network its printed voice. In Panchjanya he wrote continuously from the late 1940s through the 1960s, and the brief for this article rightly identifies those editorials and essays, preserved in the Panchjanya archive in Delhi, as the under-cited primary source for the doctrine's long gestation: the concepts of the 1965 lectures appear in the Panchjanya writings in embryonic arrangements across the dozen years before Mumbai. His books of the same period show the same double register, part historical retrieval and part doctrine. Samrat Chandragupta, a Hindi drama on the Mauryan founder, and Jagadguru Shankaracharya, a biography of the eighth-century Advaita teacher, worked the civilisational past for present instruction, and his translation of a Marathi biography of Hedgewar carried the founder's life into Hindi. None of this yet made him a political leader in the electoral sense. The pracharak was an organiser of men, not a candidate, and Upadhyay contested no election during those apprentice years. His capital was of a different kind: fluency in the Sangh's internal idiom, command of the Hindi print network, and the trust of Hedgewar's successor M.S. Golwalkar, who released Sangh pracharaks for party work when Syama Prasad Mookerjee founded the Bharatiya Jana Sangh at Delhi on 21 October 1951. Upadhyay was among the senior-most of the released organisers, and the division of labour fixed at the founding, Mookerjee supplying parliamentary eminence and public standing, the pracharaks supplying cadre and continuity, shaped the party for three decades. Read against this formation, the 1965 lectures lose their air of suddenness. The doctrine was the third decade of a single career arriving at its systematic statement, and the philosophical ambition was present from the Panchjanya years, when Upadhyay's editorials already refused both the Congress's borrowed liberalism and the communists' imported materialism by name.

## Fifteen Years Building the Jana Sangh: General Secretary from 1953

The institutional fact at the centre of Upadhyay's career is the general secretaryship. Appointed in 1953, he held the post for fifteen years, through the party's wilderness decade and into its coalition breakthrough, and he remained its organising intelligence after his elevation to the presidency in December 1967. Mookerjee's death in Srinagar detention on 23 June 1953, less than two years after the founding, deprived the Jana Sangh of its only nationally eminent figure, and the party's survival through the 1950s is the organisational achievement the historians credit to Upadhyay's general secretaryship. The record they cite is concrete. The Jana Sangh won three seats in the Lok Sabha of 1952, fourteen in 1962 and thirty-five in 1967, and its vote consolidated across the Hindi belt states through a machinery of district organisers, most of them RSS pracharaks on loan, reporting through the general secretary's office. Christophe Jaffrelot, in The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics (1996), treats this organisational consolidation as Upadhyay's signature contribution, the patient construction of a cadre party inside an electoral system dominated by the Congress's patronage machine. The organisational reading of Upadhyay begins here and, in its strong form, ends here: the man as machine-builder, the lectures as the machine's doctrinal badge. The badge interpretation underrates what the general secretary was writing in those fifteen years. The Panchjanya essays of 1953 to 1965 work through the problems the lectures later solve in sequence: the inadequacy of Western political categories to Indian conditions, the need for an economics that begins from the Indian village rather than from the Five-Year Plan's heavy-industry template, and the status of the nation as a civilisational entity prior to its state. Upadhyay's The Two Plans: Promises, Performances, Prospects, published in 1958, attacked the First and Second Five-Year Plans on grounds that anticipate the fourth lecture almost exactly: centralised planning, he argued, misreads a decentralised agrarian economy, and imported models, capitalist or socialist, fail because they answer foreign questions. The general secretaryship also fixed his position between the two frameworks the lectures would have to refuse without naming their authors as enemies. Golwalkar, his own mentor and the RSS's sarsanghchalak from 1940, had given the movement its organic account of the nation in We or Our Nationhood Defined (1939) and would compile Bunch of Thoughts in 1966. Savarkar, dead in February 1966 but dominant in authority through the 1950s, had given it the definitional test of Hindutva (1923). Upadhyay's doctrine had to differentiate a Jana Sangh philosophy from both inheritances while preserving the loyalty of cadres formed by both. The lectures perform that differentiation by altitude: by moving the argument to the level of philosophical first principles, where a new construction could stand beside the inherited ones without openly displacing them. The years supply one further credential. From 1951 the Jana Sangh sat inside the republic's argument about what the founding settlement had decided, and the party's parliamentary spokesmen fought that argument on the terrain of [the founding triangulation as comparator](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/), Patel's statecraft, Nehru's programme and Ambedkar's constitutionalism supplying the positions every rival had to answer. Upadhyay's answer, when it came in 1965, declined the terrain itself.

### What was Upadhyay's Political Diary column?

Alongside the Panchjanya editorship, Upadhyay wrote the Political Diary column in the Organiser, the RSS's English-language weekly published from Delhi, through the 1950s and 1960s. The column commented on parliamentary contests, Congress leadership disputes and foreign policy in a compressed, aphoristic English that reached readers the Hindi press could not, and the collected columns circulated as the book Political Diary.

The column matters to the lectures' genealogy because it trained Upadhyay in a second idiom. The Panchjanya essays could presume the tradition; the Organiser column had to argue before readers who did not. The discipline of the second idiom, definition before assertion and concession before rebuttal, is visible in the Mumbai lectures' structure, where each rival doctrine is first stated in its attractive form and only then refused. The general secretary who emerges from the full record, Panchjanya, Organiser, The Two Plans and the party's internal circulars together, is neither the plaster saint of the post-2014 treatments nor the mere quartermaster of the organisational histories. He is a party intellectual using an organiser's posts as a philosopher's study, and [the Jana Sangh phase Upadhyay led](/2019/09/24/bharatiya-jana-sangh-phase-1951-1977/) is unintelligible unless both halves of that description are kept in view at once.

## The Panchjanya Decade: The Architecture Before Mumbai

The lectures' speed of composition, four evenings and a fifty-page monograph, has encouraged the belief that the doctrine was improvised for the session. The documentary record corrects the belief. Between 1953 and 1965 Upadhyay wrote continuously for the publications he had founded, and the Panchjanya file for those years, preserved in the journal's Delhi archive, contains the doctrine in pieces: the refusal of borrowed frameworks argued editorially against each new Congress plan and each new communist manifesto in turn; the village economy defended against the Second Plan's industrial priorities; the nation described, years before the word chiti was fixed to it, as a civilisational personality with a direction of its own. The pieces acquire their order only in 1965, but the pieces themselves are the work of a decade, and a reading that begins at Mumbai mistakes the systematisation for the invention. Three pre-1965 texts carry the heaviest load. The Two Plans: Promises, Performances, Prospects (1958) is the most substantial: a book-length audit of the First and Second Five-Year Plans that measures the plans against their own announced targets and finds the failures structural rather than incidental. Its argument is the fourth lecture in statistical dress. The plans fail, Upadhyay contends, because they were drawn from foreign templates, the Soviet for industry and a diluted Fabianism for agriculture, and a template drawn for another civilisation's chiti cannot organise this one's economy without amputating the units, village, artisan, small producer, in which this economy actually lives. Devaluation: A Great Fall, his pamphlet on the 1966 devaluation of the rupee, postdates the lectures by a year and shows the doctrine applied: the devaluation is condemned not as a technical error but as a confession that an economy built on borrowed models had arrived at the foreign-exchange wall those models always concealed. Between the two stands the long run of the Political Diary and the Panchjanya leading articles, in which the polemical targets rotate, Congress planning, communist collectivism, the Swatantra Party's liberal economics after 1959, while the standard of judgment stays constant: each doctrine is weighed by what it makes of the whole person, and each is found to have enthroned a fragment. The gestation record matters to the adjudication for a precise reason. The organisational-antecedent reading, which treats the lectures as a doctrinal badge struck for a party occasion, cannot explain a decade of preparatory argument in journals of small circulation, written by a general secretary whose organisational duties left him no idle hours, on questions, the theory of the person, the status of the nation, the criteria of machines, that no election of the 1950s required him to answer. Badges are struck quickly and for audiences; the Panchjanya decade was struck slowly and for readers numbered in thousands. The hagiographic reading fails the same record from the other side: the essays argue, concede, revise and occasionally contradict the positions the lectures finally fix, and a saint's relics do not show drafts. What the archive shows is a philosopher working in the only study his career afforded him, the editorial chair of a movement weekly, and the Mumbai lectures are that study's findings read aloud.

## Lecture One: The Refusal of Borrowed Frameworks

The first Mumbai lecture, delivered on 22 April 1965, performs the clearance on which the other three depend. Its opening move is diagnostic. India, Upadhyay argues, won political independence in 1947 without achieving clarity about the direction of its collective life, and the republic's planners filled the vacancy with borrowed doctrines: the West supplied democracy, liberal rights and the ideal of the autonomous person; the socialist bloc supplied planning, state ownership and the ideal of the classless order. Both borrowings err in the same way. Each takes a fragment of the human being for the whole. Western liberalism, in the lecture's account, knows the person as a bearer of appetites and rights, an individual whose freedom consists in pursuing artha and kama, wealth and desire, under rules of fair competition. Marxist collectivism knows the person as a unit of labour and a member of a class, whose fulfilment arrives through the collective's triumph and whose individuality is a bourgeois residue. Against both, the lecture sets the integral person: a unity of body, mind, intellect and soul, whose progress is stunted whenever any one constituent is fed at the expense of the rest. The word integral, which the English title carries and the Hindi Ekatma, one-souled or unified, states more exactly, performs the doctrine's first commitment. A philosophy is disqualified, for Upadhyay, if it is partial, if it organises collective life around one faculty, one class or one appetite. The criterion is philosophical rather than patriotic. Borrowed doctrines fail first because they are partial, and only then because they are borrowed; an indigenous doctrine that was partial would fail by the same test. This matters for the comparison the article must adjudicate. Savarkar's Hindutva (1923) is not partial in this sense, since it defines an identity rather than a theory of the person, and Golwalkar's organic account is not partial either, since it subordinates the person to the national whole on principle. Upadhyay's first lecture stakes a third ground: the person is the primary unit, the person's constitution is fourfold, and any order, national or economic, stands condemned if it develops one constituent by amputating the others. The lecture also plants the national question inside the anthropology. A people's collective life, Upadhyay argues, has a direction given by its civilisational disposition, and progress consists in movement along that direction rather than in imitation of another people's path. The claim is asserted in the first lecture and cashed out in the third, where the disposition receives its name, chiti. Critics of the lectures, from the communist press of 1965 onward, have read the first lecture as a mere clearing of the throat, nationalist rhetoric before the doctrine begins. The sequence refutes the reading. The purushartha framework of the second lecture is the direct answer to the first lecture's charge of partiality: the classical scheme is produced as evidence that Indian thought already possesses an account of the person in which material pursuit, pleasure, righteousness and liberation are jointly provided for, and the economics of the fourth lecture then inherits its constraints from that account. Remove the first lecture and the rest becomes a miscellany; keep it, and the four lectures form a single deduction.

## Lecture Two: The Four Purusharthas as a Developmental Framework

The second lecture, delivered on 23 April 1965, supplies the doctrine's positive anthropology by adopting the classical scheme of the four purusharthas, the aims of human life as Indian thought enumerates them: dharma, artha, kama and moksha. Upadhyay's handling of the scheme is architectural rather than antiquarian. The four aims are not listed as pieties; they are assigned functions in a theory of development. Artha, wealth and the power that accompanies wealth, is legitimate and necessary: a philosophy that despises material provision condemns the person to the very unfreedom it deplores, and the lecture is explicit that poverty is no spiritual credential. Kama, desire and its satisfaction, is likewise legitimate: the person's emotional and aesthetic life is a constituent of fulfilment, not a temptation to be suppressed. Dharma regulates both. Dharma in the lecture's usage is the order of conduct, at once ethical, social and cosmic, that holds artha and kama within bounds compatible with the person's other aims and with the collective's continuance. Moksha, liberation, stands as the orienting end: the person's final self-realisation, which gives the pursuit of the other three their direction and prevents artha from hardening into greed or kama into appetite without limit. The integration is the argument. Western capitalism, on the second lecture's analysis, liberates artha and kama from dharma and so produces the consumption-driven person of the fourth lecture's critique. Marxist collectivism subordinates artha and kama to a state that has abolished dharma as superstition and moksha as opium, and so produces the administered person. The purushartha scheme refuses both amputations by constitutional design: it does not balance the aims by compromise, granting each a percentage of the person's week, but binds them into one movement of a single life. Upadhyay then ties the anthropology to the person's fourfold constitution announced in the first lecture. Body, mind, intellect and soul each have their corresponding satisfactions, and a social order is judged by whether its arrangements let an ordinary person pursue all four aims without the order itself blocking one of them. The judgment standard that falls out of the scheme is antyodaya in embryo, though the word's programmatic career comes later: an order fails when the person at the bottom of it cannot pursue the four aims, whatever its aggregate wealth. The second lecture is also where the doctrine's distance from Golwalkar becomes measurable. In Bunch of Thoughts, compiled a year after Mumbai, the person's aims are absorbed into the nation's: the five unities of country, race, religion, culture and language define the collective, and personal fulfilment is service to that whole. In the second Mumbai lecture the direction of justification runs the other way. The nation is justified, where it is justified, as the arena in which persons pursue the purusharthas; the collective serves the integral person before the person serves the collective. That inversion, quiet in delivery and total in implication, is the philosophical hinge of the whole doctrine.

### How does the integral person differ from the aims of life in the lectures?

The lectures distinguish the person's constitution from the person's aims. The constitution is fourfold: body, mind, intellect and soul, the faculties an integral philosophy must feed together. The aims are the four purusharthas: dharma, artha, kama and moksha, the ends a complete life pursues. Constitution answers what a person is; the purusharthas answer what a person is for.

The distinction does real work in the lectures' polemics. Liberal individualism is charged with mistaking one faculty, the desiring and reasoning individual, for the whole constitution. Marxism is charged with mistaking one aim, a materially defined artha collectivised, for the whole scheme of aims. By keeping constitution and aims in separate columns, Upadhyay can concede the partial truth of each rival: liberalism sees the person's separateness truly, Marxism sees the person's material need truly, and each errs by promoting its fragment into a totality. The concession structure is the lectures' most philosophical habit, and it recurs in the economics, where capitalism's productive energy and socialism's egalitarian intention are each granted before both systems are refused. The four-lecture content-and-architecture map set out in the next section fixes these moves in citable form, because the doctrine's distinctness is a property of the whole architecture, and architectures are compared whole or not at all.

## The Four-Lecture Map: Content and Architecture

The findable artifact of the lectures is a map rather than a quotation, because the doctrine's claim to distinctness rests on the arrangement of its parts. The map exposes the commitment that single-lecture quotation always misses. Each lecture answers the question the previous lecture raises: partiality in doctrine demands an account of the whole person; the whole person demands a collective arena proportioned to the person's aims; the arena demands an economic order that does not contradict them. The contrasts in the final two columns are equally structural. Against Savarkar, the lectures supply what a definitional framework cannot: a theory of the person, a scheme of aims and an economics. Against Golwalkar, the lectures reverse a direction: where the organic framework descends from the national whole to the person, the integral framework ascends from the person to the nation, and the nation it reaches is a disposition to be awakened rather than an organism to be obeyed. These are the distinctions the philosophical-substantive reading exists to state, and the map is the form in which they can be cited, taught and tested.

## Lecture Three: Chiti as the Civilisational Anchor

The third lecture, delivered on 24 April 1965, makes the doctrine's most distinctive conceptual move. Every nation, Upadhyay argues, possesses a chiti: its innate disposition, the civilisational essence formed across its history, which determines the direction in which the nation's collective life can move without violence to itself. The analogy the lecture develops is with the soul of the person. As the soul integrates body, mind and intellect into one life, so chiti integrates a people's laws, institutions, arts and memories into one continuing civilisation. Institutions imported against the chiti fail, however rational their design, because an institution is an instrument and the chiti decides what the instrument is for. Institutions grown within the chiti endure, however archaic their form, because they express the disposition they serve. The political consequences follow in order. A constitution is not self-validating: it is legitimate in the lectures' sense when it operates within the parameters the chiti sets, and the Constituent Assembly's document of 1949 is therefore an instrument to be judged, not a revelation to be worshipped. A party programme likewise stands under judgment: policies that contradict the chiti, however large their majority, set the state against the nation's own direction. The lecture pairs chiti with virat, the nation's awakened strength. Virat is chiti in action: the condition in which the people, conscious of their civilisational disposition, act together as the limbs of a body act together, so that diversity of language, occupation and region composes into strength rather than friction. The lecture's image is hydraulic as much as organic: as prana, the life-breath, holds the body's organs in one functioning whole, so a strong virat holds the nation's diversity in one effective will, and democracy itself, the lecture claims, succeeds only where virat is awake. The pairing lets Upadhyay answer the objection that chiti is a conservative brake. Chiti supplies direction; virat supplies energy; the doctrine wants both, a nation that knows its disposition and acts on it with collective force. The contrasts fix the concept's novelty. Savarkar's anchor is double and territorial: pitribhu, the fatherland, and punyabhu, the holy land, coincide in India for the Hindu and fail to coincide for the Muslim and the Christian, and belonging is decided by that coincidence. Golwalkar's anchor is the organic nation itself, defined by the five unities and prior to every individual claim. Chiti is neither a territory nor an organism. It is a disposition, a characteristic bent of a civilisation, discernible in its history and binding on its institutions as a standard of fit rather than as a test of blood or belief. The distinction is the doctrine's strongest claim to philosophical originality, and its most contested one: critics in the scholarly literature ask how a disposition is to be identified except by the say-so of those who claim to speak for the civilisation, and who corrects the identification when the claimed chiti is disputed between Indians themselves. The lecture does not answer the epistemic objection directly; it treats the chiti as self-evident to the awakened nation, and the adjudication later in this article marks the objection as the point where the philosophical reading must record a genuine gap rather than an exegete's oversight.

## Lecture Four: The Decentralised Economic Order

The fourth lecture, delivered on 25 April 1965, draws the economic consequences, and it is here that the doctrine most plainly exceeds anything in Savarkar or Golwalkar. The lecture's opening position is a double refusal, stated in the text's most quoted sentence in paraphrase: the doctrine wants neither capitalism nor socialism, since both systems fight over the person without understanding the person. Capitalism is refused on the analysis already built. A system that organises production around the multiplication of desires treats the person as an appetite with a wallet; its advertising manufactures the wants its factories then supply, its concentration of ownership converts economic power into political command, and its measure of progress, aggregate consumption, records the feeding of kama after kama has been severed from dharma. Socialism, meaning in 1965 the centralised planning order of the Soviet type and its Indian admirers, is refused on the mirror analysis. A system that vests ownership in the state treats the person as a labour unit; it abolishes the private monopolist by enthroning the state monopolist, administers artha without kama's satisfactions and without dharma's limits, and delivers the administered person of the first lecture's critique. The positive programme follows from the purusharthas. Production must guarantee the basic minimum first: food, clothing, housing, education and health for every person, since a person denied the minimum cannot pursue any aim beyond survival. Beyond the minimum, the order must maximise the number of hands at work rather than the volume of output per hand, because work is the person's participation in artha and mass unemployment is therefore a philosophical failure before it is a statistical one. The institutional forms specified are decentralisation at every joint: small-scale and cottage industry as the economy's base, large industry confined to what small units cannot produce, ownership dispersed among producers rather than concentrated in companies or the state, agricultural primacy respected in a land where most persons worked the soil, and swadeshi, the preference for home production, maintained as the economy's outer wall. Technology is judged by the same standard: a machine is welcome when it multiplies the worker's capacity and suspect when it abolishes the worker. The programme had a decade of preparation behind it. The Two Plans (1958) had already attacked the Second Five-Year Plan's heavy-industry strategy for starving agriculture and village industry of capital, and the Organiser and Panchjanya essays of the early 1960s return repeatedly to the village as the Indian economy's natural unit. The fourth lecture systematises those polemics into doctrine, and the systematisation is the point: the Jana Sangh's economic resolutions before 1965 had listed preferences; after 1965 the preferences descended from a philosophy, and could be defended, amended and taught as deductions rather than tastes.

### What does the decentralised economic order propose for industry?

The fourth lecture assigns production to the smallest unit capable of it. Cottage and small industry form the economy's base, village and district units hold most manufacture, and large industry is reserved for what small units cannot make. Ownership follows the same gradient: dispersed among producers and local bodies, never concentrated in a state monopoly or a private combine.

The industrial programme completes the doctrine's ascent from person to economy. Because the person is integral, work must engage the person's faculties rather than reduce the worker to a machine's attendant; because the aims are four, the economy must deliver the minimum for all four pursuits rather than maximise a single output figure; because the nation's chiti is agrarian and decentralised in its historical disposition, the industrial order that fits India disperses rather than concentrates. Each economic claim carries its philosophical warrant visibly, which is precisely what distinguishes a philosophy's economics from a manifesto's. The general secretary who drafted party programmes for fifteen years knew the difference and chose, in April 1965, to publish the warrants.

## Antyodaya: The Last Person as the Doctrine's Test

Running through the lectures, and named in the party's later vocabulary as antyodaya, the rise of the last, is a test the doctrine applies to every arrangement it considers: judge an order by the condition of the person standing last within it. The test is implicit in the second lecture's architecture. If the aims of life are four and belong to every person by constitution, then an order in which the bottom person can pursue none of them, because hunger consumes the body, ignorance closes the intellect and dependence crushes the will, stands condemned by the doctrine's own premises whatever its aggregates report. The fourth lecture converts the test into policy order: the guaranteed minimum comes first in the sequence of production, before luxury output, before export, before the monuments of planning, because the minimum is the material form of the last person's claim. The test distinguishes the doctrine from both economic rivals more sharply than the celebrated double refusal does. Capitalism's defence, growth first and distribution by consequence, fails the test in timing: the last person is reached last, if the reaching is left to consequence. State socialism's defence, equality administered from the centre, fails the test in agency: the last person is a recipient of the plan, counted in its statistics and absent from its decisions, where the doctrine, with its decentralised units and dispersed ownership, casts the last person as a producer whose work and property are the economy's base rather than its residue. The comparison Indian readers reach for, and which the scholarly literature on the doctrine records, is with Gandhi's talisman, the note of 1948 in which Gandhi proposed testing every act by recalling the face of the poorest and weakest person and asking whether the act would benefit that person. The resemblance is genuine and the difference is instructive. The talisman is a rule of conscience addressed to the decision-maker's imagination, a discipline for the powerful. Antyodaya in the lectures is a structural criterion addressed to the design of orders: it does not ask the minister to remember the last person; it requires the economy's units, ownership and sequence of production to be built so that the last person's pursuit of the four aims is the order's first charge. Whether any government invoking the word has submitted its budgets to the structural test is the question the post-2014 section presses, with the record it finds. Inside the doctrine's own terms, the test has teeth enough to bite its friends: a Jana Sangh or Bharatiya Janata Party programme that concentrated ownership, starved the village unit or placed spectacle before the minimum would fail by Integral Humanism's standard more completely than by any rival's, since the standard is the doctrine's own deduction and not an opponent's platform. The critics' reply is also internal, and the adjudication's evenhandedness records it: a test without a measuring office is a sentiment, and the lectures appoint no institution to apply antyodaya against a finance ministry, leaving the doctrine's sharpest instrument, like its chiti, dependent on the conscience of those who invoke it. The concept has an ancestry the doctrine's expositors acknowledge and the scholarly literature confirms. John Ruskin's Unto This Last (1860) reached Gandhi in South Africa and issued in his Gujarati rendering Sarvodaya, the welfare of all, and Vinoba Bhave carried the sarvodaya banner into the Bhoodan land-gift movement after 1951. Upadhyay's antyodaya stands in that lineage and departs from it at the decisive point. Sarvodaya names the end, the rise of all, and leaves the order of rising to goodwill. Antyodaya names the order: the last person, antya, rises first, and an arrangement has not begun to succeed until the person at the queue's end has begun to rise. The conversion is characteristic of the lectures' method. A moral intuition that in Gandhi remained a discipline for the powerful and in Vinoba an appeal to the landed becomes, in Upadhyay's hands, a criterion of institutional design, a rule by which budgets, units of production and sequences of provision can be audited and found wanting. The Jana Sangh's programmes after 1965 carried the word into resolutions and manifestos, and the party's economists used it to mark their distance from both the Congress's growth-first planning and the communists' equality by administration: the first reaches the last person last, the second reaches that person as a statistic, and antyodaya proposes to reach that person as a proprietor, a producer and a citizen of the smallest self-governing unit. The word's later career, as the label of schemes whose design its author would have audited severely, belongs to the invocations examined below; the concept itself, queue-order as justice, is among the lectures' most portable and least borrowed ideas.

## The First Difference: Against Savarkar's Territorial-Cultural Framework

The comparison with Savarkar is the comparison the doctrine's defenders reach for first, and [Savarkar's territorial-cultural framework as comparator](/2019/09/07/savarkar-hindutva-1923-reanalysed/) shows why the two constructions, often shelved together, answer different questions. Hindutva (1923), written in Ratnagiri internment and published under the pseudonym A Maratha, is a definitional treatise. Its question is membership: who is a Hindu, and thereby who belongs to the Hindu nation. Its answer is the famous coincidence: a Hindu is one for whom India is both pitribhu, fatherland, and punyabhu, holy land, the land of his ancestors and the land of his religion's founding. The definition is territorial and civilisational at once, and it draws its boundary by geography and sacred history, admitting the Sikh, the Buddhist and the Jain, whose holy lands lie within India, and excluding the Muslim and the Christian, whose holy lands lie outside it. The treatise is famously indifferent to theology: Savarkar, an atheist, counts the Hindu by descent and sacred geography rather than by belief or ritual, and the framework's subject is the nation as a cultural-historical population. Upadhyay's lectures answer a different question. Their question is not who belongs but what a human life is for and what order follows from the answer. The purusharthas do not test membership; chiti does not draw a boundary by sacred geography; the economics addresses every producer inside the territory, and the lectures nowhere apply a pitribhu-punyabhu test to any Indian. The primary units differ accordingly. Savarkar's unit is the territorially and culturally defined Hindu, a member located on a map of belonging. Upadhyay's unit is the integral person, a constitution of four faculties pursuing four aims, whose Indianness enters through chiti, the civilisation that formed the person's world, and not through a genealogical audit. The modes of articulation differ with the units. Hindutva is a definition defended by historical narration; the lectures are a deduction defended by the partiality test, each doctrine refused for the fragment it absolutises. Aishwary Kumar, whose Radical Equality (2015) and subsequent essays on Indian political philosophy model the reading of such texts as philosophy, supplies the methodological warrant for treating this difference as real rather than rhetorical: texts that organise their claims by deduction from an account of the person belong to a different genre of political thought from texts that organise theirs by definition of a people, and genre determines what a text can be asked to prove. The lectures can be asked why their economics follows, and they answer. Hindutva cannot be asked that question, because it promises a boundary, not a system. Where the two frameworks touch, the difference persists. Both refuse the Congress's secular liberalism and both ground politics in civilisation rather than in contract. Yet Savarkar's civilisation is a population with a past, and Upadhyay's is a disposition with a direction, and the politics that follows from a population is enumeration and boundary-keeping, while the politics that follows from a disposition is institutional fit and awakening. The distinction survives contact with the hard cases: on the status of the Indian Muslim, Savarkar's framework answers by definition, and Upadhyay's lectures, conspicuously, do not stage the question at all, a silence the critics count against the doctrine and the defenders count as its universalism, and which the adjudication below records as genuinely ambiguous evidence.

## The Second Difference: Against Golwalkar's Organic-Social Framework

The comparison with Golwalkar cuts deeper, because Golwalkar was Upadhyay's mentor, the release of the pracharaks was Golwalkar's decision, and the lectures' differentiation from the organic framework had to be performed inside a relationship of loyalty. [Golwalkar's organic-social framework as comparator](/2019/09/18/golwalkar-bunch-of-thoughts-explained/) rests on the nation as organism. We or Our Nationhood Defined (1939) defines the nation by five unities, country, race, religion, culture and language, and demands the subordination of every partial loyalty to the whole they compose. Bunch of Thoughts (1966), the compilation of Golwalkar's speeches issued the year after the Mumbai lectures, restates the organicism for the post-independence decades: the nation is a living whole, its unity is the supreme value, and the person's fulfilment lies in selfless service to the whole, as the limb serves the body without negotiating its share. The direction of justification is unambiguous. The whole is prior; the person is justified by function within it; rights, where the vocabulary appears at all, are the whole's concessions to its parts. The Mumbai lectures invert the direction at the foundation. The person is prior: body, mind, intellect and soul compose a unity no whole may amputate, and the purusharthas are the person's aims, not the nation's. Chiti then does the work that the organism does for Golwalkar, but with the logic reversed: the nation's essence is a disposition in which persons participate, a direction their collective life takes, and the test of institutions is their service to the integral persons who compose the civilisation. Virat, the awakened strength, is the nearest analogue to Golwalkar's organic unity, and the analogy fails on inspection: virat is a condition of the people's consciousness, an awakening to be achieved, where Golwalkar's unity is a fact of national being, a datum to be acknowledged. An awakening can be refused, delayed or misdirected; a datum can only be obeyed or betrayed. The political tempers differ accordingly, and so does the economics. Golwalkar's writings touch economic organisation obliquely, through the varna-functional idiom in which each order of society performs its allotted task, and through general strictures against both communist class war and capitalist greed. No economic order is specified; none is required, since the organism metaphor assigns functions rather than designing institutions. Upadhyay's fourth lecture specifies an order: units of production, forms of ownership, criteria for machines, a minimum to be guaranteed. The specification is the signature of the philosophical mode, which must deduce institutions from principles, against the organic mode, which invokes a whole and trusts its limbs. Andersen and Damle, in The RSS: A View to the Inside (2018), document the organisational complement of this divergence: the RSS after 1968 honoured Upadhyay as the Jana Sangh's builder while its own self-understanding remained Golwalkar's, and the two frameworks coexisted inside the movement as the property of different institutions, the party philosophising in Upadhyay's idiom while the Sangh organised in Golwalkar's. That coexistence, stable for decades, is itself evidence that contemporaries registered the frameworks as distinct instruments rather than as one doctrine in two bindings.

## Dharma Rajya: The Doctrine's Account of the State

Between the third lecture's chiti and the fourth lecture's economics stands an account of the state that the lectures develop from the doctrine of dharma, and the account is routinely misread by friend and critic alike. Dharma, in Upadhyay's usage, is not religion. The lectures draw the line explicitly: religion, in the sense of a sect with a prophet, a book and a fold, is one human pursuit among others, while dharma is the order that sustains all pursuits, the set of laws, discovered rather than invented, by obedience to which a person, a society and a universe hold together. The distinction carries the doctrine's theory of political authority. A state that rules by dharma, a dharma rajya, is not a theocratic state: no sect's clergy governs, no scripture is administered as law, and the citizen's standing does not depend on confession. It is a state in which power itself stands under a law it did not make. The lectures press the point to a deliberate extreme: even God, omnipotent, acts in tune with dharma, and the king in the classical polity was honoured as Vishnu's symbol precisely as dharma's chief protector, not as its author. Sovereignty, in the modern sense of a will above law, is thereby denied at the root. The democratic consequences are drawn carefully. Democracy is accepted, and accepted as the political form consonant with virat awake, since the people's awakened strength is the only energy that can hold a vast and diverse nation in one effective will. But majority will is not self-justifying: a majority that legislates against dharma repeats, in parliamentary form, the absolutism the doctrine refuses in monarchical form. The state's proper reach is correspondingly narrow. Because the person is integral and the person's aims are pursued in family, village, occupation and association, the state that nationalises those arenas amputates the person in the name of the whole, and the fourth lecture's economic decentralisation has its political twin in a polity of layered, self-governing units with the centre confined to what the layers cannot do. The account positions Upadhyay at a measurable distance from both neighbours again. Against the Congress settlement, the lectures deny that a constitution made in 1949 exhausts political legitimacy: the document is an instrument under dharma's judgment, admirable where it fits the chiti and corrigible where it does not. Against Golwalkar, the lectures deny that the nation's unity licenses the state's supremacy: the nation is a civilisational disposition, the state is one of its instruments, and confusing the instrument with the disposition is the characteristic modern error the doctrine exists to correct. Scholarly critics press two objections against this construction, and evenhandedness requires both at full strength. The first is indeterminacy: dharma, unlike a constitution, has no authorised interpreter, and a state under an uninterpretable law is a state whose rulers interpret as they please. The second is majoritarian capture: in a polity where one civilisation's disposition is named as the standard of fit, the standard's custodians will be drawn from that civilisation's majority, and dharma rajya becomes majority rule with a metaphysical warrant. The lectures answer the first objection by dispersing interpretation across the society's own institutions and traditions, an answer that restates rather than dissolves the difficulty, and they do not address the second objection in terms, because the lectures' category of analysis is the civilisation as a whole rather than the competition of communities within it. Both gaps are recorded here as the adjudication requires: they mark the limits of the construction, and they do not touch the construction's distinctness, which is the claim under defence. The account's democratic content deserves its own statement, because the doctrine is often filed, by association with its neighbours, as ambivalent toward electoral democracy. The record runs the other way. The Jana Sangh contested the general election of 1952 within months of its founding and every election thereafter, and Upadhyay's organisational life was spent inside the disciplines of the vote: candidate selection, manifesto drafting, coalition negotiation, defeat absorbed and analysed. The lectures give that practice its theory. Democracy is the political form of virat awake: the people's civilisational will, formed by chiti and energised by awakening, expresses itself through the franchise, and no smaller body, neither a vanguard party nor a priesthood nor a planning commission, may substitute its will for that expression. The franchise in turn receives duties from the doctrine's decentralism. A vote cast for a remote centre that administers everything is a thin act; the lectures' polity thickens it by returning real power to the units the voter inhabits, village panchayat, municipality, district, so that self-government is practised at the scale where the integral person actually lives. Upadhyay's presidential address at Calicut in December 1967, his last major text, restated the position for the coalition age then opening: the party would pursue its doctrine through democratic competition and parliamentary method, and would treat opponents as adversaries within one national life rather than as enemies of it. The address and the lectures together mark the distance from any reading of the doctrine as a charter for tutelage. Where the organic framework could be, and was, read as licensing the nation's guardians to override its members, the integral framework binds the guardians first: the state under dharma, the majority under dharma, and the party itself under the standard its founder's text supplies.

## The Doctrine's Silences: Caste, Community and the Unasked Questions

A philosophical reading owes its text the inventory of what the text does not argue, and the lectures' silences are systematic enough to map. The first silence concerns caste. The lectures invoke the classical order of Indian society as a harmony of functions and treat the degeneration of that order, birth hardened into hierarchy and untouchability practised as custom, as a corruption of dharma rather than as dharma's product. Upadhyay's wider writings oppose untouchability without qualification and the Jana Sangh's practice under his secretaryship admitted organisers across caste lines as a matter of doctrine-adjacent discipline. But the lectures construct no account of caste as a structure of power: there is no analysis of how the harmony of functions is enforced, no Ambedkarite question about who graded the functions and who profits from the grading, and the purushartha scheme, which distributes aims to every person in the abstract, is never brought to bear on the concrete distribution by which birth assigned persons to aims. Critics in the scholarly and Dalit literature mark this as the doctrine's deepest evasion, the point at which the integral person stops at the boundary of the village's actual constitution. Defenders answer that a philosophy of first principles is not a social programme, and that the antyodaya test, applied honestly, condemns caste disability more radically than the lectures' silence suggests. The dispute is recorded here as genuinely open: the text supplies the standard and omits the application. The second silence concerns the communities the chiti does not name. The lectures address the nation as one civilisation with one disposition, and they neither extend Savarkar's exclusion nor construct a theory of the Indian Muslim, the Indian Christian or the tribal peoples as participants in the civilisational whole. The Panchjanya writings are less reticent than the lectures, and the movement's practice after 1965 filled the silence with positions the text does not contain, in directions its critics and its defenders each deplore for opposite reasons. A reading confined to the lectures, as this one is, must simply register that the doctrine's central concept is asserted for a civilisation whose internal plurality the text celebrates as diversity of limbs while declining to theorise the limbs' separate memories. The third silence is institutional. The lectures say what an order must do and what it must never do, and they are nearly empty of machinery: no electoral theory beyond the acceptance of democracy, no account of courts, no federal design beyond the gesture toward layered self-government, no theory of the party that was, at that moment, adopting the text. The silences share a cause that the biographical record supplies. Upadhyay wrote as a movement intellectual for an audience that possessed the machinery already, in the Sangh's organisation and the party's programme, and required from him the principles the machinery lacked. Principles were the vacancy; he filled it; and the questions a complete political philosophy must answer beyond principles were left, by the division of labour he accepted, to other hands that never took them up in his idiom. The inventory qualifies the claim without overturning it. Integral Humanism is a distinct philosophy with unfinished edges, and the edges are part of the finding.

## A Fourth Articulation: Integral Humanism and the Tripartite Frame

The pillar's own map of Hindutva, drawn in [the tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/), separates the ideology into three articulations: Savarkar's definitional treatise of 1923, Golwalkar's organic compilation of 1939 and 1966, and the electoral Hindutva assembled after 1980. The Mumbai lectures complicate the map by existing. Delivered in 1965, between Golwalkar's two texts in time and outside both in method, Integral Humanism is a fourth articulation that the tripartite frame accommodates awkwardly, as a subspecies of one of its three or as an interlude between the second and the third. The philosophical-substantive reading explains the awkwardness. The tripartite frame classifies by function: definition, organisation, election. The lectures perform a fourth function, systematisation, the conversion of a movement's inheritances into a deducible philosophy, and a frame built on three functions has no shelf for the fourth. The evidence of function is in the text's reception inside the movement itself. The Jana Sangh did not adopt Hindutva (1923) as its official ideology, though Savarkar's authority stood available; it did not adopt Bunch of Thoughts, though Golwalkar's authority stood higher still within the Sangh's own discipline. It adopted, in 1968, the lectures of its general secretary, because a political party contesting elections under a constitution requires citable first principles of a kind neither a definition of the nation nor an organiser's compilation supplies. The lectures' fourfold architecture, person, aims, civilisation, economy, is precisely a party philosophy's table of contents. The fourth-articulation claim also clarifies the doctrine's relation to the founding settlement it refused. The Constituent Assembly's generation had produced its own systematisation in the Constitution of 1949, a document whose tensions between the religion-state question and the rights-development question structured all subsequent argument. Upadhyay's lectures are the Jana Sangh's counter-systematisation: where the Constitution begins from the citizen and the state's enumerated powers, the lectures begin from the integral person and the civilisation's disposition, and each step of the two constructions can be set against the other. That the counter-systematisation was delivered as lectures to a party session rather than as articles to a constituent assembly measures the Jana Sangh's distance from power in 1965; that the lectures were still being invoked as a governing party's philosophy half a century later measures something else, which the sections on institutionalisation and invocation take up in turn.

## President for Six Weeks: Calicut, Mughalsarai and a Contested Death

Upadhyay's tenure at the party's head was the shortest of any major figure in the movement's history. At the Jana Sangh's Calicut session, held from 29 to 31 December 1967, he was elevated from the general secretaryship to the presidency, succeeding to the office Mookerjee had held at the founding. He held it for roughly six weeks. On the evening of 10 February 1968 he boarded the Sealdah Express at Lucknow, travelling toward Patna. In the early hours of 11 February 1968 his body was found beside the railway tracks at Mughalsarai station in Uttar Pradesh, a short distance from the end of a platform, near a trackside pole. The discovery ended the Jana Sangh's founding generation in a single night: of the men who had built the party in 1951, the eminence was fifteen years dead and the organiser was dead at fifty-one. The official inquiry into the death concluded that Upadhyay had been murdered in the course of a robbery aboard the train. Two men were arrested and charged; the prosecution's case, resting substantially on confessions the accused retracted as extracted under duress, collapsed, and the accused were acquitted. The Jana Sangh's leadership refused the robbery conclusion from the first week, demanded a judicial inquiry of a higher order, and held that a political assassination had been disguised as a crime of theft. The dispute never closed. Successive retellings inside the movement have treated the death as martyrdom; official records retain the robbery finding; historians of the party, Jaffrelot among them, record the circumstances as unresolved and the evidence as insufficient for either certainty. The stance of this article follows the documentary discipline the subject requires: the settled facts are the journey, the place, the date, the inquiry's conclusion and the acquittals; the motive and the hand remain disputed, and any treatment that upgrades either the robbery account or the assassination account into settled fact exceeds the record. What the death did to the doctrine is a separate and answerable question. A philosophy whose author dies at the moment of his elevation undergoes a particular fate: the text freezes. Upadhyay wrote nothing after the lectures that revises them, answered no critic, glossed no ambiguity, and the 1965 monograph therefore entered the party's life as a closed testament, to be adopted entire or not at all. The Jana Sangh chose adoption entire, and the choice, taken in grief within the year, shaped the doctrine's institutional career more than any argument in the text itself.

## Institutionalisation After February 1968: The Bombay Resolutions and the Successor Party

The institutionalisation of Integral Humanism was the Jana Sangh's first act of collective self-definition after the death. At the party's Bombay session in 1968, the first national gathering since Mughalsarai, the delegates adopted resolutions constituting Integral Humanism as the party's official ideological framework, the standard against which programmes were to be drawn and by which the party distinguished itself from the Congress, the communists and the socialists alike. The 1968 adoption converted a lecture series into a constitution of thought, and the conversion had the usual double effect of canonisation. It protected the text: no later Jana Sangh leader could quietly retire the doctrine without repudiating the martyred president in public. It also arrested the text: a canonised philosophy is expounded, applied and commemorated, but it is not developed, and the lectures' silences, on the machinery of the modern state, on the place of communities the chiti does not name, on the economics of a post-1991 world their author never saw, remained silences because no authorised successor stood in the relation to the text that its author had stood in. The party that adopted the canon carried it through the remaining Jana Sangh years, the Janata merger of 1977 and the expulsion crisis of 1980, and the Bharatiya Janata Party founded in April 1980 retained Integral Humanism in its constitution as the party's basic philosophy, a retention the more striking because the founding settlement of 1980 simultaneously advertised Gandhian socialism as the new party's public economic idiom. The two idioms coexisted without a stated reconciliation: Gandhian socialism faced the voter, Integral Humanism faced the cadre, and the party's intellectuals were left to harmonise a Bombay lecture series of 1965 with a Gandhian vocabulary Upadhyay never used. Andersen and Damle's account of the RSS's internal view (2018) adds the third institution to the picture: the Sangh, whose own doctrinal furniture remained Golwalkar's, treated Integral Humanism as the party wing's philosophy and raised no objection to its canonisation, a division of doctrinal labour that let each institution keep the text it could use. Through the electoral Hindutva assembled after 1989, temple movement, coalition management and the long apprenticeship in state governments, the lectures persisted in this constitutional sense: cited in party documents, printed in training material for cadres, prefixed to manifestos, and seldom engaged as an argument whose deductions might bind a ministry. The persistence is the institutional fact the counter-thesis must explain, and the next section's invocations after 2014 show the pattern at its most developed: the name everywhere, the argument almost nowhere, and the question of what the philosophy substantively governed left, by governments and critics alike, largely unasked. The documentary trail of the retention is continuous enough to cite. The Jana Sangh's post-1968 constitutions and policy documents carry the doctrine as the party's stated philosophy; the Bharatiya Janata Party's constitution, from its first adoption in 1980 through its later amendments, names Integral Humanism among the party's foundational commitments, in company with nationalism and national integration, democracy, and value-based politics; the party's training literature for office-bearers expounds the four lectures as a set text beside the writings of Hedgewar, Golwalkar and Savarkar; and the doctrine's vocabulary, antyodaya above all, recurs in the party's election manifestos across four decades as the name of its welfare intention. Retention in a constitution is not application in a ministry, and the philosophical reading gains nothing by blurring the two. What the trail establishes is narrower and sufficient: the lectures were never an embarrassment to be managed or a phase to be outgrown, as Gandhian socialism became for the party of the 1990s, but a permanent fixture of the movement's self-description, carried intact through every reinvention of the party's public face. A text can survive that way only if the institution believes the text says something the institution cannot say as well in any other words. The belief may outrun the reading, cadres honouring a doctrine they have not opened, but the belief itself is a historical fact about the lectures' standing, and it is the platform on which the post-2014 invocations were built.

## The Name After 2014: Antyodaya, the Centenary and the Renamed Station

The government that took office in May 2014 made Upadhyay the most invoked Jana Sangh figure of its tenure, and the invocations follow a pattern the philosophical reading exists to measure. The flagship instance is antyodaya, the doctrine's own measure of an order by the condition of its last person, carried into programme nomenclature at scale. The Deen Dayal Antyodaya Yojana, launched in September 2014, folded the national rural and urban livelihoods missions under Upadhyay's name; the Deendayal Upadhyaya Gram Jyoti Yojana of 2015 carried his name into village electrification; the Shramev Jayate labour programme of October 2014 was dedicated to him; and Antyodaya Express trains and state-level Antyodaya ration and canteen schemes multiplied the word across the railway map and the ration shop. The centenary of his birth, running from 25 September 2016 to 25 September 2017, was observed by the Union government as a year of Garib Kalyan, welfare of the poor, with exhibitions, lectures and publications in his name across the states. The fifteen-volume Deendayal Upadhyay Sampurna Vangmaya, his collected works issued by the Publications Division, was released in the same centenary season, putting the full archive, Panchjanya editorials included, into state-published print. School and university curricula in Bharatiya Janata Party governed states added chapters on his life and doctrine. In 2017 the Union government approved the Uttar Pradesh government's proposal to rename Mughalsarai station, and on 4 June 2018 the station beside whose tracks his body had been found fifty years earlier was formally renamed Pandit Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Junction. Set against this record, [the post-2014 Antyodaya programmes context](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) shows the Modi government's general method with inherited doctrine: the name is retained, the welfare idiom is amplified, and the philosophy is present as dedication rather than as argument. For the pattern is exact. The programmes named for Upadhyay are, in design, conventional welfare and infrastructure schemes of a kind every Indian government since 1991 has run: livelihoods missions reorganised rather than reconceived, electrification continued under a new dedication, a labour portal launched under his portrait. None of them is designed by the fourth lecture's criteria. A decentralised economic order of dispersed ownership, cottage production and suspicion of large units is not the economic programme of the post-2014 governments, whose signature projects ran to scale, central platforms and national uniformity. Antyodaya as a naming practice honours the doctrine's standard while the policies answer to other standards, and the renaming of Mughalsarai honours the man at the site of his death without engaging a line of his text. The invocations are therefore evidence in the adjudication, not decoration around it: they establish that the doctrine's career after 2014 ran on the symbolic track its critics describe, and they leave untouched the question the philosophical reading asks, which is not what the name has been made to bless but what the text, read closely, actually argues and how far that argument stands from its neighbours.

## Three Readings Adjudicated: Hagiography, Antecedent and Philosophy

The named disagreement this article must settle arrays three readings of the same fifty pages. The hagiographic reading, the first, treats the lectures as the testament of a saint of the movement: the author is Panditji, the text is scripture, and the appropriate activities are commemoration, quotation and the attachment of the name to good works. Its institutional carriers after 2014 are named in the previous section, centenary committees, state publications, renamed infrastructure, and its scholarly form is the commemorative volume in which the doctrine is expounded by believers for believers without an adversary in sight. The organisational-antecedent reading, the second, treats the lectures as an incident in party history: the doctrine a general secretary supplied to a party that lacked one, significant for the cohesion it provided and for the lineage it establishes to the party of 1980. Its carriers are the serious histories. Bruce D. Graham, in Hindu Nationalism and Indian Politics (1990), the standard study of the Jana Sangh's development, gives Upadhyay his due as the party's organiser and ideologue while measuring him by organisational outcomes. Craig Baxter's biography of the party, The Jana Sangh (1969), written within a year of the death, records the doctrine's adoption as an event in the party's institutional life. Jaffrelot's history of 1996 stands between the second reading and the third: Jaffrelot expounds the lectures' content with a care the pure organisational reading does not require, notes the doctrine's ambition to supply a complete alternative to Western ideologies, and then judges its practical effect small, a judgment the third reading must absorb rather than resent. The philosophical-substantive reading, the third, defended here, treats the lectures as an argument in political philosophy whose claims stand or fall by their construction: the partiality test, the purushartha architecture, the chiti standard of institutional fit, the deducible economics. Its methodological warrant comes from the practice of Indian political philosophy as Aishwary Kumar and others have rebuilt it, in which a text like the lectures is read with the same expectations of coherence, concession and consequence that a reader brings to Gandhi's Hind Swaraj or Ambedkar's Annihilation of Caste. The adjudication proceeds by asking which reading explains the most evidence with the least remainder. The hagiographic reading explains the commemorations and nothing else: it cannot explain the text's argumentative structure, its concessions to rivals, or its decade of drafts in Panchjanya, since relics are not drafted. The organisational reading explains the adoption of 1968, the party's need of a philosophy and the doctrine's constitutional persistence after 1980, and it explains them well. It cannot explain the content's surplus over the need. A party requiring a badge required fifty pages of anything; it did not require a theory of the person in four faculties, a regulatory doctrine of dharma distinguished from religion, an epistemology of civilisational disposition, and an economics deducible from all three, argued against named rivals in a structure of concession and refusal that the Panchjanya file shows under construction for twelve years. The surplus is the philosophical reading's evidence, and it is decisive on the question of what the text is. The strongest counter, stated at full strength as the stance charter requires, is the counter of influence: whatever the text is, the objection runs, its substantive effect on the politics that invoked it approaches zero; no ministry has governed by the fourth lecture, no budget has been audited by antyodaya's standard in its author's sense, and the Sangh's own doctrine remained Golwalkar's. A philosophy that changes nothing, the objection concludes, is a footnote that mistook itself for a system. The counter proves its premise and misses its conclusion. Influence measures a text's career; distinctness measures its construction, and the claim under defence concerns construction. The philosophical reading concedes the career: it has itself documented, in the institutionalisation and invocation sections, a doctrine canonised, constitutionally retained and substantively unapplied, and it records Jaffrelot's judgment of small practical effect as the scholarly consensus it does not dispute. What the reading denies is the inference from an unapplied philosophy to an absent one. The purushartha architecture does not become Golwalkar's organism because ministries ignored it; chiti does not become Savarkar's sacred geography because centenary committees preferred the portrait to the concept. The distinctions are in the text, dated April 1965, citable by lecture and page, and they survive the doctrine's neglect exactly as a neglected proof survives in mathematics: unexploited, and true or false on its own terms. The adjudication therefore finds for the philosophical-substantive reading on the question the disagreement actually poses, the nature and distinctness of the lectures, while finding for the critics on the separate question of influence, which the claim never denied.

### Which scholars produced the standard academic studies of Upadhyay and the Jana Sangh?

The standard academic treatments are Christophe Jaffrelot's The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics (1996), Bruce D. Graham's Hindu Nationalism and Indian Politics (1990) on the Jana Sangh's development, Craig Baxter's The Jana Sangh (1969), and Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle's The RSS: A View to the Inside (2018) on the movement's organisation after 1968.

The division of labour among these studies maps the disagreement the previous section adjudicated. Graham and Baxter write the party's history and meet the doctrine as an event inside it. Jaffrelot expounds the doctrine's content before discounting its effect, the halfway position the philosophical reading both uses and corrects. Andersen and Damle, interviewing the movement's leadership across decades, register the doctrine's institutional address, the party's philosophy housed in a text the parent organisation honours without inhabiting. A reader who works through the four in order watches the lectures change under the hand: programme, ideology, system, furniture. The philosophical-substantive reading is the discipline of holding the third of those descriptions steady against the gravitational pull of the other three.

## The Lectures in Print: Editions, Translations and the Collected Works

A short monograph's authority depends on its textual career, and the lectures' career has three stations. The first is the 1965 publication itself: the four lectures, delivered in Hindi under the title Ekatma Manav Darshan, were issued by the Jana Sangh as a booklet within the year, and the English rendering, Integral Humanism, followed from the party's publishing apparatus in Delhi. The translation fixed the doctrine's English vocabulary, and fixed it with losses a reader should know. Ekatma carries the sense of one-souled unity, an identity of substance between person and collective, where the English integral suggests an assembly of parts into a whole, a weaker and more mechanical claim. Manav, the human, became humanism, a word that in English drags a Renaissance and Enlightenment pedigree the Hindi does not claim. The doctrine's international reception has been conducted almost entirely through the weaker rendering, and some part of its reputation for vagueness belongs to the translation rather than to the text. The second station is the canonisation editions: after 1968 the party and its associated publishers, Suruchi Prakashan of Delhi prominent among them, kept the monograph in print as a cadre text, bound with the presidential address from Calicut and with selections from the Panchjanya essays, so that generations of Jana Sangh and Bharatiya Janata Party workers met the lectures inside a devotional frame, the martyr's testament with commentary by the faithful. The third station is the state's edition: the Sampurna Vangmaya, the fifteen-volume collected works issued by the Publications Division of the Government of India and released from 2016, which gathered the lectures with the books, the diary columns, the editorials and the speeches into a single authorised corpus. The collected works change the scholarly situation materially. Arguments about the doctrine's development, including this article's reliance on the Panchjanya decade, can be prosecuted from a public edition rather than from a movement archive, and the lectures can be quoted by volume and page against the commemorative selections. A text that began as four evenings of speech to party delegates ends, half a century later, as a state-published corpus with a party's constitution still naming its doctrine as the party's philosophy. No other Jana Sangh text has had that career, and the career, like the content, marks the lectures off from the frameworks they are shelved beside: Hindutva (1923) remained a treatise in private and movement circulation, and Bunch of Thoughts a compilation for the converted, while the lectures alone travelled the full road from party session to official philosophy to state edition.

## The Field of 1965: Nehru's Pattern, Lohia's Pillars and the Lectures' Position

The lectures entered a field already occupied, and their position in it sharpens the distinctness finding. To one side stood the governing philosophy of the republic's first two decades, the Nehruvian settlement in its late phase: democratic politics, a secular state standing neutral among religions, and a socialist pattern of society pursued through centralised planning, public-sector heavy industry and the licensing of private capital. Upadhyay's relation to that settlement was adversarial at every joint, and the lectures systematise an opposition the Jana Sangh had conducted since 1951 in resolutions and in Parliament. Where the settlement trusted the plan, the lectures trust the dispersed producer; where the settlement's secularism bracketed civilisation out of politics, the lectures install civilisation, as chiti, at politics' centre; where the settlement measured development in steel tonnage and growth rates, the lectures measure it in the four aims' availability to the last person. The opposition is total but it is an opposition within one republic's argument, conducted in the shared vocabulary of development, democracy and the nation's direction, and the lectures' polemical courtesy, concession before refusal, reflects an antagonist who expected to argue with the settlement's heirs for decades. To the other side stood the socialist opposition of Ram Manohar Lohia, the field's other great refusal of the Congress centre. Lohia's doctrine of the four-pillar polity, village, district, province and centre sharing sovereignty in a chaukhambha order, parallels the lectures' decentralisation closely enough that the Jana Sangh and Lohia's socialists could cooperate in the anti-Congress coalitions that followed the 1967 elections, the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal experiments in which Upadhyay's party sat in state governments beside Lohia's followers. The parallel is institutional and the foundations diverge absolutely. Lohia's pillars rest on a theory of caste and class as the twin engines of Indian inequality, and his seven revolutions aim at their abolition; Upadhyay's layers rest on chiti, a civilisational disposition in which caste appears as degenerated function rather than as engine of oppression, and the lectures' silence on caste as power, inventoried earlier, is the exact point of divergence. The field of 1965 therefore held three systematic answers to the republic's condition, Nehru's, Lohia's and Upadhyay's, of which only Nehru's held power and only Upadhyay's was adopted, within three years, as a party's official philosophy entire. The comparison returns the analysis to its claim with the field as witness. A doctrine is distinct when its neighbours cannot be substituted for it at any joint, and at every joint tested, person, aims, nation, state, economy, the lectures give an answer that is neither the settlement's nor the socialists', neither Savarkar's nor Golwalkar's. Five frameworks stood in the India of 1965; the Mumbai lectures are the fifth, and the only one composed, deliberately and in full view of the other four, as a philosophy.

## The Claim Restated: A Distinct Philosophy, Citable as Such

The evidence assembled across the lectures, the Panchjanya decade, the two contrasts and the three readings supports the claim in its stated form. Deendayal Upadhyay's Integral Humanism is philosophically distinct from Savarkar's territorial-cultural framework, whose question is membership and whose instrument is definition, and from Golwalkar's organic-social framework, whose question is unity and whose instrument is the subordination of part to whole. The lectures ask a third question, what a complete human life is and what order it requires, and they answer it with an architecture no summary can compress into either neighbour: a person of body, mind, intellect and soul; four aims bound by dharma and oriented by moksha; a nation anchored in chiti and energised by virat; a state confined under dharma and forbidden theocracy and absolutism alike; an economy deduced from the person's constitution and audited by the condition of the last person in it. Each element is dated, placed and citable: Mumbai, 22 to 25 April 1965, lectures one through four, with the drafts visible in Panchjanya from 1953 and the canonisation at Bombay in 1968. The adjudicated verdict on influence stands beside the verdict on construction without confusion between them. The doctrine governed little; the doctrine is, nonetheless, a philosophy, and the most exact statement of what Hindutva's tradition produced when it attempted, once, to argue from first principles rather than to define, organise or commemorate. That attempt is the lectures' title to the attention of any reader of Indian political thought, and it is why the philosophical-substantive reading, which alone takes the attempt at its word, is the reading under which Integral Humanism can be taught, tested and cited. The reading is also, in the end, the only one that leaves the text available for judgment. Hagiography places the lectures beyond judgment, in the shrine; the organisational reading places them beneath judgment, in the footnotes of a party history; the philosophical reading leaves them where their author put them in April 1965, on the lectern, open to agreement, refutation and use. A reader who takes up the monograph with the four-lecture map in hand can verify every distinction this article has drawn, can press the objections it has recorded at full strength, the indeterminate chiti, the untheorised caste order, the unbuilt machinery, and can decide whether the architecture stands. That decidability is the final mark of the philosophical text. Doctrines that cannot be tested cannot be inherited either, only repeated, and the difference between the lectures' repetition by governments and their inheritance by readers is the difference the next half-century of the text's life will be spent deciding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Deendayal Upadhyay?

Deendayal Upadhyay (born 25 September 1916 at Nagla Chandrabhan near Mathura, died 11 February 1968) was a Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh pracharak who became the chief organiser and ideologue of the Bharatiya Jana Sangh. He joined the RSS in 1937 during his student years at Kanpur, founded the Lucknow journals Rashtra Dharma (1947), Panchjanya (1948) and Swadesh (1949), and was released for party work when Syama Prasad Mookerjee founded the Jana Sangh in 1951. As general secretary from 1953 he built the party's cadre machinery across the Hindi belt after Mookerjee's death, and in April 1965 he delivered the four Mumbai lectures that set out Integral Humanism. Elevated to the party presidency at the Calicut session in December 1967, he died some six weeks later in contested circumstances at Mughalsarai.

### Q: What is Integral Humanism?

Integral Humanism is the political philosophy Deendayal Upadhyay set out in four lectures at Mumbai in April 1965 and the Bharatiya Jana Sangh adopted as its official ideology in 1968. It holds that the human person is an integral unity of body, mind, intellect and soul, and that any doctrine organised around one fragment of the person, liberal individualism around appetite and rights, Marxism around labour and class, stands condemned by its partiality. Its positive scheme adopts the four purusharthas as the aims of a complete life, anchors the nation in chiti, its civilisational essence, confines the state under dharma, and deduces a decentralised economic order of small industry and dispersed ownership. The Bharatiya Janata Party retained Integral Humanism in its constitution after 1980 as the party's basic philosophy.

### Q: What is Ekatma Manav Darshan?

Ekatma Manav Darshan is the original Hindi title of the doctrine published in English as Integral Humanism. Ekatma means one-souled or unified in substance, and darshan means a philosophy or way of seeing, so the title announces a philosophy of the human being as an indivisible unity rather than an assembly of separate faculties. Upadhyay delivered the four lectures under this title at Mumbai from 22 to 25 April 1965, and the Jana Sangh's Hindi publications carried the doctrine under the Hindi name thereafter. The English rendering loses something in passage: integral suggests parts fitted together, while ekatma asserts that the person, and by extension the nation, is one substance whose apparent parts were never separate. Scholars citing the doctrine in English generally note the Hindi title alongside the translation.

### Q: How did Deendayal Upadhyay die?

Deendayal Upadhyay died in the early hours of 11 February 1968, some six weeks after becoming president of the Bharatiya Jana Sangh. Travelling by the Sealdah Express from Lucknow toward Patna on the night of 10 February, he was found dead beside the railway tracks at Mughalsarai station in Uttar Pradesh, near the end of a platform. The official inquiry concluded that he had been murdered in the course of a robbery aboard the train; two men charged with the killing were acquitted after the confessions central to the prosecution case were retracted as extracted under duress. The Jana Sangh rejected the robbery conclusion and demanded a fuller judicial inquiry, holding the death to be a political assassination. The circumstances remain contested in the literature, and the settled record covers the journey, the place, the inquiry's conclusion and the acquittals, not the motive.

### Q: How is Integral Humanism different from Savarkar's Hindutva?

The two frameworks answer different questions. Savarkar's Hindutva (1923) is a definition of membership: a Hindu is one for whom India is both fatherland, pitribhu, and holy land, punyabhu, and the framework's work is to draw the nation's boundary by territory and sacred geography. Integral Humanism constructs a philosophy rather than a definition. Its primary unit is not the territorially placed Hindu but the integral person, a unity of body, mind, intellect and soul pursuing the four purusharthas; its account of the nation runs through chiti, a civilisational disposition, rather than through a coincidence of fatherland and holy land; and it deduces positions Savarkar never attempted, including a theory of the state under dharma and a decentralised economic order. Where Savarkar's text tests who belongs, Upadhyay's lectures test what a complete human life requires and what order follows from the requirement.

### Q: What are the four purusharthas?

The four purusharthas are the classical Indian enumeration of the aims of human life: dharma, artha, kama and moksha. Dharma is the order of right conduct that sustains persons and societies. Artha is wealth, security and the power that accompanies them. Kama is desire and its legitimate satisfaction, including the emotional and aesthetic life. Moksha is liberation, the person's final self-realisation. In the second Mumbai lecture Upadhyay adopts the scheme as a developmental framework rather than as a pious list. Artha and kama are affirmed as genuine aims, against any teaching that despises material provision or pleasure, but both are regulated by dharma, and moksha orients the pursuit of the other three, so that no single aim may swallow the rest. A social order is then judged by whether an ordinary person inside it can pursue all four aims together.

### Q: Why does the BJP invoke Deendayal Upadhyay?

The Bharatiya Janata Party invokes Deendayal Upadhyay because he is the Jana Sangh's martyred organiser and the author of the philosophy its constitution names as its own. Upadhyay built the cadre party from which the BJP descends, died in contested circumstances weeks after becoming its president, and left in Integral Humanism the only systematic doctrine the tradition produced. After 2014 the invocations multiplied: the Deen Dayal Antyodaya Yojana and allied welfare programmes took his name and his doctrine's keyword, his birth centenary was observed by the Union government in 2016 and 2017, his collected works were issued in fifteen volumes by the Publications Division, curricula added chapters on his life, and Mughalsarai station was renamed Pandit Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Junction in 2018. Critics note that the invocations honour the name more than the argument, since the doctrine's decentralised economics is not the programme of the governments that cite it.

### Q: When and where did Deendayal Upadhyay deliver the Integral Humanism lectures?

Upadhyay delivered the four lectures at Mumbai, then Bombay, on four consecutive days from 22 to 25 April 1965, during the Bharatiya Jana Sangh's national session. He spoke as the party's general secretary, the post he had held since 1953, to an audience formed largely of the party's delegates and organisers. The lectures were delivered in Hindi under the title Ekatma Manav Darshan and were published the same year as a short monograph of roughly fifty pages, with an English rendering, Integral Humanism, following from the party's Delhi publishing apparatus. The sequence of the four evenings is doctrinally deliberate: the first lecture refuses the borrowed frameworks of liberalism and Marxism, the second sets out the four purusharthas, the third introduces chiti and virat, and the fourth deduces the decentralised economic order. The Jana Sangh adopted the lectures as its official ideology after Upadhyay's death, at its Bombay session of 1968.

### Q: What is chiti in Integral Humanism?

Chiti is the concept at the centre of the third Mumbai lecture: the innate disposition or civilisational essence of a nation, formed across its history, which determines the direction its collective life can take without violence to itself. Upadhyay treats chiti as the nation's analogue of the person's soul. As the soul integrates body, mind and intellect into one life, chiti integrates a people's institutions, laws, arts and memories into one continuing civilisation. The political consequence is a standard of institutional fit. Constitutions, parties and programmes are instruments, and an instrument is legitimate, in the lectures' sense, when it operates within the parameters the chiti sets; an imported institution fails not because it is foreign but because it is set against the disposition it must serve. Critics ask how a disposition is identified, and by whom, when Indians dispute the identification among themselves. The lectures treat the chiti as self-evident to the awakened nation and do not build a procedure for contested cases.

### Q: What is virat in Integral Humanism?

Virat is the awakened strength of a nation whose chiti has become conscious in its people. Where chiti names what a civilisation is, virat names the condition in which that civilisation acts: the people, aware of their shared disposition, move together as the limbs of a body move together, so that diversity of language, occupation and region composes into collective strength rather than friction. Upadhyay's image is of prana, the life-breath that holds the body's organs in one functioning whole; a strong virat performs the same holding for the nation. The lecture draws a political consequence directly: democracy succeeds, in this account, only where virat is awake, because elections register a people's will only when the people possess a will formed by a shared civilisational self-understanding. Virat is therefore a condition to be achieved by awakening, not a fact to be presumed, and the Jana Sangh's organisational work among ordinary voters was, in the doctrine's own terms, the practical labour of that awakening.

### Q: What does Integral Humanism say about capitalism?

The fourth Mumbai lecture refuses capitalism as a system organised around a fragment of the person. Its charges are specific. A capitalist order multiplies desires in order to supply them, treating the person as an appetite whose wants its own advertising manufactures; it concentrates ownership until economic power converts into political command; and it measures progress by aggregate consumption, a figure that can rise while the last person's condition falls. Behind the charges stands the purushartha analysis of the second lecture: capitalism liberates artha and kama from the regulation of dharma, and an aim unregulated becomes an appetite without limit. The refusal is not a refusal of property, enterprise or markets as such. The doctrine defends dispersed private property and small production against both the private combine and the state monopoly, and its positive programme, small-scale industry, local economic autonomy and swadeshi production, is a capitalism of the small proprietor disciplined by dharma or it is nothing the lecture recognises as its own.

### Q: What does Integral Humanism say about Marxism?

The lectures refuse Marxism on the same ground as liberalism, partiality, applied from the opposite side. Where liberalism absolutises the individual, Marxism, in Upadhyay's account, absolutises the collective and the material: the person appears as a unit of labour and a member of a class, individuality is treated as a bourgeois residue, and history is reduced to the conflict of classes over the means of production. The first lecture answers that a doctrine which amputates the person's mind, intellect and soul in the name of the person's body repeats the error it condemns in capitalism, and that class is a real relation but not the whole of the person who stands in it. The practical conclusion follows in the fourth lecture: state ownership enthrones a single monopolist in place of many, administers artha without dharma's limits, and delivers the administered person. Upadhyay's Panchjanya writings had argued the case against Indian communism for a decade before the lectures gave it systematic form in April 1965.

### Q: What is antyodaya in Upadhyay's thought?

Antyodaya, the rise of the last, is the doctrine's test of every social and economic arrangement: judge an order by the condition of the person standing last within it. The test is deduced from the purushartha scheme. Since the four aims belong to every person by constitution, an order in which the bottom person can pursue none of them, because hunger, ignorance and dependence consume the whole of life, fails by the doctrine's own premises whatever its aggregate wealth reports. The fourth lecture gives the test a policy sequence: the guaranteed minimum of food, clothing, housing, education and health comes first in production, before luxury and before monuments, because the minimum is the last person's claim in material form. Antyodaya differs from trickle-down reasoning in timing, since the last person is reached first rather than last, and from administered equality in agency, since the last person figures as a producer and proprietor inside decentralised units, not as a recipient counted in a plan's statistics.

### Q: What was Panchjanya and what was Upadhyay's role in it?

Panchjanya is the Hindi-language weekly journal of the Sangh tradition that Upadhyay founded at Lucknow in 1948, a year after he launched the monthly Rashtra Dharma and a year before the daily Swadesh completed his publishing cluster. He edited and wrote for Panchjanya through the 1950s and 1960s, and its pages carried the editorials and essays in which the concepts of Integral Humanism were developed piecemeal across the dozen years before the Mumbai lectures: the refusal of borrowed Western and Marxist frameworks, the defence of the village economy against centralised planning, and the nation described as a civilisational personality with a direction of its own. The journal's archive in Delhi preserves the file, and scholars of the doctrine treat the Panchjanya writings as the under-cited primary source for its gestation, since they show the 1965 system under construction rather than improvised for a party session. The collected works, the Sampurna Vangmaya, later gathered the Panchjanya corpus into print.

### Q: What is the Deendayal Upadhyay Sampurna Vangmaya?

The Deendayal Upadhyay Sampurna Vangmaya is the collected works of Deendayal Upadhyay, issued by the Publications Division of the Government of India in fifteen volumes and released from 2016, during the season of his birth centenary. It gathers the whole corpus: the Integral Humanism lectures of 1965, the books Samrat Chandragupta and Jagadguru Shankaracharya, The Two Plans of 1958, the Political Diary columns from the Organiser, the Panchjanya editorials and essays of 1947 to 1968, the presidential address delivered at Calicut in December 1967, and the speeches and organisational writings of the Jana Sangh years. Its publication changed the scholarly situation of the doctrine materially. Arguments about the development of Upadhyay's thought, including the claim that the lectures systematise a decade of Panchjanya writing, can be prosecuted from a public state edition quoted by volume and page, rather than from movement archives and commemorative selections controlled by the tradition's own publishers.

### Q: How did Integral Humanism become the Jana Sangh's official ideology after 1968?

The adoption was the Bharatiya Jana Sangh's first collective act of self-definition after its president's death. At the party's Bombay session in 1968, the first national gathering since Upadhyay's body was found at Mughalsarai in February of that year, the delegates passed resolutions constituting Integral Humanism as the party's official ideological framework, the standard by which programmes were to be drawn and the doctrine distinguishing the party from the Congress, the communists and the socialists. Canonisation had a double effect. It protected the text, since no later leader could retire the martyred president's doctrine without public repudiation, and it arrested the text, since a canonised philosophy is expounded and commemorated rather than developed, and Upadhyay's silences remained silences for want of an authorised successor. The Bharatiya Janata Party carried the adoption forward in 1980, retaining Integral Humanism in its constitution as the party's basic philosophy even while advertising Gandhian socialism as its public economic idiom.

### Q: What did Upadhyay argue in The Two Plans?

The Two Plans: Promises, Performances, Prospects, published in 1958, is Upadhyay's book-length audit of India's First and Second Five-Year Plans, and the fullest statement of his economics before the fourth Mumbai lecture systematised it. He measures the plans against their own announced targets and finds the failures structural rather than incidental: the plans were drawn from foreign templates, Soviet in industry and diluted Fabian in agriculture, and a template answers the questions of the civilisation that produced it. Applied to India, centralised planning starved agriculture and village industry of capital in favour of heavy industry, misread a decentralised agrarian economy as a defect to be corrected rather than a structure to be built on, and made the state the economy's commanding monopolist. The book anticipates the lectures' positive programme in detail: production assigned to the smallest capable unit, the village respected as the economy's natural base, and progress audited by the condition of ordinary producers rather than by aggregate output.

### Q: How does Integral Humanism distinguish dharma from religion?

The lectures draw the distinction as a load-bearing part of the doctrine. Religion, in Upadhyay's usage, means a sect: a body of belief with a prophet, a scripture and a fold, to which a person belongs by confession. Dharma is not a sect and has no fold. It is the order of laws, discovered rather than invented, by obedience to which a person, a society and the universe itself hold together, comprehending ethics, social obligation and the regulation of artha and kama within the purushartha scheme. The political consequence is the doctrine of dharma rajya, a state under dharma, which the lectures insist is not a theocratic state: no clergy governs, no scripture is administered as law, and a citizen's standing does not depend on confession. What dharma rajya asserts is that power stands under a law it did not make; even the king of the classical polity was honoured as dharma's protector rather than its author, and a parliamentary majority, in this account, is as bound by dharma as a monarch.

### Q: What was Upadhyay's role in the RSS before 1951?

Before the Bharatiya Jana Sangh existed, Upadhyay spent fourteen years as an organiser of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh. He joined the RSS in 1937 while a student at Kanpur, where he took a first class degree in mathematics at Sanatan Dharma College, and he entered the Sangh's pracharak system, the cadre of full-time organisers who gave up salaried careers and family households for itinerant organisational work. Rising to joint provincial pracharak for the United Provinces while still in his twenties, he worked inside the provincial command of the Sangh through the 1940s, building shakhas and training swayamsevaks across the province. In parallel he built the Sangh's Hindi print voice at Lucknow, launching Rashtra Dharma in 1947, Panchjanya in 1948 and Swadesh in 1949. When M.S. Golwalkar released senior pracharaks for party work at the Jana Sangh's founding in October 1951, Upadhyay was among the senior-most of those released, carrying into the new party the Sangh's organisational discipline and its internal trust.

### Q: How is Integral Humanism different from Golwalkar's framework?

The difference is a reversal of direction. In Golwalkar's organic-social framework, set out in We or Our Nationhood Defined (1939) and Bunch of Thoughts (1966), the nation is a living whole defined by five unities of country, race, religion, culture and language, and the person is justified by service to that whole, as a limb is justified by the body. Justification descends from the nation to the individual. In the Mumbai lectures justification ascends. The person is prior, a unity of body, mind, intellect and soul whose four purusharthas are the person's own aims and not the nation's; the nation enters through chiti, a civilisational disposition in which persons participate, and institutions are tested by their service to the integral persons who compose the civilisation. Virat, the awakened national strength, is a condition of consciousness to be achieved, where Golwalkar's unity is a datum to be acknowledged. The economics follows the same reversal: Golwalkar touches economic questions obliquely, while the fourth lecture deduces a specified economic order from the person's constitution.
