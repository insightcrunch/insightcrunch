The first fame of Romeo and Juliet survives on a single page of a clergyman's commonplace book. In Palladis Tamia, entered in the Stationers' Register on 7 September 1598, Francis Meres set the lovers' tragedy last in a roll of six tragedies and offered the whole list as witness that its author was the most excellent English writer in both kinds for the stage. No earlier book, pamphlet, letter, or diary that has come down names the play at all. The verdict argued through this article is that the page deserves its fame and has also been made to carry more than a page can bear. Read strictly, it establishes three things beyond serious dispute: that a play called Romeo and Juliet existed by the autumn of 1598, that a contemporary with no playhouse interest credited it to Shakespeare by name, and that the play stood, in that witness's judgement, in the first rank of the author's tragedies. Read loosely, as handbooks have often read it, it gets stretched into evidence for a composition date, for the identity of the text that survives, and for a triumph at the box office. It proves none of those three. The list is the play's first record of reputation, and reputation is a specific kind of fact: narrower than popularity, harder than gossip, and exactly the kind of fact that print preserves.

The distinction matters because the cliché version of the story has no earliest chapter. Popular accounts begin the fame of Romeo and Juliet with the centuries: with Garrick, with the films, with the schoolroom. The documentary record begins instead in 1598, within a few years of the first performances, in a book compiled for schoolmasters and readers of moral sentences. What Meres wrote is short enough to quote entire, and the whole argument of this article hangs on keeping his three separate mentions of Shakespeare separate, on noticing what the list includes and what it leaves out, and on weighing the witness before weighing the testimony. A compiler of other men's sentences is an odd star witness. The argument below is that his oddity is the point: a commonplace book is a seismograph of what was already circulating, and what was circulating in 1598, far from any playhouse, included the name of this play attached to the name of its author.

## What Is Palladis Tamia, and Who Was Francis Meres?

Francis Meres was born in 1565 at Kirton in Lincolnshire, took degrees at Cambridge and at Oxford, and signed his book, in the period's formula, "Maister of Arts of both Universities." He was a clergyman who would become rector of Wing in Rutland in 1602, a translator of the Spanish devotional writer Luis de Granada, and a man connected by marriage to John Florio, the lexicographer and translator of Montaigne. He was not a playwright, not a sharer in any company, and not a London theatrical insider. His one book of lasting consequence, Palladis Tamia: Wits Treasury, was published in London in 1598, printed by P. Short for Cuthbert Burby, and entered to Burby in the Stationers' Register on 7 September of that year. The title promises the treasury of Pallas Athena, goddess of wisdom, with a probable pun on the Thames folded into "Tamia"; its motto runs "Vivitur ingenio, cetera mortis erunt": genius lives, the rest belongs to death. Few title pages have announced their own afterlife so accurately. The genius that lived turned out to be less the compiler's than the names he collected.

The book was a commercial product before it was a critical one. It announced itself as "the second part of Wits Commonwealth," following Politeuphuia, the sentence collection associated with John Bodenham and the stationer Nicholas Ling, which had run through the press three times within a year. Meres's contribution to the series is a treasury of similes and sentences arranged by topic, moving from God and nature through books, reading, philosophy, and poetry. Most of it is gathered matter, in the manner of the genre, drawn from classical authors, the Church Fathers, and modern writers including Greene, Lyly, Warner, and Sidney. Buried some two hundred and fifty pages in, with no trumpet of its own, sits the section that posterity reads the book for: "A Comparative Discourse of our English Poets, with the Greek, Latin, and Italian Poets," a chain of balanced comparisons in which each English writer is matched against an ancient forerunner. It is within that discourse, near folio 282 of the original, that Shakespeare is praised three times and that the lovers' play receives its first surviving mention in print.

### What kind of book is a commonplace treasury?

A commonplace treasury is a printed storehouse of reusable sentences, comparisons, and examples, arranged under headings for use in sermons, letters, school exercises, and talk. Its compiler's skill lies in selection and arrangement rather than invention, and its readers are schoolmasters, clergy, and literate households rather than specialists.

That genre fact governs everything the discourse can prove. The genre prizes aptness and abundance, and gathers authorities ancient and modern into a single storehouse. A commonplace book does not review; it retails. Its entries are there because they are usable, which means they are there because their subjects were already current enough to be worth a reader's while. When such a book names a play, the naming is not a critic's verdict delivered from a seat in the theatre. It is a market signal: the name had travelled far enough, and carried enough recognition, to earn a line in a volume sold on the strength of its usefulness to people who might never see the play performed. Meres's discourse also stands apart from the sentence-hoard around it. The comparisons are his own commentary in a way the gathered similes are not, and the discourse's judgements, however conventional their form, are presented as a survey of the living English field from Chaucer down to the compiler's contemporaries. The form is borrowed; the census is contemporary. Both halves of that sentence have to be held at once, and the long scholarly quarrel over Meres, examined below, is largely a failure, on one side or the other, to hold them together.

Imagine the record without the page, and its necessity comes into focus. Strike Palladis Tamia from the archive, and the first external naming of the play becomes Weever's allusive epigram of 1599, a compliment that gestures at Romeo rather than registering a title; the attribution of the play to its author in print retreats to the third quarto of 1609; the sonnets lose their first witness and drift undated until their own quarto; and the dating of the whole early canon loses the single fixed point against which its other evidence is arranged, leaving quarto title pages and inference to carry the chronology between them. Nothing about the play changes, and everything about the knowledge of the play changes. The counterfactual is not a parlour game. It is the correct measure of a document's value, which is the size of the hole its absence would leave, and by that measure the discourse's page is the largest single sheet in the early record. Fame that is never written down is real while it lasts and invisible afterward; the lovers' play's earliest fame would be of that invisible kind but for one compiler's decision to arrange his contemporaries in a chain of comparisons on an autumn's publishing schedule.

The man himself cuts both ways as a witness, and the article will not pretend otherwise. Against him: he was a provincial compiler whose knowledge of the classics, as Don Cameron Allen demonstrated in the 1930s, was heavily secondhand, his discourse stitched together from other men's books. For him: he had no conceivable motive to flatter a playing company, no share in any playhouse venture, and no reason to name twelve plays by one author unless the naming reflected what a well-read Englishman of 1598 could be expected to know. His Florio connection placed him one remove from the Southampton circle in which Shakespeare's narrative poems had been dedicated, and his publisher, Burby, was the stationer who in that same year issued the first quarto of Love's Labour's Lost with the author's name on its title page and who a year later would publish the second quarto of the lovers' play itself. The witness sat, in short, at the edge of the book trade that was turning a repertory into a reputation. What such a witness writes down is not the roar of the yard at the Theatre or the Curtain. It is the quieter and, for a historian of fame, more portable fact of what print believed was known.

The discourse also has a polemical errand, and knowing it sharpens the reading of every list in it. In the sentences leading into the comparisons, Meres complains plainly about the age's treatment of its poets: the Greek and Latin poets were honoured, while Elizabeth and her ministers left English poets to shift in neglect. The discourse that follows then exhibits a national field rich enough to answer the complaint, matching the ancients name for name and kind for kind. The roll calls are therefore exhibits in a brief for English literature, assembled by an advocate who wants the field to look strong. Advocacy does not void testimony, but it does describe its bias: an advocate exhibits his strongest proofs, arranges them for effect, and passes over what would weaken the display. For the fame of a play named in such a brief, the bias cuts in a favourable direction, since an advocate's exhibits must be his most recognizable assets. Nobody pads a national showcase with obscure titles. The lovers' play is in the discourse because its name strengthened the argument that England's stage had a Plautus and a Seneca in one man, and a name strengthens such an argument only if the audience for the book can be trusted to know it already.

## The First Survey of Its Kind: Webbe, Puttenham, and the Missing Stage

The discourse's novelty is easiest to see against the surveys that came before it. English criticism had attempted the national stocktaking twice in the generation before Meres. William Webbe's Discourse of English Poetrie of 1586 marched through the makers from Chaucer to Spenser, praising the new poetry and hoping for its reformation, in a survey whose stage is a rumour at the edge of the page. George Puttenham's Arte of English Poesie of 1589, the age's most ambitious poetics, assembled its roll of honour from the court: the makers it celebrates are courtiers and gentlemen, and its famous census of the reign's poets reads like a household list of the well-born, with the commercial playhouses and their hired pens outside the door. Neither book could have contained the lovers' play, since both predate its writing; but neither book's form had a shelf where such a play could have been put. The survey tradition, before 1598, was a survey of poems by gentlemen.

Meres broke the form open, and the break is the precondition of the play's first fame document. His discourse still wears the courtly dress of comparison with the ancients, and it still begins with Chaucer and proceeds through the reign's ornaments. But its centre of gravity has shifted to the marketplace of print and the playhouse: its Shakespeare passage measures a working dramatist, a man of the commercial stage with no court office and no university chair, and finds him the most excellent in both kinds for that stage, ahead of every university wit and gentleman maker in the survey. The twelve titles are listed the way a modern survey lists a filmography, as the public record of a professional. Nothing earlier in English does this for any playwright. The consequence for the lovers' tragedy is direct. A fame document is not merely a compliment that happens to survive; it is a slot in a structure of record, and 1598 is the year the structure acquired a slot of the right shape: a printed, dated, comparative census in which plays counted as literature and a play could be entered, attributed, and ranked by kind. Romeo and Juliet is the first English play whose fame can be watched entering such a structure, because the structure and the fame are nearly the same age.

The point should not be inflated into a claim that Meres invented literary fame for the stage. Playgoers had favourites, companies had draws, and Greene's pamphlets show a dramatist's name could be worth attacking in print years before. What the survey form added was durability and address: a compliment dies with its occasion, but a census is shelved, reprinted, and consulted, and it files its subjects where later ages look for them. The play's name entered the census at folio 282 of a treasury that schoolmasters kept buying. Between Webbe's hopes and Puttenham's household there had been no such filing cabinet for a play. Meres built the first one that survives, and the lovers' tragedy is in its earliest drawer.

## The Three Shakespeare Passages in Palladis Tamia, Kept Apart

Shakespeare is named three times in the discourse, and the first act of accuracy the subject demands is keeping the three passages apart, because popular summary habitually welds them into a single compliment aimed at the play. They are not one compliment, and only one of them concerns the play at all. The first passage belongs to the poems. Matching Ovid against his English heir, Meres writes that "the sweet witty soul of Ovid lives in mellifluous and honey-tongued Shakespeare," and offers as witness "his Venus and Adonis, his Lucrece, his sugared Sonnets among his private friends." The famous honey is here, and it sweetens the narrative poems and the manuscript sonnets. Romeo and Juliet is not named, implied, or glanced at in the sentence. Any account that lets "honey-tongued" drift across the page and settle on the tragedy is inventing praise Meres never gave it.

The second passage is the list itself, and it is built not as praise of any single work but as a proof. Matching Plautus and Seneca, "accounted the best for Comedy and Tragedy among the Latines," Meres concludes that "so Shakespeare among the English is the most excellent in both kinds for the stage," and then exhibits the evidence: "for Comedy, witness his Gentlemen of Verona, his Errors, his Love Labours Lost, his Love Labours Won, his Midsummers night Dreame, and his Merchant of Venice; for Tragedy, his Richard the 2, Richard the 3, Henry the 4, King John, Titus Andronicus, and his Romeo and Juliet." The grammar deserves slow reading. The plays are summoned as witnesses to a claim about their author. The lovers' play is therefore not complimented in its own person; it is entered as evidence, the final exhibit on the tragedy side, that its maker excelled in the kind. For a fame document this is arguably worth more than a compliment. A compliment can be courtesy. An exhibit has to be recognizable to do its work: a witness nobody has heard of testifies to nothing.

The third passage returns to the author without naming any work. As the Muses would speak with Plautus's tongue if they spoke Latin, Meres says they would speak with "Shakespeare's fine-filed phrase" if they spoke English. Taken together, the three passages build a single figure: a poet of Ovidian sweetness in verse, a dramatist of unmatched range on the stage, and a stylist whose phrasing is the imagined standard of the language itself. Elsewhere in the discourse the compiler also ranges Shakespeare among the best English writers for tragedy in a company that includes Marlowe, Peele, Kyd, Drayton, and Chapman, which confirms that the list of six was a selection from a field the author knew to be crowded, not an inventory of everything the age had produced. The play's own portion of this structure is precise and limited: one title, one possessive pronoun, one seat at the end of a list of six. The whole case for the play's early fame rests on understanding exactly how much weight that small portion can carry, and the rest of this article is the weighing.

### What does the phrase "fine-filed phrase" praise, and whose is it?

The phrase praises Shakespeare's language itself: his filed, polished, exact phrasing, imagined as the medium the Muses would choose for English as they would choose Plautus's tongue for Latin. It belongs to the author, not to any one work, and it sits in a separate comparison from the honey-tongued tribute and the roll of plays.

The separation has one more consequence that summary accounts miss. Because the praise-words attach to the poems and to the phrasing, the tragedy list has often been read as if it borrowed their warmth, as though Meres had reviewed the play and found it honeyed. He did nothing of the kind, and the play is not diminished by the correction. What the list gives the tragedy is colder and more durable than warmth: registration. In a book whose entire business was to record what counted, the play is recorded as counting. Fame in its first documentary form is rarely a rave. It is a line in a ledger kept by somebody with no reason to lie, and that is the form in which the fame of Romeo and Juliet first survives.

The machinery of the first compliment repays a slower look, because it shows what kind of claim the discourse is making for its subject. The conceit is metempsychosis, the migration of souls that Pythagoras taught and that Ovid dramatized in the last book of the Metamorphoses: as the soul of Euphorbus, the Trojan spearman, was thought to live again in Pythagoras, so the sweet witty soul of Ovid lives in Shakespeare. A compliment built on transmigration is not small talk. It asserts a succession, a lineage in which the English writer is the present vessel of a classical spirit, the heir in whom an ancient excellence has taken up residence. Meres builds such genealogies all through the discourse, pairing Chaucer, Sidney, Spenser, and the rest with their ancient counterparts, and the cumulative effect is a canon described as a family tree. To be entered in that tree at all is the discourse's characteristic honour. The lovers' play earns its line in the tree one step down, as the fruit by which the tragedian branch is known, but the step is short: the exhibit exists to prove the heir, and the heir's standing is the discourse's whole contention.

## How Much Is a Superlative Worth in a Discourse Full of Them?

A fair scepticism remains to be priced before the list section proper: the discourse deals in superlatives the way its genre deals in sentences, wholesale. Every English poet in its chain receives a classical crown of some kind; Sidney, Spenser, Drayton, and the rest are each the English somebody, matched and exalted in turn. If everyone in the book is the most excellent at something, the sceptic asks, what is the evidentiary cash value of Shakespeare being called the most excellent in both kinds for the stage? The answer lies in the shape of the crowns. The other superlatives in the discourse are single-axis honours: a poet is matched to one ancient, in one vein, for one gift. The Shakespeare comparison is built differently. It crowns its subject twice in a single sentence, for comedy against Plautus and for tragedy against Seneca, and then supports the double crown with the discourse's only full roll call of a living dramatist's works, twelve titles deep. No other figure in the survey, ancient or modern, is exhibited at that length. Measurement beats impression here: the sceptic's premise, that the praise is generic, fails against the document's own distribution of its space. Generic praise is a sentence; this is a page. And the superlative has a second, quieter distinction. It is attached to the stage, "for the stage," in a book whose honours otherwise run to page poetry, which means the compiler reached outside his treasury's usual commerce to certify a playhouse reputation. The lovers' play, as the tragedies' closing exhibit, stands inside the discourse's most elaborated act of praise, and whatever discount the genre's inflation imposes on the words, the allocation of space is not inflated. It is unique.

## The Tragedy List and the Company Romeo and Juliet Keeps

The six tragedies Meres names are Richard II, Richard III, Henry IV, King John, Titus Andronicus, and Romeo and Juliet. The company is worth dwelling on, because the list is doing two kinds of work at once. Five of the six are plays of English history and one is a Roman horror piece from the Senecan line; the lovers' tragedy is the only play in the six whose matter is a private Italian love story drawn from a novella, and the only one whose catastrophe turns on a missed message and a sleeping potion rather than on crowns, battles, and revenge. Its presence in the roll is therefore not filler. A compiler reaching for six titles to prove excellence in tragedy had the whole early repertory to draw on, including works by the author that have since vanished from his list-making into far greater fame in other kinds. He chose, as his closing exhibit, a love tragedy from Verona. Whatever else the choice means, it means the play was among the half-dozen tragedies by which its author's excellence in the kind could be illustrated to a general reader in 1598.

The list as a whole names twelve plays, six comedies and six tragedies, and the symmetry is the discourse's habit rather than a census taker's. Everywhere in the Comparative Discourse, Meres builds in balanced sets, matching ancients and moderns term for term; the as-and-so architecture of the commonplace comparison rewards rounded numbers and parallel columns. Twelve is a shaped figure, not a count of a complete works. The shape matters for the limits argued later in this article, since a list built for symmetry will include what fits the pattern and omit what does not, but it does not weaken what inclusion itself attests. A compiler padding a symmetry still pads it with names his readers will recognize; an invented or obscure title would break the comparison's persuasive contract. The six comedies make the same point from the other side. Alongside plays that survive and are read, the comedy column carries Love's Labour's Won, a title no other document of the age records and no print shop ever issued, examined in its own section below. A list that can preserve a lost play can hardly be dismissed as a copy of title pages, and a list that omits famous work, as this one does, can hardly be trusted as an inventory. It is a witness statement, with a witness's strengths and a witness's gaps.

### Where in the book does the play's name actually fall?

The name falls near the end of the Comparative Discourse, around folio 282 of the original edition, as the final title in the tragedy half of the Plautus and Seneca comparison. It is the last of six tragedies and the last play named in the discourse's only roll call of English drama.

Position should not be over-read. The order of the six tragedies is not chronological in any provable scheme, since Henry IV and the two Richards resist the sequence they are given if the sequence is pressed as a writing order, and it is not a ranking, since Meres nowhere grades within his lists. The likeliest logic is associative and rhetorical: the history plays mass together, the Roman piece follows, and the Italian love tragedy closes the column as the kind's most different specimen, the exhibit that shows the range of the excellence being proved. Ring composition of that sort is a commonplace-writer's reflex. What the closing position does give the play, without any inference at all, is memorability within the document: later readers lifting the passage, from the editors of the nineteenth century onward, have always met the lovers' names last, at the point where the list's cadence lands. A document's afterlife is part of its evidence value, and the afterlife of this page has been shaped by that final placement far more than by anything else on it.

The column's composition is also a map of what tragedy meant in 1598, and the map explains the inclusion better than any theory of the compiler's private taste. Four of the six are chronicle histories, the dominant tragic matter of the decade's stage, in which the fall of kings did the work Seneca had assigned to the fall of houses. Titus Andronicus represents the other established line, the revenge tragedy of Roman horror. Measured against that field, the lovers' play is the list's outlier and its omen: a tragedy with no king, no war, no ghost demanding vengeance, whose catastrophe is assembled from a feud's background noise, a friar's pharmacology, and a letter that miscarries. That a compiler mapping the kind in 1598 already counted such a play among the six exhibits of excellence shows the kind stretching in his hands. The discourse elsewhere names England's best writers for tragedy as a company: Sackville, Lord Buckhurst of Gorboduc's line, Doctor Legge of Cambridge, Doctor Edes of Oxford, the Mirror for Magistrates author, Marlowe, Peele, Watson, Kyd, Shakespeare, Drayton, Chapman, Dekker, and Jonson. Shakespeare appears in that roster as one tragedian among many. In the Plautus and Seneca comparison he stands alone, the only Englishman measured against the ancients in both kinds at once. The roster tells us the compiler knew a crowded field; the solo comparison tells us where he placed its leader; and the lovers' play is named inside the solo comparison, not the roster, which is the more exclusive of the two honours by a wide margin.

Titus Andronicus deserves its own sentence in the weighing, since it is the column's bridge to the comparison that frames it. Seneca is the tragedian of the framing sentence, and Titus, steeped in Senecan horror and in Ovidian citation, is the exhibit that touches the classical term most nearly: a Roman play of mutilation and revenge pies, in which the Latin tags are part of the action. Placed fifth, it hands the column over from chronicle to fable, and the lovers' tragedy, sixth, completes the traverse from public history to private catastrophe that the proof of range required.

The comedy column rewards the same slow reading, because it shows the selection principle working where no thesis depends on it. The six are The Two Gentlemen of Verona, The Comedy of Errors, Love's Labour's Lost, Love's Labour's Won, A Midsummer Night's Dream, and The Merchant of Venice: an early romantic comedy, a Plautine farce of twins, two plays built on the wordplay of courtly lovers, a fairy woodland piece of a kind nobody else was writing, and a problem play of bonds and rings that resists its own column. The spread is the argument. As with the tragedies, the compiler is not cataloguing favourites but demonstrating range, kind by kind and mode by mode, and a demonstration of range is only as good as the recognizability of its specimens. Modern readers who know the canon from the top down forget how much of the list was, in 1598, simply the current news of the stage: plays a few seasons old, unprinted in the majority, held in the memory of playgoers and the stock of one company. The discourse's two columns are a snapshot of a repertory at full stretch, and the lovers' tragedy is its closing entry because the demonstration ends, by design, at the point of maximum difference from where the histories began.

## "His Romeo and Juliet": The First Printed Attribution

Every title in the Plautus and Seneca sentence carries the same small word in front of it: his. His Gentlemen of Verona, his Errors, his Romeo and Juliet. The possessive is the discourse's machinery, since the sentence exists to assign a body of work to a man, but its effect on the lovers' play is historically singular. No printed copy of Romeo and Juliet named William Shakespeare as its author until the third quarto of 1609. The first quarto of 1597 had offered the play as "an excellent conceited tragedy" acted by the Lord Hunsdon's servants; the second quarto of 1599, the long and corrected text, still named only the Lord Chamberlain's servants who had acted it. The play's title pages spoke of a company, a venue tradition, and a genre, and were silent about a maker. A reader who bought either quarto owned a famous play by nobody in particular. Readers interested in how the play's early books present themselves can pursue the matter in [what the title pages tell us](/2026/08/28/title-pages-romeo-and-juliet/), where the contrast between the company's name and the author's absence is the whole subject.

Meres supplies the missing name in a different kind of book entirely. The attribution in Palladis Tamia is not a title page, which in the period was an advertisement set by a stationer, but a sentence in a critical survey, written by a man who gained nothing by the assigning. That is precisely why it has carried such weight with historians of the canon: it is disinterested, it is dated to the month by the Register entry, and it assigns not one play but a dozen, in a pattern that has checked out against every other record that survives. Where the list can be tested against independent evidence, against quarto title pages, Revels records, and the Folio's own attributions, it holds. The possessive pronoun in front of the lovers' title is thus the first time the reading public could have learned from print whose play this was, and it arrived not from the playhouse or the print shop that sold the play, but from a Rutland clergyman's treasury of sentences.

The timing sharpens the point, because the trade itself was discovering the name's cash value in the very months the discourse was compiled. The first play quarto ever to carry Shakespeare's name, Love's Labour's Lost, "newly corrected and augmented by W. Shakespere," came from Burby's shop in 1598; Andrew Wise's second issues of Richard II and Richard III in the same year added "By William Shakespeare" to title pages that had been anonymous in 1597. Stationers had begun to learn that the name sold books. Against that background the silence of the lovers' play's own title pages is the anomaly that Meres corrects: the second quarto of 1599, printed for the same Burby who published the discourse, still offered the tragedy as the work of a company rather than a man. The attribution that the play's own publishers withheld for another decade is the attribution the clergyman supplied within months of the first quarto. Whatever channel carried the knowledge to Rutland, the knowledge was current, specific, and ahead of the play's own packaging.

There is a further refinement. The play itself is preoccupied with the power and the poverty of names: Juliet, in the orchard, works through the discovery that the name Montague is no part of the man she loves, asking "What's in a name?" at 2.2.43 in the Oxford text edited by Jill L. Levenson, and concluding that a rose by any other name would smell as sweet. The documentary history runs the other way. For the play's fame, the name attached to it mattered enormously, because fame in print is cumulative only when it can be filed under an author. Anonymous successes dissipate; attributed successes compound. The two quartos of the 1590s sold a tragedy; Meres's sentence filed that tragedy in a canon, under a name that the same page was busy attaching to Venus and Adonis, to a sheaf of sonnets in manuscript, and to eleven other plays. Early fame, in the form that print preserves, is exactly that filing. Whoever wishes to date the moment the lovers' tragedy became Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet in the public record has one candidate document, and it is this page.

## Two Kinds of Fame Claim: The Title Page Against the Treasury

The play's own books also testify about its fame, and setting their testimony beside the discourse's shows why historians trust the clergyman further than the stationers. The first quarto's title page announces "An Excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet, as it hath been often (with great applause) publicly played" by the Lord Hunsdon's servants. The second quarto repeats the formula in a cooler register: the tragedy "as it hath been sundry times publicly acted" by the Lord Chamberlain's servants. These are fame claims, and they may well be true; a company does not usually advertise a failure, and a publisher does not usually reprint one. But they are interested claims, printed on the object being sold, by parties who profited from the selling. Applause on a title page is advertising copy, and advertising copy is evidence of what a seller hoped buyers would believe, which is one remove from what audiences actually did.

Meres's naming is the opposite kind of claim. It appears in a book that is not selling the play, written by a man with no share in the takings, addressed to readers who were buying sentences rather than tickets. Its testimony about reputation is therefore disinterested in the strict sense: nothing in the compiler's position improves if the play is famous, and nothing worsens if it is not. Disinterestedness is the quality that lets a document graduate from the history of marketing to the history of fact. The contrast runs through the whole early record of the canon. Title pages call plays excellent, famous, and applauded as a matter of formula, and the formula's very regularity drains it of information; a compiler's roll call, drawn up for another purpose entirely, has no formula to serve and so retains its signal. When the two kinds of claim agree, as they do here, the agreement is the strongest fame evidence the decade offers: the sellers said the play was applauded, and an outsider with nothing to gain filed its name among the proofs of its author's excellence. Either witness alone could be discounted. Together they triangulate.

There is a third voice in the record, later and smaller, and it belongs to the audience side of the wall. Gabriel Harvey, the Cambridge scholar and tireless annotator, jotted in the margin of his Chaucer that the younger sort took much delight in Venus and Adonis, while Lucrece and "his tragedie of Hamlet, Prince of Denmarke" had it in them to please the wiser sort; a sentence in Anthony Skoloker's Daiphantus of 1604 likewise praises Hamlet as a work that pleased across the house. Such scraps are the nearest the age comes to reportage of response, and their scarcity, two or three jottings against a shelf of title pages, is the measure of the evidentiary problem. The discourse does not solve that problem. It does something else: it certifies that by 1598 the response, whatever it was, had condensed into a reputation solid enough to be spent as an argument in a book of sentences. Reputation is the residue fame leaves in documents, and Meres is where the residue first becomes visible.

The market supplied one more witness the following year, and its testimony is the kind that costs money. In 1599 Burby issued the second quarto of the play, printed by Thomas Creede, with a title page advertising the text as "newly corrected, augmented, and amended." Behind the formula lies an expensive fact: somebody obtained a fuller and better manuscript of the tragedy, paid for its setting in type at nearly a third again the length of the first quarto, and staked the edition on the proposition that buyers of the short text, and new buyers beside, would pay for the play again in a corrected form. Publishers of the period reprinted successes and let failures die in their first editions; the whole economics of the play quarto trade turns on the distinction. A corrected and augmented reissue within two years of the first edition is therefore a balance-sheet entry in the history of the play's fame, the commercial counterpart of the compiler's registration. The two documents of 1598 and 1599 face each other across the turn of the year: in one, a clergyman with nothing to gain files the title among the proofs of an author's excellence; in the other, a stationer with money at risk pays to print the play longer and better. Disinterested registration and interested investment, arriving independently at the same valuation within months of each other, are as strong a chord as documentary evidence in this period ever strikes, and the lovers' play is the string on which it sounds.

## The Deadline Meres Fixes, and the Kind of Bound It Is

A dated list is a wall. Because Palladis Tamia was entered in the Stationers' Register on 7 September 1598, every play Meres names must already have existed, in some performable form, by that autumn; a compiler cannot exhibit a play that has not been written. For the lovers' tragedy this is the firmest external bound the early record offers, firmer in its logic than any inference from style or allusion, and the standard accounts of [when Shakespeare wrote Romeo and Juliet](/2022/03/11/when-did-shakespeare-write-romeo-and-juliet/) build their chronology against it. The bound is a latest date, a terminus ante quem: the play cannot be later than the list that lists it. Everything else about the date, the year of first composition, the season of first performance, the gap between writing and the staging the first quarto advertises, has to be argued from other evidence, and the argument from the play's internal calendar, [the Nurse's earthquake clue used to date the play](/2026/09/04/dating-play-nurse-earthquake-clue/), belongs to that larger case rather than to this page. This article keeps to its fence: the bound itself, and what kind of instrument it is.

Two properties of the bound deserve emphasis. The first is that it binds existence, not text. What existed by September 1598 was a play of that name by that author, known well enough to be exhibited. Whether the play Meres knew line for line is the play in the second quarto of 1599, which its title page calls newly corrected, augmented, and amended, or the shorter play of the first quarto, or a stage text that differed from both, the list cannot say. Scholars who cite Meres as though he had read and dated a specific edition commit a small category error with large consequences, since the whole textual history of the play, with its two early versions and their debated relation, lies on the far side of a wall the list cannot see over. The second property is that the bound is asymmetrical in its logic. Inclusion proves existence; the argument runs one way only. What the mirror-image error looks like, the attempt to make the list's silences testify, is treated in the omissions section below, where the cost of reversing the logic becomes plain.

Used within its logic, the bound has done heavy and legitimate work. Jill L. Levenson, in the Oxford edition of the play, settles the composition in the middle of the decade, around 1595 and 1596, inside a frame whose outer posts are the source tradition behind the play and the Meres wall in front of it; Brian Gibbons's Arden edition works within the same frame. The wall is what makes the frame a frame. Remove Meres, and the dating of the play rests on the quartos' existence and on inference; restore him, and a dozen plays acquire a common latest date from a single dated page, which is why the page is quoted at the front of nearly every chronology of the canon ever printed. That service is real, and it is also the seed of the overuse examined later: a document that does one thing supremely well invites its users to ask it for everything.

The wall's strength is worth one comparison, because the autumn of 1598 left other dated paper against which it can be measured. On 22 July of that year, weeks before Meres's book was entered, The Merchant of Venice went into the same Register with a proviso that it must not be printed without licence first had from the Lord Chamberlain, a scrap of trade caution that independently confirms another of the twelve as a live Chamberlain's property that summer. The Register, the title pages, and the discourse interlock. Set against them the record of the rival Admiral's Men, whose manager Philip Henslowe kept a diary of payments, performances, and receipts that lets historians watch a repertory week by week. No Henslowe served the Lord Chamberlain's Men. For the company that owned the lovers' play, no diary, no account book, and no letter book survives from the 1590s, and the chronology of its greatest asset has to be hung on such nails as the trade and a Rutland rector happened to leave in the wall. Meres is the front anchor of the early canon not because he is a magnificent witness but because, for this company in this decade, he is very nearly the only dated one whose testimony covers the whole repertory at a stroke. Scarcity, as much as quality, made the page load-bearing.

One precision remains, and it is the precision that keeps this article inside its fence. For this play taken alone, Meres's wall is not even the earliest bound: the first quarto had put the tragedy into print in 1597, a year before the discourse, and a printed play is its own proof of existence. A dating argument for Romeo and Juliet that cited Meres as its outer bound would be using the second wall where the first stands closer. What the list adds, for this play beyond the quarto's bare existence, is everything the quarto withholds: the author's name, the company of the other eleven, the judgement of excellence in the kind, and the registration of the title in the republic of letters rather than the bookstall alone. The bound is the least of the page's gifts to this particular play, however central it is to the canon at large. That is why the article has treated the deadline as one section among many and the fame as the subject: for the lovers' tragedy, Meres is not primarily a date. He is the first reader whose response survives in the form of a filing, and the filing, not the date, is what a history of fame is made of.

## Love's Labour's Won: The List's Strangest Witness

The comedy column carries a title that exists nowhere else in the records of the age: Love's Labour's Won. No quarto of it was ever printed, no performance of it is recorded, no other writer mentions it, and it has no certain existence outside the eight syllables Meres gives it between Love's Labour's Lost and A Midsummer Night's Dream. Generations of scholarship have worried at the ghost. E. K. Chambers inclined to identify it with The Taming of the Shrew, a surviving play that Meres omits and that a doubled title might disguise. The recovery in 1953 of a fragmentary bookseller's list from 1603, the stock list of Christopher Hunt of Exeter, weakened that identification severely, since the fragment enters "loves labors won" and "the taming of a shrewe" as separate printed books, two titles in one inventory. The mainstream position since has been that Meres named a real play that has since been lost, one of the vanished majority of the Elizabethan repertory, preserved only because a compiler in Rutland wrote it down.

For the lovers' play, the ghost in the next column is the list's character reference. Consider what its presence excludes. A Meres who had built his roll of plays by copying the title pages of printed quartos could not have produced Love's Labour's Won, because no title page bore it. A Meres inventing titles to round his symmetry could have produced anything, but invention is an odd charge against a list whose other eleven entries all check out. The lost play is therefore positive evidence about the list's sources: its maker's information about the stage reached beyond the print shop, into the repertory itself, whether through London visits, correspondence, the talk of the book trade, or acquaintance in circles where new plays were news. A witness demonstrably informed about a play that print never touched is a witness whose naming of a printed play deserves credit. The tragedy list's final entry borrows credibility from the comedy list's ghost, and the borrowing is legitimate.

The ghost has attracted rival explanations, and their fortunes track the list's credit with unusual fidelity. Chambers's inclination toward The Taming of the Shrew was the great age's solution: it tidied the canon, explained an omission, and cost only the assumption that Meres had used a variant title. T. W. Baldwin, the American scholar who studied the lost play most intensively, likewise laboured to keep it inside the surviving canon. The bookseller's fragment recovered in 1953 cut the ground from both: a list of plays in print by August 1603, kept by Christopher Hunt of Exeter and preserved by chance in the binding waste of a later book, names "loves labors won" as a printed book in its own right, separate from "the taming of a shrewe" a few entries away. Identifications with other survivors, All's Well That Ends Well and Twelfth Night among them, have had advocates since, drawn by the antithetical title's suggestion of a sequel or a pendant. None has carried the field. The standing conclusion, that a real comedy of that name was acted, was known to a provincial compiler, and has vanished entire, is the most eloquent commentary the period offers on the fragility of theatrical fame. One title in twelve survives only here. The other eleven include the lovers' play, and the difference between its fate and the ghost's is, in the first instance, the difference print and reprinting made; but the ghost is the control case that proves the list was sampling a repertory much larger than its own survival rate.

The ghost also sets the list's information in its true scale. The Elizabethan stage produced plays by the hundred, and most are gone past recovery; a survey that can catch one of the vanished in passing is sampling a living repertory, not cataloguing a shelf. When Meres names Romeo and Juliet in such company, the naming reads as one item in a report on what the London stage then held, filed by a man whose beat, however unlikely his address, included plays that no stationer had yet commodified. Fame that reaches a Rutland study through repertory talk is still fame, and it is a different evidentiary species from fame inferred from a publisher's decision to print, since it registers recognition among people rather than investment by a firm.

## The Sugared Sonnets Among His Private Friends

The Ovid passage holds the discourse's other famous datum: Shakespeare's "sugared Sonnets among his private friends." In one subordinate clause, Meres records that sonnets by the author of the lovers' play were circulating in manuscript among a private circle a full decade before the sonnet quarto of 1609 put them into print. The remark is the earliest external evidence for the sonnets' existence, and it has anchored every account of their composition and transmission since. Its presence in the same discourse as the play list is not a digression from this article's subject but a reinforcement of it, because it completes the portrait of the reputation the page preserves. The fame documented in Palladis Tamia is a double fame: public, in the theatres and the bookstalls, where a tragedy could be acted by the Lord Chamberlain's servants and sold by Burby; and private, in manuscript, where poems passed hand to hand among friends whose names the record mostly withholds.

The two fames illuminate each other. A writer known only for plays might be a company servant whose work the stationers owned; a writer whose unpublished poems were already talked of in a clergyman's treasury was a literary figure, a name that carried value in circles that never bought a penny admission to the yard. When a reader collates such scattered witnesses, the scraps of the Meres dossier, the Register entry, the quarto title pages, the allusions that multiply after 1598, the value of a disciplined research notebook is plain, and many students of the play keep theirs in [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the documents can sit side by side until their pattern shows. The pattern here is that by 1598 the name Shakespeare signified in both economies at once. Romeo and Juliet, named in the public column of that double fame, partook of both: a stage success acquiring a literary address, filed in the same breath as sonnets that had never faced a printer.

How a rector in Rutland came to know of sonnets that their author had not printed is a fair question, and the record supplies a plausible channel rather than a mystery. Both of the narrative poems Meres exhibits in the Ovid passage, Venus and Adonis and Lucrece, were dedicated to Henry Wriothesley, Earl of Southampton, the most conspicuous literary patron of the young dramatist's circle. Meres's own brother-in-law, John Florio, served that circle as an Italian tutor and moved in the household world where such manuscripts passed from hand to hand. No document shows a sonnet travelling down that exact road, and this article claims no more than the geography: the compiler's marriage placed him one remove from the dedicatee of the poems he praises, in an age when a sheaf of sonnets among private friends was precisely the kind of object that moved along such removals. The clause's casualness helps it. A man retailing wonders might invent a marvel; a man noting, in a subordinate clause inside a comparison about Ovid, that the poems circulate privately is reporting the ordinary furniture of his information world, and furniture is rarely invented.

John Weever's epigram of the following year shows the pattern already repeating. In his Epigrammes of 1599, the young Cambridge poet addressed a sonnet "Ad Gulielmum Shakespeare," hailed its "honie-tong'd" subject in Meres's own vocabulary, and alluded to Romeo and Richard as lovers who burn, with a glance at the saints-and-pilgrims sonnet the lovers share at their first meeting, 1.5.92 to 109 in the Oxford text. Weever is a second witness, and a dependent one in his diction, which is itself a datum: within a year, Meres's phrases were becoming the standard small change of Shakespearean compliment. The first fame document was already functioning as the source from which later fame documents drew. That is what a record of early fame is for, and it is also the beginning of the danger, since a source that everyone copies stops being checked.

The market moved even faster than the complimenters. In 1599, the year after the discourse, the stationer William Jaggard issued The Passionate Pilgrim under Shakespeare's name, a miscellany that led with versions of two of the sonnets the discourse had reported in manuscript, padded out with verses by other hands. The little book is shabby and opportunistic, and it is also a receipt: by 1599 the name on a title page was worth stealing poems for, and the private circulation Meres had noted was already leaking into print under commercial pressure. The sequence from the discourse to the miscellany is the early fame in miniature. A clergyman registers that sonnets pass among friends; a publisher, reading the same market, prints what he can gather under the name; the name grows more valuable with each transaction. The lovers' play, filed in the discourse beside the sonnets, appreciated in the same portfolio, and neither the registrar nor the pirate had any reason to conspire in the valuation. Independent greed and independent registration agreeing on a price is as close as the book trade comes to an audited figure.

## The Page's Second Life: Schoolbook and Survival

Palladis Tamia had a commercial afterlife of its own, and the afterlife is a fame datum of the second order: the book that registered the play's reputation was itself reprinted for the classroom. In 1634, a generation after the first edition, the treasury was reissued in London under the Wits Commonwealth title as a treasury of divine, moral, and philosophical similes "more particularly for the use of schools," and again in 1636 in a further reprint. The discourse's roll call thus went on being read, in schoolrooms, while the play it names was passing through its own seventeenth-century editions and its Restoration adaptations. There is a pleasing recursion in the fact. The first fame document of Romeo and Juliet survived not because scholars treasured it, for its scholarly century had not yet come, but because schoolmasters found its sentences useful, which is to say because the commonplace economy that produced it kept it in circulation long enough for the antiquarians to arrive. Documents about fame have their own fame to survive, and this one's survival was as unglamorous and as effective as its testimony.

The physical survival matters to the argument in one more way. Because the book was a saleable, reprinted object rather than a unique manuscript, its testimony cannot be suspected of being a later interpolation into a private paper: the Register entry, the printed edition of 1598, and the reissues form a public chain that any sceptic can inspect. When the editors of the nineteenth century began quoting folio 282, they were quoting a page that had been in continuous public existence since the year of its writing. The chain also disciplines the one romantic error the document invites, the picture of a lone prophet crying up a neglected genius in the wilderness. Meres was not rescuing an unknown. He was selling a treasury whose entries presupposed the fame they recorded, and the book's own reprinting proves the presupposition was commercial common sense. The first fame of the lovers' play was ordinary enough to be a selling point in a schoolbook. That ordinariness, preserved in an unbroken chain of print, is the surest sign that the fame was real.

## What Meres Knew That Print Could Not Tell Him

Count the twelve against the print record of September 1598 and the list's independence sharpens. By the month of the Register entry, print had produced Titus Andronicus, Richard II, Richard III, the first instalment of Henry IV, Love's Labour's Lost, and, in 1597, the lovers' play itself. Six of the twelve, at the most generous count, had faced a compositor. The other six had not: The Two Gentlemen of Verona, The Comedy of Errors, and King John would wait for the Folio of 1623 for any printing at all; A Midsummer Night's Dream and The Merchant of Venice reached print in 1600; and Love's Labour's Won never reached it. Meres therefore names six plays that no reader of quartos could have assembled from title pages, and one of those six existed, so far as every other record shows, only on a stage and in the memories of its audiences. Whatever his failings as a Hellenist, the compiler of the discourse was plugged into theatrical intelligence of a currency that print alone cannot explain.

The thinness of the surrounding record throws the list's density into relief. Before Meres, print had noticed the dramatist only glancingly: Robert Greene's Groatsworth of 1592, published after Greene's death, warned three playwrights against an "upstart crow" beautified with their feathers and parodied a line from 3 Henry VI, "tiger's heart wrapped in a player's hide," which proves a playhouse line could be famous enough to travesty six years before the discourse; and William Covell's Polimanteia of 1595 praised "sweet Shakespeare" among the poets. Hostile pamphlet, passing compliment, and then, in 1598, a survey. The shelf of lifetime references to the dramatist is short enough that every item on it has been read to pieces, and the discourse is the shelf's only systematic item, the single place where a contemporary stands back and arranges the whole early output in order. That is the root of its magnification in scholarship. Documents that arrive in crowds can be cross-examined against each other; a solitary survey gets believed or doubted as a whole, and the fight over Meres's character, treated below, is the sound of four centuries cross-examining a witness who has no corroborating colleague of his own scale.

The lovers' play sits exactly on the seam between the two kinds of knowledge, and its position there is instructive. Meres could have known the title from [the first quarto of 1597, the play's first printed text](/2026/03/27/first-quarto-1597-bad-quarto/), which Burby's trade colleague John Danter had printed the year before; nothing in the list proves he saw the play acted rather than read. But the distinction, often pressed as a weakness, matters less than its pressers think. If he knew the play from its quarto, the list attests that the printed tragedy had travelled into a provincial study and registered there as one of the six tragedies that proved its author's excellence. If he knew it from the stage or from stage talk, the list attests the repertory fame that the quarto's own title page boasts of when it calls the play "an excellent conceited tragedy" often acted with great applause. Either route is a fame route. The document does not tell us which road the name travelled, and an honest reading says so; but both roads end at the same destination, a play recognizable far beyond the playhouse within a few years of its first performances.

### How does one publisher connect Meres's book and the play's second quarto?

Cuthbert Burby published both. He took Palladis Tamia into the Stationers' Register in September 1598 and issued the second quarto of the lovers' play, printed by Thomas Creede, in 1599, as he had issued the first named Shakespeare quarto, Love's Labour's Lost, in 1598. One stationer's list thus joins the first fame document to the play's standard early text.

The Burby connection also disciplines speculation about the list's sources. The discourse was not compiled in a vacuum sealed against the trade; its author's own book passed through the hands of the stationer most closely concerned, that year, in converting Shakespeare's stage work into print. No document shows Burby feeding titles to Meres, and this article claims none. But the shared imprint is a reminder that the worlds of the playhouse, the print shop, and the commonplace study were one small London world, in which a Rutland rector with a Florio marriage connection and a Paul's Churchyard publisher were separated by very few handshakes. The list's authority does not rest on Meres's genius, which was modest. It rests on his placement at a junction of the information network of 1598, and on the checkable accuracy of what the junction delivered.

## The Autumn of 1598: A Canon Caught Mid-Formation

The list's moment deserves a section of its own, because the autumn of 1598 catches the company, the author, and the canon at a hinge. The Lord Chamberlain's Men were between homes, working at the Curtain while the lease war over the Theatre, their old playhouse in Shoreditch, ground toward the famous solution of the following year, when the timbers would be carried across the river and reborn as the Globe. That autumn the company staged Ben Jonson's Every Man in His Humour, the hit that announced a new generation of comic writing, and the cast lists preserved in Jonson's folio place Shakespeare among its actors. The repertory Meres surveyed was therefore not a back catalogue but a working company's current stock, performed by men whose leading tragedian was Richard Burbage and whose clown was Will Kempe. When the discourse names twelve plays as the proofs of one author's excellence, it is taking a snapshot of the shelf from which that company drew its afternoons, at the last moment before the Globe, Hamlet, and the great Jacobean sequence redrew the picture entirely.

The hinge quality explains both the list's composition and its later usefulness. Everything in it belongs to the pre-Globe career: the early histories, the Roman experiment, the comedies of the mid-decade, the lost Won, and the lovers' tragedy standing for the lyric manner at its most finished. Everything the world now reads first in the canon, Much Ado About Nothing, Henry V, Julius Caesar, Twelfth Night, Hamlet, and the tragedies after them, lies on the far side of the page. A historian holding the discourse holds a dated photograph of the moment just before the explosion, and photographs derive their value from the sharpness of their edges. Because the page is dated to a month and its inclusions are checkable, it divides the canon into a before and an after with a precision no stylistic argument can match. The lovers' play stands at the photograph's edge, the most recent of the tragedies it contains on the usual composition estimates, the closing exhibit of the early career. Its position in the document thus performs one final service: it marks the point from which the fame that began in a commonplace book would be carried forward by the Globe's audiences, the Folio's editors, and four centuries of readers, none of whom needed a Rutland rector to tell them the play was famous, because the rector's page had started the filing that made the fame cumulative.

## The Omissions: What the List Leaves Out

The list is twelve plays long, and the author's output by the autumn of 1598 was not. Missing from the roll are the three plays on Henry VI and The Taming of the Shrew, works that modern chronology places in the early 1590s and that any complete census of the canon to that date must include. The counting inside the list is loose in the same instructive way. Meres enters "Henry the 4" as a single item, although the Henry IV matter already filled two plays, of which the first had been printed that year and the second was on the stage; a census taker would have written thirteen or fourteen where the compiler wrote twelve. The unit of the list is not the play as a bibliographer counts plays. It is the title as a reader remembers titles, massed by subject where subjects merge. A document whose arithmetic is mnemonic rather than archival will have edges exactly where memory and arrangement let them fall, which is where the omissions are. The omissions are not in dispute; their meaning is. One scholarly tradition treats the list as a repertory sketch shaped by the compiler's sources and his symmetry, in which case absence signifies nothing beyond the sketch's edges. Another reaches for explanations: the omitted plays were entangled with other companies' repertories, with Pembroke's Men and the wreckage of the plague years, and a list whose knowledge runs along Chamberlain's Men lines might simply not extend to them. A third, represented in Chambers's inclination about Love's Labour's Won, dissolves an omission by reassigning a title, supposing the Shrew present in disguise under the Won name, which the 1603 bookseller's fragment later made hard to hold.

The omissions have a forward edge as well as a backward one. Edward III, printed anonymously in 1596 and in modern times assigned in part to Shakespeare by a growing body of attribution study, is also absent, a further reminder that the compiler's horizon was his own reading and report rather than any archive. And the list stops exactly where the next act begins. Chambers observed that the earliest play not mentioned is probably Much Ado About Nothing, itself a work of 1598 on the usual dating, which suggests a survey closed just as the new season's work was arriving: the discourse catches the canon in the last instant at which its twelve could look like the story. Every subsequent landmark of the early fame, Weever's epigram in 1599, the second quarto's augmented text, the sonnet quarto of 1609, belongs to a record that thickens rapidly once the page has shown what a record looks like.

For this article's purpose the omissions carry one clear lesson and one warning. The lesson is that the list is a sample drawn by a man arranging a comparison, not an audit of the canon; its maker never promised completeness, and the shaped number twelve warns against expecting it. The warning concerns the logic of silence. If the list can omit Henry VI, plays certainly in existence, then it can omit anything, and an argument that treats absence from Meres as evidence of non-existence builds on sand. Yet exactly that argument has been made, famously, about Julius Caesar: the inference, classic since the nineteenth-century editors and repeated by M. W. MacCallum, that so typical a tragedy would certainly have been named had it existed, so that its absence is "nearly proof positive" of a date after 1598. The Caesar dating happens to be supportable on other grounds, including Thomas Platter's diary of a tragedy of Julius Caesar seen in September 1599. The Meres limb of the argument, however, is the weak limb, and its long respectability shows how easily a good document's misuse becomes tradition. Inclusion in the list is testimony; exclusion is weather. The lovers' play benefits from the strong direction of the logic and is owed honesty about the weak one, since the same page cannot be a perfect register for what it names and a perfect register for what it does not.

## Compiler or Critic? The Fight Over Meres's Authority

Every generation of scholars has had to decide what Meres was, because the value of the list rides on the character of its maker, and the decisions have swung between two portraits. In the first, Meres is the earliest Shakespeare critic: an acute, learned surveyor whose comparisons preserve a contemporary's informed judgement of the field, the man who saw, while the ink was wet, that the author of the lovers' play stood first among English dramatists in both kinds. In the second, he is a harmless drudge of the commonplace industry, a retailer of other men's sentences whose discourse is a patchwork, whose classical learning was borrowed plumage, and whose elevation into a critic is a later construction by scholars who needed a witness and gilded the one they had. The disagreement is genuine, central to the subject, and adjudicated here rather than merely displayed.

The case for the drudge was made with most learning by Don Cameron Allen, who edited Meres's critical writing in the 1930s and used the edition to prosecute its author. Allen showed that the Comparative Discourse is stitched together from unacknowledged borrowings, that its classical parallels descend through intermediate compilations rather than from any reading of the ancients, and that the inflated estimates of Meres's acuteness were partisan work: scholars desirous of magnifying Shakespeare, Sidney, Spenser, and Jonson had laboured, in Allen's account, to magnify the critic who praised them all. The demonstration stands. Anyone who reads the discourse with its sources open beside it can watch the stitching happen, and Jason Scott-Warren's modern study of Meres's commonplacing, which identifies further sources such as Jean Dadré's Loci communes of 1577 and examines the annotated books of the compiler's own library, confirms the method while refusing Allen's contempt. In the culture of the commonplace book, Scott-Warren's Meres is not a fraud but a practitioner: recycling was the period's technology of thought, and originality lived in selection and arrangement.

The inflation Allen prosecuted had a literal form that deserves recording, because it shows what the evidence was up against. Admirers of the discourse, needing its witness to be weightier, had promoted its author into a professor at Oxford or at Cambridge, a chair he never held: the title page's modest boast, master of arts of both universities, describes degrees, not posts, and the schoolmaster of Wing was a parish clergyman with a commonplace book, not a don delivering judgement from a lectern. Allen's catalogue of the epithets is its own small comedy of need: acute, keen-witted, a learned and thoughtful writer, a writer of genius on one side; naive, childish, a mere compiler, a dullard on the other. Both portraits are cartoons drawn to serve a verdict about the plays. The documentary truth is plainer and, for the fame question, better than either: a literate provincial professional, plugged into the book trade and the patronage grapevine, arranging what he knew for sale to other literate professionals. Demote him from professor and he loses nothing that matters, because his testimony was never professorial. It was subscriptional: he wrote down what his market already believed.

The case for the witness rests on the record's behaviour under testing. E. K. Chambers, the great literary historian of the Elizabethan stage, built the list into the architecture of his chronology in The Elizabethan Stage and in William Shakespeare: A Study of Facts and Problems, treating its inclusions as fixed points in a structure that has largely held. Lukas Erne, in Shakespeare as Literary Dramatist, drew the further conclusion that a reputation registered in a printed treasury by 1598 is a literary reputation, evidence that the dramatist was already an author in the eyes of the reading culture and not merely a provider of scripts to a company. Between Allen's demolition of the critic and Erne's construction on the witness, the adjudication this article defends is a split verdict with the weight on the witness side. Allen wins about the discourse as criticism: its judgements are conventional, its learning secondhand, its superlatives the small coin of the comparative form, and no argument about the quality of Romeo and Juliet should be rested on Meres's taste. But the fame question was never a question about taste. It is a question about circulation, and for circulation a compiler is not a compromised witness but the ideal instrument, since a commonplace book can only retail what has already travelled. The patchwork that disqualifies Meres as a critic qualifies him as a registrar: the list is valuable not because a discerning man judged the play excellent, but because an ordinary well-connected reader already knew its name, its author, and its kind, and expected his own readers to know them too. The one correction Erne's reading needs is scope: the reputation the page proves is a reputation in the literate market, among book buyers and sentence gatherers, and it should not be silently converted into applause in the yard.

Erne's construction deserves one more turn of the screw, because its strongest form contains a distinction that cuts against its careless users. The discourse is, by title and by frame, a survey of poets: its business is poetry, its comparisons are poetic, and its Shakespeare is crowned within a history of verse. A sceptic of the literary reading can therefore object that Meres registers the plays only as a poet's by-products, evidence in a case about a maker of poems, and that no conclusion about a reading public for drama follows. The objection is the best one the compilation school has, and it should be conceded in its letter: the discourse proves that a poet's plays were famous, in a book about poetry, which is not quite the same thing as proving the plays were read as literature. The concession, however, leaves the fame argument standing, because fame is indifferent to the shelf it is filed on. A tragedy does not need to be registered by a drama critic to be registered; it needs to be known, by name, to people who were not in the yard when it played. Meres's readers met the lovers' play as an item in the bibliography of the age's most excellent maker of both kinds. However the meeting is categorized, it happened, in print, in 1598, and the categorical scruples of later scholarship do not unhappen it. The split verdict therefore refines itself once more: the page proves a literary reputation for the author and a registered fame for the play, and the second of those is the one this article set out to weigh.

### What would count as stronger fame evidence than a list?

Stronger evidence would be testimony tied to performance itself: a diarist recording a night at the play, a letter describing an audience's response, court accounts of a command performance, or the company's own repertory records. Such documents let a historian measure response rather than registration; none survives for the lovers' play in its first years.

That ladder of evidence also explains the list's peculiar durability in scholarship. It is not the best imaginable witness to the play's first fame; it is the best surviving one, and the distinction should be kept in view whenever the page is quoted. A diary in the manner of the Swiss visitor Thomas Platter, who recorded plays he saw in London in 1599, could have told posterity how the tragedy played. No Platter went to Verona's story, or none wrote it down. What went down instead was a title in a treasury, and historians have been reading that title with a magnifying glass ever since, which is a reasonable response to scarcity provided the magnification is confessed.

## How Scholarship Has Used and Overused One Page

The legitimate uses come first, because they are the reason the page is canonical. Chambers made the list the front anchor of the early chronology, the fixed point against which quarto dates, allusions, and stylistic sequences could be arranged, and the arrangement has survived a century of testing in its essentials. Editors of the play, Levenson and Gibbons among them, cite the Register entry as the bound that disciplines every other dating argument, the wall that no theory may pass through. Historians of the sonnets rest the early circulation of the poems on the same page's subordinate clause. Historians of the lost repertory cite the comedy column for a play that exists nowhere else. In each use, the document is asked for the kind of fact it is competent to give: existence, attribution, circulation, a name in a dated place. Competent use has made the page one of the half-dozen most load-bearing documents in the study of the early canon.

The page also acquired a second career as a monument of criticism, and that career fed the very inflation Allen later fought. Joseph Haslewood reprinted the discourse in his Ancient Critical Essays in 1811, and G. Gregory Smith's Elizabethan Critical Essays of 1904 installed it at the head of the English critical tradition, where anthologies have kept it since: the first sustained survey of English poets, the birthplace of Shakespeare criticism, the moment the nation began to judge its writers. There is truth in the installation, since nothing earlier attempts the survey's scope, but it quietly changed the document's job description. A passage compiled as one section of a sentence treasury became, by anthology, a founding critical essay, and founding essays are read for their judgements. Read for its judgements, the page disappoints or delights according to taste, and the fight over Meres's acuteness begins. Read for its registrations, which is the reading this article has defended, the page simply does its work. The anthologists were not wrong to preserve it. They were wrong only in the frame, and frames are how overuse starts.

A third career ran alongside the anthology one, in the allusion books. When Clement Ingleby assembled The Shakespeare Centurie of Prayse for the New Shakspere Society in 1875, gathering a hundred years of contemporary notices of the dramatist, the discourse's Shakespeare passages stood near the head of the collection as its most substantial early item, the exhibit around which Greene's hostility, Covell's compliment, and Weever's echo arrange themselves as lesser satellites. The allusion-book format taught generations of readers to meet Meres as a witness in a dossier, quoted in extracts, headnoted, and cross-referenced, which is the healthiest of the page's afterlives and the one this article has followed. A witness in a dossier is neither a prophet nor a drudge. He is a deponent, and deponents are read for what they saw. What this deponent saw, or had reliably heard, by the autumn of 1598 was a stage on which a tragedy of two Veronese lovers was current enough, and credited enough to one named maker, to close the tragedy column of the age's most ambitious survey of its own literature.

The overuses are the shadow of the same virtues. The first is the slide from bound to date: handbooks that report the play as written by 1598 and let the phrasing ripen, in student essays and programme notes, into written in 1598, converting a wall into a birthday. The second is the slide from inclusion to review: the list's presence in the tragedy column retold as though Meres had singled the play out for praise, sometimes with the honey of the Ovid passage smeared across it, manufacturing a rave from a roll call. The third is the argument from silence, examined above, in which the omissions are made to testify against plays the compiler never undertook to census. The fourth is subtler and concerns attribution itself. The list's authority became so great that stationers traded on it: when The Troublesome Reign of King John was reissued in 1611 and again in 1622, its title pages acquired first the initials and then the full name of Shakespeare, an attribution modern scholarship rejects for that older play and which the presence of a King John in Meres's roll plausibly encouraged, since the list's King John and the old play were easy to confuse. A document strong enough to fix a canon is strong enough to bend attributions outside it, and the bending is part of the page's real history.

The pattern across all four overuses is the same: a fact of registration promoted, step by step, into a fact of a stronger kind. The corrective is not scepticism about the page, which has earned its credit, but grammar: attention to what its sentences actually assert. Meres asserts that in 1598 a knowledgeable compiler could exhibit twelve plays, by one named author, as proof of excellence in comedy and tragedy, and expect the exhibition to persuade. Scholarship at its best has always come back to that assertion and no further, and the play's early fame, which is the subject here, needs nothing further. A tragedy that can serve as the closing exhibit in such an argument, within a few years of its first performances, was famous in the only sense the sixteenth century could file and the twenty-first can verify.

## Verdict: The Value of a Single Page

The verdict, stated at the outset and now argued, can be given its final shape. Meres's list is the first fame document of Romeo and Juliet: the earliest surviving record in which the play appears not as a commodity on its own title page but as a fact in somebody else's book, named, attributed, and ranked by kind among the works of the most excellent English dramatist of the age. Its value is highest where its competence lies. It closes the question of the play's existence by 1598, it opens the documentary history of the play's attribution to Shakespeare, and it registers a reputation that had escaped the playhouse into the hands of readers who dealt in sentences. Its value is lowest where later need has pressed hardest: it does not date the composition, does not identify the text, does not review the play, and does not hear the audience. A single mention is a thin thread, and the brief for this article named the thinness as its complication. The thread holds because the mention is early, dated, disinterested, and checkable, and because everything the record has since produced, the second quarto's boasts, Weever's echoing compliment, the gathering allusions of the new century, hangs consistently from it. The play's first fame was not a bonfire. It was a line in a ledger, and the ledger has outlasted every bonfire since.

The verdict is corrigible, and its corrigibility should be stated as plainly as its confidence. A discovered Revels account, a dated letter, a diarist's page from the middle 1590s could demote the discourse tomorrow from first witness to second, and the demotion would cost the play nothing and this article only a superlative. What no plausible discovery can do is promote any rival above it on the evidence that exists, or convert its roll call into a review after the fact, or make its silences testify. Documents earn their authority by surviving audits, and this one has survived the audits of Chambers, who built on it, of Allen, who prosecuted its maker, and of the commonplace scholarship that reframed its method. The fame of Romeo and Juliet in its own decade is a small, hard fact with a name and a date attached: Francis Meres, September 1598, one line at the end of a list of tragedies. Everything larger that the play became was built, in part, on the accumulations that line began. That is the whole of the claim, and it is enough: every later age's easy certainty that the play was famous from the first rests, at the bottom of the documentary pile, on registrations of this modest kind, and of those registrations this single line, written by a stranger to the playhouse within a few years of the first performances, is the first to survive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Meres note, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Meres note is the table in this article that separates, item by item, what Francis Meres's mention of the play establishes from what it leaves open. The pattern it exposes is consistent across the whole record: the page is strong on existence, attribution, and circulation, and silent on composition dates, texts, rankings, and audiences. Every overuse of the document in later scholarship turns out, on inspection, to be a crossing of that line, in which a fact of registration has been promoted into a fact of a stronger kind than a list can supply.

### Q: Who was Francis Meres, and what is Palladis Tamia?

Francis Meres was a Lincolnshire clergyman and schoolmaster, born in 1565, a master of arts of both Cambridge and Oxford, later rector of Wing in Rutland, and a translator of devotional prose connected by marriage to John Florio. Palladis Tamia: Wits Treasury, printed by P. Short for Cuthbert Burby and entered in the Stationers' Register on 7 September 1598, is his commonplace treasury of sentences and similes, issued as the second part of Wits Commonwealth. Its Comparative Discourse of English poets against the Greek, Latin, and Italian poets contains the first critical survey to name Shakespeare's plays.

### Q: What exactly does Meres say about Romeo and Juliet?

Meres says nothing about the play's plot, characters, or quality; he names its title once, as the last of six tragedies exhibited to prove its author's excellence in the kind. The sentence runs that as Plautus and Seneca are accounted the best for comedy and tragedy among the Latins, so Shakespeare among the English is the most excellent in both kinds for the stage, and for tragedy it offers as witness "his Richard the 2, Richard the 3, Henry the 4, King John, Titus Andronicus, and his Romeo and Juliet." The play is evidence in an argument, not the subject of a review.

### Q: Is Meres's book the first surviving mention of the play by name?

Yes, with one careful qualification: it is the first surviving mention of the play by name in any document other than the play's own early editions. The first quarto of 1597 had already printed the tragedy itself under its title, so the play existed in print before Meres wrote. What Palladis Tamia adds is the first external registration: the title recorded in somebody else's book, attached to an author's name, and offered to readers as a known quantity. No letter, diary, account book, or pamphlet earlier than Meres's page that names the play has ever been found.

### Q: Did Meres praise the play itself, or its author and his poems?

The praise in the discourse attaches to the author and to his poems, not to the play. The famous compliment, that the soul of Ovid lives in "mellifluous and honey-tongued Shakespeare," is witnessed by Venus and Adonis, Lucrece, and the sugared sonnets; the tragedy is nowhere in that sentence. A second compliment, to Shakespeare's "fine-filed phrase," likewise names no work. The play's own treatment is colder and stronger: inclusion as an exhibit of excellence in tragedy. Conflating the honey-tongued passage with the list manufactures a review of the play that Meres never wrote.

### Q: Which plays stand beside the lovers' tragedy in Meres's tragedy list?

Five plays stand beside it: Richard II, Richard III, Henry IV, King John, and Titus Andronicus. Four are histories of English kings, and one is a Roman revenge tragedy in the Senecan line, which makes the lovers' play the list's only private love tragedy and its only Italian story. Its selection as the closing exhibit is therefore a choice with information in it: the compiler, needing six tragedies to prove range as well as excellence, ended the column with the play that showed the author's kind stretched furthest from chronicle and revenge toward novella, lyric, and domestic catastrophe.

### Q: Why does the little word "his" before the play's title matter?

The possessive matters because it is the play's first printed attribution to Shakespeare. Neither the first quarto of 1597 nor the second of 1599 names an author; both title pages credit the acting company, the Lord Hunsdon's servants and then the Lord Chamberlain's servants. Meres's roll call, by contrast, is built of possessives, his Gentlemen of Verona, his Errors, his Romeo and Juliet, and it appeared in a critical survey by a writer with no playhouse interest. The play's own books would not carry the author's name until the third quarto of 1609.

### Q: What is Love's Labour's Won, and why does its presence strengthen the list?

Love's Labour's Won is a comedy title that Meres lists between Love's Labour's Lost and A Midsummer Night's Dream and that no other document of the age records; the mainstream view holds it a real play now lost, after a bookseller's list of 1603, recovered in 1953, entered it separately from The Taming of the Shrew. Its presence strengthens the play list because a compiler copying printed title pages could not have invented it: no title page bore the name. The ghost title proves the list's information reached into the repertory beyond print.

### Q: What does Meres reveal about Shakespeare's sonnets?

In the Ovid comparison, Meres lets fall that Shakespeare's "sugared Sonnets" were passing "among his private friends," which is the earliest external evidence that the sonnets existed and were circulating in manuscript. The remark fixes 1598 as the outer bound for a body of the poems, a decade before the sonnet quarto of 1609, and it shows the compiler's intelligence running through private as well as public channels. For the play's fame the clause matters as context: the name Meres attaches to the tragedy already carried a private, manuscript reputation beside its public one.

### Q: Which plays in the list had never been printed when Meres named them?

Six of the twelve had never been printed: The Two Gentlemen of Verona, The Comedy of Errors, and King John, none of which reached print until the Folio of 1623; A Midsummer Night's Dream and The Merchant of Venice, printed in 1600; and Love's Labour's Won, which was never printed at all. Only Titus Andronicus, the two Richards, the first part of Henry IV, Love's Labour's Lost, and the lovers' play itself had faced a compositor. A list half built of unprinted plays cannot be a catalogue of books, which is the point on which its independence as testimony rests.

### Q: Which existing plays are missing from the list, and what follows from that?

The three Henry VI plays and The Taming of the Shrew are missing, although modern chronology places all four in the early 1590s, years before the discourse was compiled. What follows is a limit on the list's logic rather than a scandal in it: the roll is a shaped selection of twelve, six and six, built to fill a comparison, and its maker never promised a census. Because plays certainly in existence can be absent, absence from the list can never prove that a play did not yet exist, and arguments built on its silence start from a premise the document itself refutes.

### Q: Does the order of Meres's tragedy list rank the plays?

No ranking should be read into the order. Meres nowhere grades the works inside his lists, and the sequence of the tragedies obeys the discourse's habits of association and symmetry rather than any scale of merit: the histories mass together, the Roman play follows, and the Italian love tragedy closes the column as the kind's most different specimen. Nor is the order a reliable chronology of composition. The closing position of the lovers' play has given it prominence in the document's afterlife, since excerptors and readers meet it last, but prominence of placement is not a verdict of preference.

### Q: What does the book's own Stationers' Register entry establish?

The entry of Palladis Tamia in the Stationers' Register on 7 September 1598 establishes the date from which the list's testimony runs: the book was in the trade's hands by that autumn, so everything it names already existed to be named. It also names the book's publisher, Cuthbert Burby, the stationer who issued Love's Labour's Lost that year and the second quarto of the lovers' play the next. A Register entry is a trade record, not a review or a diary, and its precision is exactly the precision a bound requires: dated, public, and indifferent to the contents it timestamps.

### Q: How did E. K. Chambers use Meres's list in his history of the stage?

E. K. Chambers, in The Elizabethan Stage and in William Shakespeare: A Study of Facts and Problems, built the list into the load-bearing architecture of the early chronology. Its inclusions became fixed points: twelve plays certified as existing by September 1598, around which quarto dates, allusions, and stylistic arguments could be arranged and tested. Chambers also inclined to identify the mysterious Love's Labour's Won with The Taming of the Shrew, dissolving one of the list's omissions by renaming, a position the bookseller's fragment recovered in 1953 later made difficult to hold. His framework for the list's inclusions has largely endured.

### Q: Why did Don Cameron Allen distrust Meres as a critic?

Don Cameron Allen, editing Meres's critical writing in the 1930s, demonstrated that the Comparative Discourse is stitched from unacknowledged borrowings and that its classical parallels descend through intermediate compilations rather than from firsthand reading of the ancients. He concluded that Meres was a compiler of modest learning and conventional judgement, and that the grander estimates of his acuteness were partisan inflation by scholars eager to magnify the authors Meres praised. The demonstration of the borrowing stands; the inference that a borrower cannot register fame is the step later readers of the commonplace culture have declined to take with him.

### Q: Can a compiler of other men's sentences be a reliable witness to fame?

For fame, a compiler is arguably the ideal witness, because a commonplace book can only retail what has already travelled. Originality in that culture lived in selection and arrangement, as Jason Scott-Warren's study of Meres's commonplacing shows, and the selection here is contemporary and checkable: eleven of its twelve plays are independently documented, and the twelfth is a lost play no title page could have supplied. The compiler's weakness as a critic, his conventional taste and secondhand learning, barely touches his strength as a registrar of what a well-connected reader in 1598 already knew by name.

### Q: What did John Weever's epigram add to the record a year later?

John Weever's Epigrammes of 1599 added a second, younger voice, and showed Meres's language already becoming the standard currency of compliment. His sonnet addressed "Ad Gulielmum Shakespeare" hails a "honie-tong'd" poet in the discourse's own epithet and alludes to Romeo and Richard as lovers who burn, with a glance at the saints-and-pilgrims sonnet the lovers exchange at their first meeting. Weever confirms the direction of travel, from registration toward allusion, but his dependence on Meres's phrasing also warns that early witnesses may copy one another, and copying is not corroboration of the same weight.

### Q: Did Meres know the plays from the stage or from print?

The document does not say, and both routes remain open for the lovers' play, which existed in a printed quarto from 1597. For the list as a whole, print alone cannot explain it, since half its titles were unprinted and one was never printed. Stage talk, repertory report, and book-trade intelligence must have fed the compiler, directly or at a remove. For the fame question the undecided route matters less than it first appears: a title known from a quarto in a provincial study and a title known from the stage are both titles in circulation, and circulation is what the list attests.

### Q: What can the list never tell us about the play's first audiences?

The list can tell us nothing about the size, composition, behaviour, or response of the audiences who first saw the tragedy. It records one reader's registration of a title, not a crowd's experience of a performance: no receipts, no diary of a night at the Theatre or the Curtain, no letter describing applause or tears survives from the play's first years, and Meres was not writing that kind of document. His page proves the play was known in the literate market. The leap from known to beloved, or from registered to triumphant, is an inference later ages supply at their own risk.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Meres note to argue the play's early fame?

An essay should cite the note to keep its claims at the strength the document supports: the play existed by September 1598, was attributed to Shakespeare in print by a disinterested contemporary, and stood among the six tragedies exhibited as proof of his excellence. Argued at that strength, the early-fame case is unanswerable. The essay should then name what it is not claiming: no composition year, no review, no ranking, no audience. Precision of that kind is not a weakening of the argument but its distinction, since the mention survives every audit that the vaguer versions of it fail.
