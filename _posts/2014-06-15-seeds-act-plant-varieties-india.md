Every bag of seed sold in India raises two entirely different legal questions, and Indian law answers them in two entirely different statutes that public discussion persistently confuses. The first question, the one the Seeds Act 1966 exists to answer, is whether the seed is any good: whether it will germinate, whether it is what the label claims, whether the farmer who buys it in the planting season is being sold the variety named on the packet or a substitute that will fail in the field when it is too late to do anything about the failure. The second question is whose the variety is: whether the genetic material inside the seed belongs, in the eyes of the law, to a breeder who spent years developing it, to the farming communities that selected and conserved its ancestors over generations, or to no one at all. The Seeds Act 1966 answers the first question and only the first. The Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 answers the second, and answers it in a way no other country has attempted.

The argument of this article is that the 2001 Act is a genuine conceptual innovation with a permanent internal tension built into it, and that it cannot be understood by reading either of its two rights in isolation. The statute grants a breeder an exclusive right over a registered variety, of the kind that intellectual property law grants over an invention or a book, and in the same statute it grants every farmer the right to save seed of that protected variety, sow it, resow it, exchange it, share it and sell it, subject to a single restriction about branded seed. The value of the breeder's right depends on how much of the market the farmers' exemption occupies, and the farmers' exemption is not a grudging exception carved out of the right but a co-equal grant stated in the statute's own non obstante clause. Where the boundary between the two grants actually sits has been worked out in one high-profile dispute about a potato, and that dispute, followed carefully, teaches more about the statute than the text alone does.

The confusion between the two statutes matters practically and not merely taxonomically. A farmer who saves seed of a protected variety and sells the harvest as grain commits no legal wrong at all, yet the belief that seed saving infringes the breeder's right is widespread enough to have shaped real litigation and real fear in real villages. A seed dealer who sells substandard seed of a notified kind faces a quality prosecution under a statute passed in 1966, with penalties that were modest when enacted and are derisory decades later, yet accounts of seed regulation regularly describe the trade as governed by a modern registration law that Parliament has in fact never passed. A third regime, covering genetically modified crops, is governed by neither statute, and the public argument about seed sovereignty constantly imports assumptions from that separate debate into this one. Separating the regimes is the necessary first step, and this article keeps them separate throughout.

## Two statutes, two different questions

The Seeds Act 1966 describes itself, in its own preamble, as an Act to provide for regulating the quality of certain seeds for sale, and that modest description is exact. The Act is a consumer protection and market regulation statute for a physical commodity. It empowers the Central Government to notify kinds and varieties of seed for the purposes of the Act, prescribes minimum limits of germination and purity for notified seed, requires that seed of a notified kind offered for sale carry a truthful label with prescribed particulars, establishes laboratories in which samples can be tested, creates inspectors who can draw those samples, and provides a system of voluntary certification under which seed produced under supervision is marked as certified. It says nothing, anywhere, about who owns a variety, who bred it, or who may multiply it. A variety can be the subject of a breeder's exclusive right under the 2001 Act and simultaneously be notified seed under the 1966 Act, and the two facts have nothing to do with each other.

The Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001, Act 53 of 2001, which received the assent of the President on 30 October 2001, is by contrast an intellectual property statute through and through. Its long title states its purposes: to establish an effective system for the protection of plant varieties, the rights of farmers and plant breeders, and to encourage the development of new varieties of plants. Its preamble records the reasoning. India had ratified the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights, and Article 27.3(b) of that agreement required members to protect plant varieties either by patents, or by an effective sui generis system, or by a combination of the two. The preamble also records the domestic reasoning that ran alongside the treaty obligation: that it was necessary to recognise and protect the rights of farmers in respect of their contribution, made at any time, in conserving, improving and making available plant genetic resources, and to protect plant breeders' rights in order to stimulate investment in research and development in both the public and the private sector.

The two statutes therefore differ in every dimension that matters. They differ in age by thirty-five years and in intellectual ancestry by more: the 1966 Act belongs to the Green Revolution's project of getting improved planting material into fields at scale, while the 2001 Act belongs to the era of trade law and intellectual property. They differ in their regulatory technique, the older statute working through notification, standards and inspection of a physical product, the newer through registration of an intangible right and civil enforcement of it. They differ in their defendants: a prosecution under the Seeds Act is typically brought against a dealer or producer who sold seed that failed a standard or lacked a proper label, while an infringement action under the 2001 Act is brought against a person who produces or markets a protected variety without authority. And they differ in what compliance looks like, because compliance with the Seeds Act is demonstrated by a label and a test report, while compliance with the 2001 Act is demonstrated by staying off someone else's registered variety or by holding a licence.

One further statute-adjacent instrument completes the quality side of the picture, and it is the instrument through which the seed trade is actually controlled day to day. The Seed (Control) Order 1983, made under section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act 1955, requires a person carrying on the business of selling, exporting or importing seed to hold a licence, subjects that licence to conditions, and empowers controllers and inspectors to police the trade. The 1966 Act sets the standards; the control order licenses the sellers. Any account of Indian seed law that stops at the 1966 Act misses the licensing layer through which most enforcement contact between the state and the seed trade actually occurs.

## What existed before each statute

Before 1966, the seed that most Indian farmers sowed came from their own previous harvest or from a neighbour's, and the market in purchased seed was small, local and governed by little more than general contract and the Indian Penal Code's provisions on cheating. The Green Revolution changed the economics of that informality. The high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice introduced in the mid-1960s produced their yields only when the planting material was genetically true and vigorous, and a farmer who bought degenerated or mixed seed lost the entire advantage the new varieties promised. The state responded by building a production and distribution infrastructure, including the National Seeds Corporation established in 1963 to produce foundation and certified seed, and by legislating. The Seeds Act 1966, Act 54 of 1966, which received assent on 29 December 1966, supplied the legal framework for that infrastructure: a statutory committee to advise on standards, laboratories to test, and a certification system to guarantee the chain from breeder seed to the bag sold in the market. The Seeds Rules followed in 1968, and systematic seed certification began at the end of that decade.

Before 2001, a plant variety as such had no legal owner in India and could have none. The Patents Act 1970 excluded plants from patentability, as it still does, and no registration system for varieties existed. A public research institution that bred a successful variety released it into the public domain, where anyone could multiply and sell it; the breeder's reward was institutional, in budgets and professional standing, not proprietary. A private breeder had no appropriability mechanism at all beyond the biological protection that hybrid seed provides by its nature, since the progeny of a hybrid does not breed true and the farmer must return to the seller each season. That gap was tolerable while Indian plant breeding was dominated by public institutions, including the Indian Council of Agricultural Research and the state agricultural universities. It became a live policy problem in the 1990s, when trade commitments made some form of plant variety protection obligatory, when private seed companies were expanding, and when the question of what farmers themselves had contributed to the genetic stock became politically impossible to ignore.

The two statutes thus grew out of two different failures. The 1966 Act answered the failure of an unregulated market to guarantee that seed was what it claimed to be. The 2001 Act answered the absence of any legal recognition, in either direction, of the interests bound up in a variety: neither the breeder who developed it nor the farming communities whose selections over centuries had produced the material from which it was developed held any right the law would recognise. The second statute's originality lies in its refusal to choose between those two unrecognised interests.

## The Seeds Act 1966: a quality statute and nothing more

The architecture of the Seeds Act is compact, twenty-five sections in all, and its logic is a chain. At the head of the chain sits the Central Seed Committee, constituted under section 3 to advise the Central and State Governments on matters arising out of the administration of the Act and to carry out the functions assigned to it. Section 4 provides for a Central Seed Laboratory and State Seed Laboratories, the testing infrastructure on which the Act's promises depend, because a germination standard without a laboratory to measure it is only words. The Seeds (Amendment) Act 1972 added the certification superstructure, inserting sections 8A to 8E, which establish the Central Seed Certification Board and associated committees to coordinate certification across states whose certification agencies do the field-level work.

The operative trigger of the whole Act is notification under section 5. The Central Government may, after consultation with the Committee, notify any kind or variety of seed to be a notified kind or variety for the purposes of the Act, for the whole of India or for specified areas, if it considers notification necessary or expedient for the purpose of regulating the quality of seed of that kind or variety to be sold for purposes of agriculture. Everything else in the Act hangs off that notification. Seed of kinds and varieties that have not been notified sits outside the Act's operative provisions altogether, which is the first and most consequential limit on the statute's reach: its quality discipline applies to the notified list, not to the seed trade as a whole.

Section 6 carries the standard-setting power. On the recommendation of the Committee, the Central Government may specify the minimum limits of germination and purity with respect to any notified seed, and the particulars with which the mark or label on a container of such seed must comply. Section 7 then regulates the sale itself. No person may sell, keep for sale, offer to sell, barter or otherwise supply seed of a notified kind or variety unless the seed is identifiable as to its kind or variety, conforms to the minimum limits of germination and purity specified under section 6, and bears a mark or label showing the correct particulars in the prescribed manner. The label is the statute's central technology. In a market where the buyer cannot test what he is buying and where the defect reveals itself only months later in a failed stand of crop, the mandatory truthful label converts an unverifiable promise into a prosecutable statement: if the seed does not match the label, or the label is missing or false, the offence is complete without waiting for the harvest.

### Does the Seeds Act 1966 give anyone ownership of a variety?

No. The Act confers no proprietary right on any person in any variety. It regulates the quality of seed offered for sale, through notification, minimum standards of germination and purity, and compulsory truthful labelling of notified seed. Rights in a variety, where they exist at all, arise only under the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001.

That negative is worth dwelling on because it is the single most common confusion in writing about Indian seed law. Notification under section 5 is sometimes described in trade literature as a kind of approval or registration of the variety by the government, and in a functional sense it is a gate: a variety that is not notified cannot be sold as seed within the Act's framework, and the notification system is the route by which publicly bred varieties are released for multiplication and sale. But a gate is not a title. Notification creates no exclusivity, prevents no competitor from selling seed of the same variety, and gives the breeder of a notified variety no claim against anyone. The variety release system operated through the Central and State variety release committees, which recommend notification for varieties bred in the public system, is an administrative quality and agronomy process, not an intellectual property register. The 2001 Act's register, discussed later in this article, is the only place in Indian law where a variety becomes someone's property.

The Act's coverage is also worth stating precisely, because its definition of seed is functional and wide. Seed under the Act means the seed of food crops, of oil crops, cotton seed, seeds of cattle fodder, and all types of vegetative propagating material used for sowing or planting purposes. The definition matters for crops like potato, where the planting material is a tuber rather than a botanical seed, and for sugarcane and other vegetatively propagated crops. A statute about quality of botanical seed alone would have missed much of Indian agriculture; the 1966 draftsmen avoided that error, and the potato at the centre of the great plant variety dispute later in this article is planting material squarely within the older statute's conception of seed even though the dispute itself arose under the newer one.

## Certification, inspection and the enforcement machinery

Sections 8 to 11 of the Seeds Act build the certification system. Section 8 empowers the State Government, or the Central Government in relation to Union territories, to establish a certification agency for a state by notification, charged with carrying out the functions entrusted to certification agencies under the Act. Section 9 empowers the agency to grant a certificate for seed of a notified kind or variety where it is satisfied that the seed conforms to the prescribed standards. Section 10 empowers it to revoke a certificate granted through misrepresentation or where the holder has failed without reasonable excuse to comply with the conditions of the certificate or has contravened the Act or the rules. Section 11 provides an appeal for a person aggrieved by a decision of the certification agency, to such authority and within such time as may be prescribed.

The certification system works through generations of multiplication. Breeder seed, produced under the supervision of the breeder or the breeding institution, is multiplied into foundation seed, and foundation seed into certified seed, with field inspection and laboratory testing at each stage to preserve genetic purity and germination. Certified seed carries the certification tag that is the system's public face. The economics of the system explain both its design and its limits. Certification is a service someone must pay for, and the inspection and testing chain adds cost at each generation, so certified seed is dearer than uncertified seed of the same variety. A compulsory certification regime would have imposed that cost on every bag sold in the country and required an inspection establishment of a size no state possessed. The draftsmen of 1966 therefore made certification voluntary and labelling compulsory, a deliberate allocation: the state's coercive power enforces truth in the market's statements, while the stronger guarantee of certification is available to those who choose to buy it.

### Is seed certification compulsory under the Seeds Act 1966?

No. Certification under the Seeds Act is voluntary. A producer may sell seed of a notified kind without a certificate, provided the seed conforms to the minimum limits of germination and purity and carries the prescribed label with correct particulars. What the Act makes compulsory is truthful labelling, not certification; certification is an additional, optional guarantee.

That distinction, between certified seed and truthfully labelled seed, organises the entire Indian seed market and explains its persistent quality problem. Truthfully labelled seed is seed sold on the producer's own declaration, bearing the label the Act requires but never passing through the certification agency's inspections. The declaration is legally enforceable, and a false label is an offence, but enforcement depends on the inspectorate drawing samples and the laboratories testing them, which is where the chain is weakest. Sections 12 and 13 provide for seed analysts and seed inspectors, the latter deemed public servants, and sections 14 to 16 give the inspector power to take samples of seed from any seller or person transporting or delivering seed, prescribe the procedure for sampling and dispatch, and make the seed analyst's report the evidential centrepiece of any prosecution. The design is coherent. Its chronic weakness is capacity: sampling is episodic, laboratories are overloaded, and a prosecution concluded years after a failed sowing compensates nobody for the lost season.

The trade provisions complete the scheme. Section 17 restricts the export and import of seed of notified kinds and varieties unless the seed conforms to the minimum limits and bears the prescribed label, and section 18 empowers the Central Government to recognise seed certification agencies of foreign countries for the purposes of the Act. Section 23 empowers the Central Government to give directions to State Governments, and section 25 is the rule-making power under which the Seeds Rules 1968 were made, prescribing among much else the contents and manner of labelling, the functions of the Committee and the laboratories, and the procedure for certification and appeal.

The penalty provision is where the statute's age shows most plainly. Under section 19, a person who contravenes any provision of the Act or the rules, or prevents a seed inspector from taking samples or exercising his powers, is punishable for a first offence with a fine that may extend to five hundred rupees, and on a subsequent conviction with imprisonment for a term that may extend to six months, or a fine that may extend to one thousand rupees, or both. Section 20 adds that the seed in respect of which a contravention was committed may be forfeited to the Government on conviction, and section 21 attributes offences by companies to the persons in charge of the business, subject to a due diligence defence. Five hundred rupees was a real sum in 1966. As the price of a season's seed for even a small holding has risen into the thousands, the deterrent arithmetic of the provision has collapsed, and the forfeiture of a consignment plus the commercial consequences of a stop-sale under the control order do whatever deterrent work the quality regime achieves. Successive reform proposals have fastened on this provision, and its inadequacy is one of the few points on which the seed industry and farmer organisations agree.

## The licensing layer: the Seed (Control) Order 1983

The statute that a seed dealer actually deals with, in the ordinary course of business, is often not the Seeds Act at all. It is the Seed (Control) Order 1983, issued by the Central Government under section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act 1955, the provision through which the Centre has long controlled the production, supply and distribution of commodities declared essential. Seed is an essential commodity for this purpose, and the control order builds a licensing regime on that foundation: a person carrying on the business of selling, exporting or importing seed must obtain a licence from the appointed licensing authority, the licence carries conditions about the sources from which seed may be obtained and the records to be kept, and inspectors under the order may enter premises, inspect stocks and records, and direct that seed not be sold where the order is contravened. The wider machinery of the parent statute, including its treatment of control orders generally, is the subject of [the guide to the Essential Commodities Act 1955](/2014/03/01/essential-commodities-act-1955-guide/), which owns that analysis.

The relationship between the two quality instruments is worth stating exactly, because commentary frequently merges them. The Seeds Act is a standards statute: it defines what counts as acceptable seed of a notified kind and what a seller must declare about it. The control order is a trade statute: it decides who may trade and on what terms, and its sanctions, licence suspension and cancellation among them, operate directly on the trader's ability to stay in business. A dealer can comply with every labelling requirement of the 1966 Act and still lose the right to trade for breach of a licence condition, and a dealer can hold a flawless licence and still be prosecuted for selling seed that fails the standards. The two regimes interlock without merging, and the practical enforcement of seed quality in India is the product of both, supplemented by state-level administration, since the inspectors, analysts, laboratories and licensing authorities are overwhelmingly state functionaries even though both instruments are central in origin.

The control order also illustrates a structural feature of Indian seed governance that the reform debate keeps rediscovering. Because the licensing layer rests on the essential commodities power rather than on the Seeds Act, amending the Seeds Act alone would not modernise the trade regime, and replacing the Seeds Act without touching the control order would leave licensing untouched. Reformers who speak of a single new seed law are therefore proposing, whether they say so or not, a consolidation of instruments made under different powers for different purposes, which is one reason the drafting task has repeatedly proved harder than the politics suggested.

## The replacement that never arrived: the Seeds Bills

The Seeds Act has outlived several attempts to replace it, and the history of those attempts is the clearest evidence of what Indian seed reform is actually about. A Seeds Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 9 December 2004 and referred to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Agriculture on 17 December 2004. The 2004 Bill proposed the change the industry had long sought: compulsory registration of all varieties offered for sale, replacing the notification system for marketed seed, together with stronger penalties, a framework for the accreditation of certification, and a compensation route for farmers whose purchased seed failed to perform. The Standing Committee examined the Bill and recommended extensive changes, and the government accepted a large body of amendments in principle. The Bill nevertheless never completed its passage, through successive sessions and successive governments, and lapsed from the active agenda while remaining the reference text for the debate.

A revised version prepared around 2010 carried many of the committee's amendments, including strengthened protection for the farmer's right to save, use, exchange and sell unbranded seed, and a compensation mechanism for seed failure that routed claims through consumer protection machinery. That routing was itself one of the contested points: farmer organisations argued that a cultivator whose crop failed because seed did not perform as claimed would get no effective remedy from consumer fora designed for retail grievances, and pressed for compensation machinery inside the seed law itself. A fresh draft Seeds Bill was put into the public domain in 2019, again proposing compulsory registration of varieties for sale and performance disclosure by producers, and again drawing opposition on the same cluster of grounds, and it too was not enacted. In November 2025 the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare released a further draft Seeds Bill for public comment, proposing mandatory registration and substantially higher penalties, which at the time of its release stood as a draft and not as law. The 1966 Act, with its five hundred rupee fine, remains the governing quality statute throughout.

The persistence of the failure deserves analysis rather than a shrug, because the same alignment of interests has defeated bills of different governments. The seed industry, represented by the National Seed Association of India, has consistently sought a modern statute with compulsory registration, arguing that registration of every variety sold, backed by performance standards, is the only way to drive spurious and substandard seed out of the market and to give legitimate producers a level field. The industry's factual premise is not fanciful: in a written answer in the Rajya Sabha in August 2025, the Minister of State for Agriculture, Ram Nath Thakur, informed the House that of 5,97,859 seed samples tested by the Centre and the states over the preceding three years, 43,001 were found non-standard. A market in which roughly one sample in fourteen fails the standard is a market with a real quality problem, whatever view one takes of the remedy.

Farmer organisations and seed rights campaigners, among them the Alliance for Sustainable and Holistic Agriculture, have opposed each draft on grounds that are equally specific. Compulsory registration, they argue, prices small producers and farmer-breeders out of the formal market through compliance costs, treats the farmer primarily as a consumer of purchased seed rather than as a producer and conserver of seed in his own right, and risks narrowing the space in which farm-saved and exchanged seed circulates, which is the space in which most Indian sowing actually happens. They have demanded, in addition, price regulation of seed and a compensation mechanism internal to the seed law. The states, for their part, administer the existing machinery and have resisted drafts perceived as centralising. A bill that must simultaneously satisfy an industry seeking market discipline, a farm movement defending an informal seed economy on which most cultivation depends, and states guarding their administrative turf has repeatedly found no majority for any particular text, and the default has been the statute of 1966. The gap is real; so is the disagreement about what would fill it, and describing the gap as simple regulatory neglect misdescribes a genuine political stalemate.

## The choice India made in 2001: a system of its own

The plant varieties statute cannot be understood without the fork in the road that produced it. Article 27.3(b) of the TRIPS Agreement obliged India, as a member of the World Trade Organization, to provide for the protection of plant varieties either by patents or by an effective sui generis system or by any combination of the two. Patents were never a serious candidate: the Patents Act 1970, in section 3(j), excludes plants and animals in whole or in part and essentially biological processes for the production or propagation of plants and animals from the definition of invention, a position the patentability analysis in [the guide to the Patents Act 1970](/2019/08/15/patents-act-1970-explained/) examines in full. The realistic choice was between acceding to the International Union for the Protection of New Varieties of Plants, known as UPOV, and adopting its model law, or designing a domestic system.

UPOV's 1991 Act, the version to which new members accede, grants the breeder a strong right and treats the farmer's practice of saving seed as an optional exception that member states may permit, within reasonable limits and subject to safeguarding the legitimate interests of the breeder, and in practice largely confined to the farmer's own holding. Suman Sahai of the Gene Campaign, the most persistent public critic of the UPOV route through the drafting years, argued that a model built for commercial agriculture in industrialised countries would, transplanted to India, restrict precisely the practices on which Indian smallholders depend: saving seed from one harvest for the next, exchanging seed within the village, and selling surplus seed to neighbours. The opposing view, pressed by the seed industry and by trade negotiators, was that a home-grown system risked being judged ineffective against the treaty standard and that UPOV accession would integrate India into international breeding and seed trade. India chose the home-grown system and did not accede to UPOV. The Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 is the result, and its preamble, unusually for an Indian statute, argues the choice on the face of the Act, reciting the farmers' contribution to the genetic stock in the same breath as the treaty obligation.

The choice had a consequence that is easy to miss. A sui generis statute drafted afresh had to decide from first principles what a farmer is in law, and the Indian draftsmen answered with a definition of unusual width: a farmer under the Act is a person who cultivates crops by cultivating the land himself or through any other person, or who conserves and preserves, severally or jointly with another person, any wild species or traditional variety, or adds value to such wild species or traditional variety through selection and identification of useful properties. The conserver is inside the definition alongside the cultivator. That definitional decision is the foundation on which the farmers' variety, the recognition and reward provisions, and the community rights of the Act all rest, and it has no equivalent in the UPOV model, where the farmer appears only as a potential infringer carrying a limited privilege.

The design also departed from the template in treating farming communities, not only individual breeders, as right-holders. The Act's recognition that generations of selection by cultivators produced the landraces from which modern varieties are bred, and that this contribution sounds in legal entitlement rather than in gratitude, was the statute's answer to the biopiracy debates of the 1990s, in which Indian genetic material had been carried abroad and claimed as invention elsewhere. Whether the machinery built on that recognition works is a separate question, addressed later in this article. The recognition itself, in statutory form, was the innovation.

## The machinery of the 2001 Act: Authority, Registry and registration

The Act creates its own institutional world. Section 3 establishes the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Authority, a body corporate charged with promoting the development of new varieties of plants and protecting the rights of farmers and breeders, which was set up in 2005 and which maintains, through the Registrar-General and the Registrars of the Plant Varieties Registry, the National Register of Plant Varieties. The Registry is where applications are filed, examined and, if successful, converted into certificates of registration; the Authority superintends the system, frames the crop-specific guidelines under which varieties are tested, and decides the contested questions the Act reserves to it, including benefit sharing, community claims, farmers' compensation claims, compulsory licences and revocations. The separation between a Registrar who processes and an Authority that adjudicates mirrors the structure of other Indian intellectual property registries, with the difference that this Authority also carries an affirmative mandate for farmers' rights and conservation that a patent or trade mark office does not.

Four categories of variety may be registered, and the categories themselves encode the statute's theory. A new variety is one that has not been sold or otherwise disposed of, by or with the consent of the breeder, earlier than one year before the application date in India, or earlier than four years outside India, six years in the case of trees and vines. An extant variety is one that is notified under section 5 of the Seeds Act 1966, or is a farmers' variety, or is a variety about which there is common knowledge, or is otherwise in the public domain; the category exists so that the existing stock of varieties, including the public system's released varieties, could be brought onto the register rather than leaving the register to future breeding alone. A farmers' variety is a variety traditionally cultivated and evolved by farmers in their fields, or a wild relative or land race of a variety about which the farmers possess common knowledge. An essentially derived variety, dealt with in section 23, is a variety predominantly derived from an initial variety, or from a variety itself predominantly derived from the initial variety, while retaining the expression of the essential characteristics of the initial variety, yet clearly distinguishable from it; the category exists to prevent the cosmetic modification of another's variety from founding a fresh right, and registration of an essentially derived variety requires the authorisation of the breeder of the initial variety.

Section 15 states the criteria against which registrability is judged. A new variety is registrable if it satisfies novelty, as defined through the sale windows just described, together with distinctness, uniformity and stability: the variety must be clearly distinguishable from any other variety whose existence is a matter of common knowledge at the time of filing, it must be sufficiently uniform in its relevant characteristics subject to the variation expected from its mode of propagation, and its relevant characteristics must remain unchanged after repeated propagation. The criteria are known across the seed world by the initials DUS, for distinctness, uniformity and stability, and they are tested, not merely asserted: the applicant's variety is grown out in DUS test centres under crop-specific guidelines and compared against reference varieties over growing seasons, a process that makes plant variety registration slower and more empirical than any other Indian intellectual property registration. Extant varieties and farmers' varieties are judged against the distinctness, uniformity and stability criteria without the novelty requirement in the same form, since a variety in the public domain or traditionally cultivated cannot by definition be new, and the Authority has framed adapted guidelines for farmers' varieties in recognition that landraces are genetically more heterogeneous than the uniformity criterion, applied without adaptation, would permit.

The procedure runs from application to certificate through recognisable registry stages. Section 14 permits an application for registration, and section 16 identifies who may apply: a person claiming to be the breeder of the variety, a successor or assignee of the breeder, a farmer or group or community of farmers claiming to be the breeder, a person authorised on their behalf, or a university or publicly funded agricultural institution claiming to be the breeder. The application must contain a denomination for the variety and the prescribed information, including an affidavit about the lawful acquisition of genetic material and a complete passport data of the parental lines, disclosing the geographical location in India from which genetic or parental material was taken and any contribution of a farmer, village community, institution or organisation in developing the variety. That passport data requirement is the evidentiary foundation for benefit sharing: the Act makes the applicant declare the community contribution at the door, rather than leaving communities to discover and prove it later. After examination and DUS testing, the application is advertised, and section 21 permits any person to oppose the registration within the prescribed period on the grounds the Act specifies. If no opposition succeeds, the Registrar registers the variety and issues the certificate under section 24. Section 29 excludes certain material from registration altogether, including denominations that mislead or offend and, in a provision with no counterpart elsewhere, varieties that employ genetic use restriction technology, the so-called terminator technology that renders harvested seed sterile, which the Act refuses to protect in terms.

### How long does protection of a registered plant variety last?

Section 24 fixes the term: nine years initially for trees and vines, six years for other crops, renewable for the remaining period up to eighteen years from registration for trees and vines and fifteen years in other cases, with extant varieties protected for fifteen years from their notification under the Seeds Act.

The mechanics of that term deserve a sentence of their own, because the initial certificate is not the whole term. Registration lapses unless the holder seeks review and renewal and pays the prescribed fees, so the register is periodically purged of varieties their holders no longer value enough to maintain, and the outer limits, eighteen and fifteen years, run from registration or, for extant varieties, from the earlier notification date under the Seeds Act, which means an extant variety notified long before its registration may arrive on the register with part of its term already spent. For a variety of an annual crop, fifteen years is a long commercial horizon, longer than the market life of most varieties, which are typically displaced by successors well inside the term; the term's practical bite falls on the exceptional variety that remains commercially dominant for a decade or more, which is precisely the class of variety over which disputes are worth litigating.

## The breeder's right and its built-in limits

Section 28 states what registration confers: the certificate confers on the breeder, or on his successor, his agent or his licensee, an exclusive right to produce, sell, market, distribute, import or export the variety. Registration of agents and licensees is provided for, so that the chain of title is a matter of record. Breach of the exclusive right is infringement within section 64, and the Act supplies the civil remedies, injunctions and damages or an account of profits, that intellectual property statutes ordinarily supply, with the High Court as the forum for infringement suits and for appeals from the Authority. So far, the statute reads like a conventional intellectual property law, and the limits that follow are best understood against that conventional baseline, because each of them is a deliberate departure from it.

The first limit is the researchers' right in section 30. Any person may use a registered variety for conducting experiments or research, and may use it as an initial source of variation for the purpose of creating other varieties. The right to breed from a protected variety, which is the lifeblood of plant improvement since every variety is bred from earlier varieties, is therefore preserved against the exclusive right. The boundary sits at commercial production: where a researcher uses a protected variety repeatedly as a parental line for the commercial production of another variety, the authorisation of the breeder of the protected variety is required. The second limit is benefit sharing under section 26. When a variety is registered, the Authority invites claims for benefit sharing from persons, groups and organisations, determines the amount payable having regard to the extent and nature of the genetic material contributed and the commercial utility and demand for the variety, and the breeder deposits the amount determined into the National Gene Fund constituted under section 45, into which compensation amounts, royalties and other receipts also flow, and out of which the Act's rewards and community payments are made. The third limit is the community right in section 41, under which a claim may be made on behalf of a village or local community that has contributed significantly to the evolution of a registered variety, with the compensation determined by the Authority and paid into the Gene Fund for the community's benefit.

The fourth limit is the compulsory licence in section 47. At any time after the expiry of three years from the date of registration of a variety, any person interested may apply to the Authority for a compulsory licence on the ground that the reasonable requirements of the public for seed or other propagating material of the variety have not been satisfied, or that the seed or propagating material is not available to the public at a reasonable price. The Authority, after hearing the breeder, may order the breeder to grant a licence on terms it settles, including the royalty, if the ground is made out. The provision imports into plant variety law the access logic of patent law's compulsory licensing, adapted to a subject matter where non-supply has a seasonal consequence: a variety unavailable at sowing time is unavailable for a year, not for a week. The fifth limit is revocation under section 34, under which the Authority may revoke a registration where, among other grounds, it was granted on the basis of incorrect information, or to a person not eligible, or where the breeder has failed to provide required information, documents or propagating material, or where the grant is not in the public interest. The sixth limit, the protection of innocent infringement in section 42, shields a farmer who infringes a breeder's right without awareness of its existence, a provision whose relationship to the farmers' rights clause is taken up below. Taken together, the six limits mean that the breeder's right under this statute is an exclusive right surrounded, on every side, by statutory qualifications that in most intellectual property systems would be exceptional. That is the design, and the farmers' rights clause is not the seventh item on the list but the provision that changes the character of the whole.

## Section 39: the farmers' rights that change everything

Section 39 is headed Farmers' Rights, and its first sub-section opens with a non obstante clause: notwithstanding anything contained in the Act. The opening words matter, because they establish the priority of what follows over the breeder's exclusive right in section 28. Four entitlements are conferred. A farmer who has bred or developed a new variety is entitled to registration and protection in like manner as a breeder, which is the provision that makes farmers' varieties registrable by farmers themselves. A farmer engaged in the conservation of genetic resources of land races and wild relatives of economic plants, and their improvement through selection and preservation, is entitled in the prescribed manner to recognition and reward from the Gene Fund. A farmer is protected against innocent infringement, in terms developed by section 42. And then comes clause (iv), the provision around which the whole statute turns: a farmer shall be deemed to be entitled to save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share or sell his farm produce including seed of a variety protected under the Act, in the same manner as he was entitled before the coming into force of the Act.

The proviso draws the only line: the farmer shall not be entitled to sell branded seed of a protected variety. The Explanation defines branded seed as any seed put in a package or any other container and labelled in a manner indicating that such seed is of a variety protected under the Act. The line is therefore not between saving and selling, nor between use and commerce, nor between the farmer's own field and the market. A farmer may sell seed of a protected variety, in the market, for money, in quantity. The line is between selling that seed as his own farm produce, which the statute protects, and selling it dressed in the identity of the protected variety, in packaging that holds it out as the breeder's variety, which the statute forbids. The rationale is coherent once seen: what the breeder owns is the variety as a commercial identity and a production system, and the farmer's sale of unbranded seed does not trade on that identity even though it competes with the breeder's product, while the branded sale appropriates the identity itself. Whether the line is commercially tolerable is the standing argument about the Act, but where the line sits is not in doubt, and Kapil Shah of the Kisan Beej Adhikar Manch stated the farmer's side of it precisely during the potato dispute: the registrant's right is a right over production of the variety, and even there, farmers retain the right to produce seed and to sell seed of a protected variety provided it is unbranded.

### Where exactly does the farmer's right in section 39 stop?

At branded seed. Section 39(1)(iv) entitles a farmer to save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share or sell farm produce including seed of a protected variety, as he could before the Act. The proviso withdraws one act: selling branded seed, meaning seed packaged and labelled as being of the protected variety.

Two further provisions complete the section. Section 39(2) addresses the converse vulnerability, the farmer as buyer of a breeder's seed. Where propagating material of a registered variety has been sold to a farmer by the breeder or his authorised agent or licensee, the breeder must disclose to the farmer the expected performance of the variety under given conditions, and if the material fails to provide the expected performance under those conditions, the farmer may claim compensation in the prescribed manner before the Authority. The provision converts a marketing claim into a statutory representation: performance promised is performance owed, subject to proof of the conditions and the failure. Section 39(3) removes the fee barrier, providing that a farmer or a group or organisation of farmers shall not be liable to pay any fee in any proceeding before the Authority, the Registrar, the Tribunal or the High Court under the Act. A farmer pursuing a claim under section 39(2) does well to keep the seed packet, its label, the purchase bill and the sowing record together, and keeping those papers in one private place, of the kind that [an encrypted workspace built for protecting a journalist's sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) exists to provide, is often the difference between a claim that can be proved and one that cannot. Nothing in this account is advice on an individual dispute; it states the general position under the two statutes, and anyone facing proceedings needs advice on his own facts, because limitation periods and forum rules are unforgiving.

The international significance of clause (iv) should not be understated. In the UPOV 1991 model, the farmer's saving of seed is an exception each member state may choose to allow, bounded by the breeder's legitimate interests. In the Indian statute, the farmer's entitlement is a right, stated in a non obstante clause, extending to sale, and bounded only by the branded seed proviso. No other plant variety law in the world grants the farmer a statutory right to sell seed of a protected variety. That is the sense in which the Act protects both sides simultaneously, and it is also the source of the Act's standing tension, because every unbranded sale by a farmer of seed of a protected variety is a sale the breeder does not make and cannot prevent, and the size of that lawful leakage varies by crop: it is small for hybrid seed, whose progeny loses vigour, and large for self-pollinated crops and vegetatively propagated crops, where farm-saved material performs like the original.

## Breeder's right against farmer's right: the permitted acts

The table below states, for the acts a person might perform with seed of a protected variety, the position of a farmer under section 39, the position of a licensee authorised by the breeder, and whether the act infringes the breeder's right under section 28 where done without authority by a commercial producer who is neither.

| Act with seed of a protected variety | Farmer | Licensee of the breeder | Commercial producer without authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saving seed from one's own harvest | Permitted, section 39(1)(iv) | Permitted under the licence | Infringement if done to produce the variety for sale, section 28 |
| Sowing and resowing saved seed on one's own land | Permitted, section 39(1)(iv) | Permitted under the licence | Infringement where it amounts to production of the variety, section 28 |
| Exchanging or sharing seed with a neighbour | Permitted, section 39(1)(iv) | Permitted under the licence | Infringement where commercial in character, section 28 |
| Selling farm produce including seed, unbranded | Permitted, section 39(1)(iv) | Permitted under the licence | Infringement where it amounts to marketing the variety, section 28 |
| Selling seed branded as the protected variety | Not permitted, proviso to section 39(1)(iv) | Permitted under the licence | Infringement, section 28 |
| Commercial multiplication of the variety for sale as seed | Not protected by section 39 where it is a seed business rather than farm produce | Permitted under the licence | Infringement, section 28 |
| Using the variety in research or to breed a new variety | Permitted, section 30 | Permitted, section 30 | Permitted, section 30, subject to the repeated parental line limit |

The table repays a slow reading, because its pattern is the statute in miniature. The farmer column is permissive almost throughout, and its single prohibition is about branding, not about seed. The licensee column is permissive by contract. The third column is restrictive almost throughout, and its single generous entry, research, is generous to everyone equally. The system is therefore not a compromise in which each side surrendered half; it is a partition, in which the commercial seed business belongs to the breeder and his licensees, the farm economy of saved and exchanged seed belongs to the farmer, and the contested ground is the strip where the two meet, which is the sale of unbranded seed by farmers into the same market the breeder supplies.

## The stress test: the FL 2027 potato dispute

A statute containing two rights designed to constrain each other will eventually be read by a court in a case where both are invoked, and for the 2001 Act that case concerned a potato. The variety is FL 2027, a chipping potato bred for processing into crisps and supplied by PepsiCo India Holdings to contract growers for its Lay's brand. PepsiCo applied for registration of the variety in February 2011, describing it as a new variety and stating a date of first commercial sale in December 2009. The application was in fact processed and the variety registered as an extant variety, the registration being granted on 1 February 2016. The categorisation error embedded in that history, the variety having been first commercialised outside India years earlier, in Chile in 2002 according to the record before the courts, became the fulcrum of everything that followed.

In 2018 and 2019 PepsiCo sued potato farmers in Gujarat, nine or more of them, alleging infringement of its rights in the registered variety and claiming damages reported at over one crore rupees against individual farmers. The farmers' position was that they had obtained the seed in the local market, where the variety had circulated since its introduction into India years before registration, and that in any event section 39 protected them. The suits produced an immediate political reaction: farmer organisations and rights campaigners mobilised nationally against the spectacle of a multinational claiming crore-scale damages from small cultivators over a potato, and [the wider pattern of organised farmer mobilisation against seed and marketing legislation](/2014/05/01/farmers-protest-legislative-lessons/) supplied the campaign with its networks and its vocabulary. In May 2019 PepsiCo withdrew the suits unconditionally. The withdrawal ended the infringement litigation. It did not end the dispute, because in June 2019 Kavitha Kuruganti, a farmers' rights activist associated with the Alliance for Sustainable and Holistic Agriculture, applied to the Authority for revocation of the registration under section 34, on grounds that included the incorrect information in the application, defects in the documentation of PepsiCo's title as breeder, and the public interest ground, the argument being that suing farmers for exercising what section 39 protects was itself contrary to the public interest and to the Act.

By its order of 3 December 2021, the Authority revoked the registration, acting on the grounds in section 34 relating to incorrect information, ineligibility and non-compliance, and the public interest, and it subsequently rejected PepsiCo's application for renewal of the registration, by a letter of 11 February 2022. PepsiCo appealed to the Delhi High Court. A single judge, by judgment of 5 July 2023, dismissed PepsiCo's appeal in substance, upholding the revocation principally on the grounds that incorrect information had been furnished about the date of first commercial sale and that the requisite documentation had not been properly presented when registration was sought. Cross-appeals followed, and a division bench of the same court, Justices Yashwant Varma and Dharmesh Sharma, decided them on 9 January 2024. The division bench set aside the Authority's revocation order, the February 2022 letter, and the single judge's judgment to the extent it had affirmed the revocation. Its reasoning, stated at the level of principle, was that procedural infirmities which do not bear on the eligibility of the applicant or the registrability of the variety are not fatal to a registration, that the error in styling the variety new rather than extant had in any event been overtaken by the Registrar's own decision to process the application in the extant category, and that the public interest ground in section 34 could not be invoked merely because the registered proprietor had filed infringement suits against farmers. The renewal application was restored to the file of the Registrar, to be disposed of in accordance with law. Kuruganti carried the matter to the Supreme Court by special leave petitions, which were heard on 5 August 2026. The Supreme Court declined to interfere with the division bench's judgment and disposed of the petitions, observing that the High Court had addressed both the farmers' protection under section 39(1)(iv) and the public interest argument, and clarifying that individual farmers may claim the statutory protection of section 39(1)(iv) in any proceedings brought against them.

### Is the FL 2027 dispute finally over?

In the courts, effectively yes. The division bench of the Delhi High Court in January 2024 set aside the revocation and restored PepsiCo's renewal application to the Registrar, and the Supreme Court in August 2026 declined to interfere. What remains open is administrative: the Registrar's disposal of the restored renewal application, not any further appeal on the revocation.

The sequence teaches three lessons about the statute that no commentary had established before it. The first is that section 39 was never actually adjudicated against the Gujarat farmers, because the infringement suits were withdrawn before any court decided whether the farmers' conduct fell within clause (iv); the protection's practical strength in 2019 came from politics and publicity, and its legal strength was confirmed only later and obliquely, in the Supreme Court's clarification that individual farmers may plead it. The second is that section 34, the revocation power, is narrower in judicial hands than its grounds suggest: the division bench read the incorrect-information and public interest grounds as directed at eligibility and genuine statutory non-compliance, not at punishing a proprietor for enforcing its rights, however unattractive that enforcement appeared. The third is that the registration system's front end failed first: the wrong category, the wrong first-sale date and the incomplete assignment documentation all passed through examination, advertisement and grant, and were litigated a decade later at enormous cost to everyone, which is an argument about Registry capacity rather than about anyone's rights. Kuruganti's verdict on the Supreme Court's disposal, that it upheld farmers' rights in letter but not in spirit, and PepsiCo's position that the registration stands restored, can both be true of the same outcome, because the outcome settled the registration question in the company's favour while leaving the farmers' clause exactly as untested, and as available, as it was before the first suit was filed.

## The third regime: genetically modified crops sit elsewhere

Nothing in either statute governs the approval of genetically modified crops, and the conflation of that question with plant variety protection is the most persistent error in the public debate. The environmental release of a genetically engineered organism in India is regulated under the Rules for the Manufacture, Use, Import, Export and Storage of Hazardous Micro-organisms and Genetically Engineered Organisms or Cells, 1989, made under the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, and administered through the Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee in the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, with the Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation overseeing contained research. It was that machinery which approved Bt cotton for commercial cultivation in 2002 and that machinery whose recommendation on Bt brinjal was followed by a moratorium imposed by the Environment Minister, Jairam Ramesh, in February 2010. A genetically modified variety, if approved for release, would then enter the same world as any other variety: it could be notified under the Seeds Act for quality purposes and could in principle be registered under the 2001 Act if it met the criteria, subject always to section 29's refusal of protection to varieties employing terminator technology. But the environmental and food safety questions, the trait fee and technology licensing controversies that surrounded Bt cotton, and the patent question whether a transgenic trait is an invention at all given section 3(j) of the Patents Act, the question the Supreme Court handled in Monsanto Technology against Nuziveedu Seeds in 2019 by restoring the suit for trial rather than deciding patentability summarily, belong to other regimes and other articles. The seed sovereignty argument, which draws much of its energy from the genetically modified crop controversy, must not be allowed to blur this: a farmer's right to save seed of a conventionally bred protected variety under section 39 raises no environmental question at all, and the breeder's right in such a variety confers no power over any other variety, modified or not.

## The honest assessment: two genuine cases and one unresolved contradiction

The breeder's case against the 2001 Act's design deserves to be put at full strength, because it is an economic argument and not a mere complaint. Developing a commercial variety takes years of crossing, selection and multi-location trials, and the cost must be recovered from seed sales in the seasons after release. A right whose subject matter the purchaser may lawfully propagate and resell, unbranded, in competition with the right-holder, is worth materially less than a conventional exclusive right, and in self-pollinated crops and vegetatively propagated crops the lawful leakage is not marginal but structural. If the expected return falls below the cost of breeding, rational private capital moves to where returns are protectable: to hybrid crops whose biology enforces repurchase, to traits licensed under contract, or out of Indian breeding altogether. The seed industry's representatives make exactly this argument, and add the quality dimension: seed is the first input market a farmer enters each season, upstream of the output markets whose regulation [the account of agricultural marketing law](/2014/03/15/apmc-act-agricultural-marketing-india/) examines, and a state that polices output markets while leaving the input market's governing statute at 1966 penalty levels has its priorities inverted. On this view, the farmers' clause is not a balance but a slow expropriation, and its cost will be paid by farmers themselves in the form of varieties not bred for Indian conditions.

The farmer's case is equally genuine and equally economic. Seed saving is not a loophole in Indian agriculture; for most crops and most cultivators it is the system, since the formal seed sector has never supplied more than a minority of the seed actually sown, and the majority of planting material moves through farm-saved stocks and farmer-to-farmer exchange. A regime on the UPOV pattern, under which saving seed of a protected variety is a tolerated exception bounded by the breeder's interests, would in Indian conditions either criminalise the ordinary practice of the majority of cultivators or, more likely, be unenforceable against them, and a right that cannot be enforced against the population it targets corrodes the legal system that proclaims it. There is also the historical claim, which the Act's preamble itself endorses: the genetic base of every modern variety was assembled by farming communities selecting over centuries, without payment, and a statute that granted rights over the finished variety while recognising nothing in the base would privatise the last step of a collective process. Farmer organisations and campaigners such as Suman Sahai add the sovereignty point: control over seed is control over the food system, and a legal architecture that makes the farmer a perpetual purchaser transfers that control to whoever owns the register.

The two cases are not reconcilable by evidence, because they rest on different judgements about which failure is worse: the failure of under-rewarded breeding, whose costs arrive slowly and invisibly as varieties not developed, or the failure of criminalised custom, whose costs would arrive immediately and visibly in villages. India's statute does not reconcile them either. It enacts both, states the farmer's right in a non obstante clause, and leaves the boundary to be worked out by the Authority, the courts and the market. The verdict this article defends is that the design should be judged a sophisticated compromise rather than an unresolved contradiction, for one specific reason: the branded seed line converts an abstract conflict over seed into an administrable conflict over identity. What the breeder is guaranteed is the market for seed sold as the variety, under its name, with the production discipline the breeder organises; what the farmer retains is the subsistence and local-market economy of his own produce. The partition fails only where the two markets are the same market, and for the great majority of crops and holdings they are not. Where they are the same, as with a processing potato grown under contract, the dispute arrived, was fought, and was settled without the statute collapsing, which is evidence that the compromise can bear weight.

Against that verdict, the implementation record enters its qualifications. The farmers' variety registrations have been numerous, and the Authority has made recognition awards, but benefit sharing determinations under section 26 have been few relative to the register's size, and the Gene Fund's promise as a channel of value back to conserving communities remains more designed than demonstrated. The section 39(2) compensation route, the farmer's statutory remedy for a variety that fails to perform as disclosed, has not generated a visible body of decided claims. The compulsory licence in section 47 has been more discussed than used. A fair summary is that the Act's defensive provisions, the ones that stop things, have worked better than its affirmative machinery, the parts meant to move money and recognition to farmers and communities. The Seeds Act's record invites the mirror-image summary: its standards and labelling framework is sound and universally understood, while its deterrent, the five hundred rupee first fine, has been a dead letter for decades, and the replacement statute that would repair it has been defeated by the same stalemate for twenty years. Neither statute is a failure. Both are unfinished in the specific places where their draftsmen's ambitions met administrative capacity and political resistance.

## Closing verdict

Indian seed law is three regimes wearing one name, and the reader who keeps them apart understands more than most commentary on the subject. The Seeds Act 1966 is a quality statute of the Green Revolution, built on notification, standards, truthful labelling and voluntary certification, supplemented in practice by the licensing regime of the Seed (Control) Order 1983, and unreplaced despite attempts in 2004, 2010, 2019 and 2025 because the interests its replacement must satisfy cannot agree on a text. The Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 is an intellectual property statute of the trade era, which took the sui generis option the TRIPS Agreement offered, refused the UPOV template, and produced the only plant variety regime in the world that grants the breeder an exclusive right and the farmer a statutory right to save and sell the same variety's seed in the same enactment. The genetically modified crop regime is neither of these, and belongs to environmental law. The namable claim with which this article opened stands: a statute that contains two rights designed to constrain each other cannot be understood by reading either, and the FL 2027 litigation, in which the breeder's right survived its severest test while the farmer's right survived by never quite being tested, is where the meaning of the design is being worked out. For the systematic examination treatment of this cluster of agricultural statutes, [the study guide to India's agriculture laws](/2014/07/15/agriculture-laws-upsc-guide/) maps the whole field; the specialist articles own the statutes this article has had to compress, and the reader pursuing the seed question into the protest politics of the farm laws will find the thread picked up there.

## From application to certificate: the registration journey in detail

Registration under the 2001 Act is the slowest of India's intellectual property registrations, and the reason is biological rather than bureaucratic: the central question the system must answer, whether a variety is distinct, uniform and stable, can only be answered by growing the variety and watching it. The journey begins with the application. The applicant files with the Registrar, claiming to be the breeder or standing in one of the other capacities section 16 permits, and the filing must do more than identify the variety. It must assign a denomination, the generic designation by which the variety will be known, which must not be misleading, must not consist solely of figures, and must differ from denominations already registered for the same or a closely related species. It must state the novelty position, including the dates of first sale in India and abroad, which is the disclosure whose inaccuracy detonated the FL 2027 registration a decade later. It must carry the complete passport data of the parental lines from which the variety was bred, disclosing the geographical location in India from which the genetic or parental material was collected and the contribution, if any, of any farmer, village community, institution or organisation to the variety's development, supported by the prescribed affidavit about the lawful acquisition of the material used. And it must be accompanied by seed or propagating material of the variety for testing and for deposit, because the register's promises are anchored in a physical sample against which later claims can be checked.

On receipt, the Registrar examines the application for formal compliance and for the registrability criteria that can be assessed on paper, and may require the applicant to amend the application, supply further information or documents, or furnish a different denomination. The heart of the process is the DUS test. The variety is entered into the testing system maintained under the Authority's supervision, grown alongside reference varieties of the same crop at designated centres under the crop-specific DUS guidelines the Authority has framed for each crop, and observed over the prescribed growing cycles for the characteristics the guidelines specify. Distinctness is assessed against the collection of varieties of common knowledge; uniformity against the off-types appearing in the test population, judged with allowance for the variation the variety's mode of reproduction predictably produces; stability by the consistency of the characteristics across the test cycles and, where required, a further generation. For hybrid varieties, the parental lines enter the analysis, and the essentially derived variety rules govern a claimed variety that is predominantly derived from another. The test result is the evidentiary basis on which the Registrar proceeds, and its empirical character distinguishes this register from the paper examination of a patent or trade mark application: the variety must perform its identity in the field, in public, before witnesses employed by the state.

Once the Registrar is satisfied, or has resolved the deficiencies, the application is advertised in the prescribed manner, and the opposition window under section 21 opens. Any person may give notice of opposition on the statutory grounds, which include that the claimant is not entitled to apply, that the variety is not registrable under the Act, that the grant would not be in the public interest, and that the variety may have an adverse effect on the environment; the applicant answers, both sides are heard, and the Registrar decides. Opposition is the register's public audit, the moment at which a rival breeder, a farmer organisation or a community that recognises its own material in the passport data can intervene before rights crystallise rather than after. After advertisement without successful opposition, the Registrar registers the variety in the National Register of Plant Varieties, issues the certificate under section 24, and the entry in the Register is thereafter the public record of the right: its holder, its denomination, its term and its transactions, since assignments and licences take effect within the system through registration of the agent's or licensee's title. Annual fees and the review and renewal discipline of section 24 keep the record honest thereafter, purging varieties whose holders no longer maintain them.

Two features of this machinery explain much of the Act's subsequent history. The first is that the system's accuracy depends entirely on disclosures the applicant makes about matters within his own knowledge, the first sale dates and the assignment chain among them, which the Registry can test at the margins but cannot independently reconstruct; the FL 2027 litigation was, at bottom, the delayed audit of those disclosures. The second is that the register is a single national register for a continental agriculture, and the DUS infrastructure, test centres, crop guidelines, reference collections, had to be built crop by crop after 2001, which is why registration opened only some years after the Act's assent and why the early register was dominated by extant varieties of major crops, the low-hanging fruit of a system still equipping itself for novelty examination.

## One variety through both statutes: a worked passage

The separation of the two statutes is clearest when a single variety is followed through both. Take a wheat variety bred by a public agricultural university and selected for release. Its legal life begins in the variety evaluation system: after multi-location trials in the coordinated research project, the variety is identified for release, recommended by the Central or State variety release committee as the case may be, and notified by the Central Government under section 5 of the Seeds Act 1966. Notification is the variety's birth certificate as a commercial article. From that moment, seed of the variety may be sold within the Seeds Act framework, subject to the section 6 standards for germination and purity and the section 7 labelling discipline, and the certification agencies may certify its seed as it moves from breeder seed maintained by the university, through foundation seed produced under supervision, to certified seed multiplied by the state seed corporation, cooperatives and private producers under licence from the public system. No one owns the variety. Any producer who can meet the standards may multiply and sell its seed, and the university's return is the public return of adoption and yield.

Place that same variety on the 2001 Act's register. As a notified variety, it is an extant variety, and the university, or the public institution that bred it, may apply for registration; if it establishes its title as breeder, the certificate confers the section 28 exclusive right, and the institution becomes, for the remaining term measured from the Seeds Act notification, the only person entitled to produce and market the variety or to license others to do so. Nothing about the seed in any farmer's store changes. The farmer who bought certified seed of the variety last season may save its grain, sow it, resow it, exchange it, and sell his produce including seed of it unbranded, exactly as before registration, because section 39(1)(iv) holds his position harmless against the new right. What changes is the position of the commercial multiplier: the seed company that previously multiplied the public variety freely must take a licence, and the university acquires a revenue instrument, the royalty, that public breeding had never possessed. The two statutes have operated on the same bag of seed without touching each other, the 1966 Act governing whether the seed is good and honestly labelled, the 2001 Act governing who may commercially produce the variety and who may not.

The worked passage exposes the design decision that conventional intellectual property systems do not face. In patent law, registration changes everyone's position: the world is excluded, subject to narrow exceptions. Under the 2001 Act, registration changes the position only of the commercial seed trade, and leaves the farm economy of the variety, the saving, sowing and unbranded selling by the farmers who grow it, exactly where it stood the day before the certificate issued. An analyst who asks what the registration of a variety does, and answers in the conventional form that it makes the variety the breeder's property, has said something that is true of the seed trade and false of the farm, and the distance between those two truths is the distance the whole statute was drafted to create.

## Infringement, innocence and enforcement under the 2001 Act

The enforcement scheme repays attention because it is where the statute's two rights would collide if they were ever pleaded against each other. Section 64 defines infringement: a right established under the Act is infringed by a person who, not being the breeder of the variety or a registered agent or licensee, undertakes any of the acts reserved by section 28, selling, offering for sale, marketing, producing, importing or exporting the variety or its propagating material, or who applies a false denomination or indication, or who causes such acts to be done. The breeder's remedies are the civil remedies of intellectual property law, injunction, damages or account of profits, pursued in the courts, with the Act's appellate route to the High Court from the Authority's decisions. Overlaid on this conventional scheme is section 42, headed Protection of Innocent Infringement, which provides that a right under the Act is not deemed infringed by a farmer who at the time of the infringement was not aware of the existence of the right, and that no relief in the form of damages or account of profits is to be granted against a farmer who proves that he was not aware, and had no reasonable ground to believe, that the variety in the seed he dealt with was registered.

Section 42 and section 39(1)(iv) overlap without coinciding, and the difference matters for the next FL 2027, whenever it comes. Clause (iv) is a right: conduct within it is not infringement at all, however knowing, so a farmer who saves and sells seed unbranded with full knowledge of the registration acts lawfully. Section 42 is a shield for conduct outside clause (iv): a farmer who, for instance, buys seed in a market not knowing it is a protected variety and multiplies it in a manner that crosses into commercial production of the variety is protected, in the first instance and as to monetary relief, by his innocence. The Supreme Court's closing clarification in the FL 2027 matter, that individual farmers can claim the statutory protection under section 39(1)(iv) in proceedings initiated against them, directs attention to the right rather than the shield, and the practical advice embedded in the architecture is the same in both cases: the farmer's protection is pleaded as a defence by the farmer in the proceeding, which means the farmer must first be sued, or threatened, before the protection operates. Kuruganti's criticism, that farmers are effectively asked to establish in litigation rights the statute grants them in terms, is aimed at exactly this structural feature, and it is a fair criticism of every defensive right in every legal system; the Indian statute's answer is the fee exemption in section 39(3) and the innocent infringement shield, mitigations of the burden of being a defendant rather than eliminations of it.

Enforcement reality should also be recorded plainly. Infringement litigation under the Act has been rare relative to the register's size, and FL 2027 stands almost alone as a fully litigated contest, which suggests that the commercial seed trade has largely accommodated itself to the register through licensing rather than through suits, and that the Act's deterrent effect operates in the shadow of a single famous case. The rarity cuts both ways in assessment: it may evidence a system working consensually, or a right too encumbered to be worth enforcing, and the honest conclusion is that the record supports the first reading for the trade, since licensing under the Act is common and uncontentious, while the second reading remains available for the farmer-facing provisions, which have never been stress-tested to judgment at all.

## The affirmative machinery: benefit sharing, the Gene Fund and community claims

If section 39 is the Act's shield for farmers, sections 26, 41 and 45 are its attempt at a sword: machinery designed to move recognition and money toward the farmers and communities whose genetic material underlies registered varieties. Benefit sharing under section 26 begins when a variety is registered. The Authority publishes the contents of the registration and invites claims, and any person, group of persons, or governmental or non-governmental organisation may claim benefit sharing in respect of the variety, on the footing of a contribution to its evolution. The Authority determines the amount, if any, having regard to the extent and nature of the genetic material of the claimant used in the variety's development and to the commercial utility and demand for the variety in the market, and may direct the breeder to deposit the amount determined. The sums so determined, together with compensation amounts under the community and farmers' provisions and other receipts the Act specifies, are credited to the National Gene Fund constituted under section 45, and the Fund is applied to the purposes the Act lists, including amounts payable as benefit sharing and compensation, measures for conservation and sustainable use of genetic resources, and schemes to support the conservation work of panchayats and communities.

The community claim under section 41 runs parallel. Any person, group, or governmental or non-governmental organisation may apply to the Authority claiming compensation on behalf of a village or local community, on the ground that the community has contributed significantly to the evolution of a variety registered under the Act. The Authority inquires, and if satisfied, determines the compensation, which is deposited in the Gene Fund and applied for the community's benefit. Read together with the recognition and reward entitlement in section 39 for farmers conserving genetic resources of land races and wild relatives, the scheme is a complete theory of distributive justice for plant genetic resources: the breeder who used the material pays, the Fund holds and channels, the contributing community and the conserving farmer receive. The passport data requirement at the application stage, which forces disclosure of the geographical source of parental material and of community contribution, is the scheme's information engine, since a claim can only be attached to a contribution the paperwork records.

The distance between this design and its record is the most substantial implementation criticism the Act attracts, and it should be stated without varnish. Registrations have run into the thousands across crops, and farmers' varieties form a significant share of the register, with the Authority also making recognition awards to conserving farmers and communities. But benefit sharing claims have been sparse relative to the register, determinations rarer still, and the sums involved modest against the commercial value of the varieties concerned; the community compensation route has been invoked in a small number of instances; and the Gene Fund has functioned more as an account of the Act's intentions than as a significant channel of resources to conserving regions. The reasons are structural rather than sinister. A benefit sharing claim requires a claimant who knows the variety was registered, knows his community's material is in its ancestry, can prove the contribution to the Authority's satisfaction, and expects a recovery worth the effort; each condition filters out claimants, and the passport data, which should ease the proof, is only as complete as the applicant's disclosure and the Registry's scrutiny made it. Critics of the Act within the farmers' rights movement, including voices associated with the Gene Campaign tradition that helped create the statute, have treated this record as evidence that the Act's affirmative promises were drafted beyond the state's capacity or intention to deliver. Defenders answer that the register itself, and the denomination of farmers' varieties in farmers' names, is a form of protection against misappropriation whose value lies in prior art and attribution rather than in cash, and that a defensive victory of that kind is not nothing. Both assessments are partly right, and the tension between them is the implementation version of the design tension this article has traced throughout: the Act promises most where it is hardest to administer.

## The comparison the design invites: UPOV, the United States and the European Union

India's statute is best located by placing it beside the systems it declined to join. Under the UPOV Convention's 1991 Act, the breeder's right extends to production, reproduction, conditioning for propagation, offering for sale, selling, exporting, importing and stocking of the protected variety. The compulsory exceptions are narrow: acts done privately and for non-commercial purposes, acts done for experimental purposes, and acts done for breeding other varieties. The farmer's privilege is not among the compulsory exceptions at all. Article 15(2) of the 1991 Act permits a member state, in its discretion, to restrict the breeder's right in relation to certain varieties to allow farmers to use for propagating purposes, on their own holdings, the product of the harvest they obtained from the protected variety, within reasonable limits and subject to the safeguarding of the legitimate interests of the breeder. Every element of that formulation is a narrowing of the Indian position: it is optional rather than mandatory, it is framed as a restriction the state grants rather than a right the farmer holds, it is confined to the farmer's own holding, it reaches use for propagation and not sale, and it is expressly subordinated to the breeder's legitimate interests. A farmer selling saved seed of a protected variety to a neighbour is, in the 1991 system, an infringer unless national law has made an exception the breeder's interests tolerate; in the Indian system he is exercising a statutory right stated in a non obstante clause. That is the whole difference between the two philosophies, and it is a difference of kind.

The United States, which protects varieties through the Plant Variety Protection Act administered by the Department of Agriculture and, for many modern traits, through utility patents, supplies the instructive middle case. Its statute carries a crop exemption permitting a farmer to save seed and to sell saved seed for reproductive purposes to another farmer in limited quantities, and the scope of that exemption was fought through the federal courts, reaching the Supreme Court in Asgrow Seed Company against Winterboer in 1995, where the Court read the exemption narrowly and held that farmers who sold the greater part of their harvest as seed for planting had exceeded it. The American experience demonstrates that the boundary between farm produce and seed commerce is contested wherever it is drawn, and that courts asked to police it tend to ask quantitative questions, how much was sold, to whom, for what purpose, that the Indian statute answers in advance by choosing the branding line instead of a quantity line. The European Union's Community plant variety right, granted by the Community Plant Variety Office, permits farm-saved seed of specified agricultural crops subject, for most farmers other than small farmers, to payment of an equitable remuneration to the right-holder, a royalty on the saved seed itself. The European solution thereby prices the farmer's privilege; the American solution rations it; the UPOV 1991 solution subordinates it; the Indian solution refuses to treat it as a privilege at all. Each system thereby declares what it thinks a variety is: a product, a technology, an invention, or, in the Indian case, the last link in a chain of collective creation whose contributors retain rights in the final product. Plant variety law is a small field that contains, in unusually pure form, the largest question in intellectual property: what a society owes those whose unremunerated work made the protected thing possible.

## Price: the question neither statute answers

Neither the Seeds Act nor the plant varieties Act regulates the price of seed, and the omission has generated its own satellite law, because seed price is the point at which every abstraction in this field becomes a number a farmer must pay before sowing. The instrument the Centre has used is, once again, the essential commodities power. When conflict over the price of Bt cotton seed and the trait fee charged by the technology provider became acute, the Government issued the Cotton Seed Price (Control) Order 2015 under section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act 1955, fixing the maximum sale price of Bt cotton seed packets and the trait value payable to the technology licensor, and revising those figures in subsequent years. The technology provider, Mahyco Monsanto Biotech, contested the regime, and the trait fee dispute ran through licensing terminations, arbitration and litigation for years. The episode belongs in this article for one reason: it demonstrates that the governance of seed in India is assembled from powers scattered across statutes, the quality power in the 1966 Act, the trade licensing and price powers in the Essential Commodities Act, the property power in the 2001 Act, and the environmental power in the 1986 Act, and that a reform programme addressed to only one of the four will repeatedly be ambushed by the other three.

The pending Seeds Bills have each had to decide what to do about price, and the decision has been one of their recurring breaking points. The industry position is that statutory price control destroys the return on breeding and drives technology out, pointing to the withdrawal of new trait introductions from the Indian cotton market during the price control years as the demonstrated consequence. The farmer organisation position is that seed is an essential input sold to buyers with no bargaining power at the one moment of the year when they cannot walk away, and that an unregulated price for a legally protected variety is a state-created monopoly rent collected at sowing time. Drafts of the replacement statute have generally included an emergency price power, a power for the Government to fix prices in specified situations such as seed shortage or abnormal price rise, rather than standing price control, and even that compromise formulation has satisfied neither camp. The 2001 Act itself stands deliberately aside from the question: a breeder's right under it is a right to exclude, not a right to any particular price, and the compulsory licence in section 47, with its reasonable price ground, is its only internal instrument against pricing that denies the public access to a variety. Whether that abstinence is principled restraint or a gap depends, once more, on which failure one fears more.

## The field as it is: formal seed, farm-saved seed and the reach of the statutes

Any assessment of these statutes must end in the field, because the field is where their reach is decided. Indian seed supply divides into a formal sector, public institutions, seed corporations, cooperatives and private companies producing certified or truthfully labelled seed through the systems described in this article, and an informal sector, the farmer's own saved seed and the seed exchanged or sold within villages and local markets. The informal sector supplies the larger part of the seed actually sown for most crops, and for the self-pollinated crops that dominate Indian acreage, wheat, rice, pulses and oilseeds grown from farm-saved stocks, it supplies the overwhelming part. Seed replacement rates, the share of area sown with fresh certified or quality seed in a season, have historically been low for most crops, rising substantially only where hybrid technology or rapid varietal turnover gives the farmer a recurring reason to buy. This is the ground truth against which both statutes operate: the 1966 Act's labelling discipline governs the formal market and barely touches the informal one, since farm-saved and neighbour-exchanged seed is not sold in labelled containers in the course of a seed business, and the 2001 Act's farmers' clause is, in substance, the legal recognition of the informal sector's legitimacy.

That recognition has a consequence for enforcement priorities that is rarely stated. A legal system in which the majority practice is lawful by design does not need to police the majority, and the plant varieties Act has never needed to: no enforcement apparatus patrols village seed exchange, because the Act does not ask it to. The entire coercive weight of the statute falls on the formal sector's edge, the commercial multiplier and the branded packet, which is exactly where a small inspectorate and a small register can hope to be effective. Critics who describe section 39 as a surrender mistake a jurisdictional fact for a policy choice: the alternative to recognising the informal sector was not suppressing it, which no Indian state could do, but driving a permanent wedge between the statute book and the practice of most cultivators, with the statute discredited and the practice unchanged. The comparative evidence supports the draftsmen. Jurisdictions that have tightened saved-seed rules against a resistant farming practice have bought litigation and resentment rather than compliance, while the Indian register has grown, licensing has normalised, and the one great confrontation the system produced ended with the right intact and the farmers' clause clarified rather than cut down.

The same realism should govern expectations of the quality statute. The Seeds Act cannot make seed good; it can make seed claims answerable, and its real product over six decades has been the infrastructure of answerability, the notified variety list, the laboratories, the certification chain and the label, on which the entire formal seed economy, including the private industry that dominates it, was built. When reformers describe the 1966 Act as obsolete, they are right about its penalties and its silence on registration, performance liability and price, and wrong if they mean its framework has failed: the framework is the market's skeleton, and the repeated failure to replace it owes as much to the risk of disturbing a working skeleton as to the politics of the replacement's new bones. The seed in the farmer's hand at sowing time is, in the end, mostly his own or his neighbour's, warranted by acquaintance rather than by law. Indian seed law's distinctive achievement, across both statutes, is to have built its formal systems around that fact instead of against it, granting rights where rights can operate, in the commercial market, and withholding prohibition where prohibition would only teach contempt for law, in the farm economy that feeds the country.

## Reading the National Register as evidence

The National Register of Plant Varieties is usually treated as a bureaucratic artifact, but read as a whole it is evidence about who breeds in India and what the Act has actually been used for. Its first characteristic is the weight of extant varieties. When registration opened, the register filled with varieties already in the public domain, above all the released varieties of the public research system, notified under the Seeds Act and registered by the institutions that bred them or by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research on their behalf. This was partly defensive: registration converted a public variety into a recorded title, establishing the public system's authorship against future private claims built on the same material. It was partly fiscal, since a registered extant variety can be licensed for royalty where an unregistered one could only be given away. And it shaped the register's character for years, because a register dominated by the existing stock tells a different story from a register of novel breeding: the Act's first decade was spent recording the past of Indian plant breeding as much as incentivising its future.

Its second characteristic is the farmers' variety stream, which has no parallel in any other register in the world. Farmers and farming communities, often assisted by agricultural universities, non-governmental organisations and the Authority's own facilitation, have registered varieties they selected and conserved, and the denominations recorded include landraces of rice, millets, pulses and vegetables whose names carry the geography and the community of their origin. The legal effect of such a registration is double-edged in a way the holders do not always appreciate. It gives the community a title it can assert, and it fixes the variety's identity and provenance in a public record that defeats later misappropriation, which is the biopiracy defence operating as designed. It also subjects the community's variety to the same maintenance discipline as a company's, fees after the concession structure, renewal at the statutory intervals, and the ever-present possibility that a variety conserved precisely because it was outside commerce acquires a paper existence that commerce can then trade. The register's third characteristic is what is scarce on it: essentially derived varieties, the category through which the Act polices cosmetic derivation, figure little in practice, because derivation disputes are expensive to prove against the initial variety's holder and the authorisation requirement has channeled would-be derivations into negotiation rather than registration contests; and private new varieties, while steadily present, are concentrated in the crops where private breeding investment was already strong, vegetables, cotton, maize and hybrid rice prominent among them, which confirms that the Act accelerated an existing geography of breeding investment rather than redrawing it.

Read with those three characteristics in mind, the register supports a measured conclusion about the sui generis experiment. The Act did not create a breeding industry; one existed, in the public system, before it. It did not redirect private investment toward orphan crops; the market did that deciding, not the statute. What it did was to give every participant in the existing system, the public institute, the private breeder, the individual farmer-breeder and the conserving community, a common legal language of title in which claims could be stated, recorded and, on the single occasion it mattered most, defended. A register's deepest function is evidentiary: it converts disputes about memory and reputation into disputes about a record, and the FL 2027 litigation, for all its cost, was argued throughout on documents, dates, categories and register entries. That is the rule of law operating in a field where, a generation earlier, there was no law to rule.

## Three identity systems: denomination, label and brand

A final distinction clears up most remaining confusion about the branded seed line, and it concerns the three different identity systems that attach to a bag of seed. The denomination is the variety's official generic name under the 2001 Act, assigned at registration, unique within its species, and free for all to use as the name of the variety; it functions like the generic name of a medicine, identifying what the thing is rather than whose it is. The label is the Seeds Act's instrument, the compulsory statement of kind, variety, germination, purity, lot and producer that travels with notified seed offered for sale; it identifies the physical contents and makes factual claims about them that the seller must be able to prove. The brand, the trade mark and get-up under which a company markets its seed, identifies commercial origin, and is governed by trade mark law, which neither of the two seed statutes displaces. A single packet can carry all three identities at once, and the three systems answer to three different statutes and three different wrongs: misuse the denomination and the plant varieties register is engaged, falsify the label and the Seeds Act prosecution follows, imitate the brand and the trade mark owner sues.

Section 39's branded seed proviso is best understood as the point where the first and third systems touch. The farmer may sell seed of the protected variety, which necessarily involves naming the variety he is selling, since a buyer of seed is buying a denomination's promise. What he may not do is sell it as branded seed, packaged and labelled in a manner indicating that the seed is of the protected variety in the sense the Explanation specifies, that is, held out under the identity the registration created, in a container that appropriates the variety's commercial presentation. The line the proviso draws is therefore not between speech and silence about the variety's name, but between the farmer selling his produce, identified by its denomination as any seed must be, and the farmer entering the breeder's market presentation, where the packet itself asserts the registered identity. Courts have not yet had to trace that line in a decided case, because the FL 2027 farmers were sued for growing the variety rather than for branding its seed, and the distinction's precise location, how much labelling turns farm produce into branded seed, remains one of the Act's genuinely open questions, to be described structurally rather than resolved by assertion: it will turn, when it comes, on whether the container and label indicate the protected variety as the source-identity of the seed, and not merely as its description. Until then, the prudent farmer sells his produce as his produce, and the prudent breeder aims his enforcement at commercial multiplication and branded sale, and the space between those two prudences is the space the statute intended both to occupy.

## The fourth neighbour: the Biological Diversity Act 2002

The plant varieties Act does not operate alone even within the law of genetic resources, and a complete map must mark its boundary with the Biological Diversity Act 2002, enacted a year later to give effect to the Convention on Biological Diversity. The biodiversity statute regulates access to Indian biological resources: it requires approval of the National Biodiversity Authority for specified access and use by foreign persons and entities, and, in the provision that touches this article's subject directly, it restricts the grant of intellectual property rights, in or outside India, based on research or information on a biological resource obtained from India, without the prior approval of the National Biodiversity Authority. A breeder who develops a variety from Indian genetic material and seeks protection therefore potentially faces two statutory regimes with two different benefit logics: the plant varieties Act, whose benefit sharing is breeder-funded, claim-driven and channelled through the Gene Fund toward farmers and communities, and the biodiversity Act, whose access and benefit sharing is state-mediated, approval-driven and channelled through the biodiversity authorities and funds. The two systems were drafted in the same policy moment, in the aftermath of the turmeric, neem and basmati controversies that made biopiracy a household word, and they overlap without interlocking: neither statute's clearance discharges the other's, and the plant varieties application's passport data disclosure, recording the geographical origin of parental material, is the practical bridge between them, since it is the document from which a biodiversity question would first be spotted.

For the ordinary run of cases the overlap is theoretical, because most Indian varieties are bred by Indian institutions from material lawfully in their collections, and the biodiversity approvals bite principally on foreign access and on intellectual property claims asserted abroad. Its significance for this article is architectural. India's answer to the global argument over genetic resources was not a single statute but a settlement across several: property in varieties with farmers' rights inside it, sovereign control over access to resources, quality discipline over the seed trade, and environmental control over engineered organisms. The settlement's coherence is debatable, and its administration is plainly fragmented across ministries and authorities, but its existence explains why arguments that treat the plant varieties Act as if it were the whole of Indian seed law keep misfiring: the Act is one instrument in a suite, drafted to leave the other questions to the other instruments.

## The open questions the statutes leave

A pillar account should close its analysis by naming what remains unsettled, in durable terms. Under the 2001 Act, five questions stand open. The location of the branded seed line, between describing a variety and holding seed out under its protected identity, awaits a decided case. The threshold of derivation for essentially derived varieties, how different is different enough to escape the initial breeder's authorisation requirement, has been administered more than litigated, and its technical criteria will be tested when a valuable variety attracts a near-copy. The relationship between the defensive plea of section 39(1)(iv), the innocent infringement shield of section 42 and the infringement definition of section 64 will need a court's full attention the first time a breeder sues a farmer who pleads all three. The affirmative machinery's scale, whether benefit sharing and community compensation can be made to move resources in proportion to the register's size, is an administrative question the Authority's practice will answer over time or not at all. And the statute's coverage of the newest breeding technologies, varieties produced by genome editing and other techniques that leave no transgene, will test the DUS framework's assumption that a variety's identity is written in characteristics a field trial can observe, a question the environmental regime is confronting in parallel through its own categories.

Under the 1966 Act, the open question is singular and legislative: whether Parliament will enact a replacement, and if it does, whether the replacement resolves or merely re-stages the three-way contest among industry discipline, farmer protection and state administration that has defeated every attempt since 2004. The draft released for public comment in November 2025 proposed mandatory registration of varieties for sale and substantially enhanced penalties, and the response to it rehearsed the familiar positions, which suggests that the 1966 Act's longevity owes less to inertia than to the absence of a coalition for any particular successor. Until that changes, Indian seed law remains what this article has described: a quality statute from the Green Revolution, a licensing order from the essential commodities regime, an intellectual property statute that protects both sides of the seed question at once, an environmental regime for engineered crops, and a biodiversity regime for the resources beneath them all, five instruments whose joints the practitioner must know, because the seed trade, the breeding industry and the farmer all live in the joints.

## The release system: how a public variety reaches notification

Between the breeder's plot and the section 5 notification stands an evaluation machine that the Seeds Act presupposes but does not itself create, and no account of the quality regime is complete without it. Public varieties in India are developed overwhelmingly within the coordinated research system coordinated by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research, in which a candidate variety is tested across agro-climatic zones over successive seasons in the All India Coordinated Research Project for its crop, against the best existing varieties, for yield, disease resistance, quality and stability. A variety that survives identification in that system is proposed for release: varieties of national importance go to the Central Sub-Committee on Crop Standards, Notification and Release of Varieties for Agricultural Crops, and varieties of state importance to the corresponding state committee, and on the committee's recommendation the variety is notified under section 5. Notification is thus the legal shadow of an agronomic judgement, and the Seeds Act's list of notified kinds and varieties is, in effect, the roll of Indian public plant breeding.

The production chain that follows notification runs on an indent system. The Department of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare collects indents for breeder seed from state governments and seed producing agencies, the Indian Council of Agricultural Research organises breeder seed production by the breeding institutions and agricultural universities that maintain the varieties, and the resulting breeder seed passes to foundation seed production and then to certified seed multiplication by the National Seeds Corporation, the state seed corporations, cooperatives and private producers participating in the chain. The chain's integrity is the certification system's whole concern, and its chronic stresses are mundane: indents placed late, breeder seed production shortfalls in a bad season, multiplication ratios that differ by crop, and the lag of two or three seasons between a variety's notification and certified seed reaching districts in quantity, during which the informal system, farmers obtaining the new variety's seed from demonstration plots and early adopters, often diffuses the variety faster than the formal chain. That last phenomenon, diffusion ahead of certification, is neither illegal nor a failure of the statutes: it is the informal sector performing its historic role, and the 2001 Act's design, which protects the farmer's circulation of seed rather than policing it, is the later statute's recognition of the same reality the release system had always quietly depended upon.

## The farmer as applicant: registering a farmers' variety in practice

The farmers' variety is the register's most distinctive entry, and its practical pathway shows the Act's intentions colliding gently with administrative reality. A farming community that has maintained a landrace, say a scented rice of a particular valley or a drought-hardy millet of a dry district, and wishes to register it begins where any applicant begins, with an application to the Registry, but the materials it can offer differ from a company's. It has no laboratory records and no dated first sale; what it has is the variety itself, the community's knowledge of its cultivation and characteristics, and its presence in the fields over generations. The Act accommodates this: the farmers' variety is defined by traditional cultivation and evolution by farmers, or by common knowledge among them about a wild relative or land race, and the application may be made by the farmer, a group or community of farmers, or a person authorised on their behalf, which in practice has meant agricultural universities, Krishi Vigyan Kendras, non-governmental organisations and the Authority's own outreach carrying the paperwork for communities whose asset is knowledge rather than documentation. The fee exemption philosophy of section 39(3), and concessional fee schedules for farmers' varieties, lower the financial barrier; the real barrier is the DUS process, since a landrace is genetically heterogeneous by nature and by design, maintained as a population rather than a uniform line, and the adapted examination the Authority applies to farmers' varieties, assessing distinctness and the essential characteristics without demanding the uniformity of a bred line in the same degree, is therefore not a concession to sentiment but a technical necessity without which the category would be empty.

Registration, when it comes, names the community or its representatives as holders and records the denomination, and its effects are chiefly three. It establishes prior art and provenance against any later claimant, Indian or foreign, who might seek rights over the same or derived material. It entitles the holders to the section 28 right for the farmers' variety, so that commercial multiplication of the community's variety by an outside seed business requires its licence, converting the community from a source others drew upon freely into a licensor at the table. And it connects the variety to the Act's reward and benefit apparatus, since a registered farmers' variety in the ancestry of a later commercial variety grounds a benefit sharing claim in a way an unrecorded contribution struggles to do. Against these gains stand the burdens of maintenance, renewal at the statutory interval and response to any opposition or revocation proceeding, burdens that fall on communities least equipped with legal assistance, so that the survival of farmers' variety registrations depends heavily on the institutional patrons who helped file them. The category's record, a substantial and growing roll of denominations held by farmers and communities, is therefore best read as an achievement of assisted participation, and its fragility as a standing argument for the Authority to treat shepherding farmers' titles as part of its mandate rather than as registry routine.

## The import gate: quarantine and the movement of germplasm

One further instrument completes the practical map, at the border. Seed and planting material entering India passes a plant quarantine gate erected not by either seed statute but by the Destructive Insects and Pests Act 1914 and the Plant Quarantine (Regulation of Import into India) Order 2003 made under it, administered by the Directorate of Plant Protection, Quarantine and Storage, with the National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources at New Delhi processing germplasm imports for research, including the material from which new varieties are bred. The gate's purpose is biosecurity: imported seed can carry pests, pathogens and weed seed that Indian agriculture does not have, and the quarantine order conditions import on phytosanitary certification, inspection and, for specified material, post-entry quarantine growing. Section 17 of the Seeds Act adds the quality layer at the same border, restricting import of seed of notified kinds to seed conforming to the minimum limits and bearing the prescribed label, and the plant varieties Act adds the property layer, since an imported variety may be registered in India only within its novelty windows and only on the disclosures section 18 demands. The three layers answer, characteristically, three different questions about the same consignment: is it safe, is it good, and whose is it. A practitioner who asks only one of the three will eventually be surprised by the other two, which is as good a summary as any of what this cluster of statutes, taken together, requires a reader to hold in mind at once.

## The tag on the bag: certification classes and what the colours certify

The certification system announces itself to the buyer through a tag, and the tag's colour is a compressed statement of where in the multiplication chain the seed stands. Breeder seed, the nucleus material produced under the breeder's direct supervision and used to raise foundation seed, carries a golden yellow tag. Foundation seed, the progeny of breeder seed produced on seed farms and approved farms under certification supervision, carries a white tag. Certified seed, the progeny of foundation seed produced for sale to farmers, carries an azure blue tag, and it is the blue tag that the purchasing farmer ordinarily sees and that has become the market's shorthand for assured seed. Truthfully labelled seed, which bypasses the certification chain and rests on the producer's declaration under the Seeds Act and Rules, is identified by an opel green label, so that the two species of assurance, the state's inspected chain and the producer's own word, are visually distinguishable at the point of sale for anyone who knows the code.

The code's weakness is that most buyers do not know it, and a colour system communicates only to the initiated. The standards behind the tag, the Indian Minimum Seed Certification Standards, prescribe for each crop the isolation distances that keep a seed plot from cross-pollinating with neighbouring crops, the field inspection counts at which off-types and diseased plants are rogued out, the maximum permissible off-type percentages, and the laboratory standards for germination, physical purity, genetic purity and seed health and moisture that the harvested lot must meet before the tag issues. The tag therefore certifies a process as much as a product: it says that this seed was grown apart, inspected standing, sampled and tested, and passed. When a certified lot nevertheless fails in a farmer's field, the failure analysis runs back through that process, and the certification agency's records of the seed plot and the sample are the evidence, which is why section 10's revocation power and the agency's inspection archive matter beyond their modest place in the statute's text. The farmer reading the tag in a dealer's shop is reading the visible tip of the 1966 Act's entire administrative philosophy: that in a market where quality cannot be seen, the state will manufacture trust procedurally, lot by lot and tag by tag, and will stake the credibility of its own agencies on the colour of a piece of card.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Seeds Act 1966 regulate?

The Seeds Act 1966, Act 54 of 1966, regulates the quality of seed offered for sale. It empowers the Central Government to notify kinds and varieties of seed under section 5, to prescribe minimum limits of germination and purity and the particulars a label must carry under section 6, and it makes the sale of notified seed conditional on conformity with those limits and a truthful label under section 7. It establishes seed laboratories, seed analysts and seed inspectors, and a voluntary certification system run by state certification agencies. It does not regulate who owns a variety, and it confers no intellectual property right on anyone.

### Q: What is the difference between certified seed and truthfully labelled seed?

Certified seed has passed through the certification chain supervised by a state certification agency under sections 8 to 11 of the Seeds Act 1966: the seed crop is grown under isolation requirements, inspected in the field, sampled and tested in a laboratory, and sold with a certification tag, azure blue for certified class. Truthfully labelled seed skips that chain. The producer sells it on his own declaration, with the label the Act and the Seeds Rules require, stating kind, variety, germination and purity. The declaration is legally enforceable and a false label is an offence, but no agency has inspected the crop. Certified seed carries an institutional guarantee; truthfully labelled seed carries the producer's word backed by prosecution if the word is false.

### Q: What does the Plant Varieties and Farmers Rights Act protect?

The Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001, Act 53 of 2001, protects plant varieties as intellectual property. On registration, it gives the breeder of a new, extant, farmers' or essentially derived variety an exclusive right under section 28 to produce, sell, market, distribute, import and export the variety, for a term fixed by section 24. In the same statute it protects farmers: section 39 entitles farmers to save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share and sell farm produce including seed of a protected variety, short of selling branded seed, and protects farmers' varieties, conservation work and claims to compensation. It protects neither seed quality in trade, which the Seeds Act governs, nor genetically modified organisms, which environmental law governs.

### Q: What are the DUS criteria for registering a plant variety?

DUS stands for distinctness, uniformity and stability, the three qualities section 15 of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 requires of a registrable variety, with novelty added for a new variety. Distinctness means the variety is clearly distinguishable from every variety of common knowledge at filing. Uniformity means it is sufficiently uniform in its relevant characteristics, allowing for the variation its mode of propagation produces. Stability means those characteristics remain unchanged after repeated propagation. The criteria are not assessed on paper: the variety is grown in DUS trials at designated centres under crop-specific guidelines framed by the Authority and compared with reference varieties over growing seasons.

### Q: What is a farmers variety and how is it registered?

A farmers' variety is a variety traditionally cultivated and evolved by farmers in their fields, or a wild relative or land race of a variety about which farmers possess common knowledge, as the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 defines it. It is registered by application to the Plant Varieties Registry, made by the farmer, a group or community of farmers, or a person authorised for them; universities and other institutions often assist with the application. The variety must meet distinctness, uniformity and stability criteria as adapted for farmers' varieties, since a landrace is a population rather than a uniform line. Registration names the farmers or community as holders and gives them the breeder's exclusive right for that variety.

### Q: Can a farmer save and sell seed of a variety protected under the Plant Varieties Act?

Yes. Section 39(1)(iv) of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 states that a farmer is deemed to be entitled to save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share or sell his farm produce including seed of a variety protected under the Act, in the same manner as he was entitled before the Act came into force. The clause opens with the words notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, so the breeder's exclusive right in section 28 does not override it. One limit applies: the farmer may not sell branded seed of the protected variety, meaning seed packaged and labelled as being of that variety. Unbranded sale of his own produce as seed is protected.

### Q: How does benefit sharing work under the plant varieties Act?

When a variety is registered under section 26 of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001, the Authority invites claims for benefit sharing. Any person, group or organisation that contributed genetic material to the variety's development may claim, and the Authority determines the amount payable by the breeder, considering the extent and nature of the genetic material contributed and the commercial utility and demand for the variety. The breeder deposits the amount in the National Gene Fund. The application-stage requirement that breeders disclose complete passport data of parental lines, including the geographical source of material and any community contribution, supplies the record on which claims are assessed.

### Q: What is the National Gene Fund?

The National Gene Fund is the fund constituted under section 45 of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001. Benefit sharing amounts determined under section 26, compensation amounts payable under the community rights provision in section 41 and the farmers' provisions, royalties and other receipts specified by the Act are credited to it. The Fund is applied to paying benefit sharing and compensation, to supporting the conservation and sustainable use of genetic resources, and to schemes for the benefit of farming communities that conserve such resources. It is the financial channel through which the Act's promise, that the value created by a registered variety flows partly back to those who conserved its genetic base, is meant to operate.

### Q: What is an essentially derived variety under the PPV and FR Act?

An essentially derived variety is a variety predominantly derived from an initial variety, or from a variety itself derived from the initial variety, which retains the expression of the essential characteristics of the initial variety but is clearly distinguishable from it. Section 23 of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 governs its registration. The category exists to stop a breeder from making a minor, cosmetic change to another breeder's variety and claiming a fresh exclusive right over what is in substance the same variety. An essentially derived variety can be registered only with the authorisation of the breeder of the initial variety, and the rights over it are worked out between the two breeders on that footing.

### Q: When can the Authority order a compulsory licence for a registered plant variety?

Section 47 of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 allows any person interested to apply to the Authority for a compulsory licence, but only after three years have passed from the date of registration of the variety. The applicant must show that the reasonable requirements of the public for seed or other propagating material of the variety have not been satisfied, or that the seed or propagating material is not available to the public at a reasonable price. After hearing the breeder, the Authority may order the breeder to grant a licence on terms it settles, including royalty. The provision is the Act's internal safeguard against a protected variety being withheld from the market or priced beyond reach.

### Q: What happens if a registered variety fails to perform as the breeder claimed?

Section 39(2) of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 answers this. Where propagating material of a registered variety has been sold to a farmer by the breeder or by his authorised agent or licensee, the breeder must disclose the expected performance of the variety under given conditions. If the material fails to provide that expected performance under those conditions, the farmer may claim compensation in the prescribed manner before the Authority. The claim turns on proof: the farmer needs the purchase record, the label and the disclosed performance claim, and evidence of the conditions under which the crop was grown and how it failed. No fee is payable by a farmer in proceedings under the Act.

### Q: Can a registration under the PPV and FR Act be revoked?

Yes. Section 34 of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 empowers the Authority to revoke a registration and the protection granted with it. The grounds include that the registration was granted on the basis of incorrect information, that it was granted to a person who was not eligible for it, that the breeder failed to provide the information, documents or material the Act requires, that the breeder failed to provide an alternative denomination when directed, that the breeder failed to supply propagating material to a compulsory licensee, and that the grant of protection is not in the public interest. Revocation is not automatic: the Authority acts on application or inquiry, after giving the breeder an opportunity to be heard, and its order is appealable to the High Court.

### Q: What can a researcher do with a protected variety without the breeder's permission?

Section 30 of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 preserves the researchers' right. Any person may use a registered variety for conducting experiments or research, and may use it as an initial source of variation for the purpose of creating other varieties, without the breeder's authorisation. This keeps the breeding process itself open, since every new variety is bred from existing varieties. One boundary applies: where a protected variety is used repeatedly as a parental line for the commercial production of another variety, rather than as a starting point for genuinely new breeding, the authorisation of the breeder of the protected variety is required. Research and breeding are free; repeated commercial parasitism on the protected variety is not.

### Q: What happened in the PepsiCo potato variety case?

PepsiCo India held registration, granted on 1 February 2016 as an extant variety, for the potato variety FL 2027 used for its Lay's crisps. In 2018 and 2019 it sued Gujarat potato farmers for infringement, claiming over one crore rupees in damages against individual farmers; after a national campaign by farmer organisations it withdrew the suits unconditionally in May 2019. The activist Kavitha Kuruganti then sought revocation, and on 3 December 2021 the Authority revoked the registration, citing incorrect information in the application and public interest, and later rejected renewal. A single judge of the Delhi High Court upheld the revocation on 5 July 2023. A division bench set the revocation aside on 9 January 2024, and the Supreme Court declined to interfere on 5 August 2026.

### Q: Are plant varieties patentable in India?

No. Section 3(j) of the Patents Act 1970 excludes from the definition of invention plants and animals in whole or any part thereof, other than micro-organisms, and essentially biological processes for the production or propagation of plants and animals. A plant variety as such therefore cannot be patented in India, however it was developed. Plant varieties are protected instead under the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001, the sui generis system India built to meet its TRIPS obligation. Distinct questions can arise about particular biotechnological inventions, such as a gene construct or a process, and those have been litigated separately, but no patent can claim the variety itself.

### Q: Why did India not join UPOV?

UPOV, the International Union for the Protection of New Varieties of Plants, offers a model breeders' rights law, and its 1991 Act treats the farmer's saving of seed as an optional exception that member states may allow within reasonable limits, on the farmer's own holding, and subject to safeguarding the legitimate interests of the breeder. Indian policymakers and campaigners, Suman Sahai of the Gene Campaign prominently among them, argued that this model was built for commercial agriculture in industrialised countries and would restrict the saving, exchange and sale of farm-saved seed on which Indian smallholders depend. Article 27.3(b) of TRIPS allowed an effective sui generis system instead, so India legislated its own, and granted farmers positive rights rather than a bounded privilege.

### Q: Why has the Seed Bill never been passed?

A Seeds Bill introduced in the Rajya Sabha in December 2004 proposed replacing the Seeds Act 1966 with compulsory registration of all varieties sold, stronger penalties and a compensation route for failed seed. The Parliamentary Standing Committee recommended extensive changes, and although revised versions were prepared around 2010, and fresh drafts were circulated in 2019 and again for public comment in 2025, none was enacted. The stalemate is structural: the seed industry wants registration and market discipline, farmer organisations resist compulsory registration and demand price regulation and internal compensation machinery, and states guard their administrative role. No draft has yet assembled a majority, so the 1966 Act remains in force.

### Q: What is the Seed (Control) Order 1983 and why does it matter to seed dealers?

The Seed (Control) Order 1983 is a control order made under section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act 1955. It requires every person carrying on the business of selling, exporting or importing seed to hold a licence from the licensing authority, subjects the licence to conditions about sourcing, records and premises, and empowers inspectors to enter, inspect and stop sales in case of contravention. For a seed dealer it is the operative law of daily business: the Seeds Act 1966 sets the quality standards and labelling duties for notified seed, but it is the control order that decides whether he may trade at all, and licence suspension or cancellation is the sanction with immediate commercial effect.

### Q: Does the Seeds Act 1966 punish the sale of spurious seed, and how strongly?

It punishes the sale of seed that contravenes the Act or the Seeds Rules, which includes seed of a notified kind that fails the prescribed germination and purity limits or lacks the required truthful label, and section 20 permits forfeiture of the seed on conviction. But the penalties in section 19 are the weakest part of the statute: a fine extending to five hundred rupees for a first offence, and on a subsequent conviction imprisonment extending to six months, or a fine extending to one thousand rupees, or both. Those figures were set in 1966 and never revised, which is why enforcement relies heavily on stop-sale directions and licence action under the Seed (Control) Order 1983, and why every Seeds Bill has proposed far higher penalties.

### Q: Who regulates genetically modified crops?

Neither the Seeds Act 1966 nor the plant varieties Act. The environmental release of genetically engineered organisms is regulated under the Rules for the Manufacture, Use, Import, Export and Storage of Hazardous Micro-organisms and Genetically Engineered Organisms or Cells, 1989, made under the Environment (Protection) Act 1986. The Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee in the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change appraises proposals for environmental release, with the Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation overseeing contained research. That machinery approved Bt cotton in 2002 and considered Bt brinjal before a moratorium was imposed in 2010. A genetically modified variety that clears that regime still needs Seeds Act compliance to be sold and can seek registration under the plant varieties Act.
