The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte is the book readers quote most and finish least. Its opening sentences circulate everywhere, detached from the narrative they were written to govern, while the narrative itself, dense with the names of French politicians dead for more than a century and a half, defeats readers who arrived expecting a treatise and found a chronicle. That mismatch between fame and finishability is the problem this guide exists to solve. It supplies the scaffolding the text assumes: a timeline of the French crisis from the February revolution of 1848 to the coup of December 1851, a cast organised by the class fractions Marx assigns to each actor, an explanation of the celebrated opening that separates what Marx credited to Hegel from what Marx added himself, and a sustained account of why this short, furious, partisan book has become the standing internal evidence against the crudest version of the doctrine attached to its author's name.

![The Eighteenth Brumaire read closely, tragedy and farce, class fractions and Bonaparte - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The organising claim can be named at once, because a reader who holds it from the first page will find the whole book rearranged around it. The claim is the determinism counter-text: the Eighteenth Brumaire is the strongest evidence against reading Marx as an economic determinist, precisely because it is his most detailed application of his own method to a concrete political sequence. A determinist text would move from economic position to political conduct by a short and reliable road. This book moves by every other road available. Classes split into fractions and the fractions pursue opposite strategies. A bourgeoisie trades political power away in order to keep social power intact. A peasantry delivers the decisive vote to a name rather than a programme. An executive machine built across generations of French government grows heavy enough to stand above the society it administers. Tradition, costume, slogan and memory do causal work on nearly every page. Accident keeps intervening: a general dies, an election date falls where it falls, a rival faction refuses a compromise at the one moment compromise might have held. Any honest summary of the book has to report all of that, and a theory of Marx that cannot survive contact with his most admired piece of political analysis is a theory that needs revising. The guide keeps returning to the counter-text claim because the book keeps supplying it with evidence.

A second promise governs the pages ahead. Nothing here requires prior knowledge of French history, and nothing here pretends the history is simpler than it is. Where Marx's narrative depends on a reader knowing who the Legitimists were, or why an assembly elected to write a constitution spent its energy trying to unmake a president, the explanation is given at the point of need. Where Marx's judgements are contested by later historians, the contest is reported rather than smoothed over, because the book is a partisan pamphlet as well as an analysis and its characterisations of individuals are weapons in a fight, not verdicts from a bench. Marx wrote within weeks of the events, from exile, in anger, for a journal an ocean away. The speed and the anger are part of the evidence. So is the brilliance, and the guide takes the strongest objection to that brilliance seriously in its own section: that the class attributions are asserted with more confidence than the evidence offered can carry, and that the prose has charmed readers out of asking for the missing demonstrations. A guide that hid that objection would fail the book, which deserves readers who can admire it and audit it at the same time.

## What the Eighteenth Brumaire is and is not

Begin with the physical facts, because they discipline everything else. Marx wrote the work across the winter of 1851 and 1852, in London, in the immediate aftermath of the coup it anatomises, and it appeared in 1852 in New York, in a journal called Die Revolution published by his friend Joseph Weydemeyer, a German émigré officer turned journalist who had carried Marx's manuscripts across the Atlantic before. The first issue of that journal was given over to the essay entire. A German edition followed only much later, and the second edition of 1869, published in Hamburg, carried a new preface in which Marx looked back across seventeen years, compared his account with the rival treatments by Victor Hugo and by Pierre Joseph Proudhon, and stated what he took his own method to have been. That preface matters to a reader because it is Marx's own retrospective framing of the book, and because the contrasts he draws there, between Hugo's thunderous denunciation of one villain and Proudhon's attempt to derive the coup from prior economic development, locate his own procedure between moral biography and mechanical derivation. He claimed to have shown how the class struggle in France created circumstances that enabled a mediocrity to play a hero's part. The sentence is a fair table of contents for the whole work.

Genre is the first thing new readers get wrong, usually because the book's reputation arrives before the book does. It is not a theoretical treatise with French illustrations. It contains no chapter of definitions, no systematic exposition of a method, no numbered propositions. It is a narrative of roughly three years and nine months of French politics, organised into seven parts, written in a prose that veers between parliamentary reportage, classical allusion, biblical parody, theatre criticism and outright invective. The theory is inside the narrative, carried by the choices of what to explain and what to treat as needing no explanation, and a reader hunting for a detachable doctrine will skim past the places where the analysis actually happens. Those places are often small: a sentence explaining why a faction of the bourgeoisie preferred order to its own parliamentary rule, a paragraph on what a uniform and a famous surname could do among smallholding peasants, an aside on why a phrase repeated by a party spokesman had come to mean the opposite of what it said. Reading the book well means reading it slowly at exactly the points where a summary would speed up.

Nor is it a book about the French Revolution of 1789, despite a title that points at that revolution's calendar. The connection is real but indirect, and the title section below untangles it fully. The subject is the Second Republic, the regime born in February 1848 when the July Monarchy of Louis Philippe collapsed under a banquet campaign, barricades and abdication, and the subject ends with that republic's destruction by its own elected president. Between those poles sits the June Days of 1848, when Parisian workers rose after the closure of the National Workshops and were shot down by troops under General Cavaignac; the presidential election of December 1848, which Louis Bonaparte won by a margin that stunned every organised party; the long parliamentary duel between the Legislative Assembly and the president; the suffrage law of May 1850 that struck millions of voters from the rolls; and the coup of 2 December 1851, staged on the anniversary of Austerlitz and of the first Napoleon's coronation, which dissolved the Assembly, arrested its leaders, and prepared the Second Empire proclaimed a year later. A reader who can hold those five or six stations in mind can follow Marx anywhere in the book, because Marx returns to them the way a dramatist returns to a set.

The book's afterlife explains why a guide is worth writing at all. Political scientists reach for it when they need the ancestor of every later account of executive autonomy. Historians who reject the larger architecture of Marx's thought still assign it, because its narrative energy and its feel for the texture of a political crisis survive the rejection. Examination syllabuses quote the opening paragraph and the peasantry passage and leave the rest in shadow, which produces generations of students who can recite two set pieces and cannot say what happens in part four. Quotation collections strip the opening of its attribution, handing the whole tragedy and farce sentence to Marx or, in the opposite error, to Hegel. Each of those afterlives distorts in its own direction. The sections that follow take the distortions one at a time, but the repair always begins in the same place: with the events, in their order, with their actors named.

One more preliminary deserves its own paragraph, the question of stance. Marx was not a neutral observer of the Second Republic and never claimed to be. He had edited a revolutionary newspaper in the Rhineland during the German events of 1848 and 1849, had been expelled from Prussia, then from Paris, and was writing from a London exile sustained partly by journalism and partly by Engels in Manchester. He despised Louis Bonaparte, feared and loathed the Party of Order, and had watched the movement he supported defeated in country after country. The Eighteenth Brumaire is written from inside that defeat, and its judgements of persons carry the marks of it: deputies are savaged, generals are mocked, the president is treated as a grotesque adventurer propped up by a drunken praetorian circle Marx calls the Society of December 10. Later historiography has revised plenty of these portraits, in both directions, and the guide flags the sharpest divergences where they occur. What the partisanship does not do is disqualify the analysis, any more than a prosecutor's brief is disqualified by being a prosecutor's brief. It means the argument must be checked at the points where the rhetoric runs hottest, which is a rule for reading any strong political writing and a rule this book rewards handsomely.

## Why the book carries a calendar in its title

The title names a date in a calendar that no French citizen had used for half a century, and unpacking it repays the effort because the title is the book's argument in code. During the French Revolution, the revolutionary government replaced the inherited calendar with a republican one, renaming the months after seasonal phenomena. Brumaire, from the French word for fog, was the second month of that calendar, running from late October into late November. On 18 Brumaire of the year VIII, which converts to 9 November 1799, the general Napoleon Bonaparte overthrew the Directory in a coup d'état and installed the Consulate, with himself as First Consul, on the road to the Empire he proclaimed in 1804. The original Eighteenth Brumaire is therefore Napoleon's seizure of power, the moment the revolution was closed down by its own most successful general.

Marx's subject, the coup of 2 December 1851, was carried out by the first Napoleon's nephew, Louis Bonaparte, a man whose entire political capital consisted of the family name and the legend attached to it. By titling the nephew's coup with the uncle's date, Marx performs the book's central comparison before the narrative even begins. The uncle's coup is the original, staged by a figure of genuine military and administrative gifts at the head of a revolutionary army. The nephew's coup is the repetition, staged by a political adventurer whose claim on power is inherited costume. Tragedy and farce are already present in the title, in calendrical form, and a reader who grasps that has grasped the frame into which every episode of the narrative will be fitted. The dating carries one more layer. Louis Bonaparte chose his own anniversaries with care, striking on 2 December, the date of his uncle's coronation in 1804 and of the victory at Austerlitz in 1805. Marx's title answers that self-dramatisation with a colder anniversary of his own choosing, as if to say that the date the nephew should be filed under is not the coronation but the coup, and not even his own coup but his uncle's.

The calendar reference misleads two kinds of reader, and the guide corrects both now rather than later. Readers who know a little revolutionary history sometimes open the book expecting an account of 1799 and are bewildered to find railway shares, suffrage laws and parliamentary speeches from 1850. Readers who know none sometimes assume Brumaire is a person, a place, or a faction. Neither error survives the first sitting if the scaffolding is in place. Brumaire is a month. The eighteenth of it is the day the first Bonaparte ended a republic in all but name. Marx's title claims that the second Bonaparte, in ending the Second Republic in fact, repeated the day in a lower register. Everything the nephew does in the narrative is measured against the uncle's shadow, by his enemies, by his supporters, and above all by himself, and the measuring is the tragedy and farce machinery at work in concrete political form rather than as a slogan about history in general.

## The opening lines, attribution first

No passage in Marx is quoted more often, and none is quoted with its attribution in worse repair. The book begins with a remark about Hegel, proceeds to the most famous sentence Marx ever wrote about historical agency, and closes its first paragraph with an image of the dead weighing on the living. Each element does different work, and each is regularly misdescribed in a different way, so the guide takes them in order and slowly.

### Did Marx say history repeats itself first as tragedy, then as farce?

Partly. He wrote that Hegel remarked great historical events occur twice, and added that Hegel forgot to say the first time as tragedy and the second as farce. The famous formulation is Marx's addition to an observation he credited to Hegel.

Sit with the structure of that sentence, because the structure is the correction. Marx does not write that Hegel said events recur first as tragedy and then as farce. He writes that Hegel remarked somewhere that great world-historical facts and personages occur twice, and then, with a schoolmaster's raised eyebrow, that Hegel forgot to add the grading. The repetition belongs to the remark attributed to Hegel. The grading, tragedy for the first occurrence and farce for the second, is Marx's contribution, offered as a completion of Hegel rather than as a quotation from him. Quotation collections that hand the entire sentence to Hegel credit him with a joke he never made, at least not in any passage scholars have located, and the honest scholarly position is that the remark Marx attributes to him has no exact source in Hegel's published writings, however Hegelian its spirit may be. Collections that hand the entire sentence to Marx erase the attribution Marx himself built into it, and with it the sentence's tone, which is that of a pupil improving a master's observation in the act of borrowing it. A whole article in this series is devoted to such cases, and readers who enjoy attribution puzzles will find the catalogue at [misattributed marx quotes](/2013/08/15/misattributed-marx-quotes/), where the Brumaire opening keeps company with other sentences that have wandered away from their authors.

One further detail belongs here, because it shows the line being born in conversation rather than descending from a system. In the weeks before Marx drafted the book, Engels had written to him from Manchester joking about the coup in strikingly similar terms, treating the nephew's performance as a parody staged on the uncle's anniversary. The correspondence matters for two reasons. It reminds the reader that Marx and Engels thought together, in letters, at speed, and that formulations polished in the published text often began as quips in that exchange. It also underlines how immediate the tragedy and farce contrast was: not a law of history derived from a philosophy seminar, but the obvious description, to two exiles watching from across the Channel, of a nephew in a borrowed uniform re-enacting the founding gesture of the family legend and getting the tone wrong.

### Why does the opening passage matter to the whole book?

Because the opening is the method in miniature. It announces that actors understand their own actions through inherited images, that repetition in politics is never exact, and that the gap between an event and its borrowed costume is where analysis must work. Every later chapter applies that announcement to parties, classes and slogans.

The second movement of the opening is the sentence about agency, and it deserves the same slow treatment as the first. Men make their own history, Marx writes, but they do not make it just as they please. The quotation is fourteen words in the standard translation and is usually amputated at the comma in popular use, where it becomes a banner for voluntarism, as though Marx had announced that human beings are the unconstrained authors of their world. Read whole, the sentence is a balanced instrument with two pans. Human beings genuinely act, choose, organise and fight; the book that follows is full of their decisions and would be unintelligible without them. They act, however, inside circumstances transmitted from the past, circumstances they did not select and cannot rearrange at will: property relations, state machines, party organisations, memories, names. The analytical programme of the entire book is contained in the balance. For every political outcome, ask what the actors were trying to do, and then ask what the inherited circumstances permitted, encouraged or forbade. The determinism counter-text rests heavily on this sentence, because a writer who believed economic structure mechanically produced political outcomes would have had no use for its first clause and no need for its second. The sentence instead describes a field of constrained action, and the narrative that follows is the working out of that description across three years of crisis.

Then comes the image that gives the opening its gothic power. The tradition of all dead generations, Marx writes, weighs like a nightmare on the brains of the living. The shorter fragment is the quotable core, and the sentences around it explain the mechanism. In periods of revolutionary crisis, actors anxiously conjure the spirits of the past to their service, borrowing names, battle slogans and costumes to present the new scene of history in borrowed disguise. Marx illustrates from earlier revolutions: the Reformation dressing itself in the apostolic past, the English revolution speaking through the Old Testament, the French revolutionaries of 1789 performing in Roman costume, draping a bourgeois transformation in the togas of the Roman republic. The point is never antiquarian. Borrowed language does things. It can dignify a transformation and nerve its actors for sacrifices the undisguised content would not inspire. It can also imprison them, because a movement that understands itself through an earlier script will misread its own situation, fight the previous war, and discover too late that the costume has been directing the performance. The Second Republic, in Marx's telling, is haunted in a specifically degraded mode: its actors borrow not from Rome but from the first Napoleonic empire, and its central figure is himself a borrowed name walking.

### What does the image of dead generations weighing on the living actually claim?

It claims that political actors inherit not only institutions and property but a stock of names, slogans, costumes and self-images, and that in moments of crisis they reach for that stock to dignify new struggles. The borrowed past then shapes what the actors believe they are doing, sometimes disguising the content of their own actions.

Hold that claim up against the crudest picture of historical materialism and the counter-text sharpens again. In the crude picture, ideas are foam on economic waves, interesting to the literary but causally weightless. In the opening of the Brumaire, inherited ideas are assigned real force: they select which actions feel available, they supply the vocabulary in which interests are understood, they can raise a movement above its material brief or trap it inside an obsolete one. Marx does not abandon material explanation by granting this force; the borrowing itself is explained by the situation of the borrowers, and the costumes available for borrowing are themselves products of earlier material struggles. What he abandons, if he ever held it, is the shortcut. Between economic position and political conduct the book inserts representation, memory, organisation and theatre, and it never removes them. Readers who meet this machinery in the opening and then watch it operate on the Party of Order, on the petty bourgeoisie, on the peasantry and on Bonaparte himself are reading the book as its preface promised it could be read.

## The story the book tells, in one telling

Before the seven parts are walked through individually, the whole arc deserves a single uninterrupted telling, because Marx's own narration is deliberately tangled, doubling back to explain a faction's prehistory in the middle of describing its manoeuvre, and a reader with the arc already in hand can afford those doublings. The arc has a clean spine. A monarchy falls and a republic is proclaimed amid genuine popular enthusiasm. The republic's provisional government contains socialists in minor posts and governs a country whose majority is peasant, Catholic and frightened of Paris. Elections for a Constituent Assembly return a moderate republican majority with a strong conservative bloc. The closure of the National Workshops, the relief scheme that had become a state within the state, provokes the June Days, and the Assembly's general destroys the Parisian rising with artillery and mass arrests. The republic is now safe for property and traumatised by its own founding violence. A constitution is written. In December 1848 the country elects a president by universal male suffrage, and the name Bonaparte, carried into every village by legend, by almanacs, by the memory of glory and by the hope that a Napoleon would somehow relieve smallholders of taxes and debts, wins more than five million votes against divided opponents.

The second half of the arc is a slow strangulation performed by the victors on themselves. The Legislative Assembly elected in May 1849 is dominated by the Party of Order, a coalition of the two monarchist factions, Legitimists loyal to the elder Bourbon line and Orléanists loyal to the house that had reigned until 1848, united by fear of the left and divided by everything else. The coalition governs energetically against its enemies: it sends French troops to crush the Roman republic, restricts the press, purges teachers, and in May 1850 passes an electoral law whose residence requirements disenfranchise millions of the workers whose votes it fears. Yet the coalition cannot decide what it is for. Its two halves want different kings. Its parliamentarians adore parliamentary rule in the abstract and undermine it in practice, attacking the president they need and defending a constitution that schedules their own collision with him. The president, meanwhile, tours the provinces, reviews troops, cultivates the army, lets his ministers fall and takes them back, and builds a machine of prefects and loyalists inside the state. The Assembly tries to revise the constitution to permit his re-election, fails to reach the required majority in July 1851, and thereby announces to everyone, including him, that his legal term ends in May 1852 while his ambitions do not. In the small hours of 2 December 1851 he strikes: the Assembly is dissolved, its leading deputies arrested in their beds, universal suffrage theatrically restored, the army in the streets, and resistance in Paris and in parts of the provinces broken within days at the cost of killings whose scale historians still argue over. A plebiscite ratifies the coup. A year later the Second Empire is proclaimed. Marx wrote his account of this arc while its final act was still unfolding, and the seventh part of the book turns from narrative to anatomy, asking what kind of state France had grown that such an outcome could grow out of it.

### What happened in France between the February revolution and the coup?

A republic proclaimed in February 1848 passed through the June Days rising, a constitution, the election of Louis Bonaparte as president, a conservative assembly elected in 1849, a suffrage law that struck millions from the rolls, a failed campaign to revise the constitution, and a president preparing to keep power by force.

Keep it beside the text. When a name surfaces in Marx's prose without introduction, as names constantly do, the row in which that name first acts will usually restore the context faster than any footnote, because Marx thinks in phases: who held the initiative, which class arrangement made that possible, and what the episode reveals about the machinery of the whole.

Two cautions belong with the table. The class column reports Marx's assignments, not settled historical fact; later historians have disputed several of them, and the objection section below gives that dispute its full weight. The phase boundaries are also Marx's more than they are the archive's, since he organises the narrative around turning points in the class struggle as he understood it, and other periodisations, centred on ministerial politics or on the provinces, would cut the same years differently. ## Walking the seven parts

Marx divides the book into seven parts, and although the divisions follow the chronology loosely, each part has its own analytical assignment, so that reading them as a mere sequence of events misses the design. Early in the book he also offers a periodisation of the republic's life into three stretches, from February 1848 to the elections of May 1849, from those elections to the spring of 1851, and from there to the coup, and the walkthrough that follows respects his periods while naming the work each part performs. The design can be stated in one sentence: the first parts explain how the republic's victors destroyed their own coalition, the middle parts explain how the parliamentary bourgeoisie destroyed its own rule, and the final part explains what inherited machine was waiting to collect the wreckage.

The first part covers the prehistory and the founding: the character of the July Monarchy, the February days, the provisional government, and the road to the June insurrection. Its assignment is to show the February republic as a compromise nobody designed. The bourgeois republicans who took power wanted a political republic and no social one; the Paris workers who had manned the barricades expected the republic to answer the social question of labour and subsistence; the provinces wanted order and low taxes. Marx lingers over the National Workshops because they embody the contradiction in institutional form, a relief scheme swollen into an armed camp of the unemployed that the Assembly experienced as a threat and the workers experienced as a promise. When the workshops were dissolved, the rising followed, and Marx's account of the June Days is the emotional floor of the whole book. He treats the rising as the first great battle between the two classes into which modern society splits, and its defeat as the event that stripped the republic of its illusions. Whatever one's judgement of that framing, and historians have quarrelled with its neatness, the narrative function is exact: after June, every later actor behaves in the shadow of a demonstrated willingness to shoot, and the fear of a second June organises French politics for the rest of the book.

The second and third parts handle the constitution, the presidential election, and the strange career of the Party of Order in its first ascendancy. The constitutional debates matter to Marx because the document built a collision into the state: a president elected by the whole people for a single four year term, forbidden immediate re-election, set against an assembly also elected by the whole people, each able to claim the nation's mandate and neither able to remove the other constitutionally. He treats this not as a drafting error but as the political form of a society whose classes could not agree on who should rule, and so built two thrones and hoped the occupants would share. The election of December 1848 is then analysed as the peasantry's entrance into national politics as an independent weight, and the portrait of Bonaparte that emerges, a figure Marx presents as the chief of the lumpenproletariat raised to the summit by a peasant vote, is the book's most quoted piece of invective and the point where readers must work hardest to separate the analysis from the contempt. The analytical residue, once the contempt is discounted, is a genuine puzzle he states better than anyone: why did the largest class in France choose as its representative a man whose programme consisted of his surname, and what does that choice reveal about how the class existed politically?

The middle parts, the fourth and fifth in most arrangements of the text, slow down into parliamentary chronicle, and they are where unassisted readers most often stall. The chronicle covers the Party of Order's government: the expedition against the Roman republic, undertaken to please Catholic and legitimist opinion and to crush a sister republic whose existence embarrassed the French one; the laws restricting the press and the clubs; the education law that handed influence to the clergy; the purge of republican officials and teachers; and above all the electoral law of 31 May 1850, which imposed residence conditions that struck roughly three million voters, disproportionately workers, from the rolls. Marx's treatment of that law is a set piece of his method. The Party of Order presented the law as a defence of order against demagogy. Marx reads it as the bourgeoisie publicly sawing through the branch of universal suffrage on which its own legitimacy rested, driven by a fear of the electorate stronger than its attachment to the political forms of its rule. Here the determinism counter-text gathers some of its best evidence, because the act is intelligible only through mediation: a class whose material interest lay in stable property relations pursued that interest by destroying the parliamentary instrument through which it ruled, preferring order without power to power with risk. Economic position predicts the fear; it does not predict the self-amputation, which required the intervening variables of memory, June's trauma, and the availability of Bonaparte as an alternative custodian of order.

The later parts narrate the duel between the Assembly and the president as a kind of institutional farce played in earnest. Bonaparte dismisses ministries the Assembly imposed, tours the provinces making speeches whose meaning shifts with the audience, reviews troops and lets the soldiers' cries go unpunished, loses votes in the Assembly and gains ground everywhere else. The Assembly answers with procedural weapons: inquiries, reprimands, a law on the responsibility of the president that frightens its own authors. Marx's theatre metaphor governs the telling, factions performing historic roles in a play whose ending they refuse to read, and the famous farce grading of the opening stops being a generalisation and becomes a description of technique. Within this chronicle sits the struggle over constitutional revision in the summer of 1851, when the Assembly debated permitting the president's re-election and produced a majority for revision that fell short of the three quarters the constitution demanded. The vote is the hinge of the book. Legally, the president's term now expired the following May, with an assembly and a succession crisis waiting. Politically, every deputy who voted understood that the countdown favoured the man with the army. Marx watches the Legitimists and Orléanists refuse to fuse their dynastic claims even under this pressure, each preferring the risk of Bonaparte to the triumph of the rival line, and the counter-text gains another exhibit: dynastic sentiment, a pure inheritance of tradition, weighing on the brains of the living with measurable consequences for who would hold power in France.

The final part performs the book's second great analytical movement, and the walkthrough reserves the state machinery for its own section below. Here it is enough to mark the turn. After narrating the coup, its provincial resistance and its plebiscitary ratification, Marx steps back from events entirely and asks how the French state had been built, across the absolute monarchy, the first revolution, the first Napoleon, the Restoration and the July Monarchy, into a centralised executive machine that every regime inherited, perfected and used against the society beneath it. The narrative thus ends by reversing its direction of explanation. The opening parts explain politics by the classes contending inside it; the closing part explains the arena itself, the bureaucracy and army that outlast every contender, and finds the coup's deepest condition there. A reader who reaches the final pages understanding that reversal has understood the book's architecture, and can see why later theorists of the state treated the closing pages as a founding text while narrative historians treated the opening ones as a model of political writing. The fuller treatment of autonomy belongs with the [marxist theory of the state guide](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/), which takes up Bonapartism as a concept; this guide keeps to what a reader needs while the pages are turning.

## The cast by class fraction

No feature of the book is more distinctive, or more demanding, than its refusal to let the word bourgeoisie do unassisted work. Where a cruder analysis would place two blocs on the stage, Marx populates it with fractions, and the fractions behave differently because their material lives differ. Readers who keep the fractions straight find the parliamentary chronicle legible; readers who merge them find only a muddle of deputies changing sides for no stated reason. The fullest systematic treatment in this series sits in the [marxist theory of class guide](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/), but the Brumaire fractions have their own specific shapes, and the guide sets them out here as Marx draws them.

The finance aristocracy is the fraction Marx knew longest, having anatomised it in his writings on the July Monarchy. Bankers, railway and mining concessionaires, great speculators and the crown of the Paris Bourse: under Louis Philippe this fraction had been, in his phrase, the ruling power in its most corrupt form, treating the state budget as its private revenue stream. February stripped it of political supremacy but not of wealth, and in the republic it survives as one wing of the Party of Order, drawn toward the Orléanist dynasty under which it had fattened. Its defining trait in the narrative is a preference for stability purchased at any political price, since credit, concessions and share prices tremble at every barricade. The industrial bourgeoisie, the factory owners and great merchants whose weight lay in the provinces and the manufacturing towns, had chafed under the July Monarchy's financial dominance and wanted a political order that served production rather than speculation. It supplies the Party of Order's other mass, reads its interests through protection, order and cheap administration, and discovers across the book's course that it prefers an authoritarian custodian to the uncertainties of its own parliament. Marx's account of that discovery, the bourgeoisie applauding the destruction of its own political rule to safeguard its social rule, is among the most argued over passages in the book, and the objection section returns to it.

Beneath the great fractions sits the petty bourgeoisie, the class of small shopkeepers, master artisans and minor officials, organised politically in the democratic party of the Mountain, led in the Assembly by Ledru-Rollin and speaking the language of the rights of man. Marx's treatment of this stratum is sympathetic in diagnosis and merciless in prognosis. Its members live between capital and labour, crushed by debt and rent, dreaming of credit reform and cheap government, and their representatives translate those material pressures into a politics of phrase and proclamation, forever announcing the people's sovereignty and forever discovering, at the moment of confrontation, that their class cannot fight as the workers fought in June nor rule as the great bourgeoisie rules. The democratic petty bourgeoisie exits the stage in June 1849, when its protest against the Roman expedition collapses into a shambles and its leaders flee abroad, and Marx marks the exit as the disappearance of the republic's last illusion about itself.

The industrial proletariat is the book's wounded foundation. It makes the February revolution possible, supplies the June insurrection, and after June is disarmed, decapitated by arrests and deportations, disenfranchised in part by the law of May 1850, and present thereafter mainly as the fear that organises everyone else's conduct. Readers expecting the proletariat to drive the narrative will be surprised by its marginality in the later parts, and the marginality is itself an analytical claim: that after a defeat of June's scale, a class can be removed from the political stage for a period while remaining the reason the stage exists. Around and beneath the organised classes swarm the elements Marx gathers under the lumpenproletariat, the declassed debris of every stratum, discharged soldiers, ruined adventurers, vagabonds, sharpers and porters, organised by Bonaparte in the Society of December 10 as a cheering, brawling praetorian charity. Marx's contempt for this layer is total and sometimes ugly, and modern readers are entitled to wince at it; the analytical point underneath the contempt is that a plebiscitary regime needs a clientele that owes it everything and believes in nothing, and that Bonaparte built one deliberately, years before the coup.

### Why do the classes in this book so often act against their apparent interest?

Because their interests reach them filtered through representation, memory and organisation. The bourgeoisie surrenders political power to preserve social power, the petty bourgeoisie follows phrases rather than programmes, and the peasantry votes for a name. Marx's analysis lives in those mediations, which is why the book resists any reading that moves straight from economic position to political act.

Two disciplines help a reader use the fraction analysis without being used by it. First, notice how Marx argues an attribution when he is arguing carefully. At his best he works from a fraction's revenue source and daily pressures: the finance aristocracy's dependence on state credit explains its horror of disorder better than any psychology could; the smallholder's mortgage and tax bill explain the appeal of a name that promised relief; the industrialist's order book explains his tolerance for a sabre government that kept workshops quiet. Attribution earned that way is the method working. Second, notice where the arguing stops and the labelling starts, where a faction's conduct is read back from the class position it has been assigned rather than traced forward from its circumstances. The book contains both procedures, sometimes on the same page, and the capacity to tell them apart while reading is the single most valuable skill the text teaches. It is also the honest preparation for the strongest objection, which holds that the second procedure does more work in the book than its admirers admit.

## The peasantry, the potatoes and the vote

France in 1848 was a country of small farms. The great majority of its people worked the land, and the characteristic figure of the countryside was the smallholding peasant, owner or part owner of a parcel, burdened by mortgage debt to the village usurer, squeezed by taxes that fell with special weight on the countryside, and separated from the politics of Paris by distance, by dialect, by suspicion and by the memory of what revolutions had historically brought to the village: requisitions, conscription and the tax collector. The revolution of 1789 had made this class by breaking the great estates and confirming peasant property, and the Napoleonic legend lived in the cottage as the story of the emperor who had guaranteed that settlement, carried French glory into every corner of Europe, and been taken from the people by foreign kings and domestic traitors. Marx insists on both halves of that inheritance, the material parcel and the luminous legend, because the presidential election of December 1848 cannot be explained by either alone. The smallholders voted for Louis Bonaparte in their millions because the name on the ballot named their property's guarantor, their grievance against the republic's forty five centime tax, and their indifference to every party that spoke the language of the towns.

The celebrated passage arrives in the seventh part, where Marx asks how such a class can be represented at all. The smallholding peasants, he argues, form a vast mass whose members live in similar conditions but enter into no manifold relations with one another; their mode of production isolates them, the parcel sustains a family and not a division of labour, and the French nation is formed by the simple addition of homologous magnitudes, much as potatoes in a sack form a sack of potatoes. The image is cruel and unforgettable, and it is the hinge of the book's most consequential argument about representation. Insofar as millions of families live under conditions that separate their mode of life and interests from those of other classes, they form a class; insofar as the identity of their interests fails to produce community, national connection or political organisation among them, they do not form one. The distinction between sharing a position and acting on it is the subject of the companion treatment at [class in itself and for itself](/2011/10/01/class-in-itself-and-for-itself/), and this guide deliberately leaves the conceptual machinery there. What belongs here is the narrative consequence Marx draws: a class that cannot represent itself must be represented, and its representative must appear as its master, an authority standing above it, sending it rain and sunshine from above. The sentence is the seed of the whole theory of Bonapartism, grown from a sack of potatoes.

### How should a reader follow the peasantry through the narrative?

Track three things. Watch when the smallholding peasants appear as voters delivering the presidency, when their taxes and mortgages are discussed as material grievances, and when Marx asks whether a mass sharing a way of life thereby forms a class. The celebrated sack of potatoes image belongs to that third question and rewards slow reading.

Fairness to the text requires registering a complication that later scholarship pressed hard. Marx's peasants are drawn largely from the standpoint of Paris and of the returns; the countryside of the book can seem passive, a reservoir of votes and superstition. Yet the same narrative records the tax revolts, the democratic socialist organising that spread through rural France after 1849, and the armed provincial resistance to the coup in December 1851, which was in large part a peasant and small town rising and was drowned in arrests and deportations. Historians working the departmental archives have rebuilt that rural radicalism in detail, and their findings sit awkwardly beside the sack of potatoes. The passage survives the correction better than its critics sometimes allow, since its claim concerns the structural difficulty of national self organisation for parcel peasants, not their capacity for local revolt, but the tension is real, and a reader should hold both: the analytical image, and the archives that show the potatoes organising.

## Louis Bonaparte, agent and symptom

Every reader arrives with a verdict on the book's central figure already half formed, because Marx's portrait is so vigorous that it colonises the imagination: the adventurer, the gambler, the chief of a society of drunkards and discharged jailbirds, the mediocre nephew inflated by a name. The portrait is magnificently written and deliberately unfair in the manner of a prosecution, and the historians' disagreement about the man is one of the places where this guide is required to keep both sides visible. Against Marx's grotesque, a line of historians has set a more calculating figure: a conspirator of real patience who had attempted coups in 1836 and 1840, written a coherent if opportunistic programme of Napoleonic ideas, exploited the constitution's contradictions with discipline, prepared the coup through control of the war ministry and the Paris garrison, and managed the army, the prefects and the plebiscite with a professionalism that mediocrity does not explain. Between the portraits lies a genuine historiographical dispute about agency in the crisis, and the book figures in it as evidence for the prosecution rather than as the court's judgement.

Marx's own text, read closely, is more interesting than either caricature, because it holds the man as agent and symptom simultaneously and needs him to be both. As symptom, Bonaparte is what the class deadlock secretes: the bourgeoisie unable to rule in its own name, the peasantry unable to rule in any name, the army and bureaucracy available for hire by whoever embodies order and glory together, the constitution scheduling a vacancy. On that level the coup is overdetermined, and Marx's scorn for biographical explanation is methodological: replace the nephew with another adventurer and the structure would have found its man, though perhaps a slower or clumsier one. As agent, however, the narrative keeps crediting him with the specific moves that turned a favourable structure into a successful coup on a specific night: the ministerial reshuffles that installed his creatures, the provincial tours that tested his reception, the cultivation of the officer corps, the choice of the anniversary, the refusal to be bound by the Assembly's timetable. The double treatment is the book in microcosm. Structure explains why a Bonaparte was possible and even likely; it does not roll the dice for him, and Marx, whatever the determinist caricature says, never pretends otherwise.

### Who was Louis Bonaparte and why does Marx refuse to treat him as a great man?

Louis Bonaparte was the nephew of Napoleon the First, elected president of the republic in December 1848 on an enormous peasant vote, and author of the coup that destroyed the republic. Marx treats his rise as a product of class deadlock and borrowed prestige rather than personal genius, which is precisely the analytical point.

One more thread ties the man to the opening's machinery. Bonaparte's whole career, in Marx's telling, is the farce principle personified: the repetition of the uncle in a lower register, the imperial eagle on the standards of an army whose commander in chief trades in nostalgia, the coronation anniversary honoured by a coup whose proclamation promises to restore the universal suffrage his victims had abolished. The nephew does not merely benefit from the tradition of the dead generation; he is its walking embodiment, a man whose political substance is inherited costume. That is why the book grants him a strange centrality while denying him stature. He is the point at which all the mediations meet: peasant legend, bourgeois exhaustion, military ambition, bureaucratic appetite and the calendar of a dead empire. A reader who finishes the book understanding how a figure can be at once contemptible in Marx's prose and indispensable in Marx's explanation has understood something about the method that no summary of the method conveys.

## The state machinery, the book's second plot

Running beneath the parliamentary narrative, visible whenever Marx pauses to count officials or to trace an institution's pedigree, is a second plot: the growth of the French executive machine into a body heavy enough to act on its own account. The closing part of the book gathers this plot into the open. All revolutions, Marx argues, perfected the machine instead of smashing it. The centralised state power, with its ubiquitous organs of standing army, police, bureaucracy, clergy and judicature, originates in the days of absolute monarchy; the first revolution centralised further; Napoleon perfected the governmental power as an instrument; the Restoration and the July Monarchy added nothing but a division of spoils; and the parliamentary republic, in its struggle against the revolution, strengthened the repressive measures of the executive until the machine stood ready for the adventurer who would ride it. The famous summary describes an executive power with an enormous bureaucratic and military organisation, a complicated state machinery with a host of officials, Marx says half a million strong, beside an army of another half million, an appalling parasitic body that enmeshes French society and chokes its pores. The figures are Marx's own and should be read as his polemical arithmetic rather than as audited statistics, but the structure they dramatise is the argument: a salaried population of functionaries whose material interest is the machine's survival and expansion, whatever flag flies above it.

From that anatomy grows the book's most influential idea, the relative autonomy of the state. Under ordinary parliamentary conditions, the executive serves the ruling class fractions and its independence is a matter of administrative slack. Under the conditions the book narrates, balance of exhausted classes, a bourgeoisie that has abdicated political rule to preserve social rule, a peasantry that can be represented only by a master, the executive can raise itself above society and govern in the name of order, property and religion while pursuing the machine's own appetites and its master's glory. Marx gives the paradox its sharpest form: the state seems to have made itself completely independent, yet Bonaparte still represents a class, the most numerous class of French society, the smallholding peasants, and his regime protects the material order the bourgeoisie requires even while humiliating the bourgeoisie politically. Autonomy, in the book's usage, is therefore relative twice over: real enough that the executive can defy the political wishes of the dominant class, bounded enough that it cannot overturn the property relations that class lives on. Later state theory took the passage and built schools on it, and the concept's career, from its use in debates over modern capitalist states to its role in theories of Bonapartism as a general form, is traced in the [marxist theory of the state guide](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/). For the reader of the narrative, the point to hold is simpler: the machine is the only actor in the book that never loses. Parties dissolve, assemblies are dispersed, dynasties wait in exile, and the prefects file their reports under each successive regime.

### Where does the state machinery become a character in its own right?

In the closing movement, where the executive apparatus of ministries, prefects, police and standing army stops appearing as an instrument in other hands and becomes an interest with its own appetite for survival and growth. Marx counts its officials, traces its roots under earlier regimes, and asks what class arrangement lets it rule above society.

Notice what this second plot does to the determinism question, because it is among the counter-text's strongest exhibits. A state machine with its own personnel, revenues, appetites and centuries of accumulated structure is not a transparent instrument that economic relations move like a hand in a glove. It is an inheritance, in exactly the opening paragraph's sense: circumstances transmitted from the past, weighing on the present, available for capture and capable of capture in return. When Marx explains the coup by the machine's mass, he is explaining a political outcome by an institutional sediment laid down by absolute monarchs and revolutionary assemblies, which is historical explanation of a rich and mediated kind. Readers taught to expect a theory in which the state is a simple reflex of the economy will find the Brumaire quietly impossible to file, and that impossibility, repeated across the book's best passages, is why the counter-text reading has become the standard scholarly reply to the caricature.

## The determinism counter-text, stated in full

The claim has been named since the first page, and by now the evidence has been accumulating in every section, so the guide can afford to state the argument systematically. The determinism counter-text holds that the Eighteenth Brumaire is the strongest internal evidence against reading Marx as an economic determinist, because it is his most detailed application of his method and the application is saturated with everything determinism excludes: ideology with causal force, tradition directing conduct, classes acting against their apparent interest, institutions with their own momentum, and outcomes decided by accident, timing and personality at the margin. The dispute about how to characterise Marx's theory of history as a whole has its own home at [economic determinism debate](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/), where the counter-text appears as one exhibit among several. Here the task is narrower and, for a reader, more useful: to show exactly where, in this one book, the determinist reading breaks.

Consider first the causal role the book assigns to ideas. The Napoleonic legend is not decoration on the peasant vote; it is the mechanism of the peasant vote, the form in which a material grievance became a political choice. The Roman costumes of earlier revolutions are not antiquarian colour; they are the means by which actors nerved themselves for tasks their own age could not yet name. The phrases of the Party of Order, order, property, family, religion, operate in the narrative as real instruments, rallying some constituencies and, by their hollowness, eventually discrediting their speakers. A theory in which the ideological level is epiphenomenal cannot use any of this machinery, and the Brumaire uses it constantly. Marx's own later formula about theory becoming a material force when it grips the masses is usually quoted from another context, but the Brumaire is where he shows the gripping, case by case, faction by faction.

Consider second the repeated spectacle of classes defeating themselves. The bourgeoisie of the Party of Order destroys the parliamentary conditions of its own rule because its fear of the propertyless outweighs its attachment to power. The petty bourgeois democrats proclaim the people sovereign and dissolve at the first cannon. The peasants deliver France to a pretender whose regime will tax them, conscript them and leave their mortgages untouched. In each case the explanation runs through representation: what a class's spokesmen, newspapers, memories and organisations made of its position. Between position and conduct there always intervenes an apparatus of interpretation, and the apparatus can malfunction. That is a profoundly undeterminist architecture, whatever vocabulary it is dressed in, because it locates the explanation of events in a chain with several links, each of which can bend. The textbook Marx of the caricature has one link. The Brumaire Marx has five and keeps showing the reader each of them.

Consider third the role of the unintended and the mistimed. The book's chronology is littered with contingencies that carry structural weight: the death of particular generals, the Assembly's calendar colliding with the presidential term, a revision vote that wins a majority and loses the constitution, provincial tours whose improvised phrases are read in Paris as declarations of intent. Marx does not treat these as noise around a signal. He builds turning points out of them, because the circumstances men inherit include the calendar, the casualty list and the order in which questions reach a vote. And consider finally the book's own existence as a performance of its thesis. It was written to intervene, by a man who believed that a correct analysis, published in a small journal in New York, was worth the winter it cost him. A determinist who believed outcomes were settled by economic structure alone might still write pamphlets, out of habit or hope, but the Brumaire's urgency, its determination to get the fractions and the machinery exactly right, belongs to a writer who thought understanding the conjuncture was part of acting in it.

The counter-text reading has limits, and honesty requires marking them. The book does not float free of material explanation; its fractions are defined by revenue and property, its peasants by their parcels and debts, its machine by salaries and budgets. Nobody should leave the Brumaire believing Marx thought ideas ruled the world unattached. The claim is comparative and it is specific: between the caricature, in which economic position issues political conduct the way a mould issues castings, and the book, in which position is mediated by organisation, memory, institution and chance, there is a distance so wide that the caricature cannot be a summary of the book. Since the book is the exhibit every tradition of Marx reading agrees to admire, the distance matters beyond this one text. It is the distance a student should be able to measure, with page references, whenever the caricature is presented as the doctrine.

## Money moving under the narrative

For all its theatre, the book never lets the reader forget that politics in the Second Republic ran on bills coming due. A reader who tracks the money references gains a second skeleton for the plot, complementary to the timeline, and the guide draws attention to the pattern because first time readers, dazzled by the rhetoric, often miss how much of the argument is conducted through taxes, debts and budgets.

The republic was born into a fiscal emergency and answered it in the way that sealed its reputation in the countryside. The provisional government, facing empty coffers and a commercial crisis, imposed a surcharge of forty five centimes on the franc of direct taxation, and the burden fell on the land. In the villages the republic was thereafter not the regime of February's hopes but the regime of the tax collector's surcharge, and Marx treats the forty five centimes as one of the decisive facts of the period: the material experience through which millions of peasants learned to hate the republic and to dream of the emperor who, legend promised, would relieve them. When the peasant vote of December 1848 is explained, the tax is doing explanatory work alongside the legend, grievance and glory braided together. The lesson generalises across the book. Wherever a political attitude matters to the outcome, Marx hunts for the bill that taught it.

Debt performs the same office for the smallholder's deeper condition. The parcel that the revolution had given the peasant family had been progressively encumbered, Marx argues, until the peasant's real master was no longer a landlord but capital itself, in the form of the mortgage and the village usurer who held it. The argument reframes the countryside's conservatism: the smallholder clings to his property because it is his, and the property binds him to the existing order because its defence and its burden are the same fact. Bonapartism feeds on the knot. The regime can present itself as the protector of the parcel against the town, against the tax farmer, against the socialist who would collectivise it, while the mortgage quietly collects what the legend promised to forgive.

Inside the Assembly, money fights take parliamentary costume. The wine tax, hated in the wine growing south; the salt tax, whose reduction the republic had promised; the budget votes through which the Party of Order rewarded its clients and disciplined the administration; the perpetual question of state credit, which made the finance aristocracy a hostage of every rumour of disorder: these recur as the connective tissue between the fractions and their fears. The finance aristocracy's terror of instability is a balance sheet before it is a sentiment. The industrial bourgeoisie's impatience with parliamentary government grows as commercial recovery under a quiet executive makes the talking shop look like an expensive luxury. Even the coup has its ledger: a regime founded on a plebiscite must pay its army, its officials and its pretorians, and Marx watches the imperial budget become the machine's feeding trough, the material basis on which the parasitic body he anatomised could keep growing. None of this is economic determinism in the caricatured sense, because in every instance the money works through a political form, a tax's reputation, a debt's shame, a budget's clientele. But it is materialism, unmistakably: the conviction that to understand why men acted as they did, a reader should find out what they owed, what they collected, and what they stood to lose.

## Costume and phrase in the parliamentary theatre

The opening paragraph promises an analysis of borrowed clothing, and the middle parts redeem the promise in parliamentary detail. Marx watches the actors of the Second Republic the way a theatre critic watches a repertory company: alert to the role each performer has inherited, the earlier production each gesture quotes, and the widening gap between the lines and the situation. Reading those parts well means learning to hear the performances as he heard them, and the guide isolates the technique because it is the most imitated and least understood element of his style.

The Party of Order performs legitimacy. Its deputies speak as the trustees of order, property, family and religion, a liturgy whose four nouns are meant to close argument rather than open it. Marx's counter move is to keep asking which property, whose order, and to show the coalition's two halves hearing different kings inside the same words. Legitimist and Orléanist can chant the liturgy together precisely because its terms are empty enough to hold both dynasties, and the emptiness is functional until the day a successor must actually be named, when the liturgy fails and the factions discover they have built their unity out of a phrase. The democratic Mountain performs the revolution. Its orators quote the great days, invoke the people, and draft proclamations in the cadence of an earlier assembly, and Marx, who sympathised with much of their programme, is severe about the performance: a class that can only announce itself in the borrowed thunder of a previous generation will hesitate at the exact moment its phrases require deeds, as the Mountain hesitated and dissolved in June 1849. The president performs the uncle. Every tour, every review, every carefully ambiguous toast is a quotation, and the performance succeeds because the audience, above all the peasant audience, had been rehearsing its part for thirty years of legend.

Beneath the performances Marx keeps a colder ledger of what phrases do to their speakers. A party that repeats a slogan begins to obey it; a faction that describes itself as the party of order must eventually prefer any order, including a coup's, to the disorder of its own defeat; a president who has promised everything to everyone in the vocabulary of the empire must govern as the empire's administrator or be exposed as its understudy. This is the opening's nightmare image translated into parliamentary practice. The tradition of the dead generations weighs on the brains of the living not as a museum weight but as a script weight, assigning lines, blocking exits, and punishing improvisation. Readers who grasp the technique can then turn it on the book itself, which is the final refinement the guide recommends. Marx is also a performer, writing the coup as farce because the farce frame was his chosen weapon, and asking what the frame selects and what it hides, where the laughter clarifies and where it merely wounds, is not disloyalty to the text. It is the text's own method, applied one level up, and the strongest readings of the book, including several by historians who reject its politics, have proceeded exactly that way.

## The strongest objection, at full strength

The objection deserves a section of its own and the courtesy of being stated as its sharpest critics would state it, because the guide's admiration for the book is worthless if it cannot survive the book's most damaging true charge. The charge is this. Marx's class attributions are asserted rather than demonstrated. He announces that a faction speaks for the finance aristocracy, that a ministry expresses the industrial bourgeoisie, that a vote expresses the smallholding peasantry, and the announcement carries the day by rhetorical force; the independent evidence that would connect the political grouping to the class position, membership records, economic biographies, systematic analysis of votes against interests, is largely absent from the text. The procedure can run in a circle: the faction's conduct is explained by its class character, and its class character is inferred from its conduct. Where the fit is awkward, the machinery of fractions can always be subdivided further, or the actors declared to have misunderstood their own interests, a rescue that a method should not be allowed to perform for itself too often. And the brilliance of the prose, the metaphors that make a parliamentary manoeuvre unforgettable, the portraits that fix a politician's essence in a sentence, has protected the argument from the scrutiny a plainer book would have faced within a decade. On this charge, the Brumaire is a masterpiece of political literature wearing the credentials of an analysis it gestures at more often than it performs.

Parts of the charge land and should be conceded without varnish. Marx wrote in weeks, in exile, from newspapers, pamphlets and correspondence, without access to French archives, electoral statistics in any modern form, or the private papers of the men he anatomised. He was writing interventionist journalism of genius, not a monograph, and some of his attributions have not survived the archives: the social composition of the Party of Order's electorate, the behaviour of the countryside in December 1851, the coherence of the finance aristocracy as a political actor, all look different under modern research. The confidence of the attributions exceeds the evidence on the page, and a reader trained by the book itself, trained to ask how a claim is earned, will find pages where the earning is skipped. Conceding this is not a betrayal of the text; it is the application of the text's standards to the text, which is the sincerest form of taking it seriously.

The answer begins where the objection is weakest, with the passages where the attribution is argued from material interest in full view of the reader. The account of the forty five centime tax does not label the peasantry republican or Bonapartist by decree; it traces a fiscal measure into village experience and village experience into a vote. The account of the suffrage law of May 1850 does not merely call the Party of Order reactionary; it reconstructs the deputies' fear from the composition of the electorate they had just watched vote, and derives the self destructive character of the law from the coalition's own need for legitimacy. The anatomy of the state machine argues from the payroll: half a million officials are not a metaphor but a material constituency for the machine's survival, and the argument can be checked, qualified and corrected in its own terms, as generations of historians have done. Where Marx argues this way, the objection dissolves, because this is demonstration of exactly the kind the charge demands, compressed by journalism into paragraphs instead of chapters.

The residue, after concessions on both sides, is a fair verdict that readers can carry into the text. The Brumaire is an analysis performed at uneven depth: a framework of genuine explanatory power, a set of central mechanisms, the legend, the tax, the machine, the abdication, that are argued rather than asserted, and a penumbra of attributions where the framework is applied by flair. Its literary force has indeed extended the credit of the penumbra, and the honest way to read the book is to keep reclassifying passages as one reads, asking of each attribution whether it has been earned on the page or merely announced in the book's commanding voice. That discipline, far from ruining the reading experience, is the experience at its best: the reader joins the argument instead of applauding it, which is what Marx, who spent the book's best pages refusing to applaud anyone, would presumably have wanted.

## What historians have done with the book

A reader's guide owes its reader a map of the disagreements, because the Brumaire sits inside a living historiography of the Second Republic and the coup, and Marx's narrative is one voice, the earliest and loudest, in an argument that has never closed. Three disputes matter most for a reader of the text, and in each of them the guide reports positions rather than settling scores.

The first dispute concerns Louis Bonaparte himself, and it has been running since 1852. Victor Hugo, writing his own furious account of the coup in exile, made the president a criminal of genius in miniature, a villain whose smallness was the scandal; Marx's preface of 1869 answers that Hugo's thunderbolt method makes the man appear great by treating him as the sole author of the event. Proudhon's rival account, which Marx also answers in that preface, derived the coup from the logic of preceding economic development so thoroughly that Bonaparte dwindled into an instrument of necessity, apologising for the outcome by explaining it. Marx placed his own account between the thunderbolt and the apology: the class struggle in France created the circumstances that let a mediocrity play the hero. Later historians have redistributed the weights again. Archival work on the coup's preparation has documented a conspiracy run with patience and professional competence, the war ministry staffed, the Paris command arranged, the printing of proclamations prepared in secret; work on Bonaparte's earlier career has recovered the programme of his Napoleonic writings and the two failed coups of his youth as evidence of a fixed ambition rather than an accidental elevation. Other historians answer that competence in conspiracy is compatible with Marx's structural point, since the conspiracy succeeded where identical conspiracies in 1836 and 1840 had collapsed in ridicule, and the difference lay not in the man but in the deadlock that had paralysed every alternative. A reader should know that the argument is live, that the book is a party to it, and that quoting Marx's portrait as the settled verdict on Bonaparte is a beginner's error the examiners are waiting for.

The second dispute concerns the countryside, and it has already been touched in the peasantry section. The older picture, which Marx's sack of potatoes did much to fix, showed a politically inert rural France voting its legend. Research in the departmental archives, pursued across the twentieth century and associated with historians who reconstructed the democratic socialist movement of 1849 to 1851 village by village, replaced that picture with something more turbulent: a countryside penetrated by republican organisation, secret societies, newspaper networks and tax resistance, which rose in arms against the coup across much of the south and centre and was punished with tens of thousands of arrests, deportations to Algeria and a machinery of repression the book, written before the repression's scale was public, underplays. The correction matters beyond the episode. It shows the legend and the organisation coexisting in the same villages, sometimes in the same families, and it disciplines any reader tempted to treat Marx's class portraits as ethnography. At the same time, the correction leaves the book's central political fact intact: when the countryside voted as a nation in December 1848, it voted for the name, and no rural radicalism of 1851 could retroactively change what the name had collected in 1848.

The third dispute is about inevitability, and it divides readers of the coup along a line that runs through all historical explanation. One tradition, reading backward from the Empire's eighteen years, treats the coup as the republic's appointed end: a constitution at war with itself, an electorate frightened by June, two monarchist factions incapable of fusion, an army ambitious and a president patient; on this reading the wonder is that the republic lasted as long as it did. The opposing tradition insists on the narrowness of the passages through which events actually moved: the revision vote's arithmetic, the Assembly's failure to defend itself in December, the decisions of individual commanders on the night, the resistance that in another week might have spread. Marx's text, characteristically, arms both traditions, since it demonstrates the deadlock with unmatched power and then narrates the coup as a night's work by identifiable men who might have failed. Historians who use the book well treat that doubleness not as an inconsistency to be resolved but as the accurate registration of how the crisis felt from inside it, structurally doomed and humanly open at once, which is perhaps the deepest reason the book continues to be assigned by teachers who share none of its author's commitments. Its remaining companionships are practical: readers deciding where the Brumaire belongs in a course of reading will find its place fixed in the [marx reading order guide](/2013/06/15/marx-reading-order-guide/), after the earlier historical writings and before the economic works whose method it quietly rehearses.

## Three accounts of one coup

The 1869 preface performs a service no other part of the book attempts: it places the Brumaire in a field of rival explanations and lets the reader triangulate. Marx names the two competitors himself. Victor Hugo's Napoléon le Petit, the little Napoleon, had appeared in 1852 as a masterpiece of invective, and Proudhon's study of the coup, published the same year, had attempted to justify the event as a necessary product of economic development, a revolution carried out by other means. Marx's judgements on both are brisk, and a reader who follows them learns the Brumaire's method by contrast, which is often the fastest way to learn any method.

Hugo's book explains the coup by the criminal. Its energy is moral and biographical: a perjurer, a conspirator, a man of the boulevard crime seizes a republic that trusted him, and the historian's task is execration raised to literature. Marx's objection, compressed into a famous turn, is that Hugo thereby makes his dwarf into a giant, since an event authored by one man's villainy becomes an event of world historical scale with a single sufficient cause, and the invective, intending to diminish its target, ends by magnifying him into the most powerful explanatory principle in France. Proudhon's book explains the coup by the process. Its energy is dialectical and exculpatory: the coup is derived from the preceding development, presented as historically necessary, and the derivation slides, in Marx's reading, into an apology, since what had to happen can hardly be blamed on the man who did it. Between execration and apology, Marx locates his own procedure: to show how the class struggle in France created circumstances and relations that allowed a grotesque mediocrity to play a hero's part. The sentence is worth memorising as the book's method in one line. Circumstances first, but circumstances of a specific kind, produced by struggle; a hero's part, but a part, written by the situation; a mediocrity, but one whose mediocrity is a finding about the man, not a substitute for the analysis.

A reader equipped with the triangle can classify most later writing on the coup at sight. Biographies that linger on the president's character are working Hugo's side of the field, updated with better archives. Structural histories that make the coup a footnote to grain prices or railway mileage are working Proudhon's side, with the apology usually removed. Accounts that move between the deadlock of the classes and the night of December the second, granting each its weight, are working in the space Marx staked out, whether or not their authors acknowledge the tenancy. The preface also quietly corrects a misreading of the book itself. Readers who treat the Brumaire's contempt for Bonaparte as its explanation have mistaken the part for the whole: the contempt is Hugo's share in the book, present and deliberate, but the preface insists the explanation lies elsewhere, in the circumstances that made contemptible material sufficient. Holding both, the scorn and the structure, without letting either swallow the other, is the mature way to read the text, and it is also, not coincidentally, the mature way to write about any political catastrophe in which small men do large damage.

## June, the wound the republic never closed

Every thread in the book runs back to four days in June 1848, and a reader who wants the later parliamentary manoeuvres to make emotional as well as analytical sense should pause over the June Days until their weight is felt. The National Workshops had been created by the provisional government as a concession to the demand for the organisation of labour, and had swollen into an enrolment of well over a hundred thousand unemployed Parisians, paid a small wage for make work or for idleness, organised in brigades with elected leaders, and regarded by the Assembly's majority as an army of the left maintained at public expense. In June the government moved to dissolve them, offering the young unmarried workers the army and the rest labour in the provinces. The east of Paris rose. The rising had no single programme and no commanding committee; it had barricades in the working class districts, a desperate conviction that the republic was being stolen, and four days of fighting against troops and the Mobile Guard under Cavaignac, invested with executive power by the Assembly. It was crushed with artillery, with summary killings during and after the fighting, and with arrests and transportations that emptied the districts of their militants. The death toll remains disputed; the political effect does not.

Marx's reading of June is the interpretive key he offers for everything after it, and it should be understood as a reading, powerful and contestable. In his account the June Days stripped the February republic of its founding ambiguity. Until June, all classes could inhabit the word republic and mean different things by it; after June, the republic had chosen, had defended property with grapeshot, and the workers had learned that the fraternity of February ended where the social question began. The bourgeoisie, conversely, had learned the price of its safety and had discovered a taste for the sabre that it never afterwards lost. Every later episode is dated from the wound: the Party of Order governs as a coalition of the frightened, the democrats hesitate because they remember the isolation of the insurgents, the workers abstain from the republic's defence in December 1851 in part because the republic had shot them in June 1848. Historians have complicated each element. The workshops' closure had fiscal as well as political drivers; the insurgents' motives mixed wages, honour and neighbourhood as much as doctrine; the Mobile Guard that shot them down was itself recruited from young Parisian workers, a fact that sits awkwardly on any simple two camp map of the fighting. Marx knew some of this and the book's framework survives much of it, because his claim is about consequences more than motives: whatever June was in the streets, in the republic's political memory it functioned as the demonstration that the propertied would kill to keep the social order and that the propertyless stood alone when they tested it. Political memory, the opening paragraph would remind us, is itself one of the circumstances transmitted from the past, and June is the heaviest single item in the inheritance the Second Republic carried.

## The coup as Marx narrates it

The coup of 2 December 1851 occupies less of the book than a reader might expect, because Marx is more interested in the three years that made it possible than in the night that executed it, but his narrative of the operation itself repays attention as a specimen of his art and as a checklist of the machine's components. The preparation had been administrative before it was military. The war ministry had been placed in reliable hands, the command of the army of Paris arranged, prefects sounded or replaced, and the president's half brother and a small circle of accomplices installed at the points where proclamations are printed and telegraphs sent. On the night of the first and second of December the leading deputies of the Assembly, monarchist generals among them, were arrested at home; the Assembly building was occupied; placards announced the dissolution, the restoration of universal suffrage and an appeal to the people; the army held the boulevards. Resistance gathered in pockets of Paris and was fired on, with killings on the boulevards that scandalised even some of the regime's friends, and spread far more seriously through the provinces, where tens of thousands rose and were suppressed by a repression that mixed courts martial, commissions and deportation ships. Marx, writing within weeks and from exile, had the Paris outline and the broad shape of the provincial repression; the full provincial story belongs to the later historiography discussed above, and readers should let the archives correct his proportions there.

His interpretive emphasis falls in two places that a summary usually drops. The first is the coup's theatrical borrowings. The date is the uncle's coronation day and the anniversary of Austerlitz; the proclamations speak the empire's language of glory and order; universal suffrage, abolished by the Assembly, is restored by the usurper so that the people's name can ratify the people's dispossession. Farce, in the book's usage, is not a synonym for triviality. It is the specific condition of world changing acts performed in quotation marks, by men who experience their own originality as re-enactment, and Marx's narrative of December keeps the quotation marks visible. The second emphasis is the Society of December 10, the president's private organisation of the declassed, founded in 1849 under cover of a benevolent society and used to pack his provincial receptions with drilled applause, to menace opponents and to give the regime a street arm of men who owed it meals and money. Marx's chapters on the Society are the most venomous in the book and the most quoted against him, since the contempt for the rabble sits badly with democratic pieties. The analytical residue is again separable from the venom: a plebiscitary power, resting on voters it cannot organise and parties it has dissolved, requires an ersatz organisation of clients, and the Society is Marx's name for the first draft of that requirement. Readers who can hold the residue and discount the venom are reading as the guide has recommended throughout: for the mechanism, with the rhetoric audited rather than absorbed.

## Reading a dense and allusive text

### What makes the prose so dense and so allusive?

Marx wrote at newspaper speed for readers who followed French politics in the press, so names arrive without introduction, factions are mocked through literary and biblical parallels, and whole parliamentary intrigues are compressed into a single ironic sentence. The density is a compression problem rather than a philosophy problem, and scaffolding, not simplification, is what a reader needs.

The practical consequence deserves spelling out, because generations of readers have misdiagnosed their own difficulty and abandoned the book at exactly the wrong moment. A reader who stalls in the middle parts is usually not failing at theory. The stall happens at sentences where three deputies are dispatched in a subordinate clause, where a ministerial crisis is summarised by a Shakespearean tag, where a faction is renamed for the restaurant at which its leaders dined. These are failures of reference, not of intelligence, and they yield to apparatus: the timeline and cast table in this guide, a notebook kept by faction rather than by page, a willingness to reread the final pages of one part before beginning the next. The allusions, by contrast, are rarely load bearing. Marx's literary and classical machinery, the ghosts, costumes, witches and resurrected corpses that crowd his sentences, communicates tone and judgement rather than information: it tells the reader how a manoeuvre is to be valued, not what the manoeuvre was. A reader who cannot place an allusion loses a sneer, not an argument, and should press on. The sneers can be collected on a second reading, when they are usually the reward.

A word about pace. The book was written as seven instalments for a journal and it still behaves like one: each part re-establishes its cast, reopens its account of the balance of forces, and drives toward a closing judgement. Reading it in sittings matched to the parts, with the timeline consulted at the start of each sitting, converts the density from a wall into a rhythm. Reading it in one heroic march, or in fragments quarried for quotations, produces the two standard casualties: the reader who respects the book and cannot summarise it, and the reader who can quote it and has not read it. The guide's whole design, scaffolding first and judgement after, is aimed at producing a third kind.

## Misreadings this guide exists to correct

Collect the errors in one place, since several have been corrected in passing and a reader benefits from seeing the set. The tragedy and farce formulation is Marx's addition to a remark he attributes to Hegel; it is not a quotation from Hegel, and it is not, in its first element, original to Marx either, since the remark about recurrence is the part he credits elsewhere. The sentence about men making their own history is not a charter of free will; its second clause, on circumstances not chosen, carries half the doctrine and all of the book's method. The title does not announce a book about 1789 or about the first Napoleon's coup; it announces a comparison, and the nephew's coup of 1851 is the subject throughout. The book is not a treatise from which a theory of the state or of class can be lifted without the narrative; its concepts are earned inside the narrative or not at all, which is why the state autonomy that later theory borrowed arrives only in the final part, after the classes have exhausted themselves in the reader's sight. The peasantry passage is not a sneer at rural intelligence; it is an argument about the organisational consequences of a mode of production, and it must be read beside the same book's record of rural revolt. The contempt for Bonaparte is not the explanation of Bonaparte; the preface says so in terms. And the book is not the work of a determinist manqué, failing to live up to a system located elsewhere; on the counter-text reading defended here, it is the system, such as it is, caught in the act of working, mediations and all.

## Editions, translations and the apparatus a reader needs

The textual facts can be given briefly and without salesmanship. Marx revised little between the journal publication of 1852 and the second edition of 1869, for which he wrote the preface discussed above and made light corrections; a third edition appeared after his death under Engels's supervision. English readers have been served by several translations across the twentieth century, of which the version prepared by Daniel De Leon in the eighteen nineties long set the terms in which English speakers quoted the opening, and later scholarly translations, including the one made for the collected works and the widely used version by Ben Fowkes, have tightened the rendering of the political vocabulary. The guide deliberately refrains from crowning a version, for the reasons the evergreen rules of this series require, and offers criteria instead. Choose an edition whose translation preserves the structure of the opening sentence, since the attribution to Hegel must survive the rendering intact or the book's most famous correction is lost. Choose one with notes that identify deputies, generals and newspapers, because the apparatus is not a luxury in this text but the difference between reading and guessing. Choose one that prints the 1869 preface, without which the reader lacks Marx's own triangulation against Hugo and Proudhon. And keep a map of France and the timeline from this guide within reach for the first reading; the geography of the provincial resistance and the geography of the vote are both easier to hold when the departments can be seen.

Serious readers who finish the Brumaire and want the surrounding terrain have a natural itinerary, and the guide closes its practical work by naming it. Marx's own earlier account of the same republic, The Class Struggles in France, covers the period to 1850 with a different organisation and some different judgements, and reading the two together shows the analysis being revised in real time. His later account of the Paris Commune, The Civil War in France, returns to the state machine of the Brumaire's final part and draws the opposite practical conclusion about it, after the Communards had attempted what the earlier book said no revolution had done. For keeping the reading organised across a whole text of this density, with its cast, its timeline and its disputed passages flagged for return, the study notes method described at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is the companion this guide recommends: one structured notebook per text, quotations filed under the claim they support, and the timeline kept where it can be amended as the archives correct it.

## The book among Marx's writings

Place the Brumaire on the shelf of its author's works and its peculiarities sharpen. Behind it stand the journalistic and theoretical writings of the eighteen forties: the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, with its account of the bureaucracy as a corporation guarding its mystery; the manuscripts on alienation; the polemic against Proudhon; and the Manifesto of 1848, whose opening survey of bourgeois society was written weeks before the February revolution made its author a war correspondent of a revolution in progress. The Brumaire inherits the Manifesto's cast of classes and its confidence that modern politics is unintelligible without them, and then subjects the inheritance to three years of evidence the Manifesto's authors could not have foreseen. The classes come back from France fractured, frightened, self defeating and mediated, and the later book is in part the record of the earlier book's categories being educated by events. That education is one of the most instructive spectacles in the whole corpus, and teachers who set the two texts in sequence are not making an antiquarian point; they are showing a method learning.

Beside it stands its sibling, The Class Struggles in France, written for the Neue Rheinische Zeitung's review in 1850 and covering the republic's first two years. The sibling is rawer, closer to the journalism from which it grew, and in places more schematic, above all in its early confidence that the crisis would ripen quickly into a continental revolutionary wave. The Brumaire, written after that hope had been buried by the coup, is the colder and better book: the same framework, the same cast, but with the timetable of expectation removed and the analysis of the state, barely begun in the earlier work, carried to the closing anatomy that made the book famous among theorists. Ahead of it stands the economic work of the eighteen fifties and sixties, the notebooks and the first volume of Capital, and the relation runs both ways. The Brumaire's France taught its author what a developed capitalist society's politics actually looked like, the independence of the executive, the weight of the peasantry, the resilience of inherited forms; Capital's France, in the chapters on the working day and on primitive accumulation's aftermath, quietly draws on that knowledge. The famous summary of the materialist method in the preface of 1859, with its architecture of base and superstructure, was written seven years after the Brumaire and is far more schematic than the book it is often used to interpret. Readers who approach the Brumaire through the 1859 preface will find the book constantly exceeding the summary, and the excess is not a failure of the book. It is the difference between a method advertised and a method at work, and the counter-text argument of this guide amounts, in the end, to insisting that the working version deserves priority.

## The Mountain, or the tragedy inside the farce

Between the Party of Order and the disarmed workers stood the party Marx treats with the most complicated mixture of sympathy and exasperation in the book: the democratic petty bourgeoisie and its parliamentary face, the Mountain. The Mountain took its name from the radical benches of the first revolution, and the borrowing is itself a Brumaire theme, a party seated inside a costume before it has decided what to do there. Its social base, the small masters, shopkeepers and professional men squeezed between the great bourgeoisie and the proletariat, wanted a republic that would cheapen credit, lift the weight of debt, restrain the great banks and leave the small property of its members sacred. Its leader in the Assembly, Ledru-Rollin, was an orator of genuine power and a strategist of consistent hesitation, and Marx's portrait of the type, the democrat who emerges from the people and returns to the phrase, is among the book's most quoted cruelties.

The Mountain's catastrophe arrives in June 1849, and Marx narrates it as the farce principle operating on the left. The Party of Order's government had sent a French expedition to destroy the Roman republic, in plain violation of the French constitution's promise never to employ the republic's forces against the liberty of another people. The Mountain denounced the violation, announced a demonstration in defence of the constitution, and on 13 June led its procession into the streets of Paris unarmed, proclaiming a state of siege against the government while dispersing before the government's cavalry. Its leaders fled abroad or into hiding; its newspapers were suppressed; the party never recovered its parliamentary weight. Marx's analysis refuses both available simplicities. He does not treat the fiasco as the personal cowardice of leaders, though his prose enjoys their discomfiture. He treats it as the political form of a class position: a stratum that needs the workers' energy to frighten the bourgeoisie and fears the workers' programme more than it fears the bourgeoisie will always arrive at the demonstration and discover, in the moment, that it has organised a procession where a battle was required. The petty bourgeoisie, in the book's recurring formula, is a transitional class in which the interests of two classes are simultaneously blunted, and its politics inherits the blunting. For the reader, the Mountain's chapters are the book's purest exercise in fraction analysis, because the class is small enough to see whole and its representatives' speeches can be checked against its members' balance sheets line by line, which is more than can be said for the great fractions whose spokesmen speak in liturgies.

## Four scenes read closely

A guide that has supplied scaffolding and argument owes the reader, finally, a demonstration of the reading it recommends, performed on scenes the summarised versions usually flatten. Four will serve, one from each register of the book: the constitutional, the military, the parliamentary and the prophetic.

The constitutional scene is the Assembly discovering, in November 1851, that it will not defend itself. Alarmed by the coup preparations everyone could smell, deputies associated with the Assembly's own officers proposed that the Assembly assert its constitutional right to requisition troops directly for its protection, bypassing the war ministry the president controlled. The proposal was debated and defeated, with monarchist deputies voting against arming their own institution because the precedent of an assembly commanding soldiers frightened them more than the president did. Marx lingers over the vote because it condenses his whole account of the parliamentary bourgeoisie: a class fraction so formed by procedure that it experiences its own self defence as an irregularity, and so divided that it cannot unite even to survive. He had earlier named the affliction parliamentary cretinism, the disease of men imprisoned in the parliamentary world who lose the sense of the real one outside the chamber's walls, its majorities, its points of order, its adjournments. The scene earns the diagnosis. Within three weeks the institution that had voted down its own guard was dissolved by soldiers it had declined to summon, and its leaders were arrested in their beds by policemen whose warrants the constitution it worshipped had not authorised.

The military scene is the review at Satory in the autumn of 1850, where the president, feasting regiments and cultivating the officer corps, allowed his troops to parade past shouting for the emperor while the Assembly's commander, General Changarnier, the Party of Order's own sword, still nominally held the Paris command. The months that follow are a duel of dismissals conducted through the newspapers and the order of the day: the Assembly's general removed in January 1851 by the president it was the Assembly's strategy to restrain, the deputies protesting and swallowing the protest, because dismissing their protector was legal and defending him would have required the irregular energy they had forsworn. Marx reads the army in these scenes neither as a neutral instrument nor as a class in uniform. It is an institution with its own appetites, glory, promotion, plunder, revenge for the humiliations of civilian politics, and the president who feeds those appetites is purchasing an autonomy that no vote in the chamber can subsequently repeal. The scene generalises uncomfortably well, which is why military sociologists who have never opened another page of Marx have opened this one.

The parliamentary scene is the long duel over the ministry and the dotation, the Assembly granting and refusing money, imposing and withdrawing ministers, passing votes of censure that change nothing because the president, who cannot be removed, has learned to treat censure as weather. Watch how Marx distributes sympathy here, because it surprises readers who expect a simple villain. The deputies are shown trapped inside a constitution that gives them every weapon except the decisive one, while the president is shown growing by the exercise of a patience his enemies mistake for weakness. The farce register coexists with a genuine institutional analysis: two legitimacies, each plebiscitary in origin, each constitutionally immovable against the other, grinding the republic between them until the man with the soldiers ends the argument. Constitutional designers have read the book ever since as a case study in dual legitimacy, and the reading is faithful to the text, which is why the guide insists the book is an analysis and not merely a performance.

The prophetic scene is the ending. Marx closes by looking forward from the coup to the regime it founded, and the close contains his most famous prediction about the book's subject: that the nephew's empire, founded in farce, would end by exposing the machinery it rode, and his image for the exposure is the bronze statue of the first Napoleon crashing from the top of the Vendôme column when the imperial mantle slips from the nephew's shoulders. As prophecy it should be graded as historians grade all prophecy, including its author's: the Second Empire lasted eighteen years, industrialised France, rebuilt Paris and fell in a war of its own choosing in 1870, whereupon the statue's column was indeed pulled down, by the Paris Commune the following spring, in a sequence no one in 1852 could have scripted. Marx lived to write about the Commune and to close the arc his younger self had opened, in a book whose final pages answer the Brumaire's anatomy of the state machine with the spectacle of that machine being, for seventy two days, dismantled by the city it had policed. A reader who finishes the Brumaire and turns to The Civil War in France is not changing the subject. The subject is continuing, and the guide's last practical recommendation is to let it.

## France inside a European counter-revolution

The book is intensely Parisian, and a reader can finish it forgetting that the French crisis unfolded inside a continent wide defeat, yet Marx never forgets, and the guide restores the frame because several of the narrative's judgements depend on it. The revolutions of 1848 had run from Paris to Vienna, Berlin, Milan and Budapest, and by the time Bonaparte struck in 1851 nearly all of them had been reversed: the Austrian empire restored by Russian arms in Hungary, the Prussian king recovered from his humiliations, the Roman republic, whose destruction by French troops occupies the Brumaire's middle distance, extinguished in 1849 by the soldiers of the sister republic. The Roman expedition matters inside the French story for the reasons already given, as the Party of Order's foreign sacrament and the Mountain's occasion of collapse. It matters in the European frame as the emblem of the age: republics devouring republics, the counter-revolution international and organised while the revolution remained national and rhetorical. England, the one great state untouched by revolution, watches the French crisis in Marx's telling with the interest of a creditor and a rival, and the book's first audience, the German exiles of New York reading Weydemeyer's journal, are themselves debris of the German defeat, reading France's story as the last chapter of their own. That audience explains the book's peculiar intimacy and distance at once. Marx is writing about a country he had lived in and been expelled from, for readers who had lost the same war in a different theatre, and the analysis carries a pedagogical urgency beneath the entertainment: learn how a republic dies, because the inquest is the only use left for the corpse. Readers in any later age of democratic retreat have found that urgency legible without translation, which accounts for a strand of the book's readership that neither the academy nor the movement organises.

## What the book teaches about analysing a conjuncture

Step back from the narrative and the Brumaire leaves a reader with transferable equipment, the habits of analysis that later practitioners, inside and outside the tradition, have extracted from it. The guide names the habits plainly, because naming them is how a reader carries them to other crises, other centuries, other materials.

The first habit is fraction discipline: never let a class name close an inquiry it should open. Ask of every bloc what its revenue is, how its daily reproduction runs, which of its members gain and which lose by the policy on the table, until the bloc splits into the fractions that actually act. The second habit is representation analysis: for every class, identify who speaks for it, through what organisations, newspapers, parties, parishes and legends, and measure the distance between the spoken interest and the material one, because the distance is where the politics happens. The third habit is institutional sediment: inventory the inherited machinery, the army's traditions, the bureaucracy's payroll, the constitution's calendar, the previous regime's unfinished business, and treat the inventory as a causal factor with weight, not as scenery. The fourth habit is periodisation with the courage of its turning points: divide the crisis where the balance of forces actually shifted, at the massacre, the election, the law, the failed vote, and be prepared to defend the divisions against the chronicle's smoother alternatives. The fifth habit, and the one the opening paragraph exists to teach, is costume criticism: attend to the names, anniversaries, uniforms and quotations through which the actors understand themselves, and treat their self understanding as a force in the event, capable of nerving a movement beyond its brief or of marching it, eyes open, into a previous century's defeat. A reader who practises the five habits on the Brumaire itself, fractioning its author from his legend, auditing its representations, inventorying its rhetoric, will find that the book survives its own method, which is the final test the guide proposes and the reason the determinism counter-text is not a debating trick. The text that teaches mediation can withstand being read mediately.

## A constitution with two thrones

The constitution of November 1848 deserves a section of its own, because Marx's treatment of it is the point where his institutional analysis is most exact and where later constitutional theory has most plainly borrowed from him. The Constituent Assembly, dominated by moderate republicans who had just shot down the June rising, wrote a charter for a republic they intended to be conservative: a single chamber, a president elected by direct universal suffrage for four years, ineligible for immediate re-election, ministers responsible in a pattern nobody could quite specify, and a high court for the trial of a president accused of treason. Marx reads the document as a machine for manufacturing the coup. The president, armed with the whole executive, the army, the patronage of the administration and the prestige of election by the entire people, is set over against an Assembly that can legislate and censure but cannot govern, and the term limit converts every presidency into a countdown: the occupant must either retire into private life at the end of four years or break the constitution that forbids him to continue. The charter, in Marx's cruel summary, contained an article inviting its own violation, because it made the president's legal extinction the price of his legal obedience, and placed at his disposal, in the army and the bureaucracy, every instrument required for the crime. Around the central defect sit the lesser ones he savours: the state of siege provisions that let the executive suspend the liberties the charter proclaimed, the electoral machinery left to ordinary law, so that the suffrage itself could be, and was, curtailed by statute in 1850 without touching the constitution's text. The lesson he draws is not that constitutions are fictions. It is that a constitution is a deposit of the balance of forces at the moment of writing, armed with the machinery of the state, and that when the balance shifts, the deposit's terms become either a battlefield or a suicide note. The Assembly of 1849 to 1851, elected under the document and determined to use its majorities against the president the document had created, lived inside that lesson for two years without learning it, which is why the constitutional narrative, dry as it sounds in summary, generates some of the book's most electric pages.

## The war on words: press, clubs and caricature

No account of the Second Republic's death is complete without the campaign against the printed and spoken word, and Marx, a lifelong newspaper editor who had been prosecuted, suppressed and expelled for his journalism in three countries, follows that campaign with a professional's attention. The sequence is methodical. After June 1848 the clubs, the political societies where the Parisian left debated and organised, were regulated, watched and progressively strangled; the newspapers were bound by caution money, stamp duties and prosecutions that priced opposition journalism toward extinction; the colportage laws chased cheap pamphlets from the countryside; the education laws delivered the village schoolmaster, the republic's agent in the commune, to the supervision of the priest and the mayor. Marx understands each measure as class politics conducted by other means, since the fractions that could afford caution money and lawyers experienced press law as a nuisance, while the fractions whose only capital was a printing press and a meeting room experienced it as disarmament. The war on words also explains a feature of the book itself that puzzles new readers: its saturation in the ephemera of the Paris press, the nicknames, the caricatures, the catchphrases of the boulevard papers, a whole lost world of journalistic shorthand that the narrative assumes its first readers shared. The Paris of the book thought in print at a density few capitals have matched, and its politics were conducted as an argument among newspapers to a degree that makes the modern phrase media politics sound thin. Daumier's lithographs fixed the types, the pear shaped king of the previous reign, the deputy's paunch, the pretender's moustache, and Marx's prose, which performs caricature in words, belongs to that visual and verbal culture as much as to any philosophical one. A reader who grasps that the book was written for newspaper readers about a newspaper civilisation will find its allusiveness less arbitrary and its speed less strange: it is the fastest medium of its age analysing itself.

## Writing France from London

The circumstances of composition are the last piece of scaffolding, and they belong in the guide because they explain both the book's powers and its limits better than any theory of its author's genius. Marx wrote in exile, in poverty, in the British Museum's reading room and in two rooms in Dean Street, working from the French newspapers, from parliamentary reports, from the letters of correspondents and from the conversation of the refugee colony, at a moment when his own political organisation was fracturing and his income depended on journalism placed in America. The Brumaire was written for money as well as for history, for Weydemeyer's struggling journal, and the instalments were composed against the events they described, the later parts overtaking a coup whose consequences were still unfolding as he wrote. Distance gave the book its synoptic calm about structure: from London, the fractions and the machine stand out in relief, unobscured by the daily intimacy that made Parisian journalists connoisseurs of personalities and prisoners of the week's intrigue. Distance also cost the book the provinces, whose resistance to the coup it underweights, and the interior of the conspiracy, whose competence it underrates, and a reader who knows the composition story can predict the errors before meeting them, which is what scaffolding is for. The final circumstance is the audience already described: defeated German revolutionaries in New York, reading a French inquest as their own. It accounts for the book's double register, entertainment and instruction, farce for the wound and anatomy for the lesson, and it accounts for the survival of the text when the journal that carried it died. The lesson was portable. It still is.

## The elections, one by one

Universal male suffrage was the Second Republic's founding wager and its recurring accident, and Marx follows the ballot box through the book the way other historians follow battles, because each election rearranges the stage. The sequence deserves to be held apart, since summaries compress it into a blur of votes. In April 1848 the country elected the Constituent Assembly, and the wager's first result sobered the Parisian left: a moderate and conservative majority, returned by a countryside voting for notables, tax relief and quiet, with the socialist current reduced to a rump. The lesson Marx draws is foundational for everything after: universal suffrage does not transmit the will of the most advanced class; it transmits the state of the whole society, including its least organised and most legend haunted majorities, and a revolution made in a capital must survive translation into a national electorate that did not make it.

In December 1848 the same electorate chose the president, and the result, more than five million votes for Louis Bonaparte against a divided field, is the book's central electoral fact, analysed in the peasantry section above. In May 1849 the Legislative Assembly elections consolidated the Party of Order with a large majority while returning a substantial Mountain minority, and the republic entered its long parliamentary phase with its two futures, monarchist order and democratic opposition, both seated and both armed with mandates. The by-elections of the spring of 1850 then supplied the fright that produced the suffrage law: Paris and several departments returned democratic and even socialist candidates, among them figures whose names read to the propertied as June returning through the ballot box, and the Party of Order answered by disenfranchising the mobile, the young and the poor through residence rules. Marx's commentary on the sequence is among his most quoted political judgements in the book's middle register: the bourgeoisie, confronted with the electoral consequences of its own February gift, chose to saw off the branch, preferring a mutilated suffrage administered by itself to a whole suffrage that might dismiss it. Read as a series, the elections teach the book's characteristic lesson in its purest form. Institutions do not have effects in the abstract; the same ballot box returns a constituent assembly, a Bonaparte, a Party of Order and a scare, depending on the state of the classes and the memories they carry into the booth.

## Religion as organisation

Religion enters the Brumaire not as a doctrine to be refuted but as an organisation to be reckoned with, and readers formed by the famous phrase about opium, drawn from an earlier text, are often surprised by the practical tone of the treatment here. The Catholic Church in the France of the book is a landowner, an employer, a school system in waiting and the only hierarchy that reaches every village the parties cannot. The Party of Order understands this and pays the price of alliance: the expedition to restore the Pope in Rome against the Roman republic, undertaken by a republic against a republic, is unintelligible except as the coalition's sacrament to its clerical partner, and the education law of 1850, associated with the comte de Falloux, which enlarged the Church's role in schooling and subjected the lay schoolmaster to clerical and mayoral supervision, is the domestic instalment of the same bargain. Marx reads both measures organisationally: a coalition that cannot manufacture consent through its divided dynasties rents the one apparatus that manufactures it weekly, from the pulpit, in the confessional and at the school desk.

The peasantry's faith receives the same double treatment as its legend. Belief in the village is braided with property, with the calendar of sowing and harvest, with the memory of the first revolution's war on the Church, which the countryside had resisted as an attack on its own order; the priest and the emperor between them frame the smallholder's world, and Bonapartism's genius is to present itself as the defender of both altars, the one in the church and the one in the cottage where the imperial portrait hangs. Marx's account of the clergy is not gentle, and his anticlericalism is a period piece like his other polemics, but the analytical point survives his tone: in a society where the largest class cannot organise itself nationally, whoever commands the organisations that do reach it, altar, school and prefecture, commands its politics. The state's schoolmasters and the Church's priests fight across the book's middle chapters for the village's ear, and the fight is reported with the seriousness usually reserved for ministries, because in the France of smallholders it was a ministry, the one that governed opinion where opinion was manufactured.

## Paris against the provinces

The book's geography is a politics. Paris makes the revolutions and the provinces dispose of them, and Marx builds the opposition into the narrative's rhythm: the capital invents the republic in February, the country disarms it in the April elections, the capital rises in June and is shot down with provincial acquiescence, the country enthrones Bonaparte in December, and in December 1851 the provinces, or great belts of them, rise against the Parisian coup and are crushed in their turn, in the book's least Parisian and most prophetic episode. The axis explains the suffrage's drama, since universal suffrage is precisely the mechanism that converted the provinces' numbers into the capital's subjection, and it explains the peasantry's centrality, since the provinces in this period are the peasantry, organised by parish and market town rather than by party.

The provinces of the book are not, however, the passive countryside of the sack of potatoes taken alone. Marx registers the democratic socialist propaganda that after 1849 carried republican organisation into the small towns and villages, through newspapers, pedlars, schoolmasters and secret societies, until whole departments of the south and centre had been converted into a France the Parisian parties barely knew. The coup revealed that France: the resistance of December 1851 was the largest provincial rising of the century, and its suppression, with its tens of thousands of arrests and its deportations, taught the regime where its enemies lived. Marx, writing before the repression's geography was public, gives the rising less than its later historians do, and the guide has flagged the correction already; what matters for the reader is that the book's own categories predict the event its narrative underplays. A countryside penetrated by organisation ceases, in the book's own terms, to be a sack of potatoes, and the rising is the proof that the transformation had begun. Holding the axiom and the event together, the inert mass and the insurgent departments, is the exact discipline the text demands, and it generalises: every class portrait in the book is a snapshot of a process, and the process keeps moving after the shutter closes.

## The uncle's shadow

The first Napoleon haunts the book in three distinct capacities, and separating them prevents a common confusion, since readers often treat the Napoleonic material as a single joke about a nephew's inadequacy. In the first capacity the uncle is a historical actor in his own right, the perfecter of the state machine whose anatomy closes the book: the administrator who completed the centralisation the revolution had begun, staffed the prefectures, codified the law and left every successor regime an instrument too powerful to dismantle and too useful to refuse. In the second capacity he is a legend, the peasants' emperor, the guarantor of the parcel and the glory, carried in almanacs, veterans' tales and cottage portraits, and functioning in 1848 as the most valuable political asset in France, an asset that could be inherited but not, as the legitimist and Orléanist candidates for the national imagination discovered, manufactured. In the third capacity he is a standard of measurement, the original against which the repetition is graded, and the grading is the farce argument: where the uncle's coup closed a revolution that had transformed property and law across a continent, the nephew's closes a republic that had transformed comparatively little, and is performed by a man whose qualifications for the role are genealogical.

Keeping the capacities separate clarifies the book's fairness problem and its solution. Marx's Napoleon the administrator is treated with a cold respect the nephew never receives; the machine builder's work is analysed as world historical labour, whatever its author's politics, and the analysis concedes the uncle a stature the farce grading of the nephew can be misread as denying to both. The legend, by contrast, Marx treats as a fact about the believers rather than about the believed, a deposit of the smallholders' experience and hope, and his contempt is reserved for the political entrepreneurs, the nephew first among them, who farmed the deposit. A reader who tracks which Napoleon is on the page at any moment will find the book's judgements more precise and less merely abusive than its reputation suggests, and will understand why the Vendôme column image at the close carries its charge: the bronze uncle, raised by the nephew's regime as its own certificate of descent, is prophesied to fall with the certificate's forgery exposed, the statue and the regime sharing one pedestal and one fate.

## A field guide to the book's imagery

The prose's imagery is systematic enough to be indexed, and a reader who learns the index can decode sentences that otherwise read as ornamental fireworks. Ghosts and resurrections belong to the opening's machinery: the past returns as spirit, costume and nightmare whenever the living borrow its authority, and every later haunting in the narrative, the Mountain seated in the benches of its namesakes, the Party of Order chanting the liturgy of a monarchy it cannot name, is the opening's image at work. Theatre supplies the second family: roles, costumes, prompters, audiences and the pit, the vocabulary in which Marx grades performances and distinguishes the actor who knows his part is borrowed from the actor who has forgotten. The nephew is the index's limiting case, a performer who is nothing but the costume, which is why the theatrical metaphors around him turn cruel.

Animals and monsters supply the third family, and it is the one where the audit recommended throughout this guide applies most sharply. The peasantry's sack of potatoes belongs here as an image of aggregation without organisation; the parasitic body of the state machine belongs here as an image of an institution feeding on the society it administers; the portraits of individual politicians borrow from the menagerie whenever Marx's contempt outruns his analysis, and the reader should let those passages entertain without letting them testify. Weather and disease supply the fourth family, fevers, crises, the parliamentary cretinism diagnosed like a clinical condition, the atmosphere of a capital breathing intrigue; these images carry the book's sense of a political world as a medium in which men move and are moved, half agents and half patients. None of the families is decoration in the weak sense. Each is a compressed argument, and the discipline of reading them is the discipline of expanding the compression, asking what claim the ghost, the costume, the parasite or the fever is making, and whether the narrative around it has paid for the claim in evidence. Readers who perform that expansion find the famous style becoming more, not less, impressive, because the imagery survives translation into argument at a rate few political writers in any language can match.

## The book's silences

A guide that audits its text should end the audit with the silences, because what a masterpiece omits teaches as much about its moment as what it says, and the Brumaire's omissions are neither random nor small. The Second Republic whose death the book narrates was also the regime that abolished slavery in the French colonies, a decree of the provisional government in the spring of 1848 that freed a quarter of a million people and belongs on any complete list of the republic's acts. The book barely touches it. Its France is metropolitan to the core: the colonies appear, when they appear, as postings for troublesome generals and destinations for deported insurgents, and the army that executes the coup is in part an African army in its officers' formation, men seasoned in the conquest of Algeria, a fact the narrative registers in passing while the colonial war that formed them stays offstage. Later scholarship on empire and on the republic's global footprint has filled the frame the book leaves narrow, and readers should know the frame is narrow, the more so since the machine Marx anatomises would go on to administer an empire as readily as a republic.

The second silence concerns half the population. The suffrage whose career the book follows was male by design, and the women of Paris, who organised clubs, published newspapers and petitioned for political rights in 1848 before the republic shut their clubs and their press, move through the narrative, when they move at all, as crowd and chorus rather than as actors with fractions and programmes. The omission was the age's as much as the author's, and the book's categories, applied to the material its narrative neglects, generate the questions its author did not ask: what organisations spoke for the women of the workshops and the markets, what did the republic's school and church settlements do to their daughters, whose labour in the putting out trades underwrote the fractions whose men fill the stage. Historians of women and of labour have written those chapters since, and the guide's recommendation is practical: read the Brumaire as the supreme account of the republic's public stage, and read it knowing the stage was built, lit and provisioned by people the playbill omits. The admission costs the book nothing it can afford to keep. A method that prides itself on finding the material basis of politics should be honoured, not embarrassed, when later readers extend the search for the basis beyond the founder's field of vision.

## The afterlife of the book

Few short political works have had so many second careers, and tracing them teaches a reader what in the text is load bearing, because each generation of borrowers has taken a different beam. The first borrowers were the revolutionaries of Marx's own movement, for whom the book was the authorised inquest on 1848 and a training manual in the analysis of defeat. The German social democracy that republished it treated the closing anatomy of the state machine as doctrine, and the phrase Bonapartism began its long migration from a French regime's name into a general category for executives raised above deadlocked classes, a migration whose conceptual history is traced in the state guide linked earlier in this article. The Russian revolutionaries read it as the classic study of how a revolution is strangled by its own forms, and its sentences on the peasantry and on the smashing, rather than the inheritance, of the state machine entered the debates of 1917 through Lenin's reading of the later French text, the account of the Commune that answers this one.

The twentieth century's academic borrowers took other beams. Political scientists extracted the relative autonomy of the executive and built research programmes on the extraction, returning to the final part of the book the way engineers return to a patent. Gramsci, writing his prison notebooks under a regime that invited the comparison, developed his category of Caesarism in explicit dialogue with the Bonapartism analysis, distinguishing progressive and reactionary forms of the arbiter regime and asking when the arbiter's intervention opens a situation and when it seals one. Analysts of fascism reached for the book's account of a plebiscitary executive resting on a frightened petty bourgeoisie and a declassed street arm, and argued for decades over how far the analogy ran, an argument that did the book the honour of treating its categories as hypotheses rather than as scripture. Historians of France, meanwhile, performed the slowest and most valuable borrowing: they took the narrative as the account to beat, and the archival corrections accumulated across a century, on the countryside, on the coup's competence, on the electorate's composition, are the measure of how long the book set the questions even where it lost the answers.

The quotation culture performed the strangest borrowing of all, detaching the opening sentences into a free floating wisdom about history repeating itself, applicable to elections, football seasons and fashion, and in the process dissolving exactly what the sentences were built to do. In their native habitat the lines are instruments of a specific analysis: repetition names the Second Republic's relation to the first empire, farce grades the nephew against the uncle, and the nightmare of the dead generations names a mechanism the narrative then tracks through parties and classes. Exported, they become a shrug about human folly. A reader who has worked through the book is immunised against the export version, and will notice, in the wild, how rarely the exported sentence is accompanied by the one thing its author attached to it: a credit to Hegel for half of it, and a book length demonstration of the other half.

## How to argue with this book

The guide has recommended throughout that the book be read actively, and it ends its analytical work by converting the recommendation into a protocol a reader can actually follow on a second reading, pencil in hand. The protocol has five passes, one for each of the habits the conjuncture section named, and each pass produces a different margin.

On the first pass, argue with the fractions. Each time a faction is assigned to a class, write the revenue question in the margin: what does this group live on, and does the text show it? Where the answer is shown, mark the passage as earned; where the assignment arrives by announcement, mark it as credit extended to the author, and keep a running total. The totals, honestly kept, are the reader's own verdict on the strongest objection, reached by evidence rather than by temperament. On the second pass, argue with the representations. For each class, list who speaks for it in the narrative and test the speeches against the interests the first pass established. The gaps the list exposes, between the Mountain's proclamations and the shopkeeper's ledger, between the Party of Order's liturgy and its two dynasties, are the book's real subject matter, and seeing them tabulated is the fastest route to understanding why the counter-text reading holds. On the third pass, argue with the machine. Track every appearance of the bureaucracy, the army, the prefects and the police as actors rather than as instruments, and note the moments when their own appetites change the outcome. The pass usually surprises readers who thought the state autonomy material was confined to the final part; the machine is at work from the first chapters, collecting taxes, staffing ministries, waiting.

On the fourth pass, argue with the calendar. Rebuild the chronology from the narrative alone, then check it against the timeline in this guide and against any standard chronology of the Second Republic, noting where Marx compresses, where he reorders for effect, and where the compression serves the argument. Compression is not falsification, but a reader is entitled to know where the joints are. On the fifth pass, argue with the costumes. List the borrowed names, anniversaries and uniforms the actors wear, and ask of each what work the borrowing did: whom it nerved, whom it deceived, what it prevented its wearer from seeing. The list is the opening paragraph verified against the whole text, and a reader who completes it has done something better than agree or disagree with the book. The reader has used it, which is the only tribute a working text has ever wanted. Argument conducted this way also produces the proper sequel: a reader with five passes of marginalia owns a small archive of questions, on the countryside, on the conspiracy, on the suffrage figures, that the secondary literature exists to answer, and can enter that literature as a critic rather than as a tourist.

## For students and exam candidates

Two elements of this book are directly examinable wherever it is set, and they should be revised as a pair. The first is the opening paragraph, where marks depend on attribution precision: the recurrence remark credited by Marx to Hegel, the tragedy and farce grading identified as Marx's addition, and the sentence on men making their own history quoted whole, with its circumstances clause intact, never amputated into a slogan about free will. The second is the determinism counter-text, the argument that this, Marx's most detailed political analysis, proceeds through ideology, tradition, fraction and accident rather than by deduction from economic position. A strong answer states the counter-text, supports it from two episodes, the suffrage law of 1850 and the peasant vote of 1848 answer well, and acknowledges the material anchors, the tax, the mortgage and the payroll, that keep the reading honest. Weaker answers either recite the caricature of economic determinism and cite the Brumaire as its example, which inverts the evidence, or quote the sack of potatoes as a complete theory of the peasantry, which mistakes a passage for the book. Revise the timeline until the sequence February, June, December, the Assembly, the suffrage law, revision, the coup can be reproduced without notes, because every analytical question on this text is secretly a sequence question.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The book teaches best in instalments matched to its own seven parts, with the timeline and cast table issued at the first meeting and withdrawn at the last, so that students experience the scaffolding becoming unnecessary. Two seminar exercises have unusual yield. The first assigns the attribution problem: students receive the opening paragraph in a popular rendering with the Hegel credit removed, and are asked to restore it from the text and to defend the restoration's importance, which teaches citation discipline through a case where the stakes are visible. The second assigns the earned and unearned attribution hunt described in the objection section: each student takes one class attribution from the narrative and grades the evidence Marx supplies for it on the page, distinguishing the tax and payroll arguments from the announced ones. Discussion should reserve time for the historians' corrections, the provincial resistance and the coup's competence among them, not as a debunking coda but as the seminar's real subject: how a partisan masterpiece and the archives that correct it can be read together without either being wasted. Assessment by essay on the counter-text, with the objection required in the answer, reliably separates summary from understanding.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the book's durable value is methodological before it is evidentiary. Its fraction analysis, representation analysis and anatomy of executive autonomy remain working instruments, and its closing account of the state machine is a founding document for the literature on relative autonomy, to be read alongside the later debates rather than beneath them. Its narrative, by contrast, is a primary source with a known composition history: written in exile within weeks, from press reports and correspondence, for a partisan journal, and it should be cited as such, triangulated against the departmental archives for the provinces, against the conspiracy's documentation for the coup's preparation, and against the electoral record for the votes it interprets. The liveliest research questions the text still generates concern its silences and compressions: the rural movement of 1849 to 1851, the colonial dimensions of the army that made the coup, the financial networks behind the regime it founded. For journalists, the book's transferable assets are the attribution discipline of its opening, a permanent warning against handing a famous sentence to the wrong author, and its model of the conjunctural profile, the political portrait built from fractions, machinery and costume rather than from personality alone. Quote the tragedy and farce line with its credit line attached, quote the history making sentence whole, and treat the nephew's career as the standing reminder that a politician's ridiculousness is not evidence against his prospects. The regime founded in the coup lasted eighteen years, and the journalists who had laughed best at its founder were not, in the end, the ones who had explained him.

## The pretender's programme

One service a reader's guide can perform is to restore the document that Marx's caricature tends to erase: Louis Bonaparte arrived in 1848 with a text of his own. His Napoleonic Ideas, published in 1839, presented the empire retrospectively as a system, order wedded to progress, authority consecrated by popular vote, glory abroad and improvement at home, and presented its author as the system's trustee. Two armed attempts on the first Napoleon's anniversaries of the heart, at Strasbourg in 1836 and at Boulogne in 1840, had ended in fiasco and in a life sentence in the fortress of Ham, from which he escaped in 1846 disguised as a workman, and the Ham years produced further writings, including a pamphlet on the extinction of pauperism that promised the labouring poor a Napoleonic solicitude. Marx knew this record and treats it as part of the farce, the understudy's prompt book, but the historian's correction discussed earlier begins here: the programme, however opportunistic its combinations, was coherent enough to be recognised by voters, and its promises, order for the propertied, relief for the peasant taxpayer, glory for the army, solicitude for the poor, map precisely onto the coalition the December election assembled. The nephew's victory is less mysterious, and Marx's structural account more necessary rather than less, once the prompt book is read: a prepared performance still requires a theatre in collapse before the understudy is called, and the book's anatomy of the collapse is where its explanation lives. Readers who consult the pretender's writings after the Brumaire will find the exercise bracing in both directions, the programme thinner than its author hoped and more substantial than its greatest critic allowed.

## The generals

The book's military cast deserves the same fraction discipline as its politicians, because the army of the Second Republic was not one actor but several, and the coup's outcome turned on their differences. Cavaignac, the republican general who crushed the June rising and governed France as head of the executive for the second half of 1848, embodies the first possibility: the soldier as the bourgeois republic's own sword, brutal in June and constitutional in December, when he stood for the presidency, lost to the name, and retired into an opposition of wounded legality. Changarnier embodies the second: the Assembly's commander, a monarchist manoeuvrer who held the Paris garrison as the Party of Order's insurance against both president and street, intrigued beyond his instructions, and was removed by the president in January 1851 in the dismissal the Assembly swallowed and never recovered from. The African generals embody the third: officers formed in the Algerian conquest, among them Saint-Arnaud, the war minister who executed the coup's military dispositions, and Magnan, commanding in Paris, men whose careers had taught them that civilian politics was a slower and dirtier form of the warfare they practised professionally, and whose loyalty ran to glory, promotion and the Napoleonic legend rather than to any assembly.

Marx tracks the three possibilities without sentimentalising any of them, and his treatment clarifies a point the determinism debate often muddles. The army in the book is neither the executive committee of the bourgeoisie nor an unmoved mover outside society; it is an institution whose internal economy of honour, plunder and advancement makes it available for some political uses and unavailable for others, purchasable by the man who feeds its appetites and unreliable for the class that merely instructs it. The Party of Order learned the distinction in January 1851 and the republic learned it in December. A reader who keeps the generals apart, the republican sword, the monarchist insurance and the African praetorians, will find the coup's military narrative legible without any recourse to the fiction of an army acting as one mind, and will have acquired, almost without noticing, the book's general rule for institutions: ask what the men inside them eat, what they remember, and what they hope for, before asking whom they serve.

The rank and file complete the picture, and Marx does not neglect them. The soldiers who parade, shout and finally fire are themselves conscripts drawn heavily from the peasantry the book has already analysed, young men for whom the army is wages, food, travel and the uncle's glory made tangible, and whose officers feed them, on the eve of the coup, with the wine and sausage of the imperial legend. The army's obedience in December is therefore not a mystery requiring a theory of military caste; it is the peasant vote in uniform, the legend acting through the one national institution that gathered the countryside's sons into a single body and taught them to answer to a name.

## Before February, and the social question's brief spring

The book begins its prehistory with the July Monarchy already rotting, and a short expansion of that prehistory helps a reader feel the speed of what follows. The immediate road to February ran through the banquet campaign of 1847, a device of the parliamentary opposition: since political meetings were restricted, reformers organised subscription banquets at which toasts did the work of speeches, demanding an extension of the suffrage that the ministry of Guizot had refused for years. When the government banned the Paris banquet scheduled for February 1848, the confrontation moved into the streets, the National Guard wavered and then fraternised, troops fired on a crowd on the boulevard des Capucines, and within two days Louis Philippe had abdicated and a provisional government was being proclaimed at the Hôtel de Ville under pressure from the armed crowd outside. Marx treats the February days as a revolution made by an alliance that had agreed on nothing but removal, and the banquet campaign's presence in the prehistory matters because it shows the alliance's respectable wing intending a dinner and receiving a republic.

The social question received its institutional answer in the Luxembourg Commission, and the guide pauses on it because confusion between the Luxembourg and the National Workshops is the most common factual tangle in first readings. The Luxembourg, a government commission on labour chaired in effect by Louis Blanc, with a worker, Albert, beside him, was the provisional government's concession to the demand for the organisation of labour: a parliament of trades, debating hours, wages and association, armed with prestige and denied a budget. The National Workshops were a different creation entirely, organised by the government's moderate wing partly to occupy the unemployed and partly, in Marx's reading, to build a counterweight against the Luxembourg's influence, enrolling the jobless in brigades on relief wages. The distinction is the February compromise in institutional form: the social republic received a debating chamber, its enemies received the payroll, and when the Assembly moved against the workshops in June, the workers who rose were defending the second institution in the name of the first one's promises. Marx's narrative keeps the two apart with care, and a reader who does the same will find the road to June shorter and clearer than the summaries suggest.

## The two kings in the wings

No feature of the Party of Order baffled contemporaries more, or repays a reader's attention better, than the fact that the coalition governing France in the name of monarchy could not say which monarchy it meant. The Legitimists held for the elder Bourbon line and its exiled claimant, the comte de Chambord, grandson of Charles the Tenth, in whose person the principle of hereditary legitimacy undiluted by revolution resided. The Orléanists held for the younger line that had reigned from 1830 to 1848, whose claimant after Louis Philippe's death in exile in 1850 was his grandson, the comte de Paris, and whose principle was monarchy reconciled with the revolution's settlement, the tricolour, the charter and the propertied electorate. Each faction experienced the other as a usurpation with better or worse manners, and their coalition, the Party of Order, was a marriage of two restoration projects united only by their common enemies: the republic, the democrats and the workers of June.

The negotiations to fuse the two claims, pursued in salons and through emissaries across 1850 and 1851, form the book's most exquisite study in the weight of the dead. Fusion required the Orléanist claimant to stand aside for the Legitimist, or the Legitimist to accept the revolution's flag and settlement as the price of the crown, and on those rocks of symbol and precedence the talks repeatedly foundered, while the president whose displacement was their object used the interval to build his machine. Marx savours the foundering because it is the opening paragraph enacted in drawing rooms: dynastic memory, the purest form of inherited circumstance, preventing the two most powerful factions in France from combining against the adventurer both despised, until the adventurer relieved them of the problem. The reader should resist treating the episode as comic relief. It is the mechanism by which the Assembly arrives at December 1851 divided, and it generalises into one of the book's transferable findings: coalitions of the propertied are strongest against the class below them and weakest at the point where their own pasts must be reconciled, and a usurper's best ally is frequently the etiquette of the houses he displaces.

## Thiers and the parliamentary chiefs

The Assembly's leadership deserves a collective portrait, because Marx treats its chiefs as a distinct political species: the parliamentary veterans of the July Monarchy, men who had governed France through ministries and chambers for eighteen years and who experienced the republic as an interlude in their own careers. Adolphe Thiers towers over the group in the narrative as in the period: historian of the first revolution and of the empire, minister under Louis Philippe, the Party of Order's most agile tactician, a man whose intelligence Marx never denies and whose smallness of ultimate purpose he never forgives. Around him stand the coalition's other instruments: Berryer, the Legitimists' great advocate, whose eloquence served a cause he knew to be embalmed; Montalembert, the Catholic liberal turned man of order, who carried the Church's bargain into the chamber; Odilon Barrot, the February reformer become the republic's conservative minister, presiding over the Roman expedition his younger self would have denounced. The gallery matters because the book's judgement on the parliamentary bourgeoisie is delivered through it: here are the most experienced politicians in Europe, masters of every procedural art, and their mastery is precisely their incapacity, since every resource they command belongs to a chamber whose authority drains away each time they decline to defend it by irregular means. Marx's cruellest pages follow Thiers through the manoeuvres of 1851, computing, combining, outwitting his rivals in the Assembly's game while the president, playing a different game with soldiers and prefects, renders the board obsolete. The chiefs are arrested in their beds on the morning of the coup, and the narrative's pity and contempt arrive in equal measure: pity for the spectacle of a political class destroyed by the forms it worshipped, contempt for its refusal, across two full years of warning, to learn the one lesson its own historian, Thiers the scholar of revolutions, might have taught it, that institutions survive only where somebody is willing to break the rules of the institution to save them, a willingness the book locates everywhere except in the chamber elected to embody it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is The Eighteenth Brumaire about?

It is Marx's narrative and analysis of the French Second Republic, from the February revolution of 1848 that overthrew the July Monarchy to the coup of 2 December 1851 by which the republic's elected president, Louis Bonaparte, dissolved the Assembly and prepared the Second Empire. Written in the winter after the coup and published in New York in 1852, it follows the Constituent and Legislative Assemblies, the June Days rising, the presidential election of 1848, the Party of Order's government and the suffrage law of 1850, asking at every stage which classes and fractions held power, through what organisations and illusions they held it, and how an executive machine built over generations finally rose above them all.

### Q: Why is it called the Eighteenth Brumaire?

Brumaire was the fog month in the calendar of the first French Revolution, and on its eighteenth day, in November 1799, Napoleon Bonaparte overthrew the Directory in the coup that began his rise to emperor. Marx titles his account of the nephew's coup of December 1851 with the uncle's date to make the book's comparison before the narrative starts: the original seizure of power by a general of the revolution, repeated half a century later by an heir whose claim was the family name. The title is therefore an argument in miniature, the tragedy and farce grading applied to a calendar, and it warns the reader that everything the nephew does will be measured against the uncle's shadow.

### Q: Did Marx say history repeats first as tragedy then as farce?

Partly, and the division of credit is the point of the book's opening sentence. Marx wrote that Hegel had remarked somewhere that great historical facts and personages occur twice, and then added, as his own completion of the remark, that Hegel forgot to add the first time as tragedy and the second time as farce. The observation about recurrence is the part Marx attributes to Hegel, although no exact passage in Hegel's writings has been located for it. The grading into tragedy and farce is Marx's addition, designed for the case in hand: the first Napoleon's coup as the original, his nephew's as the diminished repetition.

### Q: What does men make their own history mean?

The sentence continues, and the continuation governs the meaning: men make their own history, but they do not make it just as they please, because they act under circumstances transmitted from the past. The first clause assigns real agency to human beings, who organise, choose and fight, and the whole book is unintelligible without their decisions. The second clause assigns equal reality to the inheritance within which they choose: property relations, state machinery, party organisations, memories and names. Read whole, the sentence is a method rather than a slogan, instructing the analyst to ask of every outcome both what the actors attempted and what the inherited circumstances permitted, encouraged or deformed.

### Q: Is the Eighteenth Brumaire hard to read?

Its difficulty is real but specific, and it is a difficulty of reference rather than of philosophy. Marx wrote at journalistic speed for readers who followed French politics in the press, so deputies, generals and factions enter without introduction, parliamentary intrigues are compressed into ironic sentences, and the prose is crowded with literary and biblical allusion. A reader equipped with a timeline of the years 1848 to 1851 and a cast list organised by class fraction will find the argument clear and often exhilarating, since the concepts are earned inside the narrative instead of being defined in advance. Read in sittings matched to its seven parts, with the cast consulted freely, the book is demanding in the way of a good chronicle, not in the way of a technical treatise.

### Q: Which classes does Marx identify in the Eighteenth Brumaire?

Marx distinguishes the finance aristocracy of bankers and concessionaires, the industrial bourgeoisie of factory owners and great merchants, the petty bourgeoisie of shopkeepers and small masters organised politically in the democratic Mountain, the industrial proletariat whose June 1848 defeat shadows the whole narrative, the smallholding peasantry that forms the largest class in France and delivers the presidency to the Bonaparte name, and the declassed elements he gathers as the lumpenproletariat, organised by the president in the Society of December 10. Each fraction receives a distinct material situation, a distinct political trajectory and a distinct mode of representation, and the refusal to merge them into two blocs is the book's signature analytical move.

### Q: What does the Eighteenth Brumaire say about state autonomy?

In its closing part the book argues that the French executive, an enormous bureaucratic and military machine of officials and soldiers built up under the absolute monarchy, the first revolution, the first Napoleon and every successor regime, had acquired interests and weight of its own. Under the deadlock the narrative describes, a bourgeoisie abdicating political rule to preserve its social rule and a peasantry able to be represented only by a master, that machine could raise itself above society and govern through Bonaparte in the name of order and property while defying the political wishes of the dominant class itself. The autonomy is relative, bounded by the property relations it protects, and the account became a founding text for later theories of the state's independence from the classes it serves.

### Q: Why do historians still read the Eighteenth Brumaire?

Historians who reject large parts of Marx's framework continue to read and assign the book for three separable reasons. Its narrative of a republic destroying itself, faction by faction and vote by vote, remains a model of conjunctural political writing whose energy and texture no archival monograph has displaced. Its analytical instruments, fraction analysis, the study of representation, the anatomy of the executive machine, continue to organise research questions even where its answers have been corrected. And it is itself a primary source of the first rank for the crisis it describes, written within weeks by the most formidable intelligence among the defeated, provided it is read as a partisan document and triangulated against the archives, which is how the profession uses it.

### Q: Do you need to know French history to read it?

No prior command of the period is required, but the book assumes a reader who knows the sequence of events, and the honest shortcut is to acquire the sequence rather than to demand that the text slow down. A reader needs the February revolution of 1848, the June Days, the presidential election of December 1848, the Party of Order's Assembly, the suffrage law of May 1850, the failed constitutional revision of 1851 and the coup of 2 December, together with a cast list connecting names to class fractions. With that scaffolding in place, supplied in this guide by the timeline and cast table, the narrative is self sufficient, and its arguments about classes, parties and the state machine can be followed and judged without further preparation.

### Q: Which edition of the Eighteenth Brumaire should you read?

Choose by criteria rather than by fashion. The text to look for is the version Marx issued with his own preface in 1869, since the preface contains his retrospective account of the method and his answers to the rival treatments by Hugo and Proudhon. The translation should preserve the structure of the opening sentence, with the remark about recurrence credited to Hegel and the tragedy and farce grading visible as Marx's addition, since a rendering that blurs that division destroys the book's most famous correction. The apparatus matters as much as the translation in this text: notes identifying deputies, generals, newspapers and allusions are the difference between reading and guessing, and an edition that supplies them generously will serve a first reader better than a more elegant one that leaves the cast unnamed.

### Q: How does the tragedy and farce line organise the whole narrative?

The grading announced in the opening sentence becomes the narrative's scoring system. The uncle's career supplies the original in every register: the coup of 1799 against the uncle's, the imperial legend against the nephew's borrowed name, the coronation anniversary against the date the nephew chose for his own seizure of power. Episodes are then measured against their originals, the Party of Order performing legitimacy in the costume of a monarchy it cannot restore, the Mountain performing revolution in the phrases of an earlier assembly, the president performing empire at reviews and on provincial tours. Farce in this scheme is not triviality; it is the condition of consequential action performed in quotation marks, and the narrative keeps the quotation marks visible until the farce collects an empire as its prize.

### Q: What should a reader track in Marx's treatment of the peasantry?

Track the three registers in which the smallholding peasants appear and notice when the narrative moves between them. In the electoral register they are the decisive voters of December 1848, delivering the presidency to a name carried by legend, almanacs and the memory of the first empire. In the material register they are debtors and taxpayers, their parcels encumbered by mortgages to the village usurer, their experience of the republic formed by the forty five centime tax. In the analytical register they pose the book's deepest question, whether a mass sharing conditions without organisation forms a class capable of representation, or must be represented by a master standing above it. The sack of potatoes image belongs to the third register and should be read beside the book's own record of rural revolt.

### Q: Why does the book treat Louis Bonaparte as both agent and symptom?

Because its method requires both registers at once. As symptom, Bonaparte is what the deadlock of the classes secretes: a bourgeoisie that has abdicated political rule, a peasantry that can be represented only by a name, an army and bureaucracy available to whoever embodies order and glory together, a constitution scheduling its president's extinction. On that level another adventurer might have filled the vacancy, and Marx's contempt for biographical explanation is methodological. As agent, the narrative credits him with the specific labour that converted a favourable structure into a successful coup: the ministerial reshuffles, the cultivation of the officer corps, the provincial tours, the choice of the anniversary, the patience to let the Assembly disarm itself. Structure explains why a Bonaparte was possible; it does not arrest the deputies for him.

### Q: How does the determinism counter-text reading answer the textbook Marx?

The textbook Marx of the caricature derives political conduct from economic position by a short road, treats ideology as decoration and leaves accident no work to do. The counter-text reading answers by pointing to the tradition's most admired piece of concrete analysis and showing that it proceeds by the opposite road: classes split into fractions that defeat themselves, interests reach their holders filtered through newspapers, legends and organisations, a state machine built over centuries acts on its own account, and outcomes turn on calendars, casualty lists and a failed vote on constitutional revision. The material anchors remain, taxes, mortgages and payrolls, but they operate through mediation at every step. A summary of Marx that cannot accommodate his best book, the reading concludes, should be revised before the book is.

### Q: What makes the growth of the state machinery the book's second plot?

Beneath the parliamentary narrative runs a quieter story that the closing part brings into the open: every regime in French history, absolute monarchy, revolution, empire, restoration and July Monarchy, inherited the centralised executive machine and perfected it instead of dismantling it, adding officials, police, prefects and soldiers until the apparatus employed a population with a material interest in its own survival. Parties rise and dissolve across the narrative, assemblies are elected and dispersed, dynasties wait in exile, and the machine alone never loses, filing its reports under each successive flag. That continuity explains the coup's deepest condition better than any conspiracy: when the classes had exhausted one another, the heaviest organised body in France was waiting to be ridden, and the book's anatomy of it founded the later theory of state autonomy.
