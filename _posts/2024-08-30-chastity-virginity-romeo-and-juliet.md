Chastity is the first fact the tragedy establishes about desire, and it establishes the fact as a refusal. Before any lover speaks a word of love on stage, the audience has heard a serving-man joke about cutting off maidenheads (1.1), and has heard Romeo report that the woman he wants "hath Dian's wit" and lives "in strong proof of chastity well-armed," proof against even "saint-seducing gold" (1.1). The play that popular memory files as the supreme celebration of young love opens, in other words, inside a regime: a system in which a woman's virginity is at once a sacred vow, a household asset, a standing joke among servants, and a military problem for the men who want it surrendered. This article reads that regime whole, from vow to wedding night to tomb, and reaches a verdict on what the play says about purity and its loss.

![Rosaline's sworn chastity and Juliet's eager wedding night in Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict can be stated at once, because the text states it everywhere once the pattern is seen. The play never treats chastity as a value in itself. It treats chastity as a currency whose worth is set by whoever holds it, and it arranges its two great cases as a controlled experiment. Rosaline holds her virginity as a vow, keeps it entire, and vanishes from the play unmissed, her purity praised in the abstract and regretted as waste in the same breath. Juliet holds hers as a bride, surrenders it inside a marriage she chose and contracted herself, in a soliloquy of unmatched frankness, and the play gives that surrender its most radiant poetry while the city around her goes on pricing, joking, and threatening as if nothing had changed. Between the vow that preserves and the loss that fulfils, the tragedy locates its moral weight not in the state of any woman's body but in the question of who governs it. Chastity in Verona is never really about purity. It is about jurisdiction.

That claim needs the whole play behind it, and the whole play supplies it. The servants' opening bawdy prices the maidenhead as spoil. The marriage market prices it as a dowry's guarantee. The Petrarchan lover prices it as a siege obstacle and curses its keeping as theft from posterity. The bawdy of Mercutio and the Nurse prices it as a riddle everyone already knows the answer to. Juliet alone refuses the pricing: in the orchard she makes her consent the condition of everything, and on her wedding night she speaks the loss of her virginity as a purchase she has already made and is impatient to enjoy. The regime's last word belongs to the fathers, who gild the dead girl into a statue, restoring in gold the untouchable purity the living girl had declined to keep on their terms. A ledger of these treatments, set out in full below, shows a pattern no single scene reveals: every speaker in the play who talks about chastity is talking about control, except the one speaker whose body is at stake, who talks about joy.

## Chastity and Virginity in Romeo and Juliet: The Verdict in Brief

A reader searching for the play's position on purity usually arrives carrying one of two ready-made answers. The first is the schoolroom answer: the lovers are innocent, their love is pure, and the bawdy around them is comic relief that the tragedy outgrows. The second is the corrective answer: the play is saturated with sexual language from its first minute, so the innocence is a myth projected by squeamish centuries. Both answers mistake a part for the whole. The text, quoted from René Weis's Arden Third Series edition throughout this article, supports a sharper claim. The play distinguishes rigorously between chastity as a condition imposed and chastity as a choice exercised, and it judges the two in opposite directions. Imposed chastity, the kind guarded by fathers, priced by suitors, and enforced by insults, is shown as a machinery of harm that ends with a girl preferring a tomb to a second wedding. Exercised chastity, the kind Rosaline vows and Juliet unvows on her own terms, is shown as an act of self-government, and the play's sympathy follows the self-government wherever it leads, including into the marriage bed.

### Is chastity ever the play's own value, or only Verona's?

Chastity is Verona's value, administered by fathers, suitors, servants, and gossips, and the play endorses none of their administrations of it. The one form of purity the text honours is self-command: Rosaline's vow respected as her right, Juliet's consent required as the price of everything, and desire celebrated only where the woman herself has set its terms and timing.

The evidence begins with the distribution of the vocabulary. Words for the maidenhead and its keeping cluster in mouths that have no stake in honouring them: Sampson boasts of maidenheads he will never be offered, in a street, to start a fight. Capulet speaks of ripeness and waiting, the language of a man storing value. Paris speaks of possession. Mercutio speaks of the whole subject as a dirty joke whose punchline is that everybody is pretending. The Nurse speaks of it as a fact of the body she has handled since the girl was weaned. Only in two places does the language of chastity turn serious and inward: in Romeo's report of a vow he cannot break, and in Juliet's own speeches, where the subject stops being her purity and becomes her will. That distribution is the argument in miniature. The regime talks about the girl. The girl, when she talks, talks about what she has decided.

It matters that the play's most famous purity image is a refusal of the regime's own astronomy. When Romeo, in the orchard, tries to swear "by yonder blessed moon" (2.2), Juliet stops him, and her reason reaches beyond coyness. The moon is Diana's, the huntress goddess of sworn virginity, and the speech that rejects the oath also rejects the whole calendar of female phases the moon stands for. She will not be sworn by, any more than she will be kept by, the goddess of the vow. What she asks instead is a contract: "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2). Desire, in her handling, is not the opposite of chastity. It is chastity's successor state, entered by covenant rather than surrendered by siege.

The sections that follow take the regime apart piece by piece: the vow at the play's threshold, the goddess who presides over it, the street language that keeps it in coarse view, the market that prices it, and the wedding-night soliloquy that retires it. A full account of the desire that drives the younger Capulet exists elsewhere in this series; the present article keeps its eyes on the regime she and Rosaline both had to answer, the rules about a woman's body that Verona enforced long before either girl spoke.

## Rosaline's Vow: The Siege That Never Happens

Everything the play knows about sworn chastity it learns from a woman who never appears. Rosaline is the regime's purest case and its first refutation, and the text is exact about the form her refusal takes. Romeo's report to Benvolio is worth hearing at length, because its metaphors do the analysis themselves: "She will not stay the siege of loving terms, / Nor bide th'encounter of assailing eyes, / Nor ope her lap to saint-seducing gold; / O, she is rich in beauty, only poor / That when she dies, with beauty dies her store" (1.1). A siege, an encounter, a bribe refused: the lover's vocabulary concedes that what he calls love is, structurally, an assault on a fortified place, and that the fortress is winning. The comedy of the speech is that Romeo experiences a woman's successful self-defence as a catastrophe happening to him.

### What exactly has Rosaline sworn, in Romeo's own report?

Rosaline has forsworn love itself, in Romeo's summary: "She hath forsworn to love, and in that vow / Do I live dead that live to tell it now" (1.1). The vow is total, a lifelong consecration rather than a refusal of one suitor.

Its effect on the reporter is the speech's real subject: her kept purity makes the man who wants her describe himself as the corpse.

The Petrarchan frame deserves a pause, because the play uses it to expose the regime's logic from inside the lover's own head. In the sonnet tradition Romeo has absorbed, the chaste beloved is cruel by definition: her refusal to surrender is the engine of the poetry, and her purity is valuable precisely because it torments. Romeo's oxymorons in the same scene, "O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1), are the sound of that machinery idling. What Shakespeare adds, and what lifts the passage out of convention, is the economic afterthought. Rosaline is "rich in beauty, only poor / That when she dies, with beauty dies her store." Her chastity is figured as hoarding, a fortune locked away from circulation, and the lover's grievance turns out to be a creditor's: posterity is owed her beauty, and she is defaulting. Benvolio's version is blunter still, delivered a few speeches earlier in the scene's logic of cure and confirmed in Romeo's echo: beauty "starved with her severity / Cuts beauty off from all posterity." A woman who keeps herself is, in this accounting, a thief of the future.

No voice in the scene answers for Rosaline. That silence is the point the article's ownership requires underlining. The play gives its sworn virgin no speech, no scene, no defence, because the regime does not require her reasons; it requires only her function, which is to be unattainable. When the Friar later dismisses the whole attachment as a love that "did read by rote, that could not spell" (2.3), the judgment lands on Romeo's reading, not on Rosaline's vow. And when the vow's holder is finally dropped from the text, dropped so completely that the wedding guest list is the last place her name does work, the play performs the regime's own valuation: a chastity that serves no household, seals no alliance, and produces no heir is, to Verona, worthless, however heaven might rate it. The fuller anatomy of Romeo's infatuation, its Petrarchan machinery and its instant evaporation at the feast, is traced in [Romeo's Rosaline Problem: Love Before Juliet](/2022/05/20/romeo-rosaline-infatuation-before-juliet/); the concern here is narrower and colder. Rosaline kept the rule perfectly, and the rule's own city forgot her by Act 2.

Set beside her the counter-case the play is already preparing. Juliet will be offered the same city, the same market, the same goddess. The difference the tragedy builds its meaning on is not that one girl is pure and the other is not. It is that Juliet's chastity has a term set by herself, expiring inside a marriage she proposes, while Rosaline's has no term at all, and the play cannot decide, and finally refuses to decide, whether the endless vow is holiness or waste. Romeo calls it both within twenty lines. That refusal, examined in the critics' section below, is where the modern argument about the play's feminism properly begins.

One more detail in the report repays attention, because it sets the pattern for every later treatment. Romeo says his beloved "hath Dian's wit" (1.1): her intelligence itself is conscripted into the goddess's service, as if a clever woman were self-evidently a cold one. The compliment is an accusation wearing court dress. Verona's imagination cannot separate a woman's mind from the disposition of her body, and Romeo, its gentlest product, reaches for the goddess of chastity to explain why a woman might simply not want him. The next section follows that goddess through the play, because Diana's moon hangs over both girls, and only one of them talks back to it.

## Diana's Cult: The Envious Moon and the Vestal's Livery

Diana is the only deity the play's lovers invoke by office rather than by decoration. Goddess of the moon, of the hunt, and of sworn virginity, she presides over the regime in its theological form, and her appearances mark the exact points where chastity stops being a social arrangement and becomes a cosmos. Romeo reaches for her in the first scene to explain Rosaline, whose wit is Diana's and whose chastity is "well-armed" like the huntress's own. He reaches for her again, transformed, in the orchard, where she survives as the moon he tries to swear by and as the "envious moon" Juliet is summoned to outshine: "Arise, fair sun, and kill the envious moon, / Who is already sick and pale with grief / That thou, her maid, art far more fair than she" (2.2). The rhetoric is gorgeous and its premise is brutal. Juliet is Diana's maid, her sworn attendant, and her beauty is already an act of treason against her mistress. To become the sun she must resign the moon's service.

The play sharpens the point with a piece of costume. Romeo urges his beloved to "cast off" the moon's "vestal livery," which he calls "sick and green" and fit only for fools to wear (2.2). A vestal's livery is a uniform, the dress of an order, and the image concedes what the regime usually hides: virginity in Verona is not a natural state but an issued garment, worn on behalf of a household and a goddess jointly, and removable. The colour assigned to it, sickly green, will return in Capulet's mouth as an insult when his daughter refuses the husband chosen for her, and the echo is one of the text's quiet verdicts. The same green that marks the virgin's uniform in the lover's compliment marks the disease of refusal in the father's curse. Between those two speeches the whole argument of this article sits: the colour of a girl's chastity depends entirely on whether its keeping serves the man describing it.

### Why does Juliet forbid the oath by the moon?

Juliet forbids the moon oath because the moon is Diana's, inconstant in its phases and patroness of the very vow she is in the act of replacing with a marriage contract: "O, swear not by the moon, th'inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2).

She will not have her covenant witnessed by the goddess of the regime she is leaving.

The refusal is usually read as a girl's practical wisdom about male promises, and it is that, but its placement makes it more. It comes inside the scene where Juliet also declines to perform the reserve the regime prescribes. She confesses that she should have "been more strange," more withholding, and that only Romeo's having overheard her unguarded soliloquy has spared her the performance: "But that thou overheard'st, ere I was ware, / My true love's passion; therefore pardon me, / And not impute this yielding to light love" (2.2). The apology names the regime's central demand on a maiden's speech, that it be hoarse with bondage, able to whisper but not to call aloud, and the speech then breaks the demand in the act of apologizing for breaking it. When the Nurse calls from inside, Juliet's aside gives the demand its epitaph: "Bondage is hoarse, and may not speak aloud, / Else would I tear the cave where Echo lies / And make her airy tongue more hoarse than mine / With repetition of my Romeo's name" (2.2). Chastity of the voice, like chastity of the body, is a muzzle; given the chance, the girl would shout.

The myth behind the goddess carries its own warning, and the play's first audiences knew the story. Diana's most famous mortal encounter is Actaeon, the hunter who saw the goddess bathing and was changed into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds for the sight. A sworn virginity defended by annihilation is the cult's foundation legend: the goddess's purity is not a state but a perimeter, and crossing it, even by accident, even by a look, is a capital offence. Romeo's language in Act 1 runs on the legend's logic without naming it. His beloved's chastity is "well-armed," her eyes are weapons in a siege, and the man who merely desires her describes himself as living dead, a hunter already destroyed by the perimeter he never crossed. The Petrarchan lover is Actaeon with the violence turned inward, and the play's gentleness toward Rosaline consists in never quite mocking the perimeter, only the man bleeding against it. Juliet's resignation of the livery is correspondingly grave: she is not leaving a metaphor but defecting from a goddess whose mythology permits no defectors, and the tragedy that follows can be read, at the cult's level, as the perimeter collecting its penalty by other means.

It is worth registering how completely the moon's cult fails to protect anyone. Diana's sworn maid Rosaline is forgotten. Diana's resigning maid Juliet is dead within the week. The moon itself is dismissed by Romeo in the tomb, where the light he carries and the light he imagines in the vault owe nothing to it. The cult persists in the play as language after it has expired as power, which is the fate of the whole regime: fathers and suitors go on speaking its vocabulary of ripeness, vestals, and green sickness long after the only girl it governed has quietly transferred her allegiance to a friar, a ring, and a rope ladder. The sacred register that gathers around Juliet as saint belongs to a different analysis in this series and is touched here only where it borrows the cult's clothes; the present point is that the moon's service, unlike the saint's shrine, is a job Juliet is described as holding and a job she resigns.

## The Street's Ledger: Bawdy, the Maidenhead Joke, and Licensed Speakers

The play's first words about sex are a boast about rape, delivered as a joke, and the placement is deliberate. Sampson, a Capulet serving-man, warms up for a street fight by promising what he will do to the Montague women once the men are beaten: "I will push Montague's men from the wall and thrust his maids to the wall," for "women being the weaker vessels are ever thrust to the wall" (1.1). Gregory draws out the refinement: Sampson will "be civil with the maids" and "cut off their heads," and when Gregory asks whether he means the heads of the maids, the answer completes the regime's crudest syllogism: "Ay, the heads of the maids, or their maidenheads; take it in what sense thou wilt" (1.1). A maidenhead, in the street's handling, is a thing a woman can be relieved of by violence, grammatically interchangeable with her life, and the interchangeability is the joke's engine.

### What does the opening boast assume a maidenhead is for?

The boast assumes a maidenhead exists to be taken: spoil of the feud, proof of one house's dominance over another's women, a trophy whose loss dishonours the men who failed to guard it. Sampson's pun makes the woman's body a battlefield annex.

The play never lets the assumption go unexamined, returning it in politer forms at every level of the city.

Notice what the exchange establishes about the regime's economics. The maidenhead's value in the street is negative and external: it matters because its taking shames the other household. Nothing in the joke concerns the maid. That grammar, ownership without reference to the owned, recurs at every altitude of Verona, in Capulet's talk of ripeness, in Paris's talk of possession, in the fathers' final talk of statues. The servants merely say in prose what the great houses will say in verse. Shakespeare's decision to give the thesis to two clowns before any principal appears is a structural verdict: the regime's logic, stated plainly, is thuggery, and everything after is thuggery with better manners.

Mercutio takes the street's register and gives it genius. His bawdy is the regime's licensed criticism, the fool's privilege of saying that the emperor's purity is a performance. Conjuring the absent Romeo in Act 2, he summons him "by Rosaline's bright eyes, / By her high forehead and her scarlet lip, / By her fine foot, straight leg, and quivering thigh, / And the demesnes that there adjacent lie" (2.1), reducing the Petrarchan icon, the well-armed saint of Romeo's oxymorons, to a catalogue of parts ending in a real-estate euphemism. The Queen Mab speech performs the same reduction on dreams themselves: the hag who rides maids as they lie on their backs "presses them and learns them first to bear, / Making them women of good carriage" (1.4). In Mercutio's cosmology, maidenhood is a brief encumbrance the world is impatient to train away, and the training is a haunting. The wit is savage, and its target is double: it mocks Romeo's worship of the unattainable girl, and it mocks the worship itself, the whole Petrarchan church in which a woman's refusal is the sacrament.

The Nurse completes the triangle of licensed speakers. A widow, a servant, and the only person in the play who has handled Juliet's body since infancy, she speaks of the girl's future in the idiom of the marriage bed with a freedom no one rebukes. Her set piece in Act 1 is the anecdote of the toddler Juliet's fall, the bump "as big as a young cockerel's stone," her dead husband's joke over the crying child: "Thou wilt fall backward when thou hast more wit, / Wilt thou not, Jule?" (1.3). The jest prophecies the wedding night over a three-year-old, in the mother's hearing, as family entertainment. It is funny, it is fond, and it is the regime in its domestic form: the girl's eventual surrender is so completely the household's property that it can be joked about before she can walk steadily, by the woman who will later carry her lover's messages and finally advise her to commit bigamy. The Nurse's later summary of the marriage market's mathematics, "women grow by men" (1.3), is the same idiom with the joke drained out. Between Mercutio's hag and the Nurse's husband, the play surrounds its heroine with a chorus that has always known what her purity is for. Her distinction, when she speaks, will be to say it herself.

## The Price of Keeping: Virginity as Household Asset

If the street states the regime as violence and the wits state it as farce, the marriage negotiations state it as accounting. Capulet's first answer to Paris is the most quoted piece of evidence, and it deserves quoting entire: "My child is yet a stranger in the world; / She hath not seen the change of fourteen years; / Let two more summers wither in their pride / Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride" (1.2). Paris counters with the market's actuarial table, "Younger than she are happy mothers made," and Capulet replies with the line that gives the whole system away: "And too soon marr'd are those so early made" (1.2). Marred is a word for damaged goods. The father's objection to an early sale is not that his daughter is a child but that the merchandise spoils; his tenderness and his husbandry are the same sentence. The fuller dissection of the negotiations, the dowry that is never named, and the repricing that follows Tybalt's death belongs to [Patriarchy and the Marriage Market in Verona](/2024/02/23/patriarchy-marriage-market-verona/); the present article needs only the chastity-specific point, which is that Juliet's virginity functions in these scenes as the asset's certificate of authenticity, the seal whose unbroken state the whole transaction presupposes and nobody mentions.

### What does Capulet's word "ripe" concede about a daughter's value?

"Ripe" concedes that the father imagines his daughter's readiness as a harvest date set by the grower, not by the girl: a fruit is ripe when the house decides to pick it. The metaphor in Act 1 delays the picking for the fruit's protection.

By Act 3 the same household, in a hurry, will call the unpicked fruit diseased, which shows the calendar was always the house's convenience.

The mother's contribution to the pricing is the book speech of Act 1 Scene 3, where Paris is offered to Juliet as a volume "in gold clasps" that "locks in the golden story," and where the mother's arithmetic is candid: "So shall you share all that he doth possess, / By having him, making yourself no less" (1.3). The daughter's unsold state is the stake she brings; the speech never asks what she wants the story to be. Lady Capulet had been a mother at Juliet's age, a fact she reports without apparent irony, so the regime reproduces itself through its own graduates: the woman who was priced becomes the price-list's reader. Against this backdrop the Nurse's earthy gloss, that women grow bigger by men, reads almost as honesty. The market's euphemisms (volume, clasps, story) and the servant's plain speech describe one transaction, in which the bride's intactness is the wrapper the goods arrive in.

Act 3 removes the wrapper's protection. When Juliet refuses Paris, Capulet's vocabulary collapses from husbandry to waste disposal: "Out, you green-sickness carrion! Out, you baggage! / You tallow-face!" (3.5). Green-sickness, the chlorosis that period medicine attributed to retained maidenhood in girls of marriageable age, is the regime's diagnostic revenge: the daughter who will not surrender her body on the house's schedule is declared sick with the very purity the house had stored her in. The insult is the market's confession that its certificate has an expiry date and that a virgin who chooses her own term is reclassified, overnight, from asset to carrion. Her mother's addendum, "I would the fool were married to her grave" (3.5), completes the repricing: a daughter who will not transfer is worth more dead than disobedient. The play will take the wish at its word.

Paris, the buyer, speaks the regime's politest dialect and is no less exposed by it. In the Friar's cell he greets the girl he has never courted with a claim of ownership, "Thy face is mine, and thou hast slander'd it" (4.1), and Juliet's answers in that scene are a masterclass in the equivocation the regime forces on the owned: every sentence true to the husband she has and false to the suitor she faces. Chastity's last administrative form in the play is this interview, in which a girl's body is discussed as a face already transferred, a slander already committed against its new owner, while the girl stands inside the conversation like a clerk minuting the sale of a house she secretly lives in.

## "Gallop Apace": The Wedding Night and the Unvowing

The centre of the play's argument about chastity is a speech the heroine delivers alone, on the afternoon of her wedding, waiting for a husband the city believes to be a stranger. "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2): Juliet summons the night the way the regime's poetry taught her to summon nothing, in imperatives, at a gallop. The speech is an epithalamium sung by the bride to herself, and its frankness has startled custodians of the play for centuries, because it refuses every euphemism the regime prepared for her. She asks the night to teach her the act the whole city has been joking about since the first scene: "Learn me how to lose a winning match, / Play'd for a pair of stainless maidenhoods" (3.2). Then she corrects the economics in advance, in the market's own idiom turned against it: "O, I have bought the mansion of a love, / But not possess'd it, and, though I am sold, / Not yet enjoy'd" (3.2). A dedicated reading of the speech as verse, its falconry, its Phoebus and Phaethon, and its place in the soliloquy sequence is given in [Juliet's 'Gallop Apace' Speech: Desire in Verse](/2022/07/15/juliet-gallop-apace-soliloquy-desire/); the system-level account of the wanting itself is the business of [Female Desire in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/03/01/female-desire-romeo-and-juliet/). The claim here concerns the chastity regime specifically, and it is this: the soliloquy is the moment the regime's central asset is retired by its owner, on the record, in the first person.

### What does Juliet ask the night to teach her?

Juliet asks to be taught the loss itself: "Learn me how to lose a winning match, / Play'd for a pair of stainless maidenhoods" (3.2). The paradox is exact. The match is winning because the stake is wagered inside the marriage she contracted.

The stake is her maidenhead and Romeo's imagined innocence alike; losing it is the victory the vow of Rosaline could never have described.

Compare the speech's bookkeeping with every earlier treatment in the ledger. Sampson's maidenhead is taken. Capulet's is stored. Paris's is transferred. Mercutio's is trained away. Juliet's is bought, possessed, and enjoyed, verbs in which she is alternately the purchaser and the estate, but never the theft victim and never the shelf. Even the famous impatience of the speech, the wish that she might die and have Romeo "cut him out in little stars" (3.2), keeps the agency with her: she disposes of his body in imagination the way the market proposed to dispose of hers in fact. The cliche this series has tracked throughout, the passive Juliet of the greeting-card tradition, cannot survive ten lines of this soliloquy read without cuts. Performance history, which cut the speech for generations, knew exactly what it was protecting audiences from: not indecency, but a heroine whose desire is grammatically the subject of her sentences.

The speech also completes the argument begun in the orchard. There, Juliet made her consent the contract's condition and her bounty the refutation of the hoard: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2). Rosaline's beauty, hoarded, dies with its store; Juliet's, given, multiplies. The two economies of the female body that the play sets against each other could not be stated more cleanly, and both are stated by the text, the first in the mouth of a frustrated lover describing an absent girl, the second in the mouth of the girl herself. The wedding night is where the second economy is cashed. That the night itself is never staged, that the play substitutes an aubade at dawn and a parting on a ladder, is not prudery but bookkeeping: the consummation is the one transaction in the play that belongs wholly to the two parties, and the city, the audience's surrogate in every other scene, is not invited.

What follows the night is the regime's revenge, and its swiftness is part of the evidence. Within hours of the soliloquy, the bride is being re-sold to Paris, and her refusal triggers the green-sickness curse. Within a day she is standing in the Friar's cell bargaining with a dagger, and telling the priest, in the period's most compressed statement of the regime's cruelty, that she would rather stab herself than marry the County. Her line on first hearing of Romeo's banishment had already assigned the maidenhead's fate should the marriage fail: "I'll to my wedding bed; / And death, not Romeo, take my maidenhead!" (3.2). The vow she resigns Diana's livery for is not, in the end, honoured by the city's machinery at all. Death collects what the market, the father, and the friar between them made unpayable in life.

## Thirteen: The Age Question Inside the Chastity Question

The brief for this article names the complication plainly, and it must be engaged through the text rather than waved past: Juliet is not yet fourteen, and her frank longing is spoken by a character the play itself keeps calling a child. Capulet's first description is the governing one, "My child is yet a stranger in the world; / She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2), and the Nurse's chronicle in the following scene fixes the birthday to the week: she will be fourteen on Lammas Eve. Lady Capulet, urging the match, testifies that she was herself "a mother much upon these years" (1.3), and Paris's actuarial reply has already established that mothers younger than Juliet were a commonplace of the world the play depicts. The text, in other words, insists on the age at exactly the moments it discusses the body's disposition, as if daring its audience to hold both facts at once.

Holding both facts is the honest reading, and the play's own distribution of discomfort shows where it intends the difficulty to sit. No character in the tragedy expresses a moment's unease at Juliet's age as a bride. The unease belongs to the audience, and it is produced by a collision the author engineered: the same culture that finds the soliloquy's frankness startling in a girl of thirteen also accepts, inside the play, a father offering her to a count and a mother who was married at her age. The frisson is not an accident of changing manners. It is the regime photographed from two sides. A system that treats a daughter's body as a house's stored value will always produce this double image, a child in the nursery reckoning and a woman in the marriage reckoning, and will switch between them according to the house's convenience. Capulet performs the switch within four acts: stranger in the world when Paris first asks, green-sickness carrion when she declines to be transferred, and "my soul, and not my child" in the curse that disowns her. The age never changes. The valuation does.

The soliloquy's frankness belongs in this frame. Juliet's desire is the desire of a girl the play has shown being priced, joked over, and promised since her third birthday in anecdote; her speech is extraordinary not because a young bride feels longing, which the period's marriage age made ordinary, but because she says it in the first person, to no purchaser, in verse that makes the longing hers. The discomfort a modern reader brings to her age is a legitimate response to the world the play depicts, and the play anticipates it more honestly than its defenders sometimes do: it never asks the audience to find the market wholesome. It asks the audience to notice that the only person in Verona who treats Juliet's body as belonging to Juliet is Juliet, and that the city's machinery, not her frankness, is what kills her. The age question therefore does not weaken the article's verdict. It is the verdict's sharpest evidence, because a regime that can hold a girl to be at once too young to choose and old enough to be sold has confessed that the choosing was never the point.

## The Shrine in the Hall: A Saint Who Sets Her Own Terms

The first meeting is staged as a negotiation over the terms of touching, and its form is the regime inverted. Romeo approaches Juliet inside her father's feast with the only liturgy his Petrarchan training owns, the pilgrim at the shrine: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5). The conceit casts her purity as sacred space and his desire as trespass seeking absolution. What the scene then does, uniquely in the play's treatments of chastity, is let the shrine answer. Juliet corrects the theology in real time: "Saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch, / And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss" (1.5). Touch is licensed, but on the terms she specifies, hand to hand, the kiss deferred until her own logic grants it, and granted at last with a critic's verdict on its technique: "You kiss by th' book" (1.5). The girl priced in Act 1 as a sealed volume turns out to grade the reading.

The contrast with the play's other shrine is the argument. Rosaline, in Romeo's report, is a shrine that refuses all pilgrims, and the refusal is her whole story. Juliet is a shrine that opens its own doors, admits the pilgrim whose suit she has examined, and sets the liturgy herself. The regime can process the first case, filing it under waste. It has no form for the second, because a purity that administers itself has ceased to be a property and become a jurisdiction, and Verona's instruments, edicts, ledgers, curses, are all built for property. The sonnet's fourteen lines are the sound of a jurisdiction being founded. That the founding happens in her father's hall, under the eye of the house that owns the asset, and that Tybalt's recognition of the pilgrim's voice is what eventually prices the night, only sharpens the structural point: the one moment of self-administered purity in the play occurs inside the market's own premises, and the market's response is to send for a rapier.

It is also worth noting what the sonnet refuses to say. No one in the exchange mentions marriage, ownership, or the future; the negotiation is entirely about the present disposition of a hand and then of lips, conducted in the vocabulary of devotion. Juliet's later career can be read as her attempt to give that moment a legal form the city would recognize, and the tragedy as the record of the city's refusal to recognize any form she chose. The chastity regime's deepest rule is exposed by the exception: purity is honoured in Verona exactly as long as its holder does not act like its owner.

The mechanics of the negotiation repay one more look, because the sonnet distributes its permissions with legal precision. Romeo's opening move is a request framed as an apology in advance: the hand has already touched, and the lips are offered as the fine that would smooth the offence. Juliet's answer reclassifies the touch itself, moving it from trespass to rite by observing that saints have hands and that pilgrims' hands do touch, and that palm to palm is itself the kiss the liturgy prescribes. Each quatrain thereafter is a small statute enacted and then amended by the party with the most at stake. The kiss, when it comes, arrives as the execution of an instrument both parties have drafted, which is why its famous wit has a contractual undertone no other kiss in the play possesses. Contrast the feast's other touching, the dance through which the asset was being displayed to the county's representatives, and the contrast is the scene's whole meaning: in the hall's official choreography her hand is given from partner to partner by the figures of the dance, while in the corner her hand is given by herself, once, on terms she has just rewritten. The regime could survive the kiss. What it could not survive, and what Tybalt's ear catches even before the lovers speak of names, is the sound of a negotiation in which the merchandise is holding the pen.

## The Church's Term: Sacrament, Secrecy, and the Friar's Two Registers

Between the vow and the market stands the Church, and the play is precise about the Church's actual position in the regime: it alone can end a maiden's chastity honourably, and it will do so, in this instance, in secret, for reasons of state. Friar Laurence agrees to marry the lovers because the rite "may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3), which makes the consummation an instrument of policy before it is anything else. The sacrament that retires Juliet's virginity is thus administered as diplomacy, and the secrecy that diplomacy requires is what leaves the girl, in the city's records, still an unadministered virgin available for transfer to Paris. The regime's catastrophe is administrative before it is romantic: two incompatible filings of the same body, one true and hidden, one false and public, and every later horror is a reconciliation of the files attempted too late.

The Friar's own language about the lovers registers the strain. His blessing in the cell is shadowed by the famous warning, "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6), an image that makes the consummation itself the explosion. His counsel to Romeo in banishment is franker still about the body's claims, itemizing among the blessings of exile that Juliet lives and loves him, a comfort phrased entirely in the register of possession deferred. And his earlier diagnosis of Romeo's Rosaline phase, as a love of the eyes rather than of the heart (2.3), quietly reverses the regime's valuation of the vow: the kept chastity of the first attachment is treated by the Church's representative not as holiness but as a failure of reading, an appetite of the eye that never reached the heart. The play's one churchman, in short, respects neither the market's certificate nor the Petrarchan altar. He respects the sacrament, and the sacrament is the one instrument in Verona that transfers a woman's body by her own consent, spoken aloud, in the form of a vow she makes rather than a vow made over her.

That contrast between the two vow forms deserves its own emphasis, because it organizes the whole theme. Rosaline's vow is spoken, so far as the audience ever learns, to no one the play names, witnessed by no rite, and reported only by the man it frustrates. The lovers' vows are exchanged face to face, question and answer, in the orchard and again in the cell, with conditions stated, a witness summoned, and a schedule agreed. The regime calls the first chastity and the second ruin. The play's sympathies run the other way, and they run on formal grounds: the vow that is a covenant between persons is honoured by the verse wherever it appears, and the vow that is a wall is pitied, mocked, or forgotten. Chastity in this play is judged by the company it keeps, and it keeps its best company in a marriage.

## A Counterfeit of Death: The Potion as Chastity's Last Defence

The potion plot is usually read as the tragedy's machinery of accident, but in the chastity ledger it has a plainer function: it is the only instrument left by which Juliet can keep faith with the disposition she has already made of herself. Faced with a second wedding, she goes to the cell with a knife and a schedule. Her threat to the Friar is exact about what is being defended: she will kill herself rather than be transferred, and the speech in which she accepts the vial rehearses the tomb's horrors in the order of a girl pricing her terrors against her dishonour and finding the terrors cheaper. The counterfeit death is, structurally, a second vestal livery, a state of suspended untouchability worn to defeat a transfer, and the play marks the resemblance by dressing the supposed corpse in her best robes and laying her in the vault where brides and ancestors share the architecture.

Capulet's lament over the body completes the inversion the ledger has been tracking. The father who cursed the living girl as carrion now finds, in her apparent death, the marriage language he had wanted all along, displaced onto the wrong bridegroom: Death is his son-in-law, Death is his heir, his daughter is married to Death (4.5). The personification is grief, and it is also the regime's final accounting error, because it assigns to Death the very transfer the father had been arranging for Paris, as if the household's books could only be balanced by marrying the girl to somebody. Juliet's own earlier line had made the same assignment in the first person and in defiance: death, not Romeo, would take her maidenhead if the marriage failed (3.2). Father and daughter agree on the metaphor and mean opposite things by it. She means that no living man but her husband will have her. He means that a daughter unmarried to the house's choice is a daughter delivered to the grave. The play spends its last act deciding between those meanings, and the statues are its equivocal judgment.

The aubade belongs in this section too, as the defence's brief hour of success. The one morning of the marriage, the lovers argue the lark against the nightingale in the play's most domestic scene, and the girl who was hoarse with bondage three nights earlier now manages the parting, the alibi, and the timetable like a head of household. Nothing in the scene is bawdy and nothing is coy. It is the regime's nightmare rendered as ordinary happiness: a wife in full possession of the facts, conducting her marriage in her own rooms, on her own judgment. The city answers within the hour, with a wedding date for Paris.

## The Critics on Trial: Kahn, Callaghan, Dusinberre, and the Editor

The modern argument about the play's treatment of chastity is a genuine three-way disagreement, and the article's verdict depends on settling it rather than surveying it. Coppélia Kahn, in the essay on the play collected in Shakespeare's Rough Magic and in her work on masculine identity in the tragedies, reads Juliet's story as a coming of age conducted inside a patriarchal order whose timetable is not hers. On Kahn's account, the fathers control the calendar of female maturation: a daughter becomes a woman when the house declares her ripe, and Juliet's seizure of the calendar, marrying on her own schedule and speaking her desire in her own voice, is the tragedy's creative act and its offence in one. The desire is real, the agency is real, and both are exercised with weapons the order itself supplied, marriage chief among them.

Dympna Callaghan, whose account of the ideology of romantic love in the play is developed across her work on Shakespeare's women and in Shakespeare Without Women, dissents at the root. For Callaghan, the celebrated love is not an escape from the patriarchal order but one of its products: the ideology of romantic love is the form in which the traffic in women becomes invisible, even to the woman trafficked, and Juliet's frankness is scripted by the very system it seems to defy. On this reading, praising the soliloquy as liberation mistakes the cage's finest furnishing for a door. The chastity regime and the romance are not opponents; they are the same administration wearing its two faces, the father pricing the daughter by day and the poetry pricing her by night.

Juliet Dusinberre, in Shakespeare and the Nature of Women, disputes Callaghan's premise from the period evidence. Dusinberre's Shakespeare gives his heroines a liberty of speech and self-knowledge that the conduct literature of the age, with its triad of chastity, silence, and obedience demanded of women, explicitly denied them, and the dramatist's sympathy sits with the women against the moralists. On this account the soliloquy matters precisely because no conduct book would have permitted its existence: a girl speaking her desire plainly, unrebuked by the text, is the playwright taking sides in a live quarrel about what a maiden's purity was supposed to do to her voice. The regime inside the play says bondage is hoarse. The play itself lets her shout down Echo.

The editor's evidence disciplines all three positions. René Weis, in the Arden Third Series text used throughout this article, prints the fuller second-quarto readings on which the frankness depends and glosses the regime's technical vocabulary, maidenhead, green-sickness, vestal livery, with the period senses intact. The textual point is not pedantry. Generations of performance and school editions softened or cut the soliloquy and the bawdy, manufacturing the chaste, passive Juliet of the cliche out of an expurgated script; the authoritative text never contained her. What Weis's apparatus establishes is that the frank Juliet is not a modern feminist projection onto a neutral play but the play as its first audiences received it, and any account of the theme must start from a text in which the heroine's desire is neither hidden nor punished by the verse that carries it.

Adjudication, then. Callaghan wins on the structure: the desire is speakable only inside marriage, the marriage is usable by the Friar as an instrument of policy, and the ending delivers the girl to a golden monument her fathers commission, which is the ideology completing its circuit. Kahn wins on the mechanism: the calendar is the play's real battleground, every crisis in the tragedy is a dispute about timing, the two summers, the advanced Thursday, the forty-two hours, and Juliet's tragedy is that she seizes the calendar and the city takes it back. Dusinberre wins on the text's sympathy, which is where a reading of chastity must finally stand or fall: the play gives its sworn virgin no voice and its desiring bride its greatest speeches, corrects none of her frankness, and reserves its only diagnostic insult, green-sickness, for the father's mouth at his most condemned. The composite verdict is the one this article has argued from the ledger upward. The play indicts the regime and acquits the desire, and it can do both because its subject was never purity at all. Its subject is who holds the keys to a woman's body: a goddess, a father, a market, a husband chosen by the house, or the woman herself, speaking in the first person, at a gallop.

## What the Play Finally Says About Chastity and Its Loss

The ending is the regime's last transaction and its most revealing. The fathers, reconciled over the bodies, promise each other statues in pure gold, and the girl who resigned the vestal livery, who bought the mansion and was sold and not yet enjoyed, is finally rendered in the one form the city always wanted her: precious, silent, and untouchable. The golden Juliet is chastity restored by metallurgy, purity as a public monument, and the play's closing speeches make the restoration sound like love because, within the fathers' vocabulary, it is. They have no other language in which to value her. The ledger closes where it opened, with a maidenhead's worth being computed by men in a public place, and the distance between the street boast of Act 1 and the tomb vows of Act 5 is a distance of wealth and grief, not of logic.

Against that restoration the play sets one sentence of Romeo's in the vault, spoken over the body he believes dead: "Beauty makes this vault a feasting presence full of light" (5.3), and the observation that her beauty's ensign is "crimson in thy lips and in thy cheeks" still. The regime required the dead girl to be pale, vestal, green and sick; the text insists she is none of these, that the girl who unvowed herself is, in death as in the soliloquy, flushed with the life the regime kept diagnosing as disease. It is a small verdict inside the large one, and characteristic of how this play argues: not by speeches about chastity, of which it contains none, but by the steady allocation of its poetry to the girl's own account of herself and its steady refusal to ratify anyone else's.

What the play says about chastity and its loss can therefore be stated in one sentence, and the sentence holds from the first boast to the last statue. Chastity kept by command is shown as a storage system for other people's value, and it corrupts everyone who administers it; chastity ended by the woman's own covenant is shown as the beginning of the play's only honest household, and its ending is mourned as the loss of a person, never as the spoiling of a good. Rosaline, who kept the rule and was forgotten, and Juliet, who broke the rule's administration and is remembered in the play's finest verse, are the two exhibits. The tragedy is not that a pure girl was ruined. The tragedy is that a city could imagine a girl's purity only as something kept for men or lost to them, and had no category for the third thing the play actually shows: a purity that was hers, to keep or to give, at her own gallop.

## Two Economies: The Hoard and the Bounty

The play's argument about chastity is finally an argument between two theories of wealth, and it is worth isolating them, because every scene in the ledger runs on one or the other. The first theory is the hoard. In the hoard economy, a woman's beauty and purity are a store of value that keeping preserves and spending destroys. Romeo states the theory in its purest form in the first scene, complaining that Rosaline, "rich in beauty," is "only poor / That when she dies, with beauty dies her store" (1.1). The complaint's logic is the logic of an heir auditing an estate: value locked in a single life, uninvested in posterity, is value wasted. Benvolio's echo sharpens the audit into an accusation of theft, beauty "starved with her severity" cutting beauty "off from all posterity," as if the sworn virgin were a trustee embezzling an inheritance owed to the unborn. Capulet's husbandry is the hoard theory in its managerial form, the asset stored against spoiling until the house fixes the date of its conversion into alliance. Even the fathers' final statues are hoard objects, value withdrawn from circulation permanently and displayed.

The second theory is the bounty, and the play gives it exactly one spokesman. Juliet's orchard speech on the economics of her love is the anti-hoard stated as a law of nature: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2). In the bounty economy, giving increases the giver's stock, and the certificate logic of the maidenhead, whose whole worth lay in being unspent, is exposed as a category error imported from the market in goods. A candle loses nothing by lighting another candle; the sea is not diminished by the river. The speech is usually anthologized as a love lyric, and it is one, but its argumentative function in the chastity debate is precise: it is the only moment in the play where a woman prices her own body, and the price she sets refutes the unit of account every other speaker has used.

The tragedy's plot can be retold as the collision of these economies. The hoard requires custody, schedules, and enforcement, and generates the play's machinery: the feast as showroom, the negotiations, the advanced wedding, the curse, the counterfeit corpse. The bounty requires only a covenant and a witness, and generates the play's poetry: the sonnet, the orchard, the soliloquy, the aubade. When the two economies meet head-on in Act 3 Scene 5, the hoard's language degrades within a single speech from bargaining to carrion, because the hoard has no vocabulary for an asset that has repriced itself. When they meet again at the tomb, the hoard's final bid, statues in pure gold, is an attempt to buy back into the hoard a girl whose whole meaning, in the play's own accounting, was that she could not be stored.

It follows that chastity, in the hoard economy, is not a moral condition at all but a phase of inventory, the interval between a daughter's manufacture and her conversion. The play says so in the plainest available place, its opening joke, where the maidenhead figures as something to be cut off in a street fight, inventory destroyed to damage a competitor. Everything the regime later says in verse is a refinement of that first prose, and the refinement never changes the accounting. Juliet's offence, on this reading, was never sexual. It was fiduciary. She converted the asset herself, off the house's schedule, into an economy the house could not audit, and the house's revenge was to declare the conversion a spoilage and the girl a waste product, until grief taught it to gild the loss.

## Green-Sickness: The Medicine of Maidenhood

The regime had a medical department, and the play knows its literature. Green-sickness, the chlorosis of early modern diagnosis, was understood as a disease of virgins: a disorder of girls at puberty, marked by a pale or greenish complexion, suppressed courses, languor, and strange appetites, and attributed in the Galenic tradition to retained blood that the body, denied its marital outlet, could not purge. The prescribed cure, repeated across the medical writing of the period, was marriage. A maiden's sickness was, in the physicians' account, her body's own argument for the marriage market, and the diagnosis gave the household a clinical warrant for what the household already intended: the girl's purity, kept past its date, would make her ill, and the illness would be entered as evidence against her.

Capulet's curse in Act 3 Scene 5 is that diagnosis weaponized. "Out, you green-sickness carrion! Out, you baggage! / You tallow-face!" (3.5) lands at the precise moment Juliet declines the cure the market has prescribed, a wedding to Paris on Thursday, and the insult's cruelty is diagnostic before it is personal. The father is telling the daughter that her refusal is not a position but a symptom, that a girl who will not be transferred on schedule is not disobedient but diseased, her complexion already showing the taint of the purity she insists on keeping past its term. The regime's elegance is visible in the construction: the same condition is health while the house stores it and sickness the hour the house wants it spent, and the girl's body is summoned as witness for whichever valuation the calendar requires.

The play has prepared the colour in advance. Romeo's orchard speech had already assigned the virgin's uniform a sickly green, the moon's "vestal livery" being "sick and green" and fit for fools (2.2), so that the palette of maidenhood in this text runs from the outset through pallor and disease rather than through whiteness and light. Against that palette the play sets one counter-image, placed in the tomb and easy to miss: the supposedly dead Juliet, whom the regime's medicine would predict pale, is found by Romeo with beauty's ensign "crimson in thy lips and in thy cheeks" (5.3). The girl who spent her purity on her own schedule carries colour the medical theory reserved for the properly married, and the girl in the play who kept every rule of the diagnosis, the green-sick virgin of the father's curse, was a fiction of the father's anger. The medicine, like the market, is shown describing the house's convenience and calling the description nature.

There is a further refinement worth recording, because it shows how deeply the diagnosis is woven into the play's language of female value. Lady Capulet's parting sentence over the supposed corpse reaches for the marriage formula one last time, wishing the fool had been married to her grave, and the wish concedes the medical logic entire: a daughter unmarriageable by refusal is a daughter deliverable only to the earth, since the earth is the one husband whose suit requires no consent. Between the green-sickness of the curse and the grave-marriage of the lament, the play draws the regime's medical chart in two strokes, and the chart's message is that in Verona a girl's body will be interpreted, by clinic as by market, in whatever sense the household's current transaction demands.

## Maid, Wife, Widow: The Three Estates of Verona's Women

The regime sorts women into estates, and the play is careful to exhibit one of each at full length, because the comparison shows what each estate costs. The maid is Juliet before the wedding: hoarse with bondage, priced in another room, her future body a subject for family jokes. The wife is Lady Capulet, the regime's own graduate, married at her daughter's age and a mother at the age her daughter is being offered, now administering to the next generation the transaction that administered her. The widow is the Nurse, whose marriage is behind her and whose speech is consequently the freest in the play, and the distance between the three women's voices is the regime's tariff rendered as drama: the maid whispers, the wife recites, the widow says what she likes.

Lady Capulet's case deserves the closest attention, because it refutes the charitable theory that the regime's administrators are its villains. She delivers the book speech, the market's tenderest document, without a flicker of cruelty, as a mother performing a recognised office well. She reports her own early marriage as a credential. When the crisis comes, her contribution is withdrawal and a wish for the grave-marriage, the acts of a woman whose entire education in value was the transaction now failing. The play does not hate her for it. It exhibits her as the regime's most persuasive evidence, proof that the system reproduces itself through its products, that the priced girl of one generation becomes the pricing mother of the next without ever having been offered a vocabulary in which her own history could be described as anything but the natural order.

The Nurse's widowhood purchases the opposite condition: exemption. Having passed through the estate the regime values and out the other side, she can handle the girl's body, joke about its future, carry its messages, and finally advise it into bigamy, all without the fiction of delicacy ever being required of her. Her famous counsel in Act 3 Scene 5, that a banished husband is as good as dead and Paris the better bargain, is the regime's logic spoken in the widow's free voice, the market's arithmetic with the poetry removed, and Juliet's horror at it is the moment the girl understands that even exemption serves the house. The estates thus form a closed system: the maid is priced, the wife prices, the widow brokers, and no estate in the cycle contains a woman whose body is administered by herself. Juliet's marriage is an attempt to found a fourth estate inside the second, a wifehood self-contracted, and the system's response, from every estate at once, is to close over the anomaly.

A fourth figure haunts the taxonomy from outside it. Lady Montague, the play's other mother, dies of grief offstage, reported in a subordinate clause by her husband in the final scene, having been granted almost no speech in five acts. She is the wife estate carried to its terminus, a woman so completely administered that the text itself cannot find lines for her, and her silent exit is the regime's quietest exhibit. Set the four women in a row, the sworn virgin forgotten, the self-married girl gilded, the pricing mother widowed by the peace, the silent mother dead of it, and the play's census of female outcomes is complete. Not one of them is an argument for the system that produced the row.

## The Custodians: How the Chaste Juliet Was Manufactured

The passive, spotless Juliet of popular memory is not in the authoritative text, and the history of how she got into the culture anyway is part of this article's evidence, because it shows the regime operating on the play itself. The wedding-night soliloquy was a standing embarrassment to the custodians. Stage tradition for generations cut or trimmed the speech in which the bride summons the night, and school and family editions, most famously the expurgated Family Shakespeare associated with Thomas Bowdler and his sister Henrietta, removed or softened the bawdy that keeps the maidenhead in coarse view from the first scene onward. A play whose heroine speaks her desire in imperatives and whose servants open the action with a rape joke was reissued, century after century, as a monument of innocent love, and the innocence was achieved by the simple method of deleting the girl's voice wherever the voice owned her body.

The deletions were selective in a way that deserves emphasis. The market scenes survived: fathers might price daughters, suitors might claim faces, because that machinery read as plot. What the custodians cut was the counter-evidence, the soliloquy, the bawdy, the Nurse's jests over the child, the moments where the text states plainly what the pricing is pricing. The result was a Juliet who could be exhibited as chastity's icon precisely because the record of her self-administration had been shredded, and a Rosaline problem that vanished entirely, since a culture committed to the pure heroine had no use for a first act in which purity is the obstacle and its keeper the forgotten woman. The cliche this series has tracked from its first articles, the greeting-card tragedy of two innocent children, is thus not a misreading that happened to the play. It is an edition of the play, produced by custodians who understood exactly which passages threatened the icon and removed them.

Modern textual scholarship reversed the process, and the reversal is why the theme of this article can now be argued from evidence rather than suspicion. The fuller second-quarto text that Weis prints restores the soliloquy's frankness and the street's coarseness to their full extent, and the feminist criticism discussed in the preceding section could not have been written in its present form against the expurgated script. There is a lesson in the sequence for any reader weighing the play's verdict on chastity. Verona's regime and the play's custodians performed the same operation for the same reason: both needed the girl silent about her own body, the one to sell her and the other to sanctify her, and both found the soliloquy intolerable. The text, read whole, sides against both operations in the only way a text can, by keeping the speech.

## What the Keeping Protects: Honour, Blood, and the Fear Underneath

A regime this elaborate is protecting something, and the play is candid about what it is. A daughter's virginity in a house like Capulet's is the guarantee of the bloodline's integrity: the alliance a marriage seals is worth its price only if the heirs it produces are certainly the husband's, and the certificate of that certainty is the bride's audited past. Chastity is therefore not, at bottom, a moral ornament in Verona. It is a quality-assurance system for descent, and its inspectors, fathers, mothers, nurses, gossips, are auditing an heir-making instrument. This is why the regime's anxiety attaches to daughters and not to sons, why there is no male maidenhead in the language, and why the audit's failure is imagined as catastrophe rather than sin: a compromised certificate does not merely embarrass a girl, it voids the warranty on the house's future.

The honour code that governs the men's violence is the same system viewed from the street. The feud's young men defend the house's name with rapiers while its daughters defend the house's blood with their bodies, and the play keeps the two defences in one frame: the feast at which Juliet is first priced is the feast at which Tybalt must be restrained from defending the house's honour against the uninvited guest, and the guest he wants to strike is the man who will, within two days, void every warranty the house was keeping. Capulet restrains the rapier and proceeds with the pricing, choosing the asset over the insult, and the choice is the regime's hierarchy in a single gesture. Honour can survive a swallowed insult. The ledger cannot survive an unaudited heir.

Underneath the economics sits a fear the play names only obliquely, in the Nurse's anecdotes and the fathers' curses: the fear that a daughter's desire, once admitted to exist, cannot be scheduled. The regime's whole apparatus, the hoarse bondage, the harvest calendar, the medical warnings, the showroom feast, is a technology for ensuring that a girl's wanting arrives after its purchaser and never before. Juliet's catastrophe, in the regime's own terms, is a scheduling failure. She wants first, in the orchard, in the wrong house's garden, toward the wrong house's son, and every subsequent disaster is the apparatus attempting to restore a sequence it can audit. Read this way, the play's famous haste is not a flaw in the lovers' characters but the sound of two calendars colliding, the house's calendar of storage and sale against the girl's calendar of bounty and gallop, and the tragedy is that Verona possessed no machinery for reconciling calendars except a tomb.

## The Ring and the Ladder: Instruments of a Self-Administered Marriage

The market's instruments are all containers: the clasps that lock the golden story, the orchard walls, the vault, the statue. The self-administered marriage has instruments too, and the play sets them in deliberate contrast. The first is a ring, sent by Juliet through the Nurse with the wedding arrangements, a circle that closes by the giver's own act rather than a clasp fastened by a keeper. The second is a rope ladder, the "cords made like a tackled stair" that Romeo commissions for the wedding night (2.4), an instrument whose whole meaning is ascent by invitation: the wall the regime built to keep the asset in is climbed, from inside, by a stair the asset's owner ordered lowered. Where the market's containers all assume the girl is the thing contained, the ladder assumes she is the party who decides what crosses the wall, and in which direction.

The contrast extends to the marriage's paperwork, such as it is. The market's transfer of Juliet to Paris generates feasts, guest lists, musicians, and a county wedding's full administration, all of it conducted over her head and most of it after her refusal. The self-administered marriage generates two errands, a ring and a ladder, run by a servant and a nurse, and a ceremony of a few minutes in a friar's cell with no witnesses but the priest. The play's sympathies are legible in the logistics. Everything the regime does to a daughter is elaborate, expensive, and public; everything she does for herself is brief, cheap, and exact, and the elaborate machinery is what fails. The feast's guest list delivers the enemy to her door. The county wedding's administration delivers her to the potion. The ladder, alone among the play's instruments, delivers exactly what it was built to deliver, once, on schedule, and is never heard of again.

There is a final instrument, and it belongs to neither economy: the dagger Juliet carries to the Friar's cell and lays, in the end, in her own breast with the play's most possessive sentence, "O happy dagger, / This is thy sheath" (5.3). The sheath image closes the ledger's longest account. The body that servants joked about relieving of its maidenhead, that a father stored and a suitor claimed and a husband was given by covenant, is disposed of at last by the only person the play ever allowed to hold its instruments, and the sentence she speaks over the act is a sentence of ownership. The regime's first joke and the tragedy's last image are the same anatomy read by different hands: in the street it was a threat, in the tomb it is a signature.

## Flowers on the Bier: The Re-Virginizing of Juliet

The regime's strangest operation is its last one, performed over a girl who is no longer a virgin and no longer, in the city's belief, alive. When Juliet is found apparently dead on the morning of the Paris wedding, the household's language reverts, as if by reflex, to the idiom of her maidenhood. Capulet laments that Death lies on her "like an untimely frost / Upon the sweetest flower of all the field" (4.5), and the wedding's preparations are reclassified in a breath as funeral furnishings, the bride reclassified with them. A flower frosted before opening is the period's standing figure for a virgin dead unplucked, and the figure is applied, without a flicker of correction from any speaker, to a married woman whose wedding night the audience has watched her summon, enjoy, and part from at dawn. The city simply declines to update its records. In the public ledger she dies as she was filed: a maid.

Paris completes the re-virginizing at the tomb. His nocturnal rite, strewing her bridal bed with flowers and watering it with tears, addresses her throughout as a bride in waiting, "Sweet flower, with flowers thy bridal bed I strew" (5.3), and his whole claim on the scene depends on the same un-updated filing: she is his promised maid, interrupted. The husband who actually married her enters the same scene as a criminal in Paris's account, a vandal come to do some villainous shame to the dead, because the public record contains no marriage and therefore no husband. Two men fight to the death over a woman's body in a vault, and the fight is possible only because the regime's filing system recognizes the suitor's claim and cannot recognize the wife's own disposition of herself. The re-virginizing is thus not sentimentality. It is the regime's administrative essence surviving its subject: the girl will be processed as a maiden in death because maiden is the only category under which the city ever learned to see her.

Romeo's speeches in the vault refuse the category one final time. He does not address a flower or a maid. He addresses a wife whose beauty has not surrendered its colour, whose lips and cheeks keep the ensign of the life the city keeps misdescribing, and his error about the death is the mirror of the city's error about the life: each reads the body by a filing system, the city's marked maiden, his marked corpse, and each filing is wrong in the direction of its owner's need. When Juliet wakes and finds the record closed beyond correction, her disposal of the body the regime spent five acts administering takes eleven words and a borrowed dagger. The re-virginizing fails at the last possible moment, on the testimony of the only witness whose evidence the play has ever accepted in this matter: the woman herself, acting.

## Two Confessions: Plain Speech and the Price of Being Believed

Juliet makes two great confessions in the play, and the distance between them is the regime's final measurement. The first is the orchard soliloquy overheard (2.2), in which she says what she wants, names its object, sets its conditions, and apologizes only for the impropriety of having been heard saying it unawares. The speech is the plainest in the play, and its plainness is possible because its speaker believes herself unobserved by the world that prices her: the confession is made to the night, and the night does not keep ledgers. Everything she says there is true, and the play's catastrophe never once grows out of an error in it.

The second confession is the interview in the Friar's cell (4.1), where the same girl, now a wife facing a second wedding, must speak to the two men who administer her fate, and where plain speech has become impossible. To Paris she speaks in engineered equivocations, sentences machined to be true to the husband she has and false to the suitor she faces. To the Friar she is plainer, but the plainness is a threat rather than a trust: she lays a knife on the table of the conversation and informs the Church that if its counsel fails, her own hand will administer the remedy. The regime has taught its most truthful speaker that truth is a luxury of unwitnessed rooms. In the orchard she could afford honesty because nobody who mattered was listening. In the cell, where everybody who matters is listening, honesty must wear disguises or carry weapons.

The chastity regime is the thread between the two scenes, because what cannot be said in the cell is precisely the fact of her body's disposition. The marriage that would answer every question, dissolve every claim, and expose every filing as false is the one sentence the second confession may not contain. A regime that audits a daughter's purity as an asset produces exactly this epistemology: the central fact about the asset becomes unspeakable by the asset herself, since speaking it would confess the unauthorized transfer. Her equivocations to Paris are therefore not a fall from the orchard's honesty but its continuation under occupation, the same intelligence defending the same covenant with the only weapons the room allows. Critics who find the later Juliet diminished beside the earlier one mistake the acoustics for the voice. The voice is identical. The room has changed, and the room is the regime.

That is also why the play's last allocation of plain speech matters. In the tomb inquiry, the facts are finally stated in public, by a friar, from notes, over corpses, and the statement changes nothing for anyone it concerns. The regime gets its audit at last, complete, accurate, and useless, delivered in the one room where the audited can no longer be harmed or helped by it. Juliet's two confessions frame the finding: a girl could tell the truth about her body only when no one was listening, and the city could hear the truth about her body only when she was past listening herself.

The two confessions also settle the question the whole article has been circling, of whether the play's treatment of chastity is a period document or an argument. A period document would distribute its sympathies as the period distributed them, honouring the vow, suspecting the soliloquy, and letting the father's diagnosis stand as the last medical word. The play does the reverse at every joint. It forgets the vow, crowns the soliloquy, and puts the diagnosis in the mouth of a man the scene is busy condemning. It gives the regime's vocabulary, siege, store, ripeness, clasps, carrion, to speakers whose metaphors convict them, and gives the counter-vocabulary, bounty, mansion, gallop, to the one speaker with standing in the matter. Arguments are made of allocations like these, and the allocation here is too consistent, across too many registers and too many speakers, to be an accident of a playwright transcribing his culture. Shakespeare is not reporting Verona's regime. He is prosecuting it, with the girl's own speeches as the evidence and her silences, the hoarse years before the orchard and the equivocal hour in the cell, as the damages. The verdict this article has defended is therefore the play's own, recovered from its distribution of verse rather than imposed on its plot: chastity in Romeo and Juliet is acquitted wherever it is hers, and condemned wherever it is theirs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch chastity ledger, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch chastity ledger is the table in this article collecting the play's treatments of virginity in performance order, from Sampson's maidenhead boast in the first scene to the fathers' golden statues in the last. The pattern it exposes is a correlation between speaker and judgment: whenever chastity is administered by someone other than the woman herself, by servants as spoil, by fathers as stock, by suitors as property, the scene's judgment falls on the administrator, and whenever the woman states her own position, in the sonnet negotiation, the moon refusal, and the wedding-night soliloquy, the play gives her its finest verse and its sympathy. The ledger turns that distribution into evidence a reader can check row by row.

### Q: What does Romeo mean when he says Rosaline will not "stay the siege of loving terms"?

Romeo means that Rosaline's chastity is a fortification his courtship cannot take: she will not admit his speeches, his looks, or his gifts, figured in the speech as siege, assault, and bribery in turn (1.1). The metaphor is the confession inside the complaint. A lover who describes a woman's refusal as a siege has conceded that his love operates as an attack on a defended place, and that her purity is experienced by him not as a virtue she holds but as a wall he is failing to breach. Shakespeare lets the Petrarchan machinery indict itself, since every term of the lament assigns agency to the fortress and grievance to the besieger.

### Q: Why does the play open with servants joking about cutting off maidenheads?

The play opens there because the joke states the regime's logic before any principal can dignify it. Sampson's boast, that he will cut off the maids' heads or their maidenheads, whichever sense the listener prefers (1.1), makes a woman's body an annex of the feud, valuable only as the place where the enemy house can be shamed. By giving the thesis to two serving-men warming up for a brawl, Shakespeare establishes that everything politer which follows, the talk of ripeness, volumes, clasps, and possession, is the same grammar in better clothes. The audience has heard the plain version first, and the play relies on that hearing for the rest of its five acts.

### Q: How does the Nurse's story of Juliet's childhood fall turn the wedding night into a joke told over a child?

The anecdote does it by prophecy delivered as family entertainment. Recalling the toddler Juliet's fall and the bump it raised, the Nurse repeats her dead husband's jest over the crying child: "Thou wilt fall backward when thou hast more wit, / Wilt thou not, Jule?" (1.3). The fall backward is the marriage bed, foretold over a girl of three, in her mother's presence, to general laughter. The moment shows how completely the household owns the girl's future body: her eventual surrender is so settled a family asset that it can serve as a christening-style joke before she can walk steadily, told by the woman who will later carry her lover's messages and broker her wedding night in earnest.

### Q: What work does the word "ripe" do in Capulet's first answer to Paris?

"Ripe" turns a daughter into a harvest whose date the grower sets. Capulet asks Paris to let "two more summers wither in their pride / Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride" (1.2), and the metaphor concedes the whole regime in one word: readiness is a property the house assesses from outside, the girl's own sense of her timing nowhere consulted. The word also loads the later scenes, because a household that thinks in harvests will think in spoilage when the crop refuses collection. By Act 3 the unpicked fruit is cursed as carrion, and the distance between "ripe" and "green-sickness" measures how quickly a father's tenderness expires when the asset declines his calendar.

### Q: What does Juliet's confession that "bondage is hoarse" admit about the rules governing her voice?

The confession admits that maidenly reserve was a muzzle she had been performing, not a nature she had. "Bondage is hoarse, and may not speak aloud" (2.2) names the rule the conduct tradition imposed on girls, that purity of body required poverty of voice, and Juliet speaks the line in the act of breaking it, wishing she might tear the cave where Echo lies and outshout repetition itself with Romeo's name. The play sets the admission inside its most famous love scene for a reason: the audience is meant to understand that the girl's earlier obedience, her conditional answers in her mother's chamber, was the hoarseness imposed on her, and that the woman speaking freely in the orchard is the unmuzzled original.

### Q: Why does the play give its frankest speech about virginity to characters who will never marry?

The frankness is licensed by the speakers' exemption from the asset's custody. Sampson and Gregory can joke about maidenheads because no daughter of theirs is on the market; Mercutio can anatomize purity because he will die unmarried and mocks every shrine impartially; the Nurse, a widow and a servant, has handled bodies all her life and holds no stock in the fiction of delicacy. The parents and suitors, who administer the asset, must speak of it in euphemism, volumes, clasps, ripeness, because plain speech would expose the transaction. Shakespeare distributes sexual honesty by class and by stake, and the distribution itself is the criticism: the people permitted to say what virginity is are precisely the people with no power over its disposal.

### Q: What changes in Juliet's language about her body between the orchard scene and the wedding-night soliloquy?

The change is from negotiation to possession. In the orchard (2.2) her body's language is still framed by the contract she is drafting: conditions, messengers, tomorrow, the demand that his purpose be marriage before anything else proceeds. On the wedding afternoon (3.2) the contract is sealed and the language becomes proprietary in the first person: she has "bought the mansion of a love" and is impatient to possess it, "sold" yet "not yet enjoy'd." The interval between the speeches contains a wedding, and the play marks exactly what the sacrament altered, her right to speak of her own body as an owner rather than as an applicant for permission to dispose of it.

### Q: Why does Juliet describe herself as "sold" but "not yet enjoy'd" on her wedding night?

The words are the market's idiom seized and corrected by its former merchandise. "O, I have bought the mansion of a love, / But not possess'd it, and, though I am sold, / Not yet enjoy'd" (3.2) accepts the vocabulary of sale that her parents used over her and reverses every term: she is simultaneously the buyer and the estate, the purchaser of the mansion and the goods awaiting enjoyment, and the only party left out of the transaction is the household that thought it was selling her. The line is the soliloquy's thesis in miniature, the regime's bookkeeping rewritten so that the girl appears on both sides of the ledger and her parents on neither.

### Q: What does the play stage instead of the wedding night itself?

The play stages an argument about a bird. The consummation happens offstage between the wedding and the dawn, and in its place Shakespeare gives the aubade (3.5), the lovers disputing whether the singer outside is the lark, herald of the morning that parts them, or the nightingale that would let them stay. The substitution is the point: the one transaction in the tragedy that belongs wholly to its two parties is the one the audience is not shown, because every other disposition of Juliet's body has been public business, negotiated in halls, joked about in streets, and minuted by servants. Privacy, in the staging, becomes the play's definition of what a marriage is for.

### Q: How does the play treat Rosaline's vow once Romeo stops mentioning her?

The play treats the vow the way Verona treats everything that seals no alliance: it forgets it, and lets the audience watch the forgetting. Rosaline's name does its last work on the guest list that draws Romeo to the feast, the Act 2 Chorus files her phase as "old desire" on its deathbed, and by the Friar's cell scene Romeo himself must be reminded he ever spoke of her. No character defends the vow, mourns it, or even condemns it; the sworn virgin simply exits the city's attention while keeping her purity intact. The silence is the regime's verdict on itself, since a chastity that serves no household turns out to have no more narrative value than a rumour, however perfectly it was kept.

### Q: How do the golden statues return the lovers to a purity the city can display?

The statues reverse the wedding night by metallurgy. Montague vows a figure of Juliet in pure gold and Capulet answers with Romeo's, and the girl who had resigned the vestal livery, bought the mansion, and conducted her own marriage is rendered at last in the form the regime always preferred: precious, silent, and untouchable, a virgin of the civic treasury. Gold is the material the play has associated with the pricing of her body from the clasps of her mother's book speech onward, so the reconciliation's generosity and its indictment are the same gesture. The fathers can love her in public only once she has been reconverted into the stored value they knew how to keep.

### Q: What did the period's teaching for maidens ask a girl's silence to prove?

The conduct tradition asked silence to serve as the outward certificate of an inward state: in the triad the moralists repeated for women, chastity, silence, and obedience, a quiet tongue was read as evidence of a kept body, and a forward voice as the symptom of a loose one. Writers in the tradition of Juan Luis Vives's instruction for Christian women made the connection explicit, prescribing guarded speech as the fence of virginity. The play builds its heroine against that certificate. Juliet's frankness increases in exact proportion to her security in her own covenant, so that by the period's diagnostic the most compromised girl in Verona should be the one the verse most honours, which is the contradiction the tragedy keeps forcing its audience to sit with.

### Q: Why does Juliet's frankness increase at exactly the moments her reputation is safest?

Because the play ties her speech to jurisdiction rather than to shame. While her body remains her father's asset, Juliet speaks in the conditionals and courtesies the asset's custody requires; the moment the marriage is contracted and her disposition of herself is sacramentally her own, the constraint the regime called modesty loses its function and her language becomes imperative, proprietary, and unembarrassed. The correlation is the argument in biographical form. Frankness in this play is not the opposite of chastity but its successor state under new management, and the management is hers. What Verona calls her fall, the verse consistently treats as her majority, the point at which the governed body acquires its governor.

### Q: How can an essay argue that the play defends Juliet's desire while condemning the regime that priced it?

An essay can argue it from the distribution of the verse. Set the ledger in order and the correlation carries the thesis: the speeches that administer Juliet's purity, her father's harvest talk, her mother's book speech, Paris's claim of ownership, the servants' opening boast, are all exposed by their own metaphors as transactions, while the speeches in which Juliet administers herself are given the play's highest poetry and are never corrected by any voice the text endorses. Add the textual evidence that the frankness stands in the authoritative second quarto, restored by modern editors after centuries of performance cuts, and the essay can conclude that the defence is Shakespeare's construction rather than a modern imposition on a neutral script.

### Q: Why is there no male equivalent of the maidenhead anywhere in the play's language?

The absence is the regime's asymmetry stated as vocabulary. A maidenhead is a physical certificate that can be inspected, priced, joked about, and mourned, and the play's language provides no counterpart token for any man: Romeo's innocence, where the soliloquy imagines it, must be borrowed from the female term, "a pair of stainless maidenhoods" (3.2). Male purity carries no market value in Verona because no transaction depends on it, no alliance is sealed by it, and no father's honour is stored in it. The missing word is therefore evidence rather than oversight. Chastity in this world is not a virtue expected of persons; it is a condition audited in daughters, and the lexicon keeps the accounts.

### Q: What does Benvolio's prescription of other beauties assume about how male desire is cured?

Benvolio's prescription assumes male desire is an infection of the eye treatable by a stronger dose of the same stimulus: "Take thou some new infection to thy eye, / And the rank poison of the old will die" (1.2). The homeopathy is cheerful, practical, and entirely about the man's comfort; the women in the prescription are doses, interchangeable medicines measured by their effect on the patient. Set against the play's treatment of its women, the assumption does quiet structural work. Rosaline's chastity is a blockage to be routed around, Juliet's beauty the stronger infection that succeeds, and at no point does the medical model ask what either woman is for in herself. The cure works, which is the bleakest part of the joke.

### Q: Why does Juliet ask whether Romeo's love is "honourable" before she asks anything else of him?

Because "honourable" is the one question that converts desire into jurisdiction. "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2) makes her consent conditional on the single form in which her body can change hands without becoming the market's goods or the street's spoil: a marriage she has specified, on a schedule she sets, with a messenger she appoints. The question is often read as maidenly caution, and it is in fact a constitutional act. Before it, Juliet is an asset being priced in another room; after it, no disposition of her is legitimate that does not pass through her own stated terms.

### Q: How do Rosaline's vow and the lovers' vows differ in form, when both are spoken in secret?

They differ as a wall differs from a contract. Rosaline's vow, so far as the play reports it, is unilateral, unheard by any witness the text names, and known only through the report of the man it excludes; it creates no obligations in anyone else and can be neither enforced nor released. The lovers' vows are dialogic and conditional, question and answer in the orchard, conditions stated, a witness summoned, a rite performed in the cell, obligations created on both sides that the rest of the play treats as binding in conscience and in law. The regime calls the wall chastity and the contract ruin; the play's sympathies, measured by its verse, run in the opposite direction.

### Q: Why does Paris lose Juliet to Death rather than to Romeo in the play's final accounting of her body?

Because Romeo is never allowed to become her public husband, the vacancy at the centre of the ending belongs to the bridegroom no one chose. Juliet had assigned the role in advance, vowing that death, not Romeo, would take her maidenhead if the marriage failed (3.2), and Capulet, finding the counterfeit corpse, repeats the assignment as lament, making Death his son-in-law and heir (4.5). Paris, the house's candidate, dies at the tomb still believing the transfer to him was pending, killed by the husband he never knew existed. The final accounting therefore names Death as the only bridegroom Verona's machinery actually delivered, which is the regime's balance sheet rendered as irony.
