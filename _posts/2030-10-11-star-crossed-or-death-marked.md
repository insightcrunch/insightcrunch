The Prologue to Romeo and Juliet announces the tragedy twice over, in two phrases that do not say the same thing. In one breath the Chorus calls Romeo and Juliet "a pair of star-cross'd lovers" (Prologue.6), assigning their ruin to the heavens, to stars whose adverse configuration crosses a human life the way weather crosses a sea route. Four lines later the Chorus calls their love "death-mark'd" (Prologue.9), assigning the same ruin to a mark carried on the love itself, a stamp of mortality that names no agent and blames no sky. One phrase accuses the cosmos. The other describes a condition. Generations of quotation have fused them into a single gesture of doom, and the fusion hides a genuine contest: the play that follows the Prologue spends five acts testing which of the two announcements its events will ratify, and the early printed texts, examined closely, stage the same contest in miniature before a single character speaks.

The textual half of the contest can be settled from the documents, and the documents give a lopsided result. The Second Quarto of 1599 (Q2) prints the Prologue as a complete fourteen-line sonnet, and in Q2 both phrases stand in their canonical positions, the star phrase crowning the second quatrain, the death phrase opening the third. The First Quarto of 1597 (Q1) prints a Prologue of twelve lines, and its damage falls with strange precision: "starre-crost" survives almost untouched, while the line that carries "death-mark'd love" vanishes as a line, and the strong word itself washes up inside a parenthetical patch, reassigned to the parents' rage. The First Folio of 1623 (F) settles nothing, because it omits the Prologue altogether and preserves neither phrase. Of the two most quoted fate formulas in English drama, one was never in textual danger and the other hangs by a single book.

The verdict of this study is therefore double, and both halves are argued from evidence rather than taste. On the text: editors are right to print Q2's Prologue, as editions since Nicholas Rowe's of 1709 have done, and Q1's variants belong in the apparatus and in parallel-text study, not in the reading text, because Q1's Prologue shows the profile of a speech carried by memory, its striking words retained and its syntax lost, rather than the profile of a rival authorial version. On the meaning: the rivalry between the phrases resolves into cooperation. The star phrase supplies the tragedy's metaphysical weather, a cosmic hostility that the characters themselves keep invoking and that the action never once obeys as a mechanism. The death phrase supplies the tragedy's certainty, a doom that requires no machinery because it is a property of the love as the Chorus presents it, and every death in the play is in fact produced by the human machinery of feud, haste, and failed messages. The cliché quotes one Prologue. The witnesses preserve two, and the difference between them is the difference between a fate imposed from above and a fate inscribed in the thing itself.

## What Star-Crossed Claims, What Death-Marked Claims

Before the texts can be collated, the phrases must be read as claims, because a variant matters only when the readings at stake differ in meaning. "Star-cross'd" is a transitive piece of astrology compressed into a compound adjective. To cross, in the technical vocabulary the first audience brought to the playhouse, was what adverse planetary aspects did to a nativity: malefic planets, Saturn and Mars chief among them, standing in hostile aspect to the lights or to each other, thwarted the life the chart described. The compound presupposes agents (the stars), an action (crossing, in the sense of thwarting by opposition), and patients (the lovers, to whom the crossing is done). It also presupposes a cosmos in working order, a machinery of influence that could in principle be read by a practitioner with the birth data in hand. The series study of the phrase's astrological machinery sets out that machinery in full, the aspects, the malefics, the nativities and elections of the period's practice; the point required here is grammatical. "Star-cross'd lovers" is a sentence in disguise, with the stars as its subject and doom as its verb. It accuses.

"Death-mark'd" accuses nobody. The compound is passive without an agent, a past participle whose doer has been deleted. A mark is a sign applied to a surface: a brand on livestock, a stamp on goods, a notch on a tally, the distinguishing spot by which a thing is known and claimed. To call the love death-marked is to say that the love carries death's sign on it from the start, legible to anyone who reads the surface, without specifying who applied the sign or by what instrument. Where the star phrase points upward, to a court above the ceiling of the world, the death phrase points at the object itself, at the love as a labelled thing moving through the city toward its labelled end. The one is a theory of causation. The other is a description of a property. A reader who treats them as synonyms has already surrendered the distinction the sonnet builds its second and third quatrains to hold apart.

The placement of the phrases inside the sonnet sharpens the distinction. The star phrase closes the quatrain of persons: "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue.5-6). It attaches to the lovers at the moment of their origin, a verdict delivered at birth, and birth is the astrologer's jurisdiction, the nativity the chart he casts. The death phrase opens the quatrain of consequences: "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love / And the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, nought could remove, / Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.9-12). It attaches not to the persons but to the love, and not at its birth but across its passage, the whole journey the audience is about to watch. The sonnet thus assigns each phrase its tense and territory. The stars govern the lovers' starting condition. The mark governs the love's career. Rivalry between them is possible only if a reader demands a single cause; the poem itself distributes the work, cosmology for the persons, inscription for the passion, and leaves the scenes to disclose which register the events will honour.

One further distinction will matter when the variants are weighed. The star phrase embeds a verb of action done by an external power, and verbs of that kind invite argument: a claim that the stars crossed these lives can be tested against the lives, resented, defied, disproved. The death phrase embeds no testable claim about mechanism at all, and for that reason it cannot be argued with, only confirmed or borne. The play's characters, as a later section shows, behave exactly as this grammar predicts. They quarrel with the stars repeatedly and by name. No character ever quarrels with the mark, because the mark offers nothing to quarrel with. It simply waits at the end of the passage, the way the Chorus said it would.

The speech belongs to a Chorus, a single figure standing outside the action's architecture so completely that scholarship cites the speech by its own name, Prologue, rather than by any act or scene number; the play's filing system has coordinates for everything the characters do and none for what the Chorus does. A voice with no stake in the feud, no body to bury and no statue to promise, delivers both verdicts in the same flat tone of record, and the flatness is the point. Announcements from outside the machine carry a different evidentiary weight from claims made inside it, which is why the scenes can spend five acts qualifying the star phrase without ever being able to repeal it, and why the mark, once read aloud by that uncommitted voice, reads less like an interpretation than like a fact of the exhibited object. The contest between the phrases is therefore not a debate the Chorus stages. It is a tension the Chorus deposits, in two lines of one sonnet, for the play to work on.

## The Grammar of the Compounds: An Event and an Inscription

The rivalry is built into the phrases at the level of grammar, before any question of belief arises. Both are past participles worn as adjectives, but the two participles remember different things. "Cross'd" remembers an action: crossing is something done, at a moment, by an agent, to a patient, and the participle is a compressed clause, the stars cross the lovers, frozen into an epithet. "Mark'd" remembers a result: a mark is what remains after the action of marking is finished, and the participle is a compressed state, the love bears a mark, with the action and its agent already discarded as irrelevant. The distinction governs everything that follows in the phrases' careers. An event can be investigated: it has a time, a place, an author, a mechanism, and it can be resented by its patients and disputed by its witnesses. A condition can only be observed, confirmed as instance accumulates on instance. The play's characters investigate the crossing for five acts. They merely observe the mark, in themselves and in each other, and observation is the one response the mark ever receives.

The semantic fields of the two verbs widen the gap. To cross, in the ordinary usage any hearer brought to the playhouse before the astrology was even unpacked, is to thwart: to stand across a path, to oppose a purpose, to run counter to an intention, the verb of obstruction between a will and its object. The technical sense rides on the ordinary one, since an adverse aspect is precisely an obstruction drawn on a chart, one configuration standing across another's road. To mark, in the period's field, is to single out for a fate: goods marked for a buyer, beasts marked for ownership or for slaughter, a person marked in the sense of noted, watched, and designated. The archery sense runs alongside, the mark as the target at which aim is taken, so that a marked thing is a thing already aimed at. Scripture's most famous mark, set on Cain in Genesis, singled its bearer out under a sign all who met him could read, and that mark protected even as it stigmatized, forbidding the vengeance it seemed to invite. The Prologue's mark inverts the scriptural pattern. It protects nothing and forbids nothing. It designates, and what it designates its bearer for is the one outcome the bearing of it cannot prevent.

Sound completes what grammar begins. Each compound packs two stresses into two syllables, a blow and its answer, and the pair of them gives the sonnet two hammer strokes four lines apart, the rhythm of a sentence being nailed down at both ends. Within that likeness the difference persists. "Star-cross'd" rings on the name of its agent, the star held in the mouth a fraction longer, the crossing attributed in the very act of pronouncing it. "Death-mark'd" compresses further, its second syllable swallowed in the devoiced cluster the second quarto's spelling records, so that the phrase strikes and vanishes inside its line, an inscription tapped once and left. The acoustic profiles enact the semantics with embarrassing fidelity. The crossing is loud because it has an author whose name is worth pronouncing. The mark is quiet because it has none, only a surface and a sign. A listener who never parsed a word of the sonnet's argument has still heard, in the fall of the two compounds, the difference between a doom that argues and a doom that waits.

## The Second Quarto's Prologue: The Announcement Complete

The Second Quarto, printed by Thomas Creede for Cuthbert Burby in 1599 and advertised on its title page as "newly corrected, augmented, and amended," is the witness on which the received text rests. Its Prologue stands under the heading "The Prologue" with the speech assigned to "Corus," the Chorus, and it runs the full fourteen lines of an English sonnet, three quatrains and a closing couplet, in the spelling of its printing house: "Two housholds both alike in dignitie, / (In faire Verona where we lay our Scene) / From auncient grudge, breake to new mutinie, / Where ciuill bloud makes ciuill hands vncleane: / From forth the fatall loynes of these two foes, / A paire of starre-crost louers, take their life: / Whose misaduentur'd pittious ouerthrowes, / Doth with their death burie their Parents strife. / The fearfull passage of their death-markt loue, / And the continuance of their Parents rage: / Which but their childrens end nought could remoue: / Is now the two houres trafficque of our Stage. / The which if you with patient eares attend, / What heare shall misse, our toyle shall striue to mend." Modern editions, René Weis's Arden Third Series text of 2012 among them, print the same speech in modernized dress from this copy, and the sonnet reading of the speech, its spoiling contract with the audience and its quatrain architecture, has its own study in [the Prologue sonnet that spoils the ending](/2022/03/18/romeo-and-juliet-prologue-sonnet-analysis/). The business here is the two phrases as this witness fixes them.

Fixed is the right word for the star phrase. In Q2 it reads "starre-crost," one word in the period's unstable orthography, set in a line whose verb is present tense: the lovers "take their life," a proclamation performed in the theatre's present, the Chorus announcing an action the audience is positioned to watch unfold as if under sentence already passed. The line's grammar enacts the astrology it names. A nativity is cast at a birth and read across a whole life at once; likewise the Chorus casts the lovers at line 6 and reads their whole span in a single present tense that treats the future as accomplished. Nothing in the line hedges. The crossing is stated as the lovers' defining attribute before either lover has been seen, and the attribute is given in the technical adjective of the astrologer's art, compressed from a chart into a compound.

The death phrase stands in this witness at the head of the third quatrain, and its position is its meaning. "The fearfull passage of their death-markt loue" (Prologue.9) makes three assignments in nine words. The love, not the lovers, receives the mark, shifting the doom from the persons to the relation between them. The love is given a "passage," a course to travel, so that the mark is not a static brand but a destiny unfolding in time, the very thing the audience has bought a ticket to witness. And the passage is "fearfull," dreadful and dread-inspiring at once, an epithet that belongs to the spectators' experience as much as to the lovers' fate. The spelling rewards a pause: Q2 sets "death-markt," the final consonant written as the devoiced sound the mouth actually makes, a small fossil of pronunciation preserved in type, and editors who print "death-mark'd" are restoring in apostrophe what the compositor recorded in letter. Between the two phrases Q2 thus offers a complete physics of the tragedy: an external crossing at the origin, an internal marking across the course, and a couplet that contracts with the audience to attend patiently while both are demonstrated.

The word that launches the star quatrain deserves its own note, because it contains the whole contest in miniature. The lovers issue "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes" (Prologue.5), and "fatal" carries both of the sonnet's registers inside a single adjective: concerned with fate, allotted by destiny, and causing death, deadly in effect. Before either famous compound is spoken, the poem has already fused the cosmic and the mortal in one word, as if to warn the audience that the two announcements to come are separations of something originally joined. The quatrains then perform the separation analytically, assigning the fate sense to the stars and the death sense to the mark, and the rest of the play can be read as an inquiry into whether the fusion or the separation tells the truth. On the evidence of the scenes, the separation misleads and the fusion holds: every event in the tragedy is at once allotted-seeming and death-dealing, fated in retrospect and fatal in mechanism, and "fatal" remains the most accurate single word in the speech precisely because it declines to choose between the phrase that follows it and the phrase that follows that.

The couplet's commercial metaphor completes the frame's design and is easy to underrate. The speech calls the play "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.12), figuring the performance as merchandise and the audience as customers whose patience is the price; the closing promise, that toil shall mend what the summary misses, is a tradesman's pledge appended to a prophet's announcement. The mixture is functional. A fate announced by a prophet can be resented as an imposition. A fate announced by a tradesman, as part of the goods on offer, becomes a term of the purchase, and the audience that has accepted the terms has, in a small way, consented to the doom, becoming complicit in the frame it will spend two hours testing. The phrases' authority over interpretation begins in that consent: spectators who have bought the announcement wholesale tend to credit its cosmology retail, scene by scene, until the machinery teaches them to read the second phrase over the first. The bargain is thus the first act of criticism the play performs on its audience, sorting, before a word of dialogue, the readers of slogans from the readers of structures.

### What is a third causal word doing between the two phrases?

Between the star phrase at line 6 and the death phrase at line 9, the Second Quarto sets a word from a third causal register altogether: the lovers' "misaduentur'd pittious ouerthrowes" (Prologue.7). Misadventure is the vocabulary of accident, of the mishap that befalls without intention and without cosmic instruction.

The sonnet therefore speaks three languages of causation in four lines: the stars cross, chance misadventures, death marks. A careful reading cannot collapse them into one doctrine of fate, because each word belongs to a different account of why things happen, and the Chorus, the play's most authoritative voice, declines to choose among them in the frame itself.

The presence of the accident word matters to the contest between the phrases, because it quietly limits both. If the overthrows are misadventured, then the crossing did not script them in detail; if the love is death-marked, the mark foretells the end and not the route. The sonnet's causal scheme is layered rather than single: a hostile sky as the climate, a marked love as the certainty, and mishap as the mechanism by which climate and certainty meet in particular events. That layering forecasts the play's own distribution of causes, in which a sealed door and a mistimed waking do the killing inside a frame that the heavens were said to govern. The third word is the Chorus's confession, placed between its two famous phrases, that the route from announcement to outcome runs through contingency. Critics who quote the Prologue as a flat certificate of determinism have stopped reading at the compounds and skipped the adjective that complicates them.

## The First Quarto's Prologue: The Announcement Remembered

The First Quarto, printed by John Danter in 1597 with Edward Allde completing the volume, bills itself as the play "as it hath been often (with great applause) plaid publiquely" by the Lord Hunsdon's servants, and its Prologue keeps that billing in a sense the publisher cannot have intended: the speech reads like something carried away from a performance rather than copied from a page. Set out in modern spelling, Q1's version runs: "Two household friends alike in dignity, / (In fair Verona, where we lay our scene) / From civil broils broke into enmity, / Whose civil war makes civil hands unclean. / From forth the fatal loins of these two foes, / A pair of star-cross'd lovers took their life: / Whose misadventures, piteous overthrows, / (Through the continuing of their fathers' strife, / And death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage) / Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage. / The which if you with patient ears attend, / What here we want we'll study to amend." Twelve lines stand where fourteen should. The skeleton is the same speech. The flesh has slipped, and the pattern of the slipping is the evidence on which this whole study turns. The edition itself, its printing, its title page, and its seven hundred missing lines have their full biography in [the First Quarto of 1597, the 'bad' quarto](/2026/03/27/first-quarto-1597-bad-quarto/); the concern here is narrower, the fourteen lines at its front and the two phrases inside them.

Begin where the damage does not fall. The star phrase survives with its substance intact. Q1's line 6 reads "A paire of starre-crost Louers tooke their life" in its own spelling, the compound identical to Q2's, the lovers named by the same technical adjective in the same position in the same quatrain. One change only: the verb has slipped from present to past, "tooke" for "take," so that the Chorus of Q1 reports the deaths as an accomplished fact where the Chorus of Q2 proclaims them in an unfolding present. The difference is real and worth a section of its own below, but it is a difference of tense, not of doctrine. No witness disagrees about the crossing. The most quoted phrase in the speech was never in textual danger, and any account of the Prologue's instability that treats the two fate phrases as equally unstable has failed at the first collation.

The death phrase fares otherwise, and its fate is the single most instructive variant in the speech. Q2's line 9, "The fearfull passage of their death-markt loue," does not exist in Q1. The line is gone, and with it goes the quatrain's architecture: Q2's lines 8 through 11, the death that buries the parents' strife, the death-marked passage, the continuance of the rage, and the removal that only the children's end could effect, collapse in Q1 into a two-line parenthesis, "(Through the continuing of their fathers' strife, / And death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage)," a bracketed aside hung inside the sentence that carries the traffic of the stage. Inside that patch, the wreckage of the lost lines has been salvaged and stored. "Strife" survives from the buried line 8. "Continuing" does duty for the "continuance" of line 10. And "death-mark'd" survives, dislodged from the love and reassigned to a new noun: in Q1 it is the passage of the parents' rage that is death-marked, the feud's career and not the lovers' passion that carries the stamp. A word remembered, a line forgotten, a patch built from the debris: the sequence is the signature of a text carried in memory, because memory keeps the striking word and the general drift and loses the syntax that held them in place.

The rhyme scheme registers the same loss in another notation. Q2's third quatrain rhymes on love and remove, rage and stage, the E and F rhymes of the sonnet's pattern. Q1 preserves the slant pair rage and stage across its parenthesis and traffic line, but the love and remove pair has vanished entire, because the lines that carried those rhymes are the lines that are gone. The damage, measured three ways, by lines, by syntax, and by rhyme, concentrates in exactly one place: the quatrain of the death phrase. The sonnet's second quatrain, the star quatrain, passes through transmission with its rhymes, its syntax, and its famous compound intact, its verb's tense the only casualty. The third quatrain arrives in Q1 as a parenthesis and a patch. Whatever carried the First Quarto's Prologue from stage to print shop remembered the crossing and fumbled the mark.

The parenthesis repays a grammarian's attention, because its failure is structural rather than verbal. In the second quarto, the third quatrain's sentence runs clean: the passage of the death-marked love, and the continuance of the rage, which nothing but the children's end could remove, is the two hours' traffic of the stage. Subject, relative clause, predicate, each in its place. In the first quarto, the lost line takes the subject with it, and the patch rebuilds the sentence around the wreckage: the continuing of the fathers' strife, and the death-marked passage of the parents' rage, bracketed together, are pressed into service as the thing that is the traffic of the stage. The result almost parses, the way a rebuilt wall almost stands, and its near-success is diagnostic. A reporter who had forgotten words would produce gaps. This reporter forgot governance, which word ruled which clause, and solved the problem by bracketing the uncertain material, marking the repair with the parenthesis itself, as though the type could confess what the memory could not. Editors who print the second quarto's sentence are not choosing elegance over clumsiness. They are choosing a sentence over the record of a sentence's collapse.

The parenthesis itself has a history inside the speech that sharpens the point. Both quartos set the second line, the scene-setting aside "(In fair Verona, where we lay our scene)," inside brackets, a typographical whisper in which the Chorus steps half out of its sentence to address the house about the staging. The first quarto therefore contains two parenthetical moments of entirely different kinds: one inherited, a deliberate aside present in the good text as well, the Chorus's aside to its audience; and one manufactured, the third quatrain's patch, in which the sentence itself steps aside from its own wreckage. The same punctuation performs intimacy in the first instance and emergency repair in the second, and a reader collating the two speeches can watch the bracket change function as the text degrades, from a mark of the Chorus's control over its material to a mark of the reporter's loss of it. Few speeches in the canon offer so compact a lesson in what transmission does to a text, which is one reason the Prologue has served for a century as the classroom exhibit for the whole science of collation.

One further variant belongs to the frame rather than the phrases, and it points the same way. The Second Quarto assigns the speech to a named function, printing the prefix "Corus," the Chorus made a person of the play. The First Quarto prints the speech under its bare heading, "The Prologue," with no speaker prefix beyond it, so that in the earlier book the announcement floats unattributed, a voice without an office. The difference matches the two texts' characters at every point: the good quarto institutionalizes the frame, giving its speaker a title and its speech a shape, while the reported text lets both slip toward anonymity. A frame whose speaker is unnamed is a frame already half detached from the work, and detachment is the precondition of loss; the Folio's omission of the speech outright, two decades on, is the same process carried to its end. Transmission sheds the frame first because the frame is the part of the play that belongs to no character, and what belongs to nobody is what nobody remembers to carry.

### What does the first quarto's first line do to the feud's scale?

It shrinks the feud from a civic condition to a social circle. The Second Quarto opens on "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1), two institutions matched in rank, the line balanced on its doubled "both" like an equation, the feud framed as a fact of the city's constitution.

The First Quarto opens on "Two household friends alike in dignity." The institutional noun has become a social one, the matched pair a pair of friends, and the chill formality of the original has warmed into something nearer gossip.

The shrinkage matters to the fate phrases because the frame they announce is only as large as the world it governs. A doom pronounced over two households, institutions with servants, kinsmen, marriage markets, and a prince to answer to, is a public destiny, and its working out will require the machinery of a city: edicts, banishments, quarantine orders, a watch, a tomb with a household's dead inside it. A doom pronounced over two friend groups is a private misfortune, and its machinery shrinks to temperament and luck. The First Quarto's first line thus pre-shrinks the tragedy before either fate phrase is reached, and the effect propagates: in a text where the feud has been misremembered as friendship, the star phrase reads as a flourish about two unlucky young people, and the death phrase, when it surfaces displaced inside its parenthesis, attaches to the parents' rage as though the rage were the tragedy's real subject. The variant at line 1 and the variant at line 9 are the same error at two scales, the conversion of a civic structure into a human anecdote. Editors print the second quarto's line, and they are right to, but the apparatus keeps the first quarto's reading as evidence of how easily the tragedy's public architecture collapses in the memory of anyone who was listening for the story rather than the structure.

## Where the Variants Fall: A Stable Phrase and a Displaced One

Lay the two Prologues side by side and the variants sort themselves into a profile with a clear shape. The first quatrain survives in Q1 altered in wording but intact in structure: "household friends" for "households, both," "civil broils broke into enmity" for "ancient grudge break to new mutiny," "civil war" for "civil blood," the word "civil" itself retained and redeployed three times in four lines because it was memorable, while its original placements were not. Sense persists and register changes, approximation without collapse. The second quatrain survives closer still: the fatal loins, the star-crossed lovers, the misadventures and overthrows all stand, with the pluralized noun "misadventures" loosening one grammar and the past tense "tooke" flattening the proclamation into report. The third quatrain collapses, losing two lines outright and rebuilding the survivors as a parenthesis. The couplet survives with its sense inverted in a way a later paragraph examines. Damage in the First Quarto's Prologue is not weather, falling evenly. It is a gradient, light at the opening, where an audience's attention and a reporter's memory are freshest, and catastrophic at the third quatrain, where the speech turns from persons to consequences.

The tense variant deserves its own weighing, because it is the only substantive change that touches the star phrase, and it changes the kind of fate the phrase announces. Q2's "take their life" is a present tense with the whole play inside it: the lovers take their life across the two hours' traffic, in the audience's presence, under a sentence the Chorus has passed but the stage has still to execute. The announcement and the action share a tense, and the sharing is the source of the speech's peculiar suspense, in which the end is certain and the route is not. Q1's "tooke their life" moves the deaths into a completed past, and the Chorus becomes a narrator summarizing a story already over, its audience auditors of a record rather than witnesses of an execution. Report is weaker than proclamation in the theatre for a plain reason: a reported doom can be doubted as a summary's exaggeration, while a proclaimed doom hangs over every subsequent scene as a term of the contract. The critic who prefers Q1's reading here must explain why a playwright would blunt his own instrument. The defender of Q2 need only point to the couplet, in which the speech promises that what the summary misses, the performance will supply, a promise that presupposes an audience watching events, not hearing a chronicle.

The closing couplet completes the profile. Q2 ends in confidence: "The which if you with patient eares attend, / What heare shall misse, our toyle shall striue to mend." The Chorus concedes that a summary is partial and pledges labour, the company's "toyle," to make the performance complete what the announcement omitted. Q1 ends in confession: "What here we want wee'l studie to amend." The verb of labour has become a verb of intention, and the object has shifted from the summary's omissions to the text's own wants, its deficiencies. A speech that began, in Q1, by shrinking households into friends ends by apologizing for its own gaps, and the apology is almost documentary: the Q1 Prologue knows, at some level of its transmission, that it is a diminished thing. No theory of the first quarto as a considered revision survives that couplet intact, because revisers do not close their improvements by confessing want. A memory, catching itself in the act of falling short, does exactly that.

### How do the later quartos treat the two phrases?

They treat both phrases as settled text, reprinting the Second Quarto's Prologue entire and without substantive variation. The third quarto of 1609 descends from the second and carries its fourteen lines, its star-crossed lovers in the present tense, its death-marked love at the head of the third quatrain,

and the quarto tradition that follows transmits the same readings down to the end of the play's early printing history. Dissent in the early record comes from one witness only, and it is the earliest.

The consequence for the phrases is that the early tradition, taken as a whole, votes with a single voice once the Second Quarto exists. Between 1599 and the Folio, no printer's reader, corrector, or compositor altered either compound, modernized either spelling beyond house habit, or promoted the first quarto's parenthesis into the text. The star phrase and the death phrase alike passed through the trade as fixed points, and the death phrase's fixity is the more remarkable, since its only complete early statement stood in one book and its displaced double stood in another, older one that the trade had every opportunity to consult. The tradition's behaviour is the bibliographical form of the verdict this study reaches on other grounds: the second quarto's readings were the text, and the first quarto's were the curiosity, and the phrases' survival in their canonical form owes as much to that trade consensus as to any single editor's judgment.

## The Folio's Silence: A Prologue Dropped Entire

The First Folio of 1623, the collected Works on which the play's later authority might have been expected to rest, omits the Prologue altogether. Its Romeo and Juliet opens on Sampson and Gregory trading boasts in the street, and a reader confined to the Folio would meet neither fate phrase, neither the crossing nor the mark, anywhere in the book. The omission is a fact of the Folio's copy and casting off, its compositors setting the play from printed quarto copy under the pressures of a very large volume, and the full documentary account belongs to the companion study of [the Prologue's strange textual status](/2026/07/17/prologue-textual-status/). The bearing on the present question is narrower and sharper. The Folio, the edition through which the seventeenth century read its Shakespeare, transmitted the tragedy without its announced frame, and the play survived the loss so completely that the loss itself went unremarked by generations of readers. The frame, that is, proved detachable in transmission even while it proved indispensable in interpretation: the speeches that name the tragedy's causes were the speeches the tradition could drop without noticing, a paradox that any account of the phrases' authority must absorb.

Editors since Nicholas Rowe, whose edition of 1709 first restored the speech to the collected plays, have printed the Prologue from the quarto tradition, which in practice means from Q2.

The Folio's treatment of the frame's second speech sharpens the picture. The Folio drops the Prologue and keeps, after its fashion, the Chorus speech before Act 2, printing it without the speaker apparatus that would assign it an owner. The two early witnesses thus preserve complementary halves of the frame: the First Quarto keeps a damaged Prologue and drops the second speech entire, while the Folio drops the Prologue and keeps the second speech, unattributed. A reader of the Folio alone met the frame only at its midpoint, hearing that old desire lay on its deathbed and young affection gaped to be its heir, without ever hearing the lovers called star-crossed or the love called death-marked. The death register, that is, has a Folio life of a kind: the deathbed image crossed into the collected Works while the mark itself stayed behind in the quartos. What the Folio transmits of the frame is its mortality without its metaphysics, the play's doom as a household fact rather than a cosmic sentence, which is, on the argument of this study, the half of the announcement the scenes themselves ratify. The restoration was so complete that the modern reader's sense of the Prologue as the play's natural threshold, the first thing the work says, is an editorial achievement rather than a documentary given. Both fate phrases therefore reach the modern reader on editorial credit: star-crossed on the credit of two quartos that agree, death-marked on the credit of one quarto whose line the other garbles and the Folio drops. The phrases feel equally canonical on the page of a modern edition, set in the same type by the same hand. The witnesses underneath the page tell a story of sharply unequal security, and the inequality is itself an interpretation waiting to be read: the phrase that names an external agent, memorable as a slogan and portable as a cliché, was the phrase transmission could not lose; the phrase that names an internal condition, quieter, stranger, and syntactically embedded in a subordinate clause's neighbourhood, was the phrase transmission nearly did.

## Explaining the Prologue's Damage: Memory, Draft, or Abridgement?

Three explanations have been offered for the First Quarto's condition, and the Prologue's two phrases make an unusually clean test case for choosing among them, because the damage pattern is so specific: a famous compound preserved entire in one quatrain, a famous compound preserved but displaced in the next, two lines lost, a parenthesis patched, a couplet turned apologetic. A. W. Pollard's category of 1909, the "bad quarto," named the phenomenon before explaining it, listing the 1597 Romeo and Juliet among the handful of texts he took to be stolen and surreptitious copies, reconstructed from memory by actors for a printer's profit. Harry Hoppe's study of the first quarto's text in 1948 gave the category its fullest early defence for this play, and Hoppe's handling of the Prologue remains the memorial theory's classic exhibit: a speech of fourteen lines, short enough to be carried whole by any competent reporter, arriving in print with its joinery broken in ways that memory, and memory alone, characteristically breaks things. The reporter's mind kept what performance makes salient, the striking compounds, the word "civil," the two hours' traffic, and lost what performance makes recessive, the syntax of subordination in the third quatrain and the exact assignment of a modifier to its noun.

The draft explanation inverts the direction of travel. On this account, held by many eighteenth-century editors and refurbished in modern form by scholars who resist the memorial theory, Q1 represents an earlier state of the play, a first version that Q2 then corrected, augmented, and amended, as its title page boasts. The Prologue's variants would then be the playwright's own first thoughts: "household friends" the rough phrase later sharpened into "households, both," the parenthesis a first sketch of the third quatrain later built out into full lines. Lukas Erne, whose 2007 Cambridge edition prints the First Quarto as a text in its own right and whose Shakespeare as Literary Dramatist (2003) repositioned the playwright as an author writing for readers as well as for the stage, gives the draft and abridgement explanations their most serious modern hearing, laying the theories side by side and insisting, at minimum, that the first quarto is a crucial witness to the play's early life rather than waste paper. Laurie Maguire's Shakespearean Suspect Texts (1996) subjected the memorial diagnostics themselves to audit across the suspect corpus and found the category weaker than its founders supposed, a caution that has permanently raised the standard of proof for any single-speech argument.

The adjudication this study reaches is narrow, and it is confined to the Prologue, where the evidence is densest. Against the draft theory, the parenthesis is decisive. A reviser returning to a draft quatrain improves its sense, varies its music, or replaces its images; the history of revision, in this play and elsewhere, shows authors trading the weaker phrase for the stronger, "household friends" conceivably rising into "households, both." What no reviser's draft explains is the direction of the specific damage at line 9, where a coherent line in the supposed revision, "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love," corresponds in the supposed draft to a bracketed patch in which the modifier has come loose from its noun and fastened onto another. Drafts are syntactically complete even when they are verbally crude, because the writer knows what the sentence is doing. The Q1 parenthesis is the work of someone who did not know what the sentence was doing and rebuilt it from its memorable parts, which is the condition of a hearer, not an author. Erne's caution stands as method, and his edition stands as the indispensable documentary tool; on the Prologue's evidence, Hoppe's side carries the verdict. The first quarto's Prologue is a remembered speech, and its variants are the debris of remembering, valuable as evidence about performance and transmission, inadmissible as alternative authorial readings. The abridgement explanation, that a company cut the speech for the road, fails on the same evidence: companies cut whole lines and whole speeches, as the first quarto elsewhere shows the wider play compressed, but a touring cut does not misassign a modifier inside a retained line or confess its wants in a rewritten couplet. Cutting is subtraction. The Prologue shows substitution under loss, the signature of memory at work.

The adjudication has a coda, and it is the most elegant piece of evidence in the whole inquiry. The First Quarto alone preserves, in the scene of the maskers' approach to the feast, Romeo's stipulation for the company's entrance: no "without-book prologue, faintly spoke / After the prompter, for our entrance." The line, which modern editions import from Q1 into the received text at 1.4, is a joke about a prologue delivered from memory, prompted from the wings, and it survives in exactly one early book: the book whose own Prologue was, on Hoppe's account, delivered from memory and shows every sign of it. The coincidence is too pointed to serve as proof, and this study does not press it as proof. It is, rather, the tradition's own commentary on the case, filed inside the text itself. The play knew that prologues get spoken without book. The first quarto is what a prologue spoken without book looks like once a printer sets it. And the same book that fumbled the frame's third quatrain preserved, uniquely, the play's joke about fumbling frames, which is why the editorial settlement described in the next section, the second quarto for the text and the first quarto for the evidence, is not a compromise but a description of how the two books have always divided the labour between them.

## Which Prologue Should an Edition Print?

The editorial question the brief of this study poses, which reading an edition should print, has in practice been answered with unusual unanimity, and the unanimity is worth examining rather than merely inherited. Brian Gibbons's Arden Second Series edition of 1980, Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of 2000, and René Weis's Arden Third Series edition of 2012 all print the Prologue from the second quarto, modernizing its spelling, marking its elisions, and relegating the first quarto's variants to the collation at the foot of the page. Their shared reasoning follows the copy-text logic the discipline standardized across the twentieth century: where one witness descends from authorial papers and another bears the marks of a secondary transmission, the first supplies the text and the second supplies, at most, corrections where the first is manifestly corrupt. Applied to the Prologue, the logic is not even strained, because the second quarto's speech is complete, metrically regular, and syntactically sound, while the first quarto's is defective by its own closing confession. No editor of the play has printed Q1's parenthesis in a reading text, and none, on the evidence assembled here, should.

The interesting disagreement lies one level down, over what the rejected variants are for. The older editorial habit treated Q1's Prologue as noise, fit only for a collation line. Erne's Cambridge edition of the first quarto embodies the newer position, that Q1 is a distinct version of the play with its own coherence and its own claim on attention, to be printed entire and studied in parallel, as the Oxford Shakespeare long ago printed the two texts of King Lear. The disagreement resolves, for this speech, into a division of labour rather than a choice of sides. As a reading of the line an audience or student is to quote, "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love" has no rival, and the apparatus that records "death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage" beneath it is recording the history of a loss, not entertaining an alternative. As a document of how the speech lived in the playhouse, in the memory of the men who spoke it and the ears that carried it away, Q1's twelve lines are evidence of the first order, and a parallel text is the honest format for them. Weis's edition, which prints Q2 and collates Q1 fully, and Erne's, which prints Q1 with Q2 at its elbow, are not competitors but complements, and the student who wants to know what the variants mean needs both volumes open at once.

One editorial choice inside the agreed text still deserves scrutiny, because it touches both phrases directly: modernization. The quartos spell the compounds "starre-crost" and "death-markt," unhyphenated in effect, their syllables unmarked. Modern editions print "star-cross'd" and "death-mark'd," supplying hyphens the copy lacks and apostrophes that instruct the reader's voice to swallow the second syllable, keeping the pentameter's count. The practice is defensible and, for a reading edition, right: the elision was real in performance, the quarto spelling "death-markt" itself records the devoiced, compressed pronunciation, and an unmarked "death-marked" in modern dress invites a two-syllable reading the line cannot bear. But the modern dress also launders the phrases into a false finish, two polished compounds of apparently equal manufacture, and the reader who meets them only in modern type will never guess that one of them survived transmission unscathed while the other survived it by being remembered wrong. Editorial polish, in this corner of the text, conceals exactly the instability the speech's history ought to teach. The remedy is not to print the quartos' spelling in a reading text, a pedantry that would cost more readers than it instructs. The remedy is the apparatus and the commentary, used as Weis uses them, to mark the one place in the sonnet where the text on the page is a rescue.

The settlement frays at the edges of the trade, in the editions most readers actually meet. School and paperback texts print the Prologue clean, as they must, and their notes, where notes exist at all, gloss the compounds as vocabulary, thwarted by the stars, doomed to die, without a syllable about the witnesses. A student can therefore pass through the whole of an education in the play, write essays on its fate, and never learn that the fate's most famous formulation survived by one book's margin while its twin survived by every book's consent. The loss is not antiquarian. An account of the play's determinism built on the two phrases reads differently once the phrases' own histories are known to be a story of accident, rescue, and selection, since the frame that announces necessity reached the reader by contingency. Scholarly editions know this and say it in their introductions. The discipline's open secret is that the saying happens in prose almost nobody assigns, while the speech itself is assigned to everyone, shorn of the history that would make its authority interesting.

### What happens to the two phrases when a production cuts the Prologue?

They vanish together, and the tragedy must rebuild its frame from inside the scenes. Directors have cut the Prologue across the play's stage history for the plain reason that a choral summary duplicates what the action is about to show, and a production that begins on the street brawl begins faster.

What such a production loses is not information, since the scenes supply every fact the Chorus names, but assignment: the deaths, when they come, arrive as the outcome of a feud and a series of errors rather than as the fulfilment of an announced condition, and the audience's judgment distributes itself among the characters' choices because no prior voice has claimed the outcome for the heavens or for the mark.

The cut therefore functions as an uncontrolled experiment in the phrases' rivalry, and its results are consistent enough to count as evidence. Remove both announcements and spectators reliably describe the play as a tragedy of accident and haste, a story about what went wrong; restore the Chorus and the same spectators describe a tragedy of doom, a story about what was always going to happen. The scenes do not change. The machinery does not change. Only the frame changes, and the frame is carried by two compounds in a fourteen-line speech. A production that cuts the Prologue has, in effect, voted in the contest this study adjudicates, and voted for neither phrase: it has decided that the play's causation needs no announcement at all. That the play survives the amputation so readily is the strongest practical proof that the phrases govern interpretation rather than mechanism, a proof the next sections develop from inside the text. The stars and the mark do not make anything happen. They tell the audience what kind of happening to watch for, and a frame that can be removed without breaking the machine is a frame whose power is rhetorical, which is to say, in the theatre, the only power that finally counts.

## The Stars on Trial: Star Language Inside the Scenes

Turn from the texts to the play they transmit, and the two phrases face their second adjudication: which of them the enacted tragedy ratifies. The distribution of the vocabularies inside the scenes is the first exhibit, and it is lopsided in a way the Prologue's symmetry conceals. The star compound itself is spoken once, by the Chorus, and never repeated by any character. But its vocabulary, the idiom of stars, fortune, and heaven, runs through the dialogue from end to end, always in the mouths of persons interpreting their situation, never in the machinery of the plot. Romeo, on the way to the Capulet feast, feels "some consequence yet hanging in the stars" that shall "bitterly begin his fearful date / With this night's revels" (1.4). The foreboding is precise in vocabulary and empty in content: it names no event, prevents nothing, and alters no decision, since Romeo goes to the feast anyway, deferring to the supposed pilot of his course in a speech that functions as surrender dressed as piety. In Mantua, handed the false report of Juliet's death, he answers the heavens in their own grammar: "Is it e'en so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1). The defiance is magnificent and wholly without mechanical effect. The stars do not respond, intervene, or even figure in what follows, which is a purchase, a ride, a fight in a tomb, and a draught of poison, each step chosen, timed, and executed by human beings. At the tomb Romeo speaks of shaking "the yoke of inauspicious stars" from his flesh (5.3), the vocabulary intact to the end, the causation untouched by it throughout. The stars in this play are a language the characters speak about their lives. They are never shown operating on those lives, not once, not in any scene.

Juliet's share of the fate vocabulary confirms the distribution from the other side of the love. Where Romeo's doom-idiom is celestial, hers is sepulchral. Her single extended engagement with fortune comes in the parting scene, and it is an argument rather than a submission: "O fortune, fortune! all men call thee fickle. / If thou art fickle, what dost thou with him / That is renown'd for faith? Be fickle, fortune; / For then, I hope, thou wilt not keep him long / But send him back" (3.5). She cross-examines the power her lover obeys, reasons it into a corner, and dismisses it with an instruction, a performance of exactly the agency the Chorus's star phrase denies her. Her presentiment in the same scene is not astrological at all but visual and funerary: "Methinks I see thee, now thou art below, / As one dead in the bottom of a tomb" (3.5). She does not cast a chart of the parting; she sees a corpse in a vault, the tragedy's final image delivered early, in the register of the mark rather than the register of the stars. The lovers thus divide the Prologue's two phrases between them as temperaments. Romeo speaks the crossing, believes it, defies it, and is governed by it as rhetoric. Juliet speaks the tomb, sees it, argues with everything except it, and walks toward it with her eyes open. The play's deepest distribution is not between Chorus and characters but between its two central voices, one assigned to each half of the frame.

The death compound's distribution is the mirror image. "Death-mark'd" is spoken once, by the Chorus, and never repeated either; but where the star idiom echoes through the dialogue without governing it, the mark's claim governs without echoing. No character needs to repeat the phrase, because the scenes keep confirming its content in the indicative mood: a love that proceeds, from the feast to the tomb, under the sign the Chorus read on it, through a passage whose every stage narrows toward the churchyard. The contrast in the second Chorus speech is emblematic. When the Chorus returns before Act 2, both compounds are gone, and the fate register has contracted to a single domestic image: "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.0). The astrology has evaporated after one speech; the deathbed remains. The frame's own sequel thus votes, quietly, for the second phrase's register, mortality as a condition of things, over the first phrase's register, mortality as a decree of the sky.

### How does the Friar's "greater power" speech vote between the phrases?

Friar Laurence, standing in the tomb among the bodies, delivers the play's most explicit causal verdict from inside the action: "A greater power than we can contradict / Hath thwarted our intents" (5.3). The verb reaches back across the whole play to the Prologue's first phrase, since thwarting is what a crossing does.

Without knowing he quotes, the Friar translates the Chorus's "cross'd" into narrative past tense. Yet the power he names is neither stars nor death but an unnamed greater power, a formula of confession rather than astronomy, spoken by a man accounting for a plan of his own devising that failed in his own hands.

The speech is the hinge between the two phrases, and it awards each a partial victory. To the star phrase it concedes the shape of events: something did thwart, oppose, cross the intentions of the actors, and the pattern of crossing is real in retrospect, legible in the wreckage. To the death phrase it concedes the locus of the doom: the power is not located in any chart the Friar could have cast or any aspect he could have read in advance, and it operated through the plan's own instruments, the potion, the letter, the tomb, so that the mark on the love proved a better description than the crossing of the stars. A greater power that works exclusively through human arrangements is functionally indistinguishable from the arrangements themselves, and the Friar's theology, examined cold, is the mark's philosophy wearing the crossing's vocabulary. He is the play's most learned interpreter, and his verdict is that the doom was in the design of things as they were set in motion, not in a sky that could have been consulted. The man of religion, who might have been expected to defend the heavens, instead files the heavens under a pronoun and blames the intents, his own first of all.

## Death-Marked in Practice: What Actually Kills the Lovers

Against the Chorus's two announcements, the enacted causes form a chain with no celestial link in it. The feud supplies the conditions: a city in which a marriage between the houses must be secret, a secret marriage must be defended by a fiction, and the fiction must be maintained by a potion and a letter. Haste supplies the tempo: the compressed calendar of the action, in which the wedding is advanced by a day and every plan is made against a clock its makers did not choose, is the subject of the series study of the play's five days, and its conclusion binds here, that the tragedy's timing is engineered pressure, not drift. The plague supplies the accident with a civic mechanism: Friar John is sealed in a house by the searchers' suspicion, the letter undelivered, in the episode the series study of the quarantine examines at length, and the accident is an ordinance of public health, the most municipal event in the play. The Friar's arrangements supply the single point of failure, a plan entrusted to one messenger with no duplicate sent by another hand, the choice the series study of the undelivered letter adjudicates and finds defensible in design and culpable in its missing redundancy. Poison, steel, and a dagger supply the instruments. At no joint of this chain does a star act, a planet incline, or a configuration cross. The heavens are invoked at the chain's emotional peaks and absent from its links.

The differential of speed between the play's two reports is the mark's practical mechanism, and it deserves stating plainly. Balthasar rides to Mantua with the false report of Juliet's death and arrives while the Friar's letter, the true report, is still sealed behind a quarantine door in Verona; Romeo acts on the falsehood within hours, buys the poison, and reaches the tomb before the correction can leave the city. The tragedy's decisive quantity is neither a planetary period nor a potion's term but a gap, the interval by which error outruns truth along the same road. Every element of that gap is institutional: a servant's horse against a friar's feet, a funeral's publicity against a plan's secrecy, a city's health orders against a churchman's timetable. Read the catastrophe as the working of the death phrase and this is what the phrase means in practice: a love marked in advance because the world it moved through was arranged so that its worst news would always travel fastest. The mark is not magic. It is logistics, foreseen by the one voice positioned to see the whole arrangement, and misread ever after as the stars.

The body count delivers the same verdict in arithmetic, and it catches the first quarto's error in an unexpected light. The Prologue's phrases name two deaths, the lovers', and the frame they build is sized for two. The play delivers more: Mercutio and Tybalt in the street, Paris at the tomb, Lady Montague dead of grief offstage, six deaths in the series' settled census, of whom only two were announced as star-crossed and death-marked. The excess is instructive. Every unannounced death belongs to the feud's account rather than the love's: the kinsman, the heir, the suitor, the mother, killed by the rage's ordinary operations and by nothing in the heavens that any character can point to. Measured by corpses, the tragedy's marked thing is not the love at all but the feud, the parents' rage whose passage through five days of Verona collects bodies at every stage. The first quarto's reporter, misassigning the death mark to the passage of the parents' rage, produced a reading the arithmetic prefers to the line he lost. His patch is wrong as text and right as criticism, and the double status is the most Shakespearean thing about it: the tradition's worst witness understood, in his error, where the play's doom was actually domiciled.

The Prince's closing audit, the play's official verdict on its own causes, distributes the blame in the same proportions. "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3): heaven is named as the scourge's wielder, and the instrument named in the same breath is the hate, a human property with human owners, whom the speech proceeds to punish and pardon by name. Capulet and Montague are not sentenced by a horoscope. They are sentenced by their conduct, and the golden statues they promise are a civic penalty paid in civic currency. The tragedy's final accounting thus keeps the star phrase's grammar, heaven as the court of appeal, while entering every item of the judgment on the death phrase's side of the ledger: a doom that inhered in the situation, in two households' hate and a love marked by the conditions it was born into, and that required no machinery beyond the city working exactly as the city worked. The wider philosophy of the question, the claims of fate against the claims of free will across the whole canon of readings, belongs to the companion study of [fate and free will in Romeo and Juliet](/2023/12/22/fate-and-free-will-romeo-and-juliet/); the local finding stands on its own. Inside this play, the stars are the name the survivors give to a pattern whose every link they forged themselves.

## Star-Crossed Above, Death-Marked Below: How the Frame Cooperates

The rivalry, pressed through texts and scenes alike, dissolves at the level of function, and the dissolution is the study's interpretive verdict. The two phrases are not competing explanations between which the play asks its audience to choose. They are two instruments in one announcement, calibrated to different distances. The star phrase works at the distance of the cosmos: it tells the audience, before any character appears, that the pattern about to unfold has the shape of necessity, that the end is written somewhere above the action and can be read in advance, as the Chorus is reading it. It is the phrase of the frame, licensing the Prologue's own foreknowledge and the audience's superior vantage, the pleasure of watching free creatures walk a road whose end the watchers know. The death phrase works at the distance of the object: it tells the audience what kind of thing the love is, a marked thing, carrying its end in its constitution the way a flaw in metal carries the fracture. It is the phrase of the scenes, licensing attention to mechanism, to the feud's pressure and the plan's fragility, because a marked thing will break where it is marked when the pressure comes. Necessity above, constitution below: the Chorus needs both because the tragedy it announces operates on both levels at once, feeling fated from the auditorium and proving mechanical on the stage.

Either phrase alone would deform the play it announces, and the deformations are worth naming because both are on permanent display in the play's afterlife. The star phrase alone yields the greeting-card tragedy: two innocents crushed by a picturesque sky, the feud reduced to scenery, the Friar's letter to an act of God, and every responsible adult in Verona acquitted by cosmology. The death phrase alone yields the clinical tragedy: a passion observed as a specimen, its doom a property to be catalogued by students, the lovers' own sense of a hostile heaven dismissed as a superstition the audience has outgrown. The play performed under the first frame alone becomes a consolation; under the second, a case study. The Chorus refuses both reductions by speaking the phrases together, four lines apart, inside one sonnet's architecture. The stars give the audience metaphysical permission to feel the necessity the scenes will keep declining to supply; the mark obliges the same audience to assign that necessity to the things themselves, to hate and haste and the arrangements of a city. Double-entry bookkeeping of that kind is the tragedy's method from the first quatrain to the Prince's audit, and the frame is simply the method announced in advance.

The cooperation explains the phrases' unequal afterlives, a divergence the textual record forecast. The star phrase escaped the play and became the language's property, a headline and a compliment, because it names an external, picturesque, blameless cause: lovers crossed by stars are victims of the sky, and the phrase flatters everyone it touches. The series study of the phrase's passage from astrology to pop culture traces that career in full, from the nativity's verdict to the divorce album, and establishes the point required here, that the compound is first recorded in this play in 1597 and was fixed and bequeathed by it, whatever earlier spoken life the record cannot show. The death phrase stayed home. It is the more exact description of the play and the less portable possession, because a mark on the love itself accuses the love, and the culture that made a valentine of the story had no use for an accusation embedded in the gift. Posterity kept the phrase that excuses and shelved the phrase that explains. The textual tradition had already performed the same selection in 1597, keeping the crossing safe in two witnesses and letting the mark slip toward a parenthesis in one: transmission, like quotation, preserves what flatters its carriers and fumbles what implicates them.

## Star-Crossed Abroad: The Phrases in Translation

Translation administers the same test under laboratory conditions, because a translator must decide, word by word, what each phrase is made of, and the two great versions the series has audited divide their losses along exactly the line this study draws. August Wilhelm Schlegel's German Prologue, the version that made the play a German classic, drops the stars entire: where the English Chorus derives the lovers from "the fatal loins of these two foes" as "a pair of star-cross'd lovers," the German gives a life springing from the foes' ill-fated loins, and the self-killing of "take their life" dissolves into that springing. The death phrase survives in altered dress as todgeweihte Liebe, love consecrated to death, the mark elevated from a brand into a consecration, doom planed into solemnity. The German text thus keeps the marked half of the frame and spends the crossed half, reversing the English transmission's priorities and confirming, from the opposite direction, that the two phrases are separable goods: a version may keep either and lose the other, and what it keeps determines the metaphysics its audience inherits, astrology in the one case, consecration in the other, mechanism in neither.

The German loss, examined in the series catalogue of the play's hardest translation joints, cannot be filed under necessity. German compounds as freely as English, more freely, and a star-compound at the joint was a formal possibility in the receiving language; the crossing fell not to linguistic impossibility but to a programme, the smoothing economy that everywhere in Schlegel's version relieves the original's knots in favour of solemn flow. The catalogue's distinction is the sharpest instrument the phrases' history possesses: losses divide into the necessary, which excuse a transmitter, and the chosen, which describe one. Set beside the German case, the first quarto's English loss changes character in an instructive way. The reporter's loss was neither necessary nor chosen but suffered, the failure of a memory under load; the translator's was chosen under no load at all. Between them, the two traditions map the full range of ways a frame can shrink, and in both of them the same half of the frame proves easier to keep. Solemnity travels. Astrology stays home, and the mark, which needs no cosmology at all, travels best of all when a translator lets it.

Boris Pasternak's Russian Prologue performs the complementary experiment. The sonnet keeps its fourteen lines and its rhymes, the frame's architecture entire, and inside the architecture both compounds vanish: no stars cross, no death marks, and in their place fate sets snares for the lovers. The substitution is the most eloquent gloss the phrases have received. A snare is a human device, laid by hands in a landscape, and a tragedy that opens with snares has quietly relocated its doom from the cosmos to the city, from the sky to the feud and the households that built the trap. The Russian audience met, at the threshold, the reading this study reaches at the end of its evidence: the machinery of the catastrophe is human, and the heavens are its metaphor. The series catalogue of the play in translation files the pair among its dozen hardest joints and grades the residue with an auditor's brevity: the doom crosses, the astrology does not. Between Schlegel keeping the mark and Pasternak keeping neither, the phrases' contest receives its international adjudication. Every tradition preserves the certainty. The cosmology is negotiable, and is everywhere negotiated away.

## The InsightCrunch Prologue-Variant Comparison

The collation, the editorial history, and the adjudication converge in a single reference instrument, the InsightCrunch Prologue-variant comparison, which sets the fate phrases and their contexts witness by witness so that a reader can see, in one grid, what each early text does to the announcement. The comparison is built for the seminar as much as for the study: prepared as a handout or a slide for classroom collation exercises, using a clean display tool such as [the ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) to put the witnesses on one screen, it lets a class watch the death phrase migrate in Q1 before any lecture explains what migration means. Its rows follow the speech in order, and its verdicts column records the finding this study argues in prose.

Read down the columns, the comparison tells the study's whole story without commentary. The Q2 column is a sonnet; the Q1 column is the memory of a sonnet; the Folio column is a silence. Read across the rows, the pattern of loss refuses to distribute itself evenly, gathering instead on the death phrase's quatrain and sparing the star phrase's, so that the grid's most eloquent cell is an absence: the line in which the Chorus brands the love itself is the line one witness mislays and another omits, while the line in which the Chorus blames the stars stands secure in every text that prints the speech at all. Editors print the Q2 column. Critics should keep the whole grid in view, because the play's announced fate is not a datum but a transmission, and the comparison is the record of its narrow escape.

The blank column deserves its own reading. A comparison table usually argues from what its cells contain; this one argues also from what its third column withholds, since the Folio's silence is not a gap in the evidence but a piece of it, the record of a tradition that could lose the frame entire and not notice. Every other column testifies about readings. The blank testifies about attention, about which parts of a play a culture's book-keepers treat as load-bearing and which they treat as decoration, and the verdict it records is uncomfortable for any theory that makes the Prologue the play's interpretive key: the key was, for the edition that mattered most to the seventeenth century, simply not in the lock. The phrases survived that inattention because the quartos had already done the work the Folio declined to do.

## Verdict: Print Q2, and Read the Two Phrases as One Frame

The questions posed at the outset have their answers, and they interlock. Textually, the variants are not a contest between two authorial Prologues but the record of one Prologue and its remembering: the second quarto's sonnet is the speech, the first quarto's twelve lines are its debris profile, diagnostic of memory at work, and the Folio's omission is an accident of manufacture that Rowe's restoration repaired from the quartos. Editors should print, as Gibbons, Levenson, and Weis print, the Q2 text, with Q1's parenthesis preserved in the apparatus as the most instructive variant in the speech, and with Erne's parallel Q1 available for the documentary study the variant deserves. Interpretively, the phrases are not rival theses competing for the play's allegiance but a double frame whose halves the play distributes with precision: the stars govern the language in which the characters understand their lives, the mark governs the constitution of the love the audience watches, and the machinery of the catastrophe, feud, haste, quarantine, and a single unduplicated letter, belongs entirely to the second register while speaking, at its emotional peaks, in the vocabulary of the first.

For the reader of a modern edition, the practical upshot is a habit rather than a doctrine: trust the page, and check the apparatus at the famous lines. The page will give the sonnet entire and correct, as the editorial tradition has arranged for three centuries, and nothing in this study proposes disturbing that settlement. The apparatus will give the one thing the page cannot, the knowledge that the settlement was an achievement, that the mark on the love survived by a single witness's margin and the crossing survived because a slogan is harder to lose than a sentence. Readers who acquire the habit read the Prologue differently thereafter, as a document with a biography rather than a fixture of the furniture, and the phrases themselves gain back the strangeness that three centuries of quotation have polished off them: two desperate expedients of a Chorus telling the truth twice, in two incompatible vocabularies, because one vocabulary was not enough for what it knew.

That distribution is why the cliché and the text diverge, and why restoring the variants restores the play. The cliché quotes one Prologue because it keeps one phrase: star-crossed, the slogan, the excuse, the compliment, the fate that arrives from a picturesque sky and asks nothing of anyone. The documents preserve a harder announcement, in which a love is marked in itself, legible from the first quatrain of consequences to anyone who reads surfaces, and in which the heavens are the metaphor a city uses for the pattern of its own arrangements. The First Quarto, fumbling the mark into a parenthesis about the parents' rage, came nearer the play's truth than its reporter knew: in the event, it is the rage's passage, the feud's career through five days of Verona, that carries the death mark through the action to the tomb. The second quarto's line stands, and should stand, as the text. The first quarto's error stands beside it in the apparatus as the tradition's own Freudian slip, the moment the transmission confessed where the doom in this play actually lives: not in the stars above the city, but in the mark on the things the city contains.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is the wording of "star-cross'd lovers" the same in the First Quarto as in the Second Quarto?

Yes, in substance the wording is identical, and the agreement is one of the most important facts in the Prologue's textual history. Both quartos set the compound in the same line, in the same position, attached to the same noun: the First Quarto of 1597 prints "A paire of starre-crost Louers" and the Second Quarto of 1599 prints "A paire of starre-crost louers," each in its own house spelling. The differences between the witnesses at this point are accidental rather than substantive, a matter of type and capitalization, plus one grammatical change in the verb that follows, where the first quarto's lovers took their life and the second quarto's take it. The phrase that became the play's public label was therefore never a disputed reading. Editors have never had to choose a star-crossed text, because the tradition hands them only one.

### Q: What changes when the First Quarto's lovers "took" their life where the Second Quarto's "take" it?

The tense changes the kind of certainty the Chorus offers. The Second Quarto's present tense, "take their life," proclaims the deaths as an action unfolding inside the performance, a sentence passed at line 6 and executed across the two hours' traffic that the same speech advertises. Announcement and event share a tense, and the audience watches the sentence carried out. The First Quarto's past tense, "tooke their life," converts the proclamation into a report: the Chorus becomes a narrator summarizing a completed story, and the deaths recede into a chronicle the audience audits rather than witnesses. A reported doom can be discounted as a summary's exaggeration. A proclaimed doom hangs over every scene as a term of the contract between stage and house, which is why editors print the present tense and why the past tense reads, in performance terms, as the flatter instrument.

### Q: In the First Quarto's Prologue, what is called death-marked, if the love is not?

The passage of the parents' rage is called death-marked. The First Quarto loses the Second Quarto's ninth line, "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love," as a line, and rebuilds the third quatrain as a parenthesis: "(Through the continuing of their fathers' strife, / And death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage)." Inside that patch the strong word survives with a new owner. Where the received text brands the lovers' love as the thing stamped for death, the first quarto's text brands the feud's career, the rage passing down through the households. The reassignment is almost certainly a reporter's repair rather than an authorial reading, memory salvaging a memorable word after losing its sentence. Yet the error is interpretively eloquent, since the enacted play does make the parents' rage the marked thing, the carrier that delivers death to everyone it touches across five days.

### Q: Does any character inside the play ever speak the words "death-mark'd"?

No character speaks them. The compound occurs once in the entire play, in the Chorus's ninth line, and no lover, friar, parent, or prince ever repeats it. The distribution is the phrase's character in miniature: the mark is the frame's knowledge, not the persons' vocabulary, a diagnosis legible to the audience and to no one on stage. The star idiom behaves in precisely the opposite way. The compound "star-cross'd" is likewise spoken once, by the Chorus, but its vocabulary escapes into the dialogue and runs there for five acts, in Romeo's foreboding before the feast, in his defiance in Mantua, in his talk of inauspicious stars at the tomb, in the Friar's thwarted intents. Characters can argue with the stars because the star register belongs to their speech. The mark belongs to the speech about them, and its silence inside the scenes is the sound of a condition that nobody needs to name because everybody is busy enacting it.

### Q: What would readers lose if an edition printed the First Quarto's Prologue in place of the Second's?

Readers would lose two lines and three of the speech's load-bearing claims. The lost eighth line, "Doth with their death bury their parents' strife," carries the tragedy's teleology, the promise that the deaths purchase the feud's end, without which the ending's reconciliation has no announced warrant. The lost tenth and eleventh lines carry the necessity claim, that nothing short of the children's end could remove the parents' rage, without which the catastrophe reads as one bad outcome among possible others. And the displaced ninth line costs the received text its central image, the love itself branded for death, replaced by a parenthesis about the rage. What remains is a competent summary of a feud and a pair of deaths, twelve lines that advertise a play. What vanishes is the sonnet that interprets the play in advance, which is the difference between the first quarto's billing and the second quarto's achievement.

### Q: Why does the survival of "death-mark'd" inside a broken line point to memory rather than to copying?

Because the two kinds of transmission damage texts in opposite ways. A copyist working from a page corrupts at the level of the letter: misread minims, skipped eyes, transposed words within an intact syntax, errors that presuppose the sentence was there to be miscopied. The First Quarto's third quatrain shows the inverse profile. Its most striking word survives intact while the sentence that housed the word has collapsed and been rebuilt as a parenthesis with the word reassigned to a different noun. That is the damage pattern of recall, in which salient items persist and structure perishes, the pattern Harry Hoppe's study of the first quarto made central to the memorial account of the text. A mind that had heard the speech in the theatre would keep "death-mark'd," keep "strife," keep "continuing," and lose the subordination that arranged them. A mind copying the page could lose the word. It could hardly misplace the word inside a rebuilt sentence of its own manufacture.

### Q: Could the First Quarto's death-marked passage be the playwright's own earlier wording of the line?

The possibility has been entertained for the first quarto as a whole, since the early-draft explanation of its variants has a long pedigree, but the death-marked passage is the least plausible exhibit for it. An author's draft is syntactically complete even when verbally rough, because the writer knows what the sentence governs. The first quarto's parenthesis is not a rougher version of the ninth line; it is a different sentence, assembled from the line's debris plus material from two neighbouring lines, with the modifier detached from the love and fastened to the parents' rage. Revision characteristically moves from the less to the more articulate, from "household friends" toward "households, both," and the direction of every variant in this speech runs the other way, from articulation toward approximation. Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto keeps the draft hypothesis open for the play in general, and properly so. For this line, the memorial explanation fits the damage and the draft explanation does not.

### Q: Which of the two phrases names an agent, and which names only a condition?

"Star-cross'd" names an agent; "death-mark'd" names only a condition. The star compound presupposes the stars as actors performing a crossing upon the lovers, an event with a doer, a mechanism in the period's astrology, and patients who receive it. A claim of that shape can be investigated, resented, and defied, and the play's characters do all three. The death compound is a passive participle with its agent deleted: the love is marked, and the phrase declines to say by whom, by what instrument, or at what moment. Nothing in it can be cross-examined, because it asserts a property rather than a process. That grammatical asymmetry explains the phrases' different careers inside the play and outside it. The agent-naming phrase invites argument and generates a literature of defiance and blame. The condition-naming phrase invites only recognition, and waits in its single line to be confirmed by everything that follows.

### Q: Why do modern editions print "star-cross'd" with an apostrophe that neither quarto sets?

The apostrophe is a metrical instruction, marking an elision the quartos left to the reader's ear. In the early texts the compounds appear as "starre-crost" and "death-markt," spellings that already compress the second syllable, the devoiced final letter of "death-markt" recording the swallowed pronunciation in type. A modernized text must choose between printing the words full, "star-crossed" and "death-marked," which invites a reader to sound the final syllable and break the pentameter, and printing them with the apostrophe that restores the spoken contraction. Editors from the Arden and Oxford traditions choose the apostrophe, following the pronunciation the copy-text's own spelling witnesses. The device is a small editorial kindness with one cost: it dresses both compounds in identical finish, concealing that one reading is doubly attested and the other hangs by a single witness. The apparatus, not the type, has to carry that news.

### Q: Does "death-mark'd" foretell the manner of the lovers' deaths, or only the end of their love?

Only the end, and the restriction is precise. The mark is set on the love, not on the lovers' bodies, and it specifies no instrument: nothing in the phrase predicts poison for one and a dagger for the other, a tomb for the meeting place, or a three-day delay between the deaths' causes and their discovery. The play's manners of death arrive through its machinery, the apothecary's trade, the churchyard fight, the Friar's arrival minutes late, none of which the mark describes. What the mark guarantees is the destination of the passage, that the love's course terminates in death, and it guarantees that destination in the way a brand guarantees ownership, by prior assignment rather than by mechanism. Readers who treat the phrase as a prophecy of detail overread it. The Chorus's other causal word, "misadventured," covers the route, with its accidents and mishaps. The mark covers the terminus, and the division of labour between route and terminus is the sonnet's causal scheme in miniature.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Prologue-variant comparison to argue that the two phrases perform different work?

An essay should begin from the grid's asymmetry and treat it as evidence about function rather than about luck. The star phrase's row shows a reading that transmission could not damage, doubly attested and stable, matching a phrase built for public work: a slogan with an agent in it, quotable, portable, arguable. The death phrase's row shows a reading that transmission damaged at every joint, lost as a line in one witness, omitted with the whole speech in another, matching a phrase built for structural work: a condition embedded in syntax, dependent on its sentence, perishing when the sentence perishes. Set the two rows side by side, then follow each phrase into the scenes, where the star idiom is spoken constantly and effects nothing while the mark is spoken never and describes everything. The conclusion the comparison supports is that the phrases were always different instruments, one made to be repeated and one made to be borne out, and that their textual histories are their functions, recorded in type.

### Q: Why does the Chorus give the lovers a star phrase and their love a death phrase?

Because the sonnet assigns doom at the two points where doom can attach, to persons and to the relation between them. The star phrase closes the quatrain of origins, hard against the "fatal loins" from which the lovers issue, and a verdict delivered at birth belongs to astrology's jurisdiction, the nativity cast at the start of a life. The death phrase opens the quatrain of consequences and attaches to the love, the thing the lovers make between them, whose passage across the play is the action the audience has gathered to watch. Persons receive a cosmic sentence because persons have births and charts. The love receives a mark because the love has a career, and a career can be branded at its outset for the end it will reach. Splitting the announcement this way lets the Chorus be exact: the heavens are blamed for the lovers' starting condition, and the love is described as carrying its conclusion inside itself from the first scene in which it exists.

### Q: What does the Prince's closing judgment owe to each of the Prologue's two fate phrases?

The Prince's sentence at the tomb, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love," pays a debt to both phrases and settles each in a different currency. To the star phrase it owes its grammar of appeal: heaven is named as the agent of the scourge, the court above the city entering the verdict, exactly as the crossing installed a celestial agent over the lovers' births. To the death phrase it owes its substance: the instrument heaven uses is the hate, a property of the households themselves, and the killing is done through the love, so that the doom is executed by the marked things, the feud and the passion, rather than by any star. The closing audit is therefore the Prologue read backwards with the evidence in. The frame's agent survives as a form of words. The frame's condition supplies every item in the account.

### Q: Which of the two phrases would an Elizabethan astrologer have heard as a term of his art?

The astrologer would have heard "star-cross'd," because crossing was the art's technical event. In the practice documented by the period's almanacs and manuals, a life was read from a nativity, and the malefic planets in adverse aspect, opposing or squaring the lights and the ascendant, thwarted the native in the houses they afflicted: a crossing, in the strict sense, was something one configuration did to another at a calculable moment. A practitioner handed the Chorus's sixth line could in principle have demanded the data and checked the finding, since the line compresses a chart's verdict into a compound. "Death-mark'd" would have sounded to the same ear as poetry rather than practice, a strong figure without a technique behind it: no house, aspect, or dignities correspond to a mark, and no consultation could confirm or refute one. The first phrase cites a profession. The second cites a metaphor, and the play's fate is announced in both dialects at once.

### Q: Why has "star-crossed" become the play's public label while "death-marked" remained a scholar's phrase?

The star phrase had every advantage a phrase needs for escape. It names an external and picturesque agent, the stars, whose blame costs nobody anything and flatters the lovers as victims of the sky; it compresses into a single compound that survives extraction from its line, its syntax, and its play; and it was textually secure from the start, attested in both quartos, so that no generation of readers ever met the play without it. "Death-mark'd" labour under opposite conditions. It accuses the love itself, an accusation the story's afterlife as a valentine could not use; it sits inside a subordinate movement of its sentence and resists extraction; and it was textually fragile, garbled in one witness and omitted with the whole speech in another. Posterity kept the phrase that excuses, as the first quarto's reporter had instinctively kept the phrase that survives in memory. The label and the apparatus are the record of the same selection, performed twice.

### Q: What does the word "passage" contribute to the line that carries "death-mark'd"?

"Passage" gives the mark a journey to govern. Without the word, a death-marked love would be a static emblem, a labelled object in a tableau. With it, the love becomes a traveller on a course, moving through scenes, cities, and days toward a destination, and the mark becomes the destination's sign posted at the journey's start. The word also carries its second sense within reach: a passage is a stretch of text, and the Chorus, a figure made of language, is describing the love's course and the play's course in the same breath, since the two hours' traffic named four lines later is the passage the audience is purchasing. The First Quarto's treatment confirms the word's load-bearing role. Its patch preserves "passage" even while losing the line, transferring the word to the parents' rage, as though the memory that rebuilt the sentence knew the journey had to survive even when its traveller was misremembered. The love's passage, the rage's passage, the play's passage: the word is the hinge on which all three turn.

### Q: What work does the word "fearful" do in the line "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love"?

"Fearful" assigns the audience its emotion before the emotion's object has fully appeared, and it does so with a productive ambiguity. The word can mean dreadful, causing fear, and it can lean toward fearing, full of fear, and the line profits from both pressures at once: the passage is dreadful to watch, and the love travels it in fear, of discovery, of the feud, of the next hour. Placed first among the line's descriptors, the epithet instructs the house how to hold the story it has just been told in summary, not as a curiosity but as a dread, and it colours the two hours that follow, in which scenes of comedy and lyric beauty are watched through the apprehension the epithet installed. The textual record adds a footnote of its own. The First Quarto's patch drops the word entire, along with the line it governed, and the loss is of a piece with that witness's general tendency: the reporter retained the compounds that name and dropped the epithet that tunes, keeping the announcement's facts while losing its register of feeling.

### Q: How is "death-mark'd" spelled in the Second Quarto, and what does that spelling record?

The Second Quarto spells the compound "death-markt," without hyphen or apostrophe, the final consonant written as t rather than d. The spelling records a pronunciation: in the compound's spoken form the voiced consonant of "marked" was devoiced against the preceding sound and the second syllable compressed, so that the word fell as one heavy beat in the pentameter rather than two. Compositors set what the copy and the house style gave them, and here the type preserves a fossil of the word as it sounded in the playhouse, the mark stamped quickly, in passing, inside a line whose speed is part of its meaning. Modern editions restore the apostrophe, printing "death-mark'd," to instruct the modern reader's voice toward the same compression the quarto spelling assumes. The two dresses of the word are a small history of editing in themselves, the early form recording a sound, the later form prescribing one, and both forms pointing at a syllable that was never meant to be dwelt on.

### Q: Which single early book preserves the phrase "death-mark'd love" complete?

The Second Quarto of 1599 preserves it, alone among the early witnesses. The phrase stands there entire at the head of the third quatrain, "The fearfull passage of their death-markt loue," in the only complete early statement of the line. The First Quarto of 1597 preserves the compound in displaced form, inside its parenthesis, attached to the parents' rage rather than to the love, so that the word survives while the phrase does not. The First Folio of 1623 preserves neither, having omitted the Prologue altogether. Every later text that prints the phrase, from the third quarto of 1609 through the quarto tradition to Rowe's restoration and the modern critical editions, descends from the second quarto's page at this point. The most quietly devastating formulation in the Prologue, the love branded for death in its own name, thus reaches posterity through one door, and the door is a single corrected quarto whose title page promised a text newly amended. The amendment, at this line, was the preservation.
