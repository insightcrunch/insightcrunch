The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013 did something no Indian statute had done before. It created a single body with power to inquire into corruption allegations against a serving Prime Minister, against Union ministers, against members of Parliament, and against every grade of central government official from the cabinet secretary's peers down to the lowest group of clerks and attendants. It gave that body its own inquiry and prosecution wings, authority over the Central Bureau of Investigation in cases it referred, and a procedure designed to run from complaint to trial inside fixed periods. Then, for five years and two months after the Act came into force, the body did not exist. There was no Chairperson, no member, no office receiving complaints. The statute was in force the whole time. Nothing had been repealed, nothing had been stayed by a court, and no commencement notification was missing. The institution was disabled by a single sentence in its own appointment provisions, which named an office, the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, that the electorate had not produced.

![The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013, showing the ombudsman Parliament created and the appointment mechanism that kept it empty for five years - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-25.webp)

That paradox is the reason this statute deserves a pillar treatment rather than a summary. A reader who knows only the movement of 2011 knows why the Act was passed. A reader who knows only the section headings knows what the Act says. Neither reader can answer the questions that matter: what the Lokpal was actually given power to do, as distinct from what the movement demanded; by what precise mechanism a fully enacted statute was kept unstaffed for half a decade; and why a body with jurisdiction over the head of government has produced so little visible output since it was finally appointed. Those three questions organise everything that follows.

A note on method before the detail. This pillar treats the statute as the whole record around its text: the text itself, read closely; the appointment and litigation history that determined when the text could operate; and the published output by which its operation can be judged. Where figures are contested or move by source and period, as complaint numbers in this field do, the article says so and uses the figures that a named public record, a parliamentary answer or a committee report, will support. Where the brief for this article flagged a fact for verification, the fact has been checked against the official text or a reliable account before use, and the handoff accompanying the article records each resolution.

The short answers can be stated at once, and the rest of the article exists to earn them. The Lokpal was given real but carefully filtered power: it inquires and investigates, it sanctions prosecution and files cases before special courts, but it does not itself convict, punish or dismiss anyone, and almost every route into its jurisdiction passes through a screen. The five-year vacancy was not an accident of administration but the working out of a drafting choice, the decision to compose the selection committee out of named constitutional offices rather than functions, so that the absence of one officeholder could be argued, and for years was argued, to freeze the whole machine. And the thin output since 2019 is not primarily a story of capture or sabotage; it is what the design predicts. A body that cannot act without a written complaint in a prescribed form, that must run a preliminary inquiry before it may order an investigation, that prosecutes through a court rather than deciding guilt itself, and that worked for long stretches below its sanctioned strength, will generate few outcomes that a headline can count. Judging it by convictions misunderstands what Parliament built, though judging it by nothing at all would be too kind, and the record section of this article tries to hold both truths at once.

## What existed before, and why it was not enough

India did not lack anti-corruption machinery before 2014. What it lacked was a single institution standing outside the executive's chain of command with jurisdiction across the whole of it. The Central Vigilance Commission, placed on a statutory footing in 2003, could advise and supervise but its writ ran to vigilance administration rather than prosecution, and its commissioners were appointed by the government of the day. The Central Bureau of Investigation worked under the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946, which meant that the country's principal anti-corruption investigator was, in administrative law, a police force operating on deputation, dependent on the executive for its director's tenure battles, its staff and its budget. The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 supplied the offences, but its prosecution machinery ran through sanction: before a public servant could be prosecuted, the government or authority competent to remove that servant had to permit it, a filter that placed the decision to prosecute an official in the hands of the official's own administrative superiors. Departmental inquiries handled the rest, inside the same hierarchies that had failed to prevent the conduct.

The institution India was reaching for has a long pedigree and an instructive original form. The ombudsman began in Sweden in 1809 as an officer of Parliament, not of the executive, charged with supervising the administration on the legislature's behalf, and in its classical form the office investigates, criticises and recommends, but does not prosecute: its power is publicity and parliamentary confidence. The Indian design departed from the classical model in the direction of muscle, giving the Lokpal direction over investigation and a prosecution wing of its own, because the Indian debate was never only about maladministration; it was about criminal corruption at levels where a recommendatory office was thought, on the evidence of the vigilance machinery's record, to be a paper remedy. The departure carries a cost that the classical model avoids. A prosecuting ombudsman must observe the disciplines of criminal process, screens, hearings, sanction, limitation, and each discipline is a stage at which throughput falls. Much of the disappointment with the Lokpal's output is, at root, disappointment that a body designed as a prosecutor does not produce outcomes at the speed of a body designed as a critic.

The ombudsman idea was meant to break that circle. The word Lokpal itself was proposed in Parliament in 1963 by Laxman Mall Singhvi, who adapted the Scandinavian ombudsman to Indian vocabulary: lok, the people, and pal, the protector. The first Administrative Reforms Commission, chaired by Morarji Desai, recommended in its interim report of 1966 that two authorities be created, a Lokpal at the Centre and Lokayuktas in the states, to deal with complaints against ministers and senior officials, precisely the level at which the existing vigilance machinery was weakest because it reported to the people it would have to investigate. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission, reporting on ethics in governance nearly forty years later, returned to the same conclusion and recommended a national ombudsman again, which tells its own story: by the middle of the first decade of this century, the Lokpal had become the most frequently recommended and least frequently enacted institution in Indian administrative law. The recommendation was not implemented at the Centre for forty-five years. Bills to create a Lokpal were introduced in the Lok Sabha in 1968, when the first of them passed that House only to lapse in the Rajya Sabha on dissolution, and again in 1971, 1977, 1985, 1989, 1996, 1998 and 2001, lapsing each time on dissolution or in committee, a pattern examined in the passage history of this statute. The regularity of the failure is itself diagnostic: governments of every composition introduced the bill when pressure demanded a gesture and let it die when the pressure passed, because the institution's whole purpose was to investigate the class of persons who controlled its legislative timetable. Several states moved faster on their own account. Maharashtra enacted the first state Lokayukta statute in 1971, and over the following decades most larger states acquired some version of the office, with powers that varied from the genuinely formidable to the purely decorative.

What finally carried a central bill through Parliament, after more than four decades of failure, was the mobilisation of 2011, when Anna Hazare's fasts and the India Against Corruption campaign turned a drafting question into a street question and a rival text, the Jan Lokpal Bill, competed with the government's draft in public. The full passage story, the joint drafting committee, the sense of the House resolution, the midnight collapse of the Rajya Sabha debate in December 2011 and the select committee process that followed, belongs to [the account of the Lokpal Bill and the Anna Hazare movement](/2011/12/01/lokpal-bill-anna-hazare-movement/), and it is compressed here to what the statute pillar needs. The bill passed the Lok Sabha in December 2011, was revised by a select committee of the Rajya Sabha, passed the Rajya Sabha on 17 December 2013 and the Lok Sabha, accepting the amendments, on 18 December 2013. The President assented on 1 January 2014, and the Act commenced on 16 January 2014. Its citation is Act 1 of 2014, though its short title keeps the year of its passage: the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013.

The gap between the movement's draft and the enacted text matters to everything the institution has done since, and it is flagged here and analysed near the end of this article. The enacted Act kept the Prime Minister inside jurisdiction, which the government drafts had resisted and the movement had demanded. It did not merge the anti-corruption wing of the Central Bureau of Investigation into the Lokpal, as the Jan Lokpal draft proposed. It did not bring the judiciary within reach. It did not cover the conduct of members of Parliament inside the House. And it routed the vast lower bureaucracy through a screening arrangement involving the Central Vigilance Commission rather than direct inquiry in every case. The statute that emerged was neither the government's minimal version nor the movement's maximal one. It was a negotiated instrument, and its subsequent history, the freeze, the thin record, the quarrels over form, is the history of those negotiations working themselves out in practice.

## The shape of the Act

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 is a statute of sixty-three sections arranged in chapters that follow the life of a case and the life of the institution. Its opening chapters establish the Lokpal, define its composition, selection, terms of office and removal, and create its two working wings. The middle chapters define jurisdiction, the procedure of inquiry and investigation, the powers that attend each stage, and the special courts before which prosecutions are to be tried. Later chapters deal with the declaration of assets by public servants, the annual report the Lokpal must present to the President, the bar on civil court jurisdiction, the limitation period for complaints, and, in Part III, the single section that concerns the states: Section 63, which obliges every state to establish a Lokayukta. The Schedule amends other enactments, most consequentially the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003, into which it inserts provisions allowing that Commission to inquire into matters the Lokpal refers to it concerning the lower groups of officials.

The definitions chapter does quiet but decisive work. The Act does not build its own roster of the corruptible; it adopts the definition of public servant from the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 and then extends it by the deeming provisions attached to Section 14, so the two statutes' personal scopes move together and any expansion or contraction of the corruption statute's definition flows into the Lokpal's jurisdiction without further amendment. A complaint is likewise a defined gateway: an allegation in the prescribed form, which is why the complaint rules examined later in this article are not administrative trivia but part of the jurisdictional text in substance. And the Act asserts its own priority where it applies, providing in Section 56 that its provisions have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other enactment, while Section 57 keeps it additional to, and not in derogation of, the other laws it overlaps, a pairing that tells the reader the Lokpal was meant to override obstacles and coexist with remedies, not to swallow the vigilance, disciplinary and criminal systems it directs.

Three structural facts about the Act should be fixed before any detail, because each corrects a common error. The first is that the Lokpal is a statutory body, not a constitutional one. It owes its existence to an ordinary Act of Parliament, amendable by ordinary legislation, unlike the Election Commission or the Comptroller and Auditor General, whose offices the Constitution itself creates. The second is that the Lokpal's expenses, including the salaries of its Chairperson and members, are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, which means they are provided without an annual vote in Parliament, a modest but real insulation against budgetary pressure. The third is that the Act separates finding from punishing with unusual strictness. The Lokpal inquires, investigates through agencies, sanctions prosecution and files chargesheets before special courts, recommends disciplinary proceedings to the competent authority, and reports annually to the President, with its report laid before Parliament. It does not try the case, it does not impose the sentence, and it does not itself dismiss the official. Every power it holds is a power to set another institution in motion or to remove an obstacle in that institution's path, and an assessment of the Lokpal that ignores this architecture will misread its record from the first paragraph.

In its ordinary working the Lokpal does not sit as one body of nine. The Act contemplates the institution functioning through benches constituted by the Chairperson from among the members, so that complaints can be screened and preliminary stages heard in parallel, with the full bench reserved for the decisions the statute singles out, above all the threshold decision on an inquiry against the Prime Minister. The bench structure is the institution's answer to scale, and it carries a consequence that recurs throughout this article: any function the Act assigns to the full bench is hostage to the body being at full strength, so vacancies degrade not just the Lokpal's speed but, at the top of its jurisdiction, its competence to begin.

The offences the Act works on are not its own. The Lokpal inquires into allegations of corruption, a term the Act ties to offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, the statute that defines bribery, criminal misconduct and possession of disproportionate assets by public servants. The Lokpal is therefore best understood as a jurisdiction and machinery statute layered over an existing penal code of corruption: it decides who may be reached, by what route, with what safeguards, and before which court, while the underlying offences, their elements and their punishments live in the 1988 Act, whose own pillar treatment appears elsewhere in this series. This layering explains a feature of the Lokpal that puzzles many readers. Its jurisdiction list is long and its offence list is short, and both facts are by design.

## The Schedule's quiet work: the Bureau, the Vigilance Commission and the sanction regime

A statute's Schedule is usually its tail; in this Act it is closer to a second engine room. Acting through the Schedule, the Lokpal Act amended the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003 by inserting Sections 8A and 8B, which empower the Vigilance Commission to inquire into references made by the Lokpal concerning Group B, Group C and Group D officials of the central government and of the corporations, companies, societies and local authorities owned or controlled by it, and to exercise for that purpose the machinery of vigilance inquiry. The amendment is what gives legal content to the screening arrangement described in the jurisdiction section below: the Lokpal's reach over the lower bureaucracy runs, in its first instance, through the vigilance body that already supervised those grades, rather than through a new parallel inquiry service. It also amended the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946, the charter of the Central Bureau of Investigation, in a respect that outlasted the politics of the moment: the committee that recommends the Bureau's Director, previously an officials-heavy body under the vigilance statute, was reconstituted as a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India or a nominated Supreme Court judge, matching the selection pattern of the Lokpal itself and operating alongside the Director's protected minimum tenure of two years under that Act. The Lokpal Act thus reached into the governance of the very agency it would later direct in referred cases, and the Bureau's director-selection committee in its present form is a direct descendant of the ombudsman statute, a fact almost never credited to it.

The third consequential change the Act worked on its neighbours concerns prosecution sanction under the Prevention of Corruption Act. The ordinary scheme of that Act requires the previous sanction of the authority competent to remove a public servant before a court takes cognisance of the principal offences, and a long line of case law and committee criticism had identified sanction as the point at which prosecutions of senior officials went to die, delayed for years or refused without reasons that a court could effectively test. For cases within its process, the Lokpal Act relocates the sanction decision to the Lokpal itself, as the machinery section below explains. The relocation matters beyond the cases the Lokpal actually files, because it established in Indian statute law a working alternative to executive sanction: an independent body reading the investigation file and deciding. Later reforms of the sanction regime in the corruption statute itself were debated against the background of that working alternative.

Understanding the Schedule also corrects a misreading of the Act's ambition. The enacted Lokpal was not designed as a self-sufficient anti-corruption state within the state, with its own police, its own vigilance service and its own courts. It was designed as a directing mind placed on top of existing machinery: the Vigilance Commission below it for the lower grades, the Bureau beside it for investigation, special courts beneath it for trial, and the departments behind it for discipline. Every strength and weakness of the institution flows from that choice. It could be created without building four new services, which is why its enactment was feasible. It also inherited the capacity, culture and vacancies of every service it directs, which is why its operation has been slower than its text promises.

## Who sits on the Lokpal: composition under Section 3

Section 3 of the Act establishes the Lokpal as a multi-member body consisting of a Chairperson and not more than eight members. The number is a ceiling, not an entitlement, and the distinction has mattered in practice, because the body has spent long periods below full strength without any provision of the Act being breached. Of the members, fifty per cent must be judicial members. A judicial member is a person who is or has been a judge of the Supreme Court or the Chief Justice of a High Court. A second representational rule runs across the whole body: not less than fifty per cent of the members must be drawn from among persons belonging to the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes, the Other Backward Classes, minorities and women. The two fifty per cent rules operate on different axes, one professional and one social, and a valid panel must satisfy both at once, which is one reason appointments, when they finally came, were made as a full slate rather than one vacancy at a time.

The Chairperson may come from either of two routes. The office is open to a person who is or has been the Chief Justice of India or a judge of the Supreme Court, and it is equally open to an eminent person, a category the Act defines by distinction in the fields the institution touches, including anti-corruption policy, public administration, vigilance, finance, law and management, with a record of a specified length of practice or service. The members who are not judicial members are drawn from the same pool of eminence. A floor of forty-five years applies to the age of the Chairperson and every member on the date of assuming office, and the Act surrounds the offices with the standard disqualifications of Indian institutional design: a member of Parliament or of a state legislature cannot hold the office, nor can a person convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, a person dismissed from government service, or a person holding an office of profit or connected with a political party.

### What qualifications does the Lokpal Act require for the Chairperson and members?

The Chairperson must be a present or former Chief Justice of India or Supreme Court judge, or an eminent person of distinction in fields such as anti-corruption policy, public administration, vigilance, finance, law or management. Half the members must be judicial members, and every appointee must be at least forty-five years old.

Tenure and terms are set by Sections 6 and 7. The Chairperson and members hold office for a term of five years from the date of entering office or until they attain the age of seventy years, whichever is earlier. The salary and allowances of the Chairperson are the same as those of the Chief Justice of India, and those of a member the same as those of a judge of the Supreme Court, a parity that places the institution at the top of the Indian official salary order and signals the rank Parliament intended it to hold relative to the officials it would investigate, most of whom are paid far below that level. On ceasing to hold office, the Chairperson and members are barred from reappointment to the same office and from a range of subsequent employments under the government, a cooling provision meant to remove any incentive to please the appointing executive in hope of a second career.

Removal is deliberately difficult and is placed by Section 37 in the hands of a process that begins outside the executive's sole discretion. The President may remove the Chairperson or a member on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, but only after the Supreme Court, on a reference made by the President, has inquired and reported that removal is warranted. The reference itself may be set in motion by the President, by a petition signed by at least one hundred members of Parliament, or by a citizen's petition that the President is satisfied should be referred. Alongside this principal route, the Act permits removal on specified objective grounds such as insolvency, engagement in paid employment outside the duties of office during the term, or infirmity of mind or body. The design copies the protective pattern used for the higher judiciary and the election machinery: easy neither to appoint around nor to dismiss at will, at least on paper.

The first appointments, when they came in March 2019, illustrate how the composition rules operate in practice. The slate appointed by warrant of 19 March 2019 filled the body completely: a Chairperson from the Supreme Court route, Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose; four judicial members, Justices Dilip Babasaheb Bhosale, Pradip Kumar Mohanty, Abhilasha Kumari and Ajay Kumar Tripathi, each a serving or former Chief Justice of a High Court or, in Justice Tripathi's case, the sitting Chief Justice of the Chhattisgarh High Court who resigned that office to take up the membership; and four non-judicial members drawn from the services the institution oversees, Dinesh Kumar Jain, a former chief secretary of Maharashtra, Archana Ramasundaram, who had headed the Sashastra Seema Bal, Mahender Singh, a former revenue service officer, and Indrajeet Prasad Gautam, a former Gujarat cadre civil servant. The slate satisfied both fifty per cent rules at once, the judicial half exactly, and the social half through the combined composition, and it showed the intended character of the body: half courtroom, half administration, chaired by a judge. It also showed the design's exposure. A body appointed as a single slate ages as a single slate: the first Chairperson's tenure ended on the age limit in 2022, other members' terms and vacancies fell due in clusters, and the institution repeatedly dropped below the strength its full bench procedures assume.

Behind the nine offices stands a secretariat the Act leaves largely to executive provision: a Secretary, directors for the two wings, and staff drawn substantially on deputation from the same services the Lokpal investigates. The dependence is structural and worth naming, because it qualifies every claim made for the institution's independence. The Chairperson and members are insulated by tenure, salary parity and a hard removal process. The registry that receives complaints, the officers who staff the Inquiry Wing's examinations, and the prosecutors who assist the Prosecution Wing arrive on deputation from ministries and agencies, serve for fixed stretches, and return. Parliamentary scrutiny of the institution has repeatedly returned to this point, seeking details of the operationalisation of the two wings years after the body was appointed, and the honest summary is that the Act created independent offices faster than it created an independent service to run them.

The Act also anticipates the institution being headless, which proved to be its most prescient provision after the vacancy clause in the selection process itself. Section 9 empowers the President, when the office of Chairperson falls vacant or its holder is absent or unable to act, to authorise one of the judicial members to act as Chairperson until a new appointment takes charge. This is the provision under which the Lokpal functioned from May 2022, when the first Chairperson's tenure ended, until March 2024, when a successor was sworn in: for that period a judicial member, Justice Pradip Kumar Mohanty, held additional charge by presidential authorisation, and the institution's work continued at reduced authority and visibility. The distinction between an acting arrangement and a vacancy is worth holding on to, because it explains why the second gap at the top, though nearly as long as it sounds, did not reproduce the first: the Act had a mechanism for an empty chair, and no mechanism, as events showed, for an unconstituted selection committee.

## How the Lokpal is chosen: committee, search panel and a fateful vacancy

Appointment is by the President, by warrant under hand and seal, on the recommendation of a selection committee whose composition Section 4 prescribes. The committee consists of the Prime Minister, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, the Chief Justice of India or a judge of the Supreme Court nominated by the Chief Justice, and an eminent jurist nominated by the President on the recommendation of the other four members. The design is the standard Indian high-institution pattern, visible in the appointments of the Central Vigilance Commissioner, the Director of the Central Bureau of Investigation through its own committee, and the Chief Information Commissioner, whose selection committee is composed on parallel lines and is examined in [the profile of the Central Information Commission](/2011/10/15/central-information-commission-explained/). In each case the pattern pursues the same bargain: the executive chairs and holds the largest voice, the opposition and the judiciary hold seats that make the choice publicly defensible, and no single participant can appoint alone.

Before the selection committee chooses, a search committee does the sifting. The selection committee constitutes a search committee of at least seven persons, drawn from fields including anti-corruption policy, public administration, vigilance, policy making, finance, law and management, and that panel prepares and recommends a list of names for each vacancy. The selection committee is not confined to the search committee's list, a flexibility the Supreme Court was later asked to examine, but the statutory sequence matters: search first, selection second, presidential warrant third. The sequence also creates two separate places where delay can lodge, in constituting the search committee and in convening the selection committee, and the history of the institution between 2014 and 2019 used both.

Buried in Section 4 is the sentence on which the five-year freeze turned, and its second half is as important as its first. The Act provided that the appointment of the Chairperson or a member would not be invalid merely by reason of a vacancy in the selection committee or a defect in its constitution. Read naturally, that is a saving clause of the ordinary kind: it prevents technical challenges to appointments once made. Read as the government of the day read it between 2014 and 2016, it did not answer the prior question whether the committee could meet and recommend at all when one of its named members, the Leader of Opposition, did not exist because no opposition party in the Sixteenth Lok Sabha satisfied the requirement of one tenth of the House's strength for recognition of its leader. The largest opposition party in that House held forty-four seats, short of the threshold applied under the Speaker's directions for recognition, so the office named in Section 4 stood unfilled. The government treated the committee as incapable of valid constitution. Its critics treated the saving clause as proof that Parliament had foreseen exactly this situation and legislated through it. The full argument, and the sequence by which it was resolved politically in 2016 and judicially in 2017, is set out in the section on the five empty years below, because it is the single most instructive episode in the statute's life: an Act whose operative chapters all commenced on a single day in January 2014, and whose first chapter could not be operated for five years because of who was missing from a committee room.

The machinery did eventually move through every stage the Act prescribes, and the sequence is worth recording as the template the statute intended. The selection committee, when it met in July 2018 with the Prime Minister in the chair, the Speaker, the Chief Justice of India and the eminent jurist Mukul Rohatgi among its participants, settled the composition of the search committee. The search committee of eight, chaired by Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai, a former judge of the Supreme Court, and including figures from banking, space administration, policing and the law, among them Arundhati Bhattacharya, A. S. Kiran Kumar and Ranjit Kumar, then prepared the panel. The selection committee's recommendations followed, and the President's warrants of appointment issued on 19 March 2019. From the first meeting of the selection committee to the warrants took eight months, unremarkable by the standards of Indian high appointments, which underlines the point the delay section makes: the process was never slow once it was allowed to start.

The design had in fact faltered once before the famous vacancy, in a rehearsal that is usually forgotten. In the first months of 2014, the outgoing government moved to constitute the institution under the Fifteenth Lok Sabha, which did have a recognised Leader of Opposition, and invited the jurist Fali S. Nariman to take the eminent jurist's seat on the selection committee. Nariman declined, in a letter that became public, on the ground that the process by which the search committee's panel was being assembled did not inspire confidence that the most competent and independent persons would be chosen, and the Leader of Opposition of the day recorded her own dissent against the manner in which the panel was being prepared. The episode matters because it shows the committee design generating friction even when every named office was filled: the eminent jurist seat, intended as the committee's conscience, is filled by nomination of the other members, and a jurist who doubts the process has no remedy except refusal, which is what the design received. The institution's birth was therefore troubled under two different governments for two different reasons, one procedural and one arithmetical, before it was troubled for five years by the second.

One feature of the selection design deserves separate notice because it shaped the politics of 2018. After the 2016 amendment admitted the leader of the single largest opposition party to the committee in place of a recognised Leader of Opposition, the leader of that party in the Lok Sabha, Mallikarjun Kharge, was invited to the selection committee's meetings as a special invitee rather than as a member of right, and declined to attend on the stated ground that a special invitee could neither participate fully nor record dissent as a member could. The episode is a small window into a large theme of this statute: at every stage, the contest was not over whether the institution should exist, which no participant disputed in public, but over the procedural terms on which it would come into existence, which determined whose fingerprints the first appointments would bear.

## The jurisdiction map: who the Lokpal can inquire against

Section 14 is the heart of the Act, and it repays slow reading, because its list is longer and its provisos are more consequential than the headlines about the Prime Minister suggest. Subject to the rest of the Act, the Lokpal may inquire or cause an inquiry into any allegation of corruption made in a complaint against nine classes of person. The classes begin at the top of the state: any person who is or has been Prime Minister, with the provisos analysed in the next section; any person who is or has been a Minister of the Union; and any person who is or has been a member of either House of Parliament. They continue through the official hierarchy: Group A and Group B officers of the central services, as those categories are defined by reference to the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, and Group C and Group D officials, whose complaints the Act routes through a preliminary screening connected with the Central Vigilance Commission, so that the ombudsman for the whole Union is not consumed by the service grievances of the lowest grades dressed as corruption complaints. They extend beyond government employment to the officeholders of the para-state: chairpersons, members, officers and employees of any board, corporation, authority, company, society, trust or autonomous body established by an Act of Parliament or wholly or partly financed or controlled by the Central Government.

The last two classes carry the Act into civil society, and they are drawn by money rather than by function. The directors, managers, secretaries and other officers of a society, association or trust that is wholly or partly financed by the government fall within jurisdiction where the body's annual income exceeds an amount the Central Government specifies by notification. And the officers of a society, association or trust that receives donations from a foreign source under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010 in excess of ten lakh rupees in a year fall within jurisdiction regardless of government funding. The ten lakh figure is the one hard threshold written into the section itself, and it is worth pausing on what it does. A charitable trust, a research institute, a religious endowment's administrative office or a campaign organisation crosses into the Lokpal's reach not because the state created it or controls it, but because foreign money above a modest line flows through it, on the theory that foreign-funded entities handling public-facing work should answer for corrupt handling of funds in the same forum as officials. Whether that theory justifies the reach is contested; that the reach exists is a fact of the text that summaries of the Act routinely omit.

Three boundaries complete the map. The first is federal: where an officer within the central classes is serving in connection with the affairs of a state, the Lokpal and its wings may exercise jurisdiction only after obtaining the consent of the state government concerned, a proviso that respects the constitutional position that state services answer primarily to state institutions. The second is parliamentary: Section 14 expressly preserves Article 105 of the Constitution, so nothing a member of Parliament says, and no vote a member gives, in Parliament or its committees can be the subject of a Lokpal inquiry. Whether a bribe taken in connection with such a vote stands on the same protected ground was for a generation the most notorious open question in this corner of the law. A Constitution Bench in P. V. Narasimha Rao v. State in 1998 had held that members who took bribes and voted were shielded by Article 105(2) from prosecution for the bribe, a holding that produced the spectacle of the bribe-giver being prosecutable and the bribe-taking voter immune. A seven-judge bench in Sita Soren v. Union of India in 2024 overruled that position, holding that bribery is not part of the legislative function the privilege exists to protect and that a member who accepts a bribe completes the offence independently of whether the vote follows. For the Lokpal the consequence is direct: its jurisdiction over members of Parliament, exercised consistently with Article 105 as the 2024 judgment reads it, reaches the corrupt bargain and not the legislative act, which is the line the statute's proviso was always gesturing toward. The third is derivative: the Lokpal may also inquire into any other person involved in abetting, giving or taking a bribe, or conspiring, in relation to an allegation against a person within the nine classes, so the private intermediary who carries the money is reachable through the official who received it. The armed forces stand outside the map altogether: the Act's classes are drawn from the civil public services and the bodies described, and personnel of the Army, the Navy and the Air Force answer for corruption through their own service law machinery rather than before the Lokpal. The judiciary likewise does not appear in Section 14 at all, a silence the movement's draft had proposed to fill and the enacted text deliberately kept.

Two further features of Section 14 reward attention because examination papers and practitioners both test them. The first is its retrospective reach: every class is defined to include a person who is or has been in the position, so retirement, resignation, defeat at an election or the end of a ministry does not remove a person from jurisdiction for conduct during the tenure. The anti-corruption law has always worked this way in substance, since the offences attach to the public servant at the time of the act, but the Lokpal's text makes the point expressly for the highest offices: a former Prime Minister and a former minister are named in terms, and the only clock that runs against the complainant is the seven-year limitation in Section 53. The second is the deeming device in the Explanation to the section: the officeholders of the funded and foreign-funded bodies in the last classes are deemed to be public servants under the Prevention of Corruption Act for the purposes of the Lokpal's inquiry, which is what makes the substantive offences available against persons who are not public servants under any other law. A trustee of a foreign-funded trust is, for this statute's purposes, prosecuted as if a government official, a considerable legal fiction whose justification is the public character of the funds and whose limits have yet to be fully tested in the courts.

One more limit in the section's Explanation is easily missed and frequently decisive: a complaint under the Act shall relate only to a period during which the public servant was holding or serving in the capacity complained of. The provision ties the inquiry to the tenure in which the alleged act occurred, so conduct in a private interval between two public postings, or in an earlier office whose affairs belong to a different government, is not swept into jurisdiction merely because the person later held a covered office. Read with the seven-year limitation, the Explanation gives Section 14 a defined temporal shape as well as a personal one: right person, right posting, right period.

A worked example fixes the consent proviso better than the abstract statement. An officer of the Indian Administrative Service belongs to a service whose members appear in the central classes, and a complaint about her conduct while serving as a district magistrate in a state concerns the affairs of that state, not of the Union. The Lokpal may not proceed on that complaint without the consent of the state government concerned, even though the same officer, on deputation to a central ministry the following year, would fall squarely within jurisdiction for conduct in that posting. The line the Act draws is therefore not between persons but between postings, between the affairs of the Union and the affairs of a state, and a complaint that ignores the line is liable to stall at the threshold however grave its content. Federalism in this statute is not a preamble value; it is a jurisdictional switch wired into the first section of the inquiry chapter.

## The Prime Minister question: inclusion with the doors half closed

No provision of the Act has been argued about more, in proportion to its use, than the first clause of Section 14. The movement demanded the Prime Minister's inclusion as the test of the statute's sincerity: an ombudsman who cannot look at the head of government, on this view, is an ombudsman for clerks. The government's negotiators resisted on the ground that a sitting Prime Minister exposed to inquiry by an appointed body could be destabilised by motivated complaints, with consequences for governance and for India's external position. The enacted text is a structured compromise between those positions, and it should be read as three distinct decisions rather than one.

The first decision is inclusion itself: a person who is or has been Prime Minister is within jurisdiction, in terms, for allegations of corruption. The second decision is a subject-matter exclusion: the Lokpal shall not inquire into an allegation against the Prime Minister in so far as it relates to international relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy and space. The five fields are the classic reserved domains of the head of government, and the exclusion is of allegations relating to them, not of the officeholder generally. The third decision is procedural: even for an allegation outside the excluded fields, an inquiry may begin only if a full bench of the Lokpal, consisting of its Chairperson and all members, considers the initiation of the inquiry and at least two thirds of the members approve it; the inquiry is then held in camera, and if the Lokpal concludes that the complaint deserves dismissal, the records of the inquiry are not published or made available to anyone. Inclusion, exclusion and filtration are separate levers, and most public argument about the provision seizes one and ignores the other two.

### Why does an allegation against the Prime Minister follow a different track under the Lokpal Act?

Because Parliament combined three choices: the Prime Minister is inside jurisdiction, allegations touching international relations, security, public order, atomic energy or space are outside it, and any other inquiry needs a full bench decision with two thirds of all members approving, held in camera, so that such a complaint is possible but cannot become a public spectacle.

The case against the compromise, stated at its strongest, is that the filtration swallows the inclusion. The excluded fields are elastic: a defence procurement allegation can be characterised as relating to external security, a spectrum or mining allegation can be pulled toward atomic energy or space, and the characterisation decision is itself taken behind the closed door the provision mandates. The two-thirds-of-the-full-bench threshold means that a body sitting below strength, as the Lokpal has for much of its life, may be unable to assemble the required bench at all, converting a safeguard into a practical immunity whenever vacancies persist. And the secrecy rule, defensible for a dismissed complaint, also means that a meritorious inquiry's early stages leave no public trace, so the citizen who filed cannot know whether the door closed on the merits or on the arithmetic. Critics who make this argument, among them lawyers associated with the 2011 drafting effort such as Prashant Bhushan, conclude that the Prime Minister's inclusion is formal rather than real.

The case for the compromise, stated at its strongest, begins with the comparison the critics rarely make. Ombudsman statutes in most parliamentary systems either exclude the head of government entirely or confine the office to maladministration in departments rather than personal corruption by the head. The Indian Act does neither: it names the Prime Minister first in its jurisdiction list and then regulates the manner of proceeding. The excluded fields track subjects on which an inquiry's existence, before any finding, could itself damage the state, and the in camera rule with sealed records protects a dismissed complaint from becoming a permanent public cloud, which is a protection any officeholder against whom a false complaint is filed would need. The full bench requirement answers a genuine design problem: a decision to inquire into a Prime Minister should not be one member's or one bench's call, because its institutional consequences are of a different order from an inquiry into a joint secretary. On this view, advanced by the select committee's majority reasoning when the provision was settled, the question is not whether the track is different but whether a different track is the price of inclusion at all, and the parliamentary record suggests it was: without the safeguard package, the clause naming the Prime Minister would not have survived the passage process.

The characterisation problem can be made concrete. Consider an allegation that a Prime Minister took an undue advantage in the award of a defence procurement contract. The contract is a commercial transaction, and on one reading the allegation relates to bribery in procurement, a subject squarely within the Lokpal's ordinary reach. On another reading, a defence contract relates to external security by its subject matter, and the exclusion takes the allegation out at the threshold. The Act supplies no test for choosing between the readings, no authority outside the Lokpal to review the choice, and no public proceeding in which the choice would be defended, because the threshold decision itself is taken in camera. The excluded fields are therefore not merely a list; they are a discretion, exercised invisibly, over the most consequential complaints the institution can receive. A reader evaluating the provision should weigh that discretion, and not only the list, because the list is narrow and the discretion is wide.

The honest verdict is that both arguments describe real features of the same text, and which one dominates depends on a variable the text does not control: whether the Lokpal sits at full strength with members willing to convene the full bench. A complete body makes the safeguard a filter; an incomplete body makes it a wall. That dependence, jurisdiction varying with the accident of vacancies, is a design weakness whichever side of the argument a reader takes, and it connects the Prime Minister question back to the appointment question that dominates this statute's history.

## The machinery of a case: from complaint to special court

The Lokpal works only when moved. The Act gives it no power to begin an inquiry on its own motion: every proceeding starts with a complaint, in the form prescribed, alleging corruption against a person within Section 14. The formality is not decorative. The complaint rules framed under the Act in 2020 prescribe the format, the identity particulars and an affidavit in support, and the Lokpal's handling of complaints that arrive outside that form became, as the record section shows, the single largest determinant of what the institution has visibly done. A complaint that survives the threshold enters the procedure of Section 20, which is built as a sequence of screens, each with a decision and each decision narrowing what continues.

The first screen is the preliminary inquiry. The Lokpal refers the complaint to its Inquiry Wing, the body constituted under Section 11 and headed by a Director of Inquiry, which inquires into the allegation and reports, the Act's scheme contemplating a report within sixty days of reference. For complaints against Group A and Group B officers, the Lokpal may instead or in addition draw on the Central Vigilance Commission's machinery, and for Group C and Group D officials the reference runs to that Commission under the provisions the Schedule inserted into the vigilance statute. The purpose of the screen is to separate allegations with substance from grievances, service disputes and vendettas wearing the clothes of corruption, before the coercive apparatus of investigation is set in motion and before reputations are engaged. On receiving the inquiry report, and after giving the public servant an opportunity to be heard at the stage the Act prescribes, the Lokpal has three courses open: it may order an investigation, it may direct departmental proceedings against the servant through the competent authority, or it may close the case, and where it finds the complaint false it may proceed against the complainant.

### What happens after a complaint reaches the Lokpal?

It passes through screens. The Inquiry Wing conducts a preliminary inquiry and reports; the Lokpal then decides whether to order an investigation by an agency, direct departmental proceedings, or close the case. An investigation is to be completed within six months, and a surviving case ends as a chargesheet before a special court, not as a punishment the Lokpal imposes.

Investigation, where ordered, is carried out by an agency, which may be the Central Bureau of Investigation, and here the Act makes one of its most consequential interventions. By Section 25, the Lokpal exercises superintendence over, and may give direction to, any investigating agency, including the Bureau, in respect of cases the Lokpal has referred. Officers of the Bureau engaged on a referred case cannot be transferred without the Lokpal's approval, and the Bureau's Directorate of Prosecution works under arrangements the Act and its Schedule adjusted so that prosecutions in referred cases are not conducted as ordinary government briefs. This is superintendence over referred cases, not command of the Bureau: the Lokpal does not appoint its director, does not direct its other cases, and does not absorb it, which is why the accurate answer to the popular question whether the Lokpal controls the Bureau is that it controls the Bureau's handling of its own referrals and nothing more. The movement's draft had proposed merging the Bureau's anti-corruption wing outright; Parliament chose referral superintendence instead, and the difference between the two designs is the difference between an ombudsman with a police force and an ombudsman with a right to direct one case by case.

The prosecution stage belongs to the Prosecution Wing, constituted under Section 12 and headed by a Director of Prosecution, which files the chargesheet before a special court. Special courts are constituted under the Act on the Lokpal's recommendation as to their number, and the trial is to be completed within one year of the filing of the case, extendable by three months at a time for reasons recorded, subject to an outer limit of two years. Two further features of the machinery deserve emphasis because they remove the classic veto points of Indian corruption prosecutions. The first is sanction: where the Lokpal has ordered investigation and its wing files the case, the sanction to prosecute that the Prevention of Corruption Act would otherwise demand from the government or the competent authority is the Lokpal's own to give, so the executive whose members and officials may be accused does not hold a permission slip over the proceedings, and the offences themselves, bribery, criminal misconduct and the rest, are those defined by [the Prevention of Corruption Act 1988](/2011/12/15/prevention-of-corruption-act-1988/). The second is interim protection of the alleged proceeds: the Act provides for attachment and confiscation of property acquired by corrupt means, with provisional attachment possible while prosecution is pending, so that a long trial does not itself become the means of dissipating what the trial is about.

The citizen who sets this machinery in motion typically knows something the state does not, and the Act's complaint gate is where the separate question of protecting that citizen arises, a question Parliament addressed in a companion statute treated in [the account of the Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014](/2012/01/01/whistle-blowers-protection-act-2014/). Within the Lokpal Act itself, the complainant's position is double-edged by design: the Act protects the process against abuse by punishing a false and frivolous complaint with imprisonment of up to one year and a fine of up to one lakh rupees, and it equips the person complained against with rights that accused officials under departmental regimes often lack, including legal assistance at the Lokpal's instance where requested, under Section 55. The outer boundary of the whole procedure is Section 53: the Lokpal shall not inquire or investigate into a complaint made after seven years from the date on which the offence is alleged to have been committed. Limitation periods and forum rules are unforgiving in this field, and the general position described in this article is information about the statute's design, not advice on any individual complaint, for which the prescribed form, the limitation clock and the facts of the particular case would all need separate attention.

The departmental track, which the machinery produces more often than the criminal one, deserves its own statement because it is where most substantiated complaints will end. Where the preliminary inquiry discloses misconduct that warrants discipline rather than, or alongside, prosecution, the Lokpal directs the competent authority to initiate departmental proceedings, and the authority works from the inquiry record the Lokpal's process has assembled, which is a better evidentiary foundation than most departmental proceedings begin with. The Lokpal cannot dictate the penalty, and the authority's decision remains subject to the service law and the tribunal system in the ordinary way. The design choice is defensible: dismissal and reduction in rank are service punishments embedded in cadre management, and Parliament left them there. But it completes the pattern this article keeps meeting. At every stage after the threshold, the Lokpal's power is the power to produce a record and a direction that another institution must honour, and the system's throughput is set by the slowest of the institutions in the chain. A special court trial, once a chargesheet is filed, is an ordinary criminal trial with an extraordinary timetable written over it, and appeals lie to the High Court as in any prosecution under the corruption statute; the Lokpal's role after filing is that of a complainant-institution watching its case proceed through courts it does not control, which is why the one-year trial direction, extendable to two, has the character of an aspiration addressed to the judiciary rather than a command the Lokpal can enforce.

Between inquiry and trial the Act arms the Lokpal with a set of mid-course powers that are easy to overlook and that matter in live cases. While inquiring, it exercises the powers of a civil court for summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses, requiring the discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavit and requisitioning public records, so a recalcitrant department cannot defeat an inquiry by silence. It may recommend the transfer or suspension of a public servant connected with an allegation under inquiry, addressing the classic problem of the accused official supervising the witnesses against him. And its directions to an investigating agency in a referred case bind that agency in the conduct of that case, including the bar on transferring the investigating officers without approval, which answers the equally classic problem of an investigation dissolved by reshuffle the moment it approaches a finding. None of these powers punishes anyone. All of them remove the administrative vetoes by which corruption proceedings were traditionally strangled before a court ever saw them, and together they describe the institution Parliament actually built: not a court, but a device for making sure that the ordinary courts and disciplinary authorities receive cases they cannot quietly be prevented from receiving.

Two absences in the machinery explain more about the institution's output than any presence. The Lokpal has no contempt-like power to compel the government to fill its own vacancies, no power to punish the official it finds corrupt, and no appellate shortcut around the special court's trial. Its coercive instruments during inquiry are those of a civil court for summoning witnesses and requiring documents, real but ordinary powers. Everything else it does by direction, recommendation and report. A reader who holds that architecture in mind will not be surprised by the record examined below, and will also see why the parliamentary standing committee that later reviewed the institution urged it to behave as an enabler of complaints rather than a gatekeeper of forms.

## The complaint in practice: form as the first jurisdiction

Because the Lokpal cannot move on its own, the complaint is not a preliminary to its jurisdiction; in a practical sense it is its jurisdiction. The Lokpal (Complaint) Rules, 2020, notified in March of that year, prescribe the form in which a complaint must be made: the complainant's identity and particulars, the public servant complained against, the allegation set out with supporting documents, and an affidavit affirming the complaint's truth, with the false complaint penalty of the Act drawn to the complainant's attention on the face of the process. Complaints may be filed physically or through the Lokpal's online facility, and the institution displays the status of complaints on its website as Section 20 requires. The design choices embedded in the form are each defensible in isolation. Identity deters anonymous vendettas and lets the institution call for clarification. The affidavit converts gossip into testimony and exposes the complainant to the penalty for falsehood. The prescribed format lets a small registry sort a large inflow.

Their combined effect, however, is a threshold higher than the one the Act's text describes, and the record section below shows that the threshold, not the inquiry, is where most complaints end. Three tensions run through the complaint regime and any reform debate has to choose between them consciously. The first is between access and filtration: every formality that keeps out a frivolous complaint also keeps out a frightened one, and the class of persons with first-hand knowledge of high-level corruption overlaps heavily with the class of persons with reason to fear signing affidavits. The second is between confidentiality and confidence: the proceedings that follow a complaint are substantially internal until a chargesheet is filed, which protects the wrongly accused and simultaneously makes the institution's work invisible to the public asked to trust it. The third is between the complaint's form and its substance: the parliamentary standing committee's recommendation that genuine complaints not be rejected on format alone, and the Lokpal's own order of January 2023 that complaints outside the prescribed form would not be entertained at any level, state the two poles of a disagreement the statute does not resolve, because it prescribes that a form exist and leaves the strictness of its enforcement to the institution. Form, in this Act, is where discretion lives.

The Act is also, read as a whole, an institution of clocks, and noticing them together explains both its promise and its record. One year for the states to create Lokayuktas. Thirty days, as enacted, for a public servant's first asset declaration. Sixty days for the Inquiry Wing's preliminary report. Six months for an investigation, extendable for reasons recorded. One year for a special court trial, extendable in steps to two. Seven years as the outer limitation for any complaint. An annual report to the President under Section 48, laid before Parliament, with the status of complaints also displayed publicly under Section 20. The drafters plainly believed that the pathology of Indian anti-corruption process was delay, and they legislated deadlines against it at every stage they could reach. The stage they could not reach by deadline was their own institution's birth, because no clock in the Act ran against the selection committee: the Act set no period within which the committee must meet, the search committee must be constituted, or the first appointments must be made. Every clock in the statute governs the Lokpal's treatment of others. None governed the state's treatment of the Lokpal, and the five empty years flowed through exactly that unguarded interval.

## Section 44 and the politics of asset declarations

One chapter of the Act reaches past the Lokpal's own proceedings and imposes a duty on every public servant in the country, whether or not the Lokpal ever looks at them. The duty's personal reach follows the Act's adopted definition of public servant, so it runs from the Prime Minister and the Union ministry through members of Parliament and the organised services down to the lowest group of central officials, together with the office-bearers of the funded bodies Section 14 sweeps in; the declaration regime is the one part of the Act that touches the whole public service every year rather than the few hundred persons a complaint names. Section 44, as enacted, required every public servant to declare assets and liabilities: on entering office, a declaration within thirty days of making oath or subscribing affirmation, covering the assets and liabilities of the servant, the spouse and dependent children; an annual return thereafter, filed by 31 July each year for the position as on 31 March; and publication of the statements by the competent authority of each ministry or department on its website by 31 August. The design converted asset declaration from an internal service record, which conduct rules had long required in confidence, into a public disclosure regime attached to the anti-corruption statute, on the reasoning that unexplained wealth is the visible residue of invisible corruption and that sunlight on the residue deters the act.

The provision immediately collided with administrative reality. The class of public servants under the Act runs to millions of officials across groups, together with the office-bearers of funded bodies, and the rules for the form of declaration, the competent authorities and the publication mechanics lagged the duty. Deadlines were extended by rule and by amendment of the rules repeatedly through 2014, 2015 and 2016, while the government told Parliament that the framework for filing needed more time and the declaration obligation hung in a state of announced commencement and practical deferral. The episode is a small-scale rehearsal of the statute's central theme: a duty enacted in mandatory words, dependent for its operation on subordinate machinery that the enacting moment had not built.

The publication element drew the sharpest argument, and it is the part of the original design that has worn least well. Section 44 as enacted did not merely require declaration to the competent authority; it required the statements, covering the servant, spouse and dependent children, to be published on the ministry's website. Service associations objected that publishing the assets of millions of junior officials, with their family details, exposed them to extortion, targeting and identity fraud out of all proportion to any anti-corruption gain at their level, and that the officers whose wealth actually matters, the senior grades, were already covered by conduct-rule property returns available through information requests. Transparency advocates answered that a declaration nobody can see is a declaration nobody verifies, and that the history of property returns in the services is a history of forms filed and never read. The parliamentary standing committee that examined the wider amendment bill introduced in December 2014, which contained the Leader of Opposition substitution among a broader set of changes and reported the following year, engaged with this tension directly; the 2016 Act, when it came, was narrower than that bill and settled the question by removing the statutory scheme's fixed features rather than by choosing between the arguments in terms.

The 2016 amendment resolved the immediate standoff by changing the character of the duty. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas (Amendment) Act, 2016, passed by Parliament in July 2016 and assented to in August 2016, substituted the operative part of Section 44 so that a public servant makes the declaration of assets and liabilities in the form and manner as may be prescribed, deleting the fixed thirty-day timeline from the statute and relocating the detail to rules. The same amendment dealt with the trustees and office-bearers of non-governmental organisations receiving government funds above one crore rupees or foreign funding above ten lakh rupees, whose own declarations the original scheme had swept in. Critics of the amendment, including voices from the 2011 movement, read it as the first dilution of the Act: a hard statutory deadline replaced by executive prescription is a duty whose teeth the executive controls. The government's defence was administrative: a single rigid timeline for millions of declarants, in a form not yet settled, guaranteed mass technical default, and prescription by rule is how every comparable filing regime in Indian law is in fact operated. Both readings are available on the text; what is not disputable is the direction of travel, from statutory specificity toward delegated flexibility, which recurs across this Act's history.

For the researcher, the declarations chapter also supplies the natural documentary trail of the whole subject: the published statements of ministers and senior officials are the raw material from which disproportionate-assets analysis begins, and a reader working through a ministry's published returns will find them easier to handle in bulk with [the ReportMedic office file viewer for spreadsheet and document files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which opens the office formats in which such statements are circulated without altering the underlying record.

## Section 63 and the states that did not hurry

The Act's Part III consists of one section, and its brevity is the point. Section 63 provides that every state shall establish a body to be known as the Lokayukta for the state, if not already established, by a law made by the state legislature, to deal with complaints relating to corruption against certain public functionaries, within a period of one year from the date of commencement of the Act. The deadline therefore expired in January 2015. The section prescribes no model structure, no composition, no powers and no consequence for default. It commands a result and leaves every element of the result, including whether the result is worth having, to the legislature being commanded.

The record that followed is the cleanest natural experiment in the Act. States that already had Lokayukta statutes, some of them decades old, treated the section as satisfied by existence. Maharashtra, whose Lokayukta dates to 1971, needed to do nothing. Karnataka's institution showed both poles of the state experience: under Justice N. Santosh Hegde, its report on illegal mining in 2011 contributed to the resignation of a sitting chief minister, demonstrating what a staffed and willing state ombudsman can do, and the same state's legislature later carved the institution's investigative machinery away by creating a separate anti-corruption bureau, a dilution substantially reversed only after further political and judicial contest years later. Other states enacted statutes of widely varying strength, appointed late, left the office vacant for long stretches, or in a few cases did not establish a functioning body at all within any period the section could recognise. Odisha was among the first to enact a fresh Lokayukta statute after the central Act, in 2014, yet years passed before its institution was fully manned. Kerala moved in the opposite direction, amending its older Lokayukta Act in 2022 in ways its critics, including former holders of the office, described as converting the body's findings from binding outcomes into recommendations the government could set aside, a reminder that a state Lokayukta's strength is never settled once but is renegotiated by every legislature that inherits it. No consequence followed for any of them. There was no sanction to apply, no default power in the Centre to constitute a state Lokayukta, and no court direction that could manufacture a state institution out of a one-line section; the deadline was ignored, in the brief's phrase, without consequence, because the Act had attached none.

### Why did the one-year deadline for state Lokayuktas fail to produce uniform state bodies?

Because Section 63 is a command with no machinery. It fixed one year but prescribed no model law, no composition, no powers and no penalty for default, and the Centre cannot constitute a state institution in a state's place. States with older statutes counted existence as compliance; others legislated late, weakly or not at all, at no cost.

The one-line section was not the original design, and its history explains its shape. The bill as passed by the Lok Sabha in December 2011 carried a full Part III prescribing the establishment of Lokayuktas with a model structure, on the theory that a national anti-corruption settlement should be uniform. State governments across the political spectrum objected, in the consultation processes and in Parliament, that a central statute dictating the composition and powers of a state institution trenched on the federal distribution of powers and on the states' authority over their own services, and several chief ministers made the objection a condition of their parties' support. In the select committee's revision and the final passage, the model part was withdrawn and replaced by the single obligation of Section 63: establish a Lokayukta of your own design within a year. The compromise purchased the Act's passage at the price of the states chapter's content, and the uneven map that followed is the price still being paid. It is a recurring pattern in Indian framework legislation, visible wherever Parliament legislates a national institution that depends on state counterparts: the Centre can command uniformity of existence, or it can respect diversity of design, and when it attempts the first and retreats to the second mid-passage, it typically ends with neither, an obligation of existence that several states satisfy in name and a diversity of design that includes the design of weakness.

The federal logic underneath deserves a fair statement. Police and public order are state subjects, state services answer to state governments, and a central statute dictating the composition and powers of a state ombudsman in detail would have raised genuine questions about the limits of Parliament's competence and the propriety of the design, quite apart from the politics of chief ministers legislating the terms of their own investigation. The state Lokayukta landscape that resulted, strong in a few states, dormant in many, absent in effect in some, is therefore not simply disobedience. It is what a one-section federal instruction predictably produces when the instructed parties are the very officeholders the instruction targets. The drafting lesson pairs with the lesson of the selection committee: in this Act, the provisions that failed did so at exactly the points where the text relied on the future cooperation of actors whose interests the provision constrained.

## Five empty years: how a missing officeholder froze a statute

The Act commenced on 16 January 2014. The first Chairperson of the Lokpal was appointed on 19 March 2019. Between those dates the institution existed in law and nowhere else, and the mechanism of the freeze deserves exact statement, because it is the article's central exhibit and because both partisan accounts of it are incomplete in instructive ways.

The general election of 2014 produced a Lok Sabha in which no opposition party won the one tenth of the total strength of the House that the Speaker's directions require for recognition of a Leader of Opposition. The largest opposition party, the Indian National Congress, held forty-four seats. Section 4 of the Act named the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha as a member of the selection committee. There was no such person. The government's stated position, maintained in Parliament and in court through the Attorney General, was that the selection committee could not be validly constituted without that member, that an amendment to substitute the leader of the single largest opposition party was before Parliament, and that appointments must await the amendment. On that position, the search committee was not constituted either, and the institution's constitution was suspended in fact while remaining complete in law.

The counter-position, pressed by the petitioners in the litigation described below and by the opposition parties, rested on three planks. The first was the Act's own saving provision: Parliament had expressly provided that an appointment would not be invalid merely because of a vacancy in the selection committee, which showed the statute contemplating and legislating through exactly this gap. The second was comparative: other appointment statutes had the same problem and had been amended to solve it, with the selection committees for the Central Vigilance Commissioner and the chief of the Central Bureau of Investigation substituting the leader of the largest opposition party, and the government had carried those substitutions through in 2014 without difficulty where it wished to appoint. The third was temporal: the amendment said to be indispensable was not passed until July 2016, two and a half years after commencement, and after it removed the obstacle entirely, a further two years and eight months passed before any appointment was made, during which the search committee was constituted only in September 2018. A justification that expires in 2016 cannot explain a vacancy that ends in 2019.

Both accounts capture part of the record, and the article's stance charter requires each at its strongest before any verdict. The government's reading of the text was not frivolous. Section 4 names an office, not a function: it does not say the leader of the largest opposition party, or a member nominated by the opposition benches, and a committee asked to recommend the nation's anti-corruption chief is not obviously entitled to improvise its own composition when a named member is missing, particularly where the missing member is the one whose presence legitimises the choice across the aisle. The saving clause, on the government's reading, protected appointments against later technical challenge; it did not command a depleted committee to proceed. Governments are also entitled to note that the Sixteenth Lok Sabha's opposition arithmetic was unusual, and that the drafters of 2013, legislating in a House that had a recognised Leader of Opposition, did not address a contingency that had last arisen decades earlier.

The critics' reading is stronger on the sequence, and the sequence is where the argument is won. A government that believed in good faith that only an amendment could unlock the committee controlled the parliamentary timetable throughout: it could have brought the amendment in 2014, as it did for the vigilance and investigation chiefs, and instead the amendment arrived in mid-2016 bundled with the asset declaration changes and passed both Houses within days once introduced, which suggests the obstacle was never legislative capacity. After the amendment, the remaining steps, constituting a search committee and convening the selection committee, were executive acts requiring no one's permission, yet the search committee under Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai was constituted only on 27 September 2018, more than four and a half years after commencement, and the selection process then moved under the Supreme Court's timetable rather than the government's. The verdict the record supports is therefore narrow and specific: the textual difficulty of 2014 was real, the delay of 2014 to 2016 is partly explicable by it, and the delay of 2016 to 2019 is not explained by it at all. The institution was not kept dormant by a drafting accident alone; the accident supplied the reason, and the reason outlived its own repair by nearly three years.

### Did the Act itself allow the selection committee to act despite the vacancy?

The Act named an office rather than a function, and in the Sixteenth Lok Sabha no opposition party met the one tenth strength required for its leader's recognition, so the named member did not exist. The government read that absence as disabling the committee; the saving clause pointed the other way, and the Supreme Court held the statute workable.

The end of the freeze came in stages. The amendment of 2016 substituted the leader of the single largest opposition party where no recognised Leader of Opposition exists, mirroring the solution already used for other appointment committees. The Supreme Court's judgment of April 2017, analysed in the next section, destroyed the legal basis of further waiting by holding the unamended Act workable. In July 2018 the Court, hearing the continuing proceedings, rejected the government's account of its search committee preparations as wholly unsatisfactory and demanded better. The search committee was constituted that September, with eight members under Justice Desai's chairpersonship, and in January 2019 the Court fixed the end of February 2019 for the committee to recommend its panel. The selection committee met, the President appointed the Chairperson and eight members, four of them judicial, by warrant dated 19 March 2019, and Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, a former judge of the Supreme Court then serving on the National Human Rights Commission, took the oath as the first Chairperson of the Lokpal on 23 March 2019, with the members assuming charge on 27 March 2019. Five years, two months and three days after commencement, the Act acquired its institution.

## What the Supreme Court decided: Common Cause and the workable Act

The judicial anchor of the statute is the judgment of the Supreme Court in Common Cause v. Union of India, delivered on 27 April 2017 by a bench of Justices Ranjan Gogoi and Navin Sinha. The proceedings had begun as a challenge to the search committee rules of 2014, in particular the rules that permitted the selection committee to consider, for appointment, persons outside the panel the search committee recommended, which the petitioner, a registered society long associated with public interest litigation, contended allowed the selection committee to range beyond the search committee's panel in ways the parent Act did not permit and so diluted the one screening stage the Act interposed between the executive and the appointment. The rules question and the delay question were the same question at different depths: whether the appointment process the Act designed was a discipline on the executive or a form the executive could complete at its own pace and on its own terms. By the time of judgment the case had become the vehicle for the larger question the freeze presented: whether the executive could lawfully hold an enacted statute in suspension because an amendment to it was pending in Parliament.

The Court's answer was unambiguous and is the proposition for which the case is authority. The Act as it stood was, in the Court's words, an eminently workable piece of legislation, and there was no justification to keep its enforcement under suspension until the proposed amendments were carried out. Proposed amendments, and the views of parliamentary committees upon them, were attempts at streamlining; they were not legal bars to enforcement of the law already in force. On the vacancy point the Court read the Act against the government: the appointment of the Chairperson or a member would not be invalid merely because of a vacancy in the selection committee, and the selection machinery was not paralysed by the absence of a recognised Leader of Opposition. The judgment closed by quoting Justice Krishna Iyer's observation, in the Special Courts Bill reference of 1978, on the pathology of a public law that lacks an unmincing ombudsman with power to act against those in power, a quotation that placed the new institution in a lineage of judicial impatience with exactly the gap the Lokpal was built to fill.

The judgment's limits matter as much as its holding, because they explain the two further years of delay. The Court declared the law; it did not appoint the Lokpal, fix the search committee's composition itself, or set a deadline in the 2017 judgment for the appointment to be made. The declaration removed the government's legal excuse while leaving the executive's hands on every lever of the process, and the levers moved slowly: the search committee took another seventeen months to constitute, and only the Court's continuing supervision in the same proceedings, including its January 2019 direction fixing the end of February for the search committee's panel, converted the declaration into a timetable. The episode is a compact lesson in the difference between a court holding a statute workable and a statute working. Common Cause settled the law in April 2017. The institution arrived in March 2019. Everything between those dates was administration.

## The drafting lesson: name the function, not the office

The Lokpal's freeze is usually told as a political story, but it is at bottom a drafting story, and its lesson is available to any legislature composing an appointment committee. India composes such committees constantly, for the Vigilance Commissioner, the Bureau's Director, the Chief Information Commissioner, the chairperson of the National Human Rights Commission and others, and the formulae differ in one small respect that decides everything in a hung or lopsided House. The resilient formula describes the opposition seat functionally, as the leader of the single largest opposition party, or supplies an express substitute when no recognised Leader of Opposition exists. The fragile formula names the office of Leader of Opposition and stops. The vigilance and Bureau statutes originally used forms of words that required the same repair, and when the Sixteenth Lok Sabha produced no recognised Leader of Opposition, the executive amended those statutes in 2014 to substitute the largest party's leader, promptly and without controversy, because appointments to those bodies were wanted. The Lokpal Act, enacted months earlier with the fragile formula, received the same repair only in 2016. The contrast within a single government's conduct across four statutes in the same Parliament is the cleanest evidence that the formula, not the concept, was the defect, and that the defect's cost depended entirely on how urgently the appointing authority wanted the institution filled.

There is a second, quieter drafting lesson in the Act's own saving clause. The clause providing that an appointment is not invalid for a vacancy in the committee is standard furniture in Indian institutional statutes, and its function, as the government argued, is protective: it insulates completed appointments from technical attack. But drafters who include such a clause should be understood to have contemplated the vacancy, and the better drafting practice, visible in the 2016 amendment itself, is to say expressly both things: who sits in the seat when the named office is empty, and that the committee may act meanwhile. The Supreme Court in Common Cause in substance read the Act to that effect, holding the workable statute to a workable meaning, but a court asked to supply by interpretation what an amendment later supplied by legislation is a sign of drafting debt, and the interest on this statute's debt was five years.

For the legislator and the legislative staffer, who are among this article's intended readers, the generalisable rule is therefore precise. Every appointment-dependent institution should be drafted on the assumption that any named office on its committee may be vacant at the moment of first constitution, because first constitution is exactly when the institution has no incumbents, no conventions and no defenders inside the system. A statute that survives its own birth only if the electoral arithmetic cooperates has not been commenced by its commencement notification; it has been commenced by luck. The Lokpal Act was commenced by litigation instead, and the difference between those two modes of birth marked the institution's authority from its first day.

## The record since 2019: complaints, screens and a second empty chair

An honest account of the Lokpal's output must begin by separating three numbers that public discussion merges: complaints received, inquiries and investigations ordered, and prosecutions launched. The first number is large, the second is small, and the third, on the Lokpal's own submission to Parliament's standing committee, stood for years at zero: the institution informed the committee that it had not prosecuted even a single person. The committee, examining the Department of Personnel and Training, recorded that since 2019-20 the Lokpal had received 8,703 complaints, that the overwhelming bulk of disposals turned on the complaints not being in the prescribed format, that only three complaints had been fully investigated in the period covered, and it recommended that the Lokpal act as an enabler rather than an inhibitor and not reject genuine complaints merely on the technical ground of format. The Lokpal's order of January 2023, directing that complaints not in the prescribed form would not be entertained at any level, hardened the very gate the committee questioned. Figures in this field move with the source and the period, and returns furnished in answer to information requests differ in detail from the committee's record, but the pattern across all of them is constant: a large inflow, a format screen that stops most of it at the door, a small residue that enters inquiry, and a prosecution count at or near zero.

A preliminary caution about the numbers themselves sharpens the picture. The gross inflow of the early years and the registered caseload of the later ones are different quantities wearing the same label. In the institution's first years, complaints arrived in the thousands annually by every account, the great majority outside the prescribed format and therefore counted as received but never registered as cases. After the format gate hardened, the figures the government gave the Lok Sabha for registered complaints fell to a few hundred a year: 149 registered in 2021-22, 320 in 2022-23, 166 in 2023-24 and 292 in 2024-25, with disposals tracking registrations closely. A falling complaint count in such a series can mean less corruption reported, a higher wall at the registry, or both, and the Lokpal's published data does not let a reader separate the two. What can be said is that the institution's own annual reports, two of which had been laid before Parliament by the time the government answered questions on the subject in 2023, record an ombudsman whose docket is dominated by threshold disposals, and that no presentation of the figures, by the institution or its critics, shows a prosecution pipeline of any size at the end of it.

Three explanations of that pattern compete, and they are not equally supported. The first is design: the Act requires a complaint in prescribed form with an affidavit, screens it through a preliminary inquiry before any investigation, prosecutes only through a court after a chargesheet, and provides no suo motu route by which the Lokpal could convert public knowledge of a scandal into a case. A design of successive screens produces few visible outcomes as a matter of arithmetic, whatever the zeal of the officeholders. The second explanation is capacity: the Lokpal worked for long periods below its sanctioned composition, with judicial member vacancies unfilled for over two years at one stretch, its inquiry and prosecution wings dependent on deputation and slow to be fully operationalised, a state of affairs on which the parliamentary committee sought specific details. The third explanation is intent: that an institution unwanted by the executive that must staff it will be staffed late, housed slowly and answered evasively, and that the format gate is administered strictly because strictness is convenient. The evidence establishes the first two explanations directly from the statute and the staffing record. The third is an inference from the same sequence that froze the institution between 2014 and 2019, and it should be reported as what it is: a contested political allegation that the documented chronology makes plausible to many observers without proving it against any individual decision.

The depletion was not confined to the chair. At points during the first body's life, two of the four judicial member posts lay vacant for over two years, a fact the government was asked to explain in Parliament, and the arithmetic consequence deserves emphasis: the full bench that Section 14 requires before an inquiry against a Prime Minister can even be considered consists of the Chairperson and all members, so a body sitting with vacancies is not merely slower at the top of its jurisdiction, it is disabled there, and the two-thirds approval the proviso demands is calculated against a bench that cannot be assembled. Vacancy, in this statute, is not an administrative inconvenience distributed evenly across the docket. It concentrates its damage precisely on the highest category of case, the one whose safeguards assume completeness.

The institution's second experience of an empty chair tests these explanations usefully. Justice Ghose's term ended on 27 May 2022, on his attaining the age limit the Act prescribes. This time Section 9 operated as drafted: the President authorised Justice Pradip Kumar Mohanty, a judicial member, to act as Chairperson with effect from 28 May 2022, and the Lokpal continued to function, receive complaints and sit in benches under an acting head. A regular appointment nevertheless took nearly two years: the President appointed Justice A. M. Khanwilkar, a former judge of the Supreme Court, as Chairperson in February 2024, and he was sworn in on 10 March 2024, with three judicial and three non-judicial members appointed alongside, restoring the body toward full strength. The comparison between the two gaps is the fairest summary of the institution's position. The machinery for continuity existed and worked; the machinery for appointment remained slow even when nothing in the text obstructed it; and the difference between the freeze of 2014 to 2019 and the gap of 2022 to 2024 is that the second was a delay in operating a known process, not a dispute about whether the process could run at all.

The machinery has, on occasion, operated exactly as designed at the level the design most publicised. In March 2024, acting on a complaint by one member of Parliament against another, the Lokpal's full bench directed the Central Bureau of Investigation to investigate the allegations, which concerned benefits said to have been accepted in connection with parliamentary questions, and to report within a fixed period. The episode is worth recording without exaggeration in either direction. It demonstrated every element of the statutory sequence working in public view: a written complaint, a preliminary examination, a reasoned direction to the Bureau under the Lokpal's superintendence, and a reporting obligation running back to the institution. It also demonstrated the sequence's limits, since the direction was the beginning of an investigation rather than a finding, and the member's parliamentary fate was separately determined by the House's own ethics process on its own timetable. A single visible case does not redeem a thin record, and the record's thinness is the subject of this section. But it establishes that the pipeline from complaint to directed investigation is functional law and not merely text, which is the point on which the institution's defenders and its critics most often talk past each other.

Set against this record, the frequently heard claim that India has no Lokpal is simply wrong, and its persistence is itself evidence about the institution: a body whose proceedings are largely confidential, whose complaint gate rejects most of what arrives, and whose outcomes are other institutions' prosecutions and disciplinary proceedings, generates almost no public trace of its own. The comparison with the transparency statute is instructive and is developed in [the comparison of the RTI Act and the Lokpal Act](/2012/01/15/rti-vs-lokpal-comparison/): the information commissions produce a visible artefact, the disclosed document, in lakhs of cases, while the Lokpal produces, at best, a prosecution conducted in another institution's name. Visibility is not the same as value, but a watchdog nobody can see working will be widely believed not to exist, and the Act's design gives it few lawful ways to correct the belief.

## What the movement demanded and what the statute gave

The distance between the Jan Lokpal draft of 2011 and the Act of 2013 is the standard by which the institution is usually judged in public argument, and the distance is real, though it is often described loosely. The movement's draft proposed a body with jurisdiction over the judiciary as well as the executive, over the conduct of members of Parliament inside the House as well as outside it, over the entire bureaucracy directly and without a vigilance commission screen, and over the anti-corruption wing of the Central Bureau of Investigation by absorbing it. It proposed selection by a committee with a broader and less executive-weighted composition, removal by a process centred on the Supreme Court, a citizens' charter with penalties for delayed public services folded into the same institution, and protection for whistleblowers as part of the same design. The enacted Act declined each of these. The judiciary is absent from Section 14. Article 105 is expressly preserved. Group C and Group D officials are screened through the vigilance machinery. The Bureau was directed in referred cases, not absorbed. The citizens' charter idea fell away entirely, and whistleblower protection travelled in a separate statute with its own troubled commencement history. The selection proposals show the same pattern in miniature. The movement's draft sought a selection body in which the executive was one voice among many, with the judiciary, the opposition and civil processes sharing the choice, and it coupled that breadth with a removal process it expected to be correspondingly hard to capture. The enacted committee of five gives the executive the chair and, through the eminent jurist's nomination by the other four, a strong gravitational pull on the fifth seat, leaving the opposition and the judiciary with one seat each. Defenders of the enacted design reply that appointment of an anti-corruption body is itself an act of governance for which the elected executive must answer, and that a committee the government cannot influence at all would lack a democratic owner when the institution errs. The argument is genuine, and the freeze gave it an unintended test: the question between 2014 and 2019 was never whether the executive had too much influence over the choice, but whether a choice would be permitted to occur.

Two judgments about that distance are possible, and the article's charter requires the stronger form of each. The movement's judgment, voiced by its leading drafters after enactment, is that a government negotiating under street pressure conceded the institution and retained the substance: every declined item on the list above is a channel through which accountability would have flowed, and the channels were closed one by one in committee, leaving an ombudsman strong against the retired and the junior and screened against the serving and the senior. The negotiators' judgment is that a constitutionally durable statute was the actual prize: a body reaching into the judiciary would have invited a basic structure challenge it might not have survived, an ombudsman commanding the Bureau outright would have fused investigator and prosecutor in ways Indian administrative law has reasons to avoid, and a maximal text that failed in the Rajya Sabha, as the December 2011 proceedings nearly ensured, would have left the 1968 pattern intact, a perfect bill and no institution. On the evidence of what followed, each judgment explains a different half of the record. The compromises explain the thin output, which flows from the screens. The enactment explains the output's existence at all, and also the freeze, which was possible only because the institution was real enough to be worth freezing.

For the reader assembling the cluster, the statute's own passage from agitation to enactment is told in the legislative history linked earlier in this article, and its evaluation against the transparency statute belongs to the comparison linked in the record section above. What the pillar adds is the drafting residue: read the Act's provisos as a map of the negotiation, and the institution's subsequent life becomes legible as the negotiation continuing by other means.

## The Lokpal among its neighbours: an ecology, not an empire

The Lokpal does not operate in an institutional vacuum, and several of its puzzles dissolve when its neighbours are mapped. The Central Vigilance Commission is its closest relative and its subordinate partner for the lower grades: the Commission continues its ordinary vigilance work across the services, receives the Lokpal's references for Group B, C and D officials under the provisions the Schedule inserted, and reports back through the same channel, while the Lokpal retains the senior grades and the political executive for itself. The Central Bureau of Investigation is its instrument but not its property: it investigates referred cases under the Lokpal's superintendence, keeps every other case under its ordinary governance, and its own director is chosen by a committee the Lokpal Act redesigned. The departmental authorities remain its executors for discipline: where the Lokpal finds misconduct short of a prosecutable offence, or alongside one, the punishment of dismissal, reduction or recovery still issues from the servant's own cadre authority acting on the Lokpal's recommendation and the inquiry record. The special courts are its terminus: every prosecution the institution launches is tried by a judge it did not appoint, under a penal statute it did not write, with appeals running to the High Courts in the ordinary way. And the constitutional courts stand over the whole arrangement: Section 54 bars civil courts from matters the Lokpal is empowered to determine, but the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court is untouched, and the institution's orders, like those of every statutory body, are amenable to judicial review on the ordinary grounds.

Parliament itself is the final neighbour, and the relationship is more supervisory than the Act's critics usually allow. The Lokpal's annual report goes to the President and is laid before both Houses under Section 48; the department-related standing committee examines the institution's functioning through the ministry's demands for grants, which is the channel through which the complaint statistics, the vacancy explanations and the format controversy examined in this article entered the public record; and members' questions have repeatedly compelled the government to state on the floor what the institution's own publications leave obscure, from the number of reports laid to the authorisation of the acting Chairperson under Section 9. The accountability loop the Act designs is therefore triangular: the Lokpal reports upward to Parliament, Parliament questions the executive about the institution the executive appoints and houses, and the courts police the boundaries when the other two corners stall. The freeze of 2014 to 2019 was, in this frame, a failure of the executive corner alone, repaired only when the judicial corner intervened and the parliamentary corner's committee pressure accumulated. An institution designed to be watched by three watchers was set working by the combined attention of the two it does not control.

Two consequences follow from the ecology. The first is that the Lokpal's effectiveness is a property of the system, not of the body alone: a referred investigation is only as good as the Bureau's officers, a recommendation only as effective as the department's willingness to act on it, a prosecution only as fast as the special court's docket. Assessing the Lokpal by its own annual report therefore systematically misattributes outcomes, crediting and blaming it for the performance of four other institutions. The second consequence is jurisdictional overlap, which the Act manages better than most Indian accountability statutes. The Vigilance Commission's advisory jurisdiction, the Bureau's investigative jurisdiction, the departments' disciplinary jurisdiction and the Lokpal's inquiry jurisdiction all attach to the same conduct, and the Act's referral provisions, its consent requirements for state postings, and its rule that a matter complained of to the Lokpal shall not be referred to a commission of inquiry, are the traffic rules that keep the overlap from becoming a collision of parallel proceedings. The rules are imperfect, and practitioners can point to cases where a complaint's proper home was genuinely arguable between the vigilance and ombudsman tracks. But the existence of traffic rules at all distinguishes this statute from the Indian habit of creating each new accountability body as if the previous ones did not exist.

## How to judge the Lokpal: metrics that fit the design

If convictions are the wrong measure, the reader is entitled to ask what the right ones are, and the Act itself supplies most of them. The first is threshold honesty: of the complaints registered, what proportion receive a reasoned preliminary view rather than a format disposal, and does the institution publish that proportion in the form Section 20's public status display and Section 48's annual report intend. The second is conversion quality: of the complaints that pass the preliminary screen, how many produce an investigation, a departmental reference or a chargesheet, and how the institution explains the residue it closes. The third is time: the Act's clocks, sixty days for the preliminary report, six months for investigation, a year for trial, are measurable promises, and an institution that reports its own compliance with them, stage by stage, gives the public a verdict it cannot spin. The fourth is strength: the proportion of the period for which the body sat with its full complement, including a regular Chairperson, since jurisdiction at the top of Section 14 is arithmetically impossible below strength. The fifth is the annual report itself: laid before Parliament under Section 48, debated or ignored, it is the one recurring public account the statute guarantees, and the gap between its contents and its parliamentary reception is a measure of the legislature's interest in its own creation.

Measured on those terms, the institution's record is mixed in a specific and instructive pattern. On strength it has failed repeatedly and through no fault of its own composition rules: the appointing process has left it depleted for years at a time, twice over. On threshold honesty its record is the weakest of the five, with the format gate absorbing the overwhelming bulk of its inflow and its public reporting leaving the received-versus-registered distinction for others to reconstruct. On time and conversion the published record is too thin to convict or acquit, which is itself a finding about the annual report's use. The pattern that emerges is not of an institution captured in its decisions, for its decisions are too few to capture, but of an institution diminished at its inputs: members, staff, complaints that survive its own registry. That is a quieter failure than corruption of process, and a more fixable one, since every element of it, appointments made on time, wings staffed as sanctioned, a complaint gate administered for substance, lies within the ordinary competence of the executive and the institution between them, without amending a word of the Act.

## Verdict: a committee composition as a commencement switch

The namable claim of this article can be stated in its full form. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 is the clearest case in Indian legislative history of an institution disabled not by repeal, not by non-notification, and not by judicial invalidation, but by a drafting choice inside its own appointment provisions: the selection committee was composed of named offices rather than described functions, so the non-existence of one officeholder could be argued to switch the entire statute off at the point of formation. The series to which this article belongs tracks three mechanisms of legislative inertia, the statute never notified, the statute narrowed from outside, and the statute in force but unstaffed. The Lokpal is the third mechanism in its purest form, and its lesson generalises to every appointment-dependent institution Parliament creates: draft the committee by function, with a stated substitute for every named seat, or the statute's commencement date is a promise the text itself may not keep.

The practical corollaries deserve stating for each of the readers this pillar serves. For the aspirant, the examinable core is compact and stable: Act 1 of 2014, assent on 1 January 2014, commencement on 16 January 2014; a Chairperson and not more than eight members, half judicial, half of the whole drawn from the Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, minorities and women; a five-year term to the age of seventy; jurisdiction in Section 14 with the Prime Minister's safeguard package; the two wings in Sections 11 and 12; superintendence over referred investigations in Section 25; declarations in Section 44 as amended in 2016; limitation of seven years in Section 53; and the state obligation in Section 63. For the advocate, the live questions are the threshold ones, the characterisation of the Prime Minister exclusions, the consent proviso for state postings, the limitation clock and the format gate, because Lokpal litigation will be won and lost at the doors of the institution rather than in its inquiries. For the researcher, the richest unworked material is comparative and internal at once: the complaint registry's own data, read against the annual reports, would show what the screens actually filter, and no public study has yet done that work from the primary record.

What repair would look like, if a legislature returned to the text, follows from the diagnosis and requires no reinvention. A statutory period for first constitution, and for filling vacancies thereafter, with the search committee required to be constituted within it, would close the unguarded interval through which the five years flowed. A function-based description of every committee seat, with the substitute named in the same clause, would prevent any single vacancy from being argued into a freeze. A complaint gate administered by rule rather than by institutional strictness, with a power to cure defects of form on notice to the complainant, would answer the standing committee's criticism without opening the door to anonymous accusation. And a duty to report the institution's own compliance with its internal clocks, in the annual report's first tables rather than its annexures, would give Parliament the material for the supervision it has so far exercised only through litigation's aftershocks. None of these repairs touches the Act's contested choices about jurisdiction or safeguards; all of them address the layer at which the statute actually failed, which was never its ambitions but its assumptions about the cooperation of the state that enacted it.

On the statute's merits, the balanced assessment is this. As a jurisdictional instrument the Act is genuinely strong: no previous Indian law placed the Prime Minister, the ministry, Parliament's members and the whole central official hierarchy within one inquiry body's reach, armed it with direction over the investigating agency in its cases, freed its prosecutions from executive sanction, and surrounded the result with limitation, hearing and trial disciplines that protect the accused official as carefully as the complainant. As an operating institution its record is thin for reasons that are partly designed, partly administrative and partly political, in proportions this article has tried to keep separate. And as a piece of drafting it contains, in one section, both the boldest jurisdictional sentence in Indian anti-corruption law and the vacancy that neutralised it for five years. A reader who understands that conjunction understands the Lokpal, and understands something about Indian legislation that no summary of either the movement or the sections can teach: that a law on the books is not a law in operation, and that the distance between the two is where this statute has lived most of its life. The distance is not closed. The second Chairperson's tenure runs to the age limit in 2027, the appointment question will therefore present itself again within a short cycle, and whether it is answered in weeks, as a functioning statute assumes, or in years, as this statute's history records, will say more about the institution's real commencement than anything in its first decade. The Act's text has been ready since January 2014. Its history since is the record of a state deciding, repeatedly and at leisure, whether to operate what it enacted. That record, and not the section headings, is the statute a citizen actually lives under, and it is the record this article has tried to state exactly: the powers in full, the safeguards in full, the delay in full, and the output in the institution's own numbers, so that the next time the appointment question arises, the sequence that answers it can be measured against the sequence that disgraced it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What powers does the Lokpal have over public servants?

The Lokpal may inquire into corruption allegations against public servants within its jurisdiction, order a preliminary inquiry through its Inquiry Wing, direct an investigation by an agency such as the Central Bureau of Investigation under its superintendence, grant sanction for prosecution in the cases it pursues, file chargesheets through its Prosecution Wing before special courts, recommend departmental proceedings to the competent authority, and direct attachment of property acquired by corrupt means while a prosecution is pending. It cannot itself convict, sentence or dismiss a public servant; punishment follows only from the court or the disciplinary authority acting on what the Lokpal has set in motion.

### Q: Who can be investigated by the Lokpal?

Section 14 of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 covers any person who is or has been Prime Minister, Union minister or member of either House of Parliament; Group A, B, C and D officers and officials of the Central Government; chairpersons, members, officers and employees of boards, corporations, authorities, companies, societies, trusts and autonomous bodies established by Parliament or financed or controlled by the Centre; and officers of societies and trusts crossing the Act's government funding or foreign donation thresholds. Persons who abetted, gave or took a bribe, or conspired in relation to an allegation against any of these can also be inquired into.

### Q: Is the Prime Minister within the Lokpal's jurisdiction?

Yes, in terms. A person who is or has been Prime Minister is the first category named in Section 14. The inclusion carries two limits. The Lokpal cannot inquire into an allegation against the Prime Minister in so far as it relates to international relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy or space. For any other allegation, an inquiry may begin only if a full bench of the Chairperson and all members considers the initiation and at least two thirds of the members approve it, and the inquiry is then held in camera, with its records sealed if the complaint is dismissed.

### Q: How are Lokpal members selected?

The President appoints the Chairperson and members by warrant on the recommendation of a selection committee consisting of the Prime Minister, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or, after the 2016 amendment, the leader of the single largest opposition party where there is no recognised Leader of Opposition), the Chief Justice of India or a nominated Supreme Court judge, and an eminent jurist. A search committee of at least seven persons from fields such as anti-corruption policy, public administration, vigilance, finance, law and management first prepares a panel of names for the selection committee to consider.

### Q: Does the Lokpal control the CBI?

Not generally. The Central Bureau of Investigation remains a separate agency with its own director and its full range of cases. What Section 25 of the Act gives the Lokpal is superintendence and power of direction over any investigating agency, including the Bureau, in respect of cases the Lokpal has referred to it. Officers of the Bureau working on a referred case cannot be transferred without the Lokpal's approval. The movement's 2011 draft had proposed absorbing the Bureau's anti-corruption wing into the Lokpal; Parliament instead chose control over referred cases only, which is direction case by case rather than command of the institution.

### Q: Are states required to appoint a Lokayukta?

Yes. Section 63 of the Act provides that every state shall establish a body to be known as the Lokayukta for the state, by a law made by the state legislature, if it has not already done so, within one year from the commencement of the Act, a deadline that expired in January 2015. The Act prescribes no model structure, composition or powers for the state body and attaches no consequence to default, so compliance was uneven: states with older Lokayukta statutes counted existence as compliance, others legislated late or weakly, and a few had no functioning body long after the deadline passed.

### Q: When was the first Lokpal actually appointed?

The Act commenced on 16 January 2014, but the first appointments came more than five years later. The President appointed the Chairperson and eight members by warrant on 19 March 2019, on the selection committee's recommendation, after the search committee under Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai had been constituted in September 2018 and the Supreme Court had fixed a timetable. Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, a former Supreme Court judge, took the oath as the first Chairperson on 23 March 2019, and the members assumed charge on 27 March 2019. The gap was caused by the dispute over the selection committee's composition, not by any defect in the appointments themselves.

### Q: Must public servants declare their assets to the Lokpal?

Public servants must declare their assets and liabilities under Section 44 of the Act, though the declaration is made to the competent authority of their own ministry or department rather than filed with the Lokpal as an institution. As enacted, the section required a declaration within thirty days of entering office and an annual return each year, with the statements published on the department's website. The 2016 amendment removed the fixed timeline from the statute and left the declaration to be made in the form and manner prescribed by rules, a change critics read as dilution and the government defended as administrative necessity.

### Q: What is the term of office of the Lokpal Chairperson and members?

Under Section 6 of the Act, the Chairperson and every member hold office for a term of five years from the date on which they enter upon office, or until they attain the age of seventy years, whichever is earlier. The first Chairperson, Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, demitted office in May 2022 on reaching the age limit, before completing five years. The Chairperson's salary and allowances equal those of the Chief Justice of India, and a member's equal those of a Supreme Court judge. On leaving office, the Chairperson and members cannot be reappointed to the Lokpal and face restrictions on later government employment.

### Q: How can the Chairperson or a member of the Lokpal be removed from office?

Removal is by the President under Section 37, on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, and only after the Supreme Court has inquired into the charge on a reference and reported that removal is warranted. The reference can be made by the President directly, on a petition signed by at least one hundred members of Parliament, or on a citizen's petition that the President is satisfied should be referred. The Act also permits removal on specified objective grounds, such as insolvency, taking up paid employment outside the duties of office during the term, or infirmity of mind or body. The process is deliberately modelled on the protection given to constitutional functionaries.

### Q: Is there a time limit for filing a complaint with the Lokpal?

Yes. Section 53 of the Act bars the Lokpal from inquiring or investigating into any complaint made after the expiry of seven years from the date on which the offence alleged in the complaint is said to have been committed. The bar is on the Lokpal's jurisdiction, not merely on its discretion, so a complaint about older conduct cannot be revived by showing that the corruption was discovered late. The seven-year window is one of the Act's deliberate filters, and anyone considering a complaint about events near the boundary should treat the limitation date as a hard deadline, because the Lokpal has no power to condone the delay.

### Q: What happens if a complaint filed with the Lokpal is false or frivolous?

The Act punishes a complaint that is false and frivolous or vexatious with imprisonment for a term that may extend to one year, and with a fine that may extend to one lakh rupees, on conviction. The provision sits alongside the complaint rules, which require every complaint to be supported by an affidavit, so a complainant swears to the allegation before the machinery moves. The Lokpal may also proceed against a complainant where, after a preliminary inquiry, it closes a case as baseless. Critics of the design argue that the penalty chills genuine reporting by whistleblowers; its defenders answer that an institution receiving complaints against the highest offices needs a deterrent against campaigns of motivated accusation.

### Q: Can the Lokpal take up a corruption case on its own without a complaint?

No. The Act gives the Lokpal no suo motu power. Its jurisdiction under Section 14 is engaged by an allegation of corruption made in a complaint, in the prescribed form, and the procedure of Section 20 runs from that complaint through preliminary inquiry to investigation. Public reporting of a scandal, however detailed, does not by itself set the Lokpal in motion; someone must file. This is a deliberate contrast with bodies that may act on information from any source, and it is one of the structural reasons the institution's visible output is small relative to the corruption that is publicly known at any given time.

### Q: What is the Search Committee under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act?

The Search Committee is the screening panel the selection committee constitutes under Section 4 before making its recommendations. It must consist of at least seven persons with expertise in fields the Act lists, including anti-corruption policy, public administration, vigilance, policy making, finance including insurance and banking, law and management, and its task is to prepare and recommend a panel of names for the posts of Chairperson and members. The selection committee is not strictly confined to the panel, a flexibility challenged in the Common Cause litigation. The committee that led to the first appointments was constituted on 27 September 2018 under Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai, with eight members.

### Q: What does the Inquiry Wing of the Lokpal do?

The Inquiry Wing, constituted under Section 11 and headed by a Director of Inquiry, conducts the preliminary inquiry that Section 20 places at the front of every case. When a complaint enters the system, the Lokpal refers it to the Wing, which examines the allegation, gathers the material needed to test whether a prima facie case exists, and reports back, the Act's scheme contemplating a report within sixty days of the reference. Its report drives the Lokpal's next decision: ordering an investigation by an agency, directing departmental proceedings, or closing the case. It is a fact-finding screen, not a prosecutor; investigation and prosecution belong to the agencies and to the separate Prosecution Wing.

### Q: Which offences can the Lokpal inquire into?

The Lokpal inquires into allegations of corruption, which the Act ties to offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988: principally the taking or giving of bribes and undue advantage, criminal misconduct by a public servant, and the possession of assets disproportionate to known sources of income, together with attempts, abetment and conspiracy connected with those offences. The Lokpal Act itself creates almost no substantive offences; it is a jurisdiction and machinery statute. It decides which persons can be reached, through what procedure and before which court, while the definitions, elements and punishments of the offences live in the 1988 Act and the general penal law.

### Q: Does the Lokpal need government sanction before prosecuting a public servant?

No, and this is one of the Act's most consequential departures from the ordinary scheme. Under the Prevention of Corruption Act, prosecution of a public servant ordinarily requires the prior sanction of the government or authority competent to remove the servant, a filter long criticised for letting the executive protect its own. In a case the Lokpal has inquired into and pursued, the sanction function is exercised by the Lokpal itself, and its Prosecution Wing files the chargesheet before the special court. The accused official's own administrative superiors therefore hold no permission slip over a Lokpal prosecution, though the trial itself proceeds before the court with all ordinary safeguards.

### Q: Are members of the armed forces covered by the Lokpal Act?

No. The classes of person listed in Section 14 are drawn from the civil public services, Parliament and the executive, and from the boards, bodies, societies and trusts the section describes. Personnel of the Army, the Navy and the Air Force do not fall within those classes, and corruption allegations against service personnel are dealt with under the service Acts and the military justice system, including trial by court martial where the service law so provides. The exclusion mirrors the position of the judiciary, which also does not appear in Section 14: Parliament confined the ombudsman to the civil executive and its penumbra, leaving the armed forces and the courts to their own accountability machinery.

### Q: What is the difference between the Lokpal and a Lokayukta?

The Lokpal is the Union-level ombudsman, a single body for the whole country established directly by the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, with jurisdiction over the Prime Minister, Union ministers, members of Parliament and central government officials and bodies. A Lokayukta is the corresponding state-level institution, established by each state under its own legislation, with jurisdiction over that state's chief minister (in most state schemes), ministers, legislators and state officials, on terms each state law fixes for itself. Section 63 of the central Act obliged every state to establish a Lokayukta within one year, but it prescribed no structure or powers, so state Lokayuktas vary far more in strength than the single central model.

### Q: Why was no Lokpal appointed between 2014 and 2019?

Because the selection committee could not, on the government's stated view, be constituted. Section 4 named the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha as a member, and the Sixteenth Lok Sabha had no recognised Leader of Opposition, since no opposition party held the one tenth of the House's strength that recognition requires. The government maintained that appointments must await an amendment substituting the leader of the largest opposition party, which came only in 2016; the Act's own clause saving appointments from invalidity for a committee vacancy pointed the other way, and the Supreme Court held in Common Cause in 2017 that the Act was workable as it stood. Even after the amendment, the search committee was constituted only in September 2018, and appointments followed in March 2019 under the Court's timetable.
