## A national emblem with a redacted biography

In 1940 the Moscow Conservatory took his name, in the centenary year of his birth, and in 1958 the Soviet Union founded an international competition that bears it, a fixture of the musical calendar ever since. No Russian composer was more publicly honoured by the state. Yet the editions of his letters that the same state published through those decades were cut, and cut in a particular place, so that a documented part of the man never reached print.

<!--more-->

![Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky legacy debate, the national emblem and the redacted biography - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Both facts are true at once, and they stayed true at once for most of the twentieth century. The Tchaikovsky overrated debate, the long quarrel over whether he belongs among the greatest composers or merely among the most loved, ran through the same decades and drew on the same edited record, which is one reason it has proved so hard to settle. A reader who met the composer through the standard Soviet editions met a figure engineered for the honour described above: a suffering, solitary, fastidious artist whose sorrows had no names attached to them, set inside a national story that could be taught in schools. A reader who met him through the manuscripts at Klin, the house where he spent his last years and which became a museum within a year of his death, met somebody more specific and less convenient.

How can a state celebrate a composer and censor his biography at the same time? The question sounds like a paradox, and it is usually asked as one, as though the two policies contradicted each other and one of them must have been hypocrisy. Posed that way it cannot be answered, because it assumes the emblem and the man were the same thing and that the state valued them equally. They were not, and it did not. The celebration was of a usable inheritance, melodies a whole society could recognise as its own; the censorship was of a private life that the laws and pieties of that society had declared unprintable. Each policy protected the other. The wider the fame, the more dangerous the documents became; the cleaner the documents, the safer the fame. That mechanism is worth holding in mind, because the critical arguments examined in this article ran on the same edited evidence, and some of them changed character the moment the evidence changed.

The gap between the two portraits was not an accident of incomplete publication, the ordinary lag between a life and its documentation. The letters existed whole. They survived in their thousands, in the composer's hand, in the archive of the house at Klin, alongside diaries that recorded his days in the same plain manner. The archive was never lost, and at intervals it was even catalogued by scholars who understood exactly what they were cataloguing. What was missing, for decade after decade, was permission. The documents waited in their boxes while the editions drawn from them taught two generations of readers, in Russia and abroad, to hear the music through a life with the centre removed. Judgements formed on that basis did not evaporate when the basis changed, which is a large part of the story this article tells.

The thesis of what follows is that the three long-running arguments about Tchaikovsky are one argument in three costumes. The charge that his music is sentimental, the objection that his forms are weak, the dispute over whether he is Russian enough, and the quarrel over his private life and his death all ask the same underlying question: who holds the right to define him? In each case a party claims the authority to say what he is, and in each case the claim is made from outside the evidence it invokes, by a critical tradition protecting its standard, by a national movement protecting its definition of the nation, or by a state protecting its emblem. The dispute over the death is where that fight stopped being a matter of interpretation and presented itself as a matter of fact, which is why it carries a weight the others do not.

## Three arguments that turn out to be one

Set the arguments side by side and their shared skeleton shows. The aesthetic argument says the music feels too much and builds too loosely, and it is made in the name of a formal standard worked out for other music in another country. The national argument says the music is not Russian in the right way, and it is made in the name of a definition of Russianness that was itself invented, at a datable moment, by a small circle with institutions to build. The biographical argument says the private life must be either hidden or explained, and it was made first by a devoted brother editing for posterity, then by a state editing for ideology, and finally, in reverse, by readers who treat the restored record as a key that unlocks the music. Each argument has a custodian. Each custodian has an interest. And each argument, when it is examined closely, turns out to be partly about the custodian's standing rather than about the composer at all.

None of the rows is a straw man. The sentimentality charge was made by critics of real intelligence and is still the default verdict in much casual writing. The structural objection descends from a serious theory of how music coheres. The national dispute was fought by musicians who knew the folk repertory intimately and believed, with reason on their side of the question, that a Russian music had to be rescued from German routine. The editing of the biography began in family piety, not in a ministry. And the alternative account of the death was brought into the open by a scholar who believed she was breaking a taboo in the name of the truth. Giving each case its strongest form is not a courtesy in this article. It is the method, because a custodian's claim can only be weighed once it has been stated as well as its maker could state it.

What the rows share, beyond the skeleton, is a direction of travel. Every one of them moves authority away from the scores and the documents and towards an interpreter who stands between the reader and both. The formalist stands between the reader and the symphony and explains that its climax is not earned. The nationalist stands between the reader and the folk song and explains that its setting is not authentic. The editor stands between the reader and the letters and removes the sentences that would let the reader judge. The arguments differ in everything except this. They are contests for custody, and the prize in each is the right to say, in advance, what kind of thing Tchaikovsky is.

## The sentimentality charge stated properly

Sentimental is the word that has followed this composer further than any other, and it is usually deployed as though its meaning were obvious, a raised eyebrow in adjective form. Stated properly, as its serious users intend it, it is a precise accusation and deserves to be heard precisely. It alleges that the music demands an emotional response larger than the musical events have earned: that climaxes arrive by accumulation and insistence rather than by logic, that the listener is instructed to feel rather than given grounds for feeling, and that the feeling itself is the product being sold, prefabricated, the way a cheap novel sells tears. On this account the defect is not emotion but unearned emotion, and the composer stands convicted of a kind of dishonesty, of pressing a button instead of building a case.

### Is Tchaikovsky's music sentimental?

Some of it invites the charge and some of it refutes the charge outright. The scores contain pages of sustained emotional statement that a hostile listener can call indulgent. They also contain long spans of strict construction, dance movements of complete formal poise and climaxes built by patient accumulation. Sentimental describes a performance habit better than it describes the notes.

The charge in that precise form belongs to a lineage. Its grandparents are the German formalists of the nineteenth century, for whom Eduard Hanslick's doctrine that music's content is form in motion supplied the measuring instrument: feeling that could not be pointed to in the structure was feeling the composer had smuggled in. Its parents are the conservatory and textbook traditions that inherited the instrument and applied it as a universal gauge, so that by the middle of the twentieth century a student could absorb the verdict, loosely built, emotionally overwrought, hysterical in the climaxes, without ever being shown the working. Its popular form, the form in which it reached the widest public, is the programme note that praises the tunes while apologising for the composer, as though the melodies were an accident that happened to a mediocrity.

Notice what the charge needs in order to work. It needs the music to be heard as confession, as the overflow of a particular man's inner weather, because unearned feeling is a charge about sincerity, and sincerity only arises if the music is taken as a report on its maker. Tchaikovsky's music has been heard that way more consistently than almost any other composer's, partly because the Sixth Symphony arrived nine days before his death and was promptly read as a farewell, and partly because the anecdotes of a nervous, suffering, exiled-feeling man were in circulation from the start, in Modest's biography and in the memoir literature. The sentimentality charge and the biographical portrait grew up together and hold each other up. A passage in the Pathétique that might be analysed as a registral design, a melody sunk in the basses under a held chord, is instead received as a sob, and a sob can always be accused of being staged.

The passages the charge cites are real, and naming them is part of stating the case fairly. The love theme of Romeo and Juliet, broad, hymnlike, returning at the end in apotheosis. The first movement of the Sixth Symphony, whose second theme is marked to be played tenderly and singing, and whose development erupts without the usual preparatory courtesies. The climaxes of the Fifth, where the motto theme is not developed so much as reimposed, louder, until resistance feels pointless. A critic who finds these moments coercive is reporting a genuine experience of the music, and the experience is repeatable: play the records to a sceptical listener and watch for the moment the shoulders rise. The argument of the next four sections is not that the experience is imaginary. It is that the experience has more than one possible source, and that the tradition which named it a defect in the notes never seriously tested how much of it was being supplied by the way the notes were played.

That is the second open question of this article, and it is narrower and more answerable than the first. Is the emotional excess in the music or in the performance? It opens here, inside the statement of the charge, because the charge was framed before the question was asked, and it will close once the markings themselves have been compared with what the performance tradition does to them.

## Where the sentimentality charge originates

A charge this durable has a birthplace, and this one was born in the German critical tradition of the nineteenth century, the tradition that set itself up as the custodian of musical seriousness for the whole of Europe. Its central doctrine, given classic statement by Hanslick in 1854, held that music's content is musical form itself, tones in motion, and that feeling enters a work legitimately only as it is embodied in structure. A composer whose music seemed to address the listener's feelings directly, over the head of the structure, was not merely different in method. He was suspect in morals, a purveyor of sensation. The doctrine was a powerful analytical tool. It was also a border control, and the border it policed was the one around the German canon at the moment that canon was being consolidated into textbooks and concert institutions.

Hanslick's own encounter with Tchaikovsky shows the instrument in use. Reviewing the Violin Concerto after its Vienna premiere in 1881, he granted the first movement a kind of freshness and then turned on the finale, whose gaiety struck him as brutal and common, music that assaulted the listener instead of persuading him. The review is remembered for a phrase about music that stinks in the ear, paraphrased here because the original is quoted everywhere; what matters is the move underneath the insult. A Russian folk-flavoured dance tune, presented at full brilliance, was judged by the standard of Viennese decorum and found to be a breach of manners, and the breach of manners was then recorded as a defect of art. The pattern recurs throughout the charge's history: a response of social discomfort, redescribed as a technical finding.

From Germany the charge travelled along two roads. The first was the conservatory syllabus, which carried the formalist gauge into classrooms across Europe and America and taught generations of students to hear development as virtue and repetition as confession of weakness. The second was biography. Modest Tchaikovsky's life of his brother, published in the years 1900 to 1902, presented a man of extremes, weeping, despairing, exalted, fleeing rooms and collapsing after premieres, and it presented him lovingly, as a monument of sensibility. Readers who took the music for the man, as the biography invited them to do, heard the symphonies as emotional documents and judged them by the standards applied to a confession: too much, too often, too loud. The sentimentality charge needs that confusion between the work and the worker. Where the music is treated as construction, the charge has to be argued passage by passage, and it often fails. Where the music is treated as a diary, the charge is won before the evidence is heard.

The death completed the circuit. The Sixth Symphony was premiered under the composer's direction in St Petersburg on 28 October 1893, and he was dead nine days later, in an epidemic, of a disease nobody disputes was raging in the city. Because nobody at first knew what to make of a symphony that ended in a slow fading movement, the death supplied the interpretation: the work was read as a premonition, a farewell, a suicide note in sound. Modest later said the symphony had a programme the composer would not disclose, and the undisclosed programme became a blank page on which the confession reading could write what it liked. A work that ends quietly was thereby converted into the most sentimental object in the repertory, a man's feeling about his own end, although the feeling was supplied by the audience and the calendar rather than by anything the composer said.

The English-speaking world received the charge through its own channels and added an inflection. In the criticism of the early twentieth century the music was repeatedly described in the vocabulary applied to women's feeling, hysterical, swooning, neurotic, terms that did ranking work while pretending to do descriptive work, as reception historians, Richard Taruskin among them, have documented in detail. To call a symphony feminine in that climate was to place it, without argument, below the masculine business of development. The charge's long life is partly explained by this convenience. It allowed a critic to demote the most popular serious composer in the world while appearing merely to describe him.

Russia, meanwhile, produced the mirror image of the same discomfort. The Balakirev circle's reviewers, César Cui the most persistent, attacked the young Tchaikovsky from the opposite flank, finding him routine, academic, insufficiently national, a conservatory product. He was thus too emotional for Vienna and too correct for the nationalists, and the two verdicts, laid side by side, already suggest that what was being judged was less the notes than the judge's idea of what a composer of his origin ought to be. That suggestion is the seed of the Russianness dispute, and it will be picked up in its own sections. Here it is enough to fix the origin: the sentimentality charge is not a measurement that a tradition took of the music. It is a position that a tradition took up, for reasons that included its own defence, and then taught as a measurement.

## The distinction between sentimental and emotionally direct

Everything in this argument turns on a distinction that the charge usually blurs, and drawing it cleanly is the analytical work of this article. Sentimentality, in the strict sense the word carries in aesthetics, is feeling that exceeds or evades its object: emotion indulged for the pleasure of feeling it, unearned by the occasion, disproportionate to what produced it, and typically aware of itself, watching itself feel. The sentimental work does not present an object that warrants grief or joy and let the response follow. It reaches past the object and works on the response directly, with devices whose function is to guarantee a reaction. Emotionally direct art does the opposite. It states feeling openly, at full size, without embarrassment or mediation, but it builds the occasions for feeling out of its material, so that a listener who resists can still point to the mechanism and say where the effect came from. The difference is not intensity. A direct work may be far more intense than a sentimental one. The difference is whether the intensity is produced or merely triggered.

Apply the test to the passages the charge always cites, and the results are more interesting than the verdict. The first movement of the Sixth Symphony is built on a melody introduced in the basses in near darkness, and its great climax is not an unmediated sob: it is the same material driven upward through sequences, registral expansion and a long dominant preparation, so that when the fortissimo arrives it arrives as the end of a process a listener has been following for minutes. The love theme in Romeo and Juliet sounds like pure effusion when excerpted in a concert suite, and the excerpt tradition has done the piece real damage; in the full score its final apotheosis is the resolution of a conflict structure, Friar Laurence's chorale, the feud's rhythmic violence, that has been grinding against it from the first page. Feeling at that scale, in that position, is not a button pressed. It is a debt the structure has incurred and now pays.

The honest concession belongs inside the distinction, not outside it. There is a stratum of the output where the charge lands and stays landed: some of the romances, the salon songs written for a market that wanted exactly this commodity, lean on the conventions of their genre, sighing appoggiaturas and lachrymose sequences that signify feeling rather than construct it. Tchaikovsky wrote fluent occasional music all his life and not all of it aspires beyond its function. The critics' error was one of generalisation, taking the salon stratum as the key to the symphonies and ballets, as though a composer's least defended pages revealed his essence and his most defended pages were a disguise. The same logic applied to any prolific composer would convict all of them of their potboilers.

David Brown, whose four-volume biography and analytical studies form the standard English account of the works, spent much of that account doing the passage-by-passage work the distinction requires. Brown's analyses of the symphonies track thematic processes across whole movements, the working of an introduction theme through the Fourth, the motto architecture of the Fifth, and they find a composer whose large spans are held by recurrence and transformation far more systematically than the textbook verdict allowed. A reader does not have to accept every page of Brown's defence to see what it demonstrates about the charge: stated as a claim about construction, the claim can be tested, and tested it mostly fails; stated as a claim about sincerity, it cannot be tested at all, and it mostly survives. The charge has lived longest in the form in which it is unfalsifiable.

There is a further, quieter point about the audience's side of the bargain. Sentimentality in the strict sense is a collaboration: the work offers unearned feeling and the listener accepts the offer because feeling is pleasant. A listener who weeps at the Pathétique may be responding to construction, to memory, to the theatre of the concert hall, or to the story of the composer's death, which the programme note has usually just retold. The charge assumes the first of these can be discounted and the last is the music's own fault. But a work cannot be convicted of the uses made of its biography, and the biography, in this case, was itself edited by custodians with interests of their own. The distinction, followed honestly, keeps leading out of aesthetics and into custody, which is where this article's thesis said it would lead.

## Performance tradition and manufactured excess

Between the score and the listener stands the performance, and the performance of this music acquired, early, a set of habits that feed the charge from outside the text. The habits are easy to recognise once named. Tempi sag, especially in the symphonies, so that movements marked to move are made to brood. Phrases are milked with unwritten ritardandi at every cadence, as though the music distrusted its own continuity. Climaxes are approached with a crescendo of tempo as well as volume, a lunge the score does not request. The string sound aimed at is the richest available, vibrato continuous, articulation upholstered. Played this way, almost any nineteenth-century symphony sounds lachrymose; played this way for a century, the style stops sounding like an interpretation and starts sounding like the music itself, which is exactly the confusion the charge requires.

The tradition has named founders. Arthur Nikisch, the most admired conductor of his generation, made the Sixth Symphony a personal showpiece in the years after the composer's death and played it with a flexibility that contemporaries described as hypnotic; his example taught two generations of conductors that this music licensed, even demanded, a rubato applied to whole paragraphs. The recording era then canonised the habits of the famous. A young listener learning the Fifth from a celebrated mid-century recording learned the drag and the lunge along with the notes, and carried them into the concert hall as expectations. Tradition of this kind is self-sealing. Conductors who play the music straighter risk sounding cold to ears trained on the indulgent version, so the indulgent version reproduces itself.

The counterexample inside the tradition is instructive precisely because it is not an outsider's experiment. Yevgeny Mravinsky, who led the Leningrad Philharmonic for fifty years and stood at the centre of the Soviet musical establishment, recorded the last three symphonies in 1960 with tempi held taut, climaxes built by accumulation rather than by seizure, and a refusal to underline that many listeners first experience as severity. The scores do not resist this treatment. They flourish under it, their long lines clearer, their climaxes larger for being rationed. Mravinsky's example does not prove that the indulgent tradition is wrong in some absolute sense; interpretation is not arithmetic. It proves something narrower and more useful: the excess is a variable, supplied in performance, not a constant fixed in the text. A charge that varies with the conductor cannot be a property of the notes alone.

The ballet stage added its own layer. In the theatre, the great scores were for decades subject to cuts, reorchestrations and tempo adjustments made for dancers' convenience, and the concert public often met the music first in suites assembled for effect, sequences of the most perfumed numbers with the architecture removed. The Nutcracker that conquered the world as a seasonal entertainment is a selection, and the selection principle is charm. A composer judged by his charm, excerpted, is being judged by an editor's choices as much as by his own, and the judgement then circulates as though it were about the whole man. It is the print editors' work repeated in sound: custody again, exercised by selection.

None of this makes performance a conspiracy. Conductors indulge the climaxes because audiences respond, audiences respond because they have been trained, and the training feels like love of the music, which in a real sense it is. The point for the legacy debate is narrower. When a critic writes that the music wallows, some portion of what is being described is a century of performance habit, and that portion can be measured, at least roughly, by comparing what the scores actually ask for with what the tradition actually does. That comparison is the next section's business, and it is where the second open question closes.

## What the scores ask for versus what tradition supplies

Open the scores with the charge in mind and a pattern emerges that the tradition obscures. Tchaikovsky's tempo markings are those of a composer who feared dragging more than he feared haste. The first movement of the Sixth is marked Allegro non troppo, an instruction to keep moving, and its famous second theme is marked to be played tenderly and with sweetness, a colour instruction inside a moving tempo, not a licence to stop the pulse and luxuriate. The Fifth's movements are similarly geared to proceed. His metronome-consciousness went further than markings: preparing The Sleeping Beauty for the Mariinsky in 1889, he sent the theatre detailed indications of how long numbers should last, timing the score like an engineer, because he understood that in the theatre proportion is meaning and that a tempo allowed to sag is a structure allowed to collapse. This is not the documentary profile of a composer asking to be wallowed in.

His complaints about performance point the same way. The correspondence is full of a working composer's irritation at cuts, at liberties, at conductors who treated his tempi as suggestions and his structures as raw material. He revised, he protested, he conducted his own works across Europe and America in his last years partly to establish how they should go. The man who emerges from those documents is anxious about effect, certainly, and eager for success, but the anxiety runs towards precision. He wanted the climax to land because the arithmetic under it was right.

The most telling single marking in the symphonies sits in the Fifth, whose slow movement is headed Andante cantabile con alcuna licenza, with some licence. The phrase is a window into the composer's whole position. He knew this melody would invite the player and the conductor to linger, and instead of forbidding the impulse or surrendering to it, he wrote the permission into the score and bounded it with the word some. Licence, in his text, is a measured quantity, granted at a marked place, inside a tempo that resumes. The tradition that grew up around the movement took the licence and discarded the measure, stretching the paragraph until its proportions, and with them its rhetoric of a song overheard rather than a speech delivered, were gone. Multiply that one decision by a century of conductors and the manufactured part of the excess stops being an abstraction. It can be pointed to, marking by marking, wherever a written bound was treated as an open invitation.

So the second open question can now be closed, and closed with a qualification that keeps it honest. Is the emotional excess in the music or in the performance? On the evidence of the markings, the timings and the composer's own protests, much of the excess that the charge describes is supplied by a performance tradition the scores do not require, and some of it is manufactured outright by excerpting and by theatrical habit. That is the first rung of this article's ladder, and it is stated here as the evidence supports it: the scores ask for intensity within proportion, and they punish indulgence by sprawling when indulged. The qualification is that the music is, by design, emotionally direct. It does not ironise its feelings, distance them or apologise for them, and a listener who experiences directness itself as a fault will dislike the Pathétique played as straight as Mravinsky plays it. At that point the disagreement is no longer about the composer. It is about what music is permitted to do, and that disagreement is a matter of taste wearing the clothes of technique.

The distinction matters for everything that follows, because the structural objection, examined next, makes the same move in a different register. It takes a standard formed on one repertory, applies it to another, and records the mismatch as a defect in the music rather than as a question about the standard. In the sentimentality case the hidden variable was performance. In the structural case the hidden variable is the yardstick itself, and the yardstick has a nationality.

## The structural objection and its tradition

The second argument is the respectable one, the one that wears academic dress. It holds that the symphonies, for all their melodic wealth, are weakly built: that themes are presented in gorgeous blocks and then strung together rather than developed, that transitions are filled with sequences that mark time, that climaxes are reached by repeating a phrase louder and higher instead of by transforming it, and that whole movements are suites of episodes wearing the names of sonata forms. In its strongest formulation the objection is comparative. Beethoven's material, however plain, is germinal; everything grows from it. Tchaikovsky's material, however beautiful, is terminal; it is stated, restated, decorated and abandoned, and the symphony becomes a procession of lovely, self-sufficient moments whose order could be shuffled with less loss than the verdict implies.

The tradition behind the objection has a syllabus. The theory of form codified in the German treatises of the nineteenth century, Adolf Bernhard Marx's doctrine of the sonata as an organism with a governing idea, turned one generation's practice into every generation's law, and the conservatories of Europe taught the law as though it were acoustics. A development section was not a choice but a duty; a theme that did not submit to being broken down and rebuilt was a theme that had failed its examination. By the time the objection reached the English-speaking classroom it had become reflex. The symphonies were granted their tunes and denied their architecture in a single sentence, and the sentence was copied from textbook to textbook with the confidence of a measurement.

Gerald Abraham, the English scholar who did more than most to take this music seriously in print, is the objection's most useful witness precisely because he was not an enemy. Abraham edited a symposium on the composer in 1945, wrote about the works with knowledge and sympathy, and still found himself conceding the episodic construction, the padded transition, the climax by accumulation, in terms the hostile critics could quote with pleasure. When the defence's own side keeps entering the same plea, the plea is not propaganda. It fastens on something audible. There are seams in this music that a listener can find without a score and without instruction, and the honest treatment of the objection has to begin by agreeing that the seams exist before it asks what they mean.

The objection also has its anthology of prize exhibits. The first movement of the First Piano Concerto opens with one of the most famous themes in the world and then never uses it again, a portal built for a house it does not belong to. The Fourth Symphony's first movement has been timed and measured by analysts who find its development drastically short against its exposition. The Fifth's finale imports the motto theme into a blaze of major-key triumph that many listeners, including some who love the work, experience as imposed rather than achieved. Manfred, the unnumbered symphony, sprawls by almost any standard, and its composer knew it. These exhibits will be given their full weight two sections from here, because the objection deserves its day in court. First, though, the court itself has to be examined: who built it, where, and in whose interest.

## Who made the objection and on what grounds

The objection presents itself as a finding about music in the abstract, but it has a passport. It was made, in its durable form, by critics and teachers inside the German musical tradition, at the moment when that tradition was converting its own recent past into a universal curriculum, and it applies the standards of that curriculum as though they were laws of nature. This is the second rung of the ladder, and like the first it is a claim about custody: the structural objection was made by critics inside a specific national tradition applying its own standards as universal ones. Hanslick's formalism supplied the philosophy, the belief that feeling must be embodied in form to be legitimate. The Leipzig-centred conservatory network supplied the delivery system. The Beethoven cult, by then a matter of national prestige no less than of music, supplied the exemplar against whom all symphonies were to be measured and found wanting or worthy.

The grounds, examined one by one, are local customs wearing universal dress. Organic unity is a value before it is a fact: a culture that prizes the novel of education and the developing argument will prize a symphony that behaves like one. Thematic development as the index of seriousness is a preference for one kind of musical thinking, the kind that treats a theme as a proposition. Where a tradition instead treats a theme as a character, a presence whose returns and transformations of lighting carry the argument, the development gauge reads low by design. The gauge is not broken. It is measuring a parameter the music has deliberately subordinated to others, and then reporting the subordination as an absence.

The geography of the objection proves the point better than any theory. From the German side, Tchaikovsky was too loose, too episodic, too fond of effect. From the Russian nationalist side, in the reviews of César Cui and the polemics around the Balakirev circle, the same composer was too German, too academic, a conservatory workman grinding out correct forms without a national soul. A man cannot be convicted by two courts of opposite crimes against opposite laws without the observer suspecting that the laws are local. The Russian charge at least knew itself to be a party position. The German one, exported through the syllabus, forgot.

Inside the English tradition there was a dissenting analyst, and his dissent shows what the objection looks like when it is actually argued rather than inherited. Donald Francis Tovey, whose programme essays set the standard for writing about music for concert audiences, treated the symphonies as constructions to be followed with respect, tracing the Fourth's design and the concertos' logic with the same care he gave to Brahms. Tovey did not find the music beyond criticism; he found it criticisable, which is a different and more serious status, the status of work that rewards analysis instead of being excused from it. The textbook tradition largely ignored him, because a reflex is cheaper than an argument.

Richard Taruskin, the historian whose Defining Russia Musically of 1997 rewrote the terms of this whole debate, documented how the verdict hardened into something a student absorbed without meeting an argument at all, a paragraph of received depreciation passed hand to hand. Taruskin's own position is double and he holds both halves at once: he has no patience for the myth of the suffering confessional composer, and equally no patience for the formalist demotion that fed on the myth. That doubleness is the right temper for the sections ahead. The objection is about to be given its strongest form, and then the standard it rests on is about to be questioned, and both operations have to be done at full strength or neither means anything.

## The case that the objection is correct

Here is the objection at its best, argued as its most capable advocates would argue it, on evidence any listener can check. Begin with proportion. The first movement of the Fourth Symphony states a magnificent complex of material, the fate fanfare, the waltzing main theme, the folk-like second subject, and then develops it for a span that analysts have repeatedly found short against the weight of what was stated, so that the movement's centre of gravity sits in its statements rather than in its working. The recapitulation restores rather than resolves. A listener formed on the middle Beethoven symphonies feels the imbalance as a slackening of necessity: the parts are glorious, the compulsion that binds them is intermittent. Multiply the observation across the cycle and a pattern does seem to emerge, of movements whose climaxes are achieved by the sequential repetition of a phrase at rising pitch and rising volume, a staircase anyone can climb, instead of by the transformation that makes a climax feel discovered rather than installed.

The Fifth's finale is the case's crown witness. The motto theme, which has shadowed the symphony in minor keys, returns in the major, fortissimo, as the finale's peroration, and a long line of listeners has heard that return not as transfiguration but as insistence, the musical equivalent of repeating a claim at higher volume until the room agrees. The composer himself was not easy about this symphony. His correspondence of the period records an ambivalence his admirers usually soften: pride in the work's reception alongside a private suspicion that something in it was strained and manufactured, a fear that he had forced the feeling he most wanted to be believed. When the maker enters doubts of that kind into the record, the critic who repeats them is not inventing a case.

Manfred completes the indictment's documentary side. Written to a programme supplied by the Balakirev circle, at a scale beyond any of the numbered symphonies, it is by common consent of friend and foe the most uneven large work he allowed to stand, and he thought of destroying scores more than once in his life when revision failed to satisfy him. The Piano Concerto's vanished opening theme can be defended as a portal, and has been, brilliantly; it can also be heard, by an ear not disposed to defend, as a magnificent irrelevance, an overture to a different and better concerto that was never written. The objection's advocates do not need to deny the beauty of anything. Their case is precisely that beauty is doing the work that construction ought to do, and that a symphony, as a form, is a promise that construction will do it.

A further count, pressed by the same advocates, concerns genre. The slow movements, they observe, behave like operatic arias without words, closed songs that halt the symphonic argument to exhibit a feeling, and the finales behave like ballet numbers, brilliant, periodic, built for applause rather than for consequence. A symphony whose movements keep emigrating to the theatre is, on this view, not a symphony at all but a suite in disguise, and the composer's true home, the opera house and the ballet stage, is produced as the explanation of his symphonic weakness: he was a born lyric dramatist miscast by ambition and by the prestige of the German form. This is the objection in its most dangerous shape, because it concedes the genius and disputes only the address, and because his own devotion to the theatre gives it colour. It will be answered, in effect, by the capacity argument: the ballets are the largest structures he built, and a man does not assemble The Sleeping Beauty across three acts by accident while failing, from incapacity, to assemble a symphony.

Stated this way, the case has real force, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of defence that convinces nobody. There are transitions in this music that are, functionally, vamps. There are climaxes that arrive by accumulation in the most literal sense. There is a finale, the Fifth's, whose rhetoric a listener is entitled to refuse. The question the next section takes up is not whether these observations are true. It is what follows from them, and what follows depends entirely on whether the standard that converts them into a verdict is the only standard a symphony can be held to, or merely the standard of the tradition that got to the classroom first.

## The case that it applies the wrong standard

The counter-case begins by granting the observations and disputing the metric. A symphony may cohere by development, the Beethovenian way, in which a theme is broken down, recombined and earned back. It may also cohere by return, by frame, by the dramatised recurrence of a presence in altered light, the way a novel coheres around a character rather than around an argument. The Fifth's motto is not a failed subject for development. It is a fate figure whose identity is its point: it must not develop, because what it represents does not negotiate. Its appearances frame the symphony's four movements into a single span, and the finale's major-key blaze is the end of that span, to be judged as an ending is judged, by whether the frame has held the picture, not by whether the picture was argued. Berlioz built the Symphonie fantastique on an idée fixe that behaves in exactly this way and was canonised for the innovation. The device in a Frenchman's hands is a breakthrough; in a Russian's, a crutch. The standard, inspected closely, has a flag on it.

The Sixth Symphony's first movement is the counter-case's chief exhibit, and it has been reclaimed in detail by analysts, David Brown prominent among them, who follow its actual procedure rather than its reputation. The movement is laid out as a trajectory: a dark introduction that generates the main theme, a counterstatement of extraordinary tenderness, and then a development that does not work the themes in the textbook manner but drives them into a catastrophe, a fortissimo collapse after which the recapitulation sounds like survivors picking through wreckage. Nothing here is episodic. The proportions are the meaning, and they are exact. A form that aims at trajectory rather than dialectic will always look loose to a dialectical gauge, the way a ballad looks loose to a reader who expects a syllogism. The gauge is recording the difference faithfully and misreporting it as a deficiency.

The ballets settle the question of capacity, which is the question underneath the objection. A composer incapable of large-scale construction does not build The Sleeping Beauty, a three-act span held together by a thematic system in which the opposed forces of the drama carry opposed musical materials, developed, combined and resolved across an entire evening, nor the second act of Swan Lake, whose architecture pianists and conductors who dismiss the symphonies nevertheless treat as a masterclass. Capacity is not in dispute among people who know the theatre scores. What is in dispute is only the symphonies' dialect, and a dialect is not a defect. Tchaikovsky's letters about his own forms show a craftsman arguing with himself in exactly these terms: he worried over the joinery of the Fourth, complained that a movement was patched, and then defended the patch on the ground that the feeling of the whole was honest. The standard he applied to himself was coherence of effect. The standard the tradition applied to him was coherence of procedure. The two standards produce the same evidence and opposite verdicts, which is the subject of the next section.

None of this requires the counter-case to claim that every transition gleams or that Manfred is a masterpiece. It claims something more limited and more damaging to the objection: that the verdict of structural weakness is not a discovery about the music but a consequence of a prior decision about what structure is, that the decision was made inside one national tradition for its own reasons, and that applying it silently, as though it were a law of acoustics, is an error of method before it is an error of taste. A fair hearing would state the standard, name its origin, and then ask whether the music meets the standard it actually set itself. That hearing is what the criteria sections near the end of this article attempt, with the standard for formal command declared in advance and applied to friend and foe alike.

## Why both cases use the same evidence

The two cases just argued were built, deliberately, from the same bars. The Fourth's short development is the prosecution's slackness and the defence's lyric economy. The Fifth's motto blaze is insistence to one ear and peroration to another. The Sixth's catastrophe is either a development section that collapses instead of working, or a trajectory form working perfectly. Nothing in the notes changes as the verdicts alternate. What changes is the theory of form the listener brings, and theories of form are not found in nature. They are taught, by institutions, in places, at times, which is why this dispute has lasted a century and a half without the evidence bringing it any closer to an end.

This is not relativism, and it is worth saying plainly why not. Evidence disciplines both cases. The prosecution may not claim the development is absent when it is present and functioning in an unfamiliar way; the defence may not claim the seams are invisible when they can be heard from the back row. Brown's analyses bind the defence to the notes just as the timed proportions bind the prosecution. What the evidence cannot do is choose the criterion, because criteria are choices about what music is for, and no measurement of a transition settles what a transition is for. Every long-running critical war in this article has the same shape: a genuine evidentiary base, an unexamined criterion laid over it, and a verdict that presents the criterion's output as the evidence's conclusion. Spotting the shape is the reader's chief defence, and it applies to the national argument next as cleanly as it applied to the formal one.

Watch the mechanism work on a single passage and it becomes impossible to unsee. Take the second theme of the Sixth Symphony's first movement, the melody marked to be played tenderly, in tempo. The prosecution hears a self-contained song inserted into a symphony, a tune that asks to be loved rather than a proposition that asks to be developed, and notes that when it returns it returns essentially unchanged, as songs do. The defence hears the same facts and draws the opposite plan: a theme whose function is to be the still centre the catastrophe interrupts, whose unchanged return after the wreck is the movement's whole emotional argument, restatement as survival. Both hearings are accurate reports of the notes. Neither can refute the other by pointing, because they are not disagreeing about what is on the page. They are disagreeing about what a symphony movement is, and that disagreement was settled for each of them in a classroom, years before either heard this symphony, by teachers who presented a local settlement as a universal one.

The same shape will reappear in the dispute over the death, in a sharpened and more dangerous form, because there the two sides also share an evidence base, the doctors' statements, the timeline, the epidemic, and differ over what that evidence is allowed to mean. In the formal dispute the hidden criterion was a theory of form. In the death dispute the hidden criterion is a theory of the man: whether the documented record of a life, as restored, makes one account of its end thinkable and the other unthinkable. Keeping the shape in view is what allows the later sections to weigh documents instead of atmospheres.

There is a consequence for the composer's standing that should be recorded before moving on. A verdict that depends on a hidden criterion cannot be appealed, because there is nothing stated to appeal against. Tchaikovsky's reputation among critics spent a century under sentence from a court whose rules were never read out in his hearing. The popularity that kept his music in every hall in the world was, in that court, not a defence but a further count in the indictment, and it is to that strange transaction, the conversion of an audience's love into evidence of guilt, that the argument now turns.

## The popularity penalty

Measure him by the box office and the verdict of the other two arguments becomes inexplicable. Few composers of any century have been performed more: the last three symphonies are fixtures on every continent, the First Piano Concerto and the Violin Concerto are the entry points through which millions have met the orchestra, and the ballets, above all The Nutcracker in its seasonal occupation of the world's stages, generate a share of the classical audience's total attendance that no living institution can ignore. Measure him by the critical literature across the same span and the temperature drops forty degrees. Between those two measurements lies the popularity penalty, the standing assumption in a certain critical culture that an audience's love is itself a datum about the music, and an unfavourable one.

### Is Tchaikovsky underrated by critics?

By the critics who set the textbooks, yes, for most of a century. His performance figures have always placed him in the first rank, while the standard histories long treated him as a gifted populariser with defective forms. The gap between the two standings is itself the most reliable evidence in the whole debate.

The penalty has a specimen everyone knows. The work that carries his name furthest into the general culture is the 1812 Overture, cannon, bells and anthem, and he composed it to a commission for a public celebration with his own low estimate on record: he told his patroness in a letter that he had written it without love and doubted it had artistic worth. Critics have quoted his verdict against the piece ever since, usually without noticing what the transaction proves. The composer judged his occasional music more harshly than his judges did, and the piece the public loves most is the piece he valued least, which means the public's Tchaikovsky and the critics' Tchaikovsky are different selections from the same catalogue. The penalty is partly an artefact of selection, like the ballet suites: each side generalises from the portion of the output that reached it.

Nor is he the penalty's only victim, which is how we know it is a mechanism and not a judgement on one man. Rachmaninoff spent the same century beloved by audiences and patronised by the critical establishment for the same supposed crimes, emotional directness and tunes people could remember. Puccini paid it in opera. In each case the structure is identical: the qualities that fill halls are redescribed as the qualities that empty them of significance. What varies is only the vocabulary, sentimentality here, commercialism there, and the variation gives the game away, because a measurement does not change its units from case to case.

The penalty's selectivity is its most revealing feature. Beethoven is popular. Mozart is popular. Their popularity is never entered as evidence against them, and the reason is not that their music is more difficult, since much of it is not. The reason is the order in which their canonisation happened: they passed through the academy first, were certified by analysis and scholarship, and reached the crowd carrying the certificate. Tchaikovsky reached the crowd first and the academy has been auditing the route ever since. Popularity that arrives before permission reads, to the custodians of permission, as popularity that bypassed them, and the bypass is what is actually being punished. The audience is not on trial in these verdicts. The custodian's authority is.

The mechanism is not even specific to orchestral music. The [legacy debate around Chopin](/2011/05/21/frederic-chopin-legacy-debate/) collected the same penalty on a different pretext, the small forms and the salon, from a composer whose audiences were as devoted and whose critics were as certain that the devotion proved the charge. Two composers, two centuries' identical reflex, one transaction: wherever a public's love arrives before the academy's permission, the love itself will be entered in the indictment.

## Does wide appeal count against a composer

Logically, the penalty is a confession. To treat wide appeal as evidence of shallowness is to make a claim about audiences, that they are corrupt or corruptible judges, and to smuggle that claim into a verdict about notes. This is the third rung of the ladder, stated as the evidence supports it: popularity has functioned as evidence against him in critical writing, which is a position about audiences rather than about music. A critic is entitled to distrust crowds. What the critic is not entitled to do is enter the distrust as a finding about a symphony. The symphony does not know who is listening to it. Its transitions are exactly as sequential, its climaxes exactly as earned or unearned, in a hall of students as in a hall of subscribers, and an analysis that changes with the audience has stopped being analysis.

The penalty also served a professional interest, and naming the interest is not cynicism but sociology. A connoisseur culture needs objects that require mediation. The critic's, the programme annotator's and the syllabus maker's authority rests on the existence of value that is not self-evident, music whose worth a guide must explain. A composer whose worth announces itself to a first-time listener in a single hearing is, from that professional standpoint, a threat to the mediation market, and the threat is managed by reclassification: the immediate is renamed the superficial, and depth is defined as whatever requires the mediator. Modernist taste-making in the twentieth century gave this interest a philosophy, difficulty as the badge of seriousness, but the interest predates the philosophy and survives it, because it is structural rather than doctrinal.

It is here, at the hinge between the audience and the state, that the first open question of this article can be closed. How can a state celebrate a composer and censor his biography at the same time? Because the two acts are one act of custody, performed on the two halves of an inheritance. The state took the popularity, which the critics despised, and made it a national asset, filling the competition hall and the schoolroom with the melodies. The state took the biography, which the popularity made dangerous, and removed from print the parts that would have complicated the asset. Celebration without censorship would have exposed the emblem to the documents; censorship without celebration would have wasted the most useful Russian composer alive in the public's affections. There is no paradox and there never was. There is a ministry doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the same arithmetic the critics were doing from the other side: both treated the audience's love as a fact about power, and neither treated it as a fact about the music.

For the listener, the practical conclusion is modest and usable. When a verdict on a composer is delivered, ask what it is a verdict on. If the evidence cited is the size or the enthusiasm of the audience, the verdict is sociology, and may be good or bad sociology, but it is not criticism and should not be allowed to set the price of the symphonies. The remaining arguments in this article, the national one and the biographical one, repeat this pattern with higher stakes: a party defines a category, the composer is measured against the category, and the measurement is announced as a property of the man.

## The Russianness dispute

No argument about this composer has been fought longer, or with more flags planted, than the argument over his nationality as a musician. It began in his lifetime, in the journals of St Petersburg, and it was from the first an argument between institutions. On one side stood the conservatory: the Russian Musical Society and the conservatories at St Petersburg, founded in 1862, and Moscow, founded in 1866, enterprises of the Rubinstein brothers that taught counterpoint, harmony and form by the German method and aimed to make Russia a producer of professionals who could stand in any European capital. On the other stood the circle around Mily Balakirev, Balakirev himself, César Cui, Modest Mussorgsky, Alexander Borodin and Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov, with the critic Vladimir Stasov as their publicist: self-taught or defiantly under-taught musicians who held that a Russian music had to be built from Russian materials, folk song, church chant, the rhythms of the language, and that the conservatory's German routine was a colonial occupation of the Russian ear.

### Was Tchaikovsky a Russian or a Western composer?

He was a Russian composer formed by a European training, and by maturity the two inheritances cannot be separated in his music without breaking it. His schooling, his forms and his ambitions were the conservatory's; his melodic instincts, his folk materials and his subjects were Russia's. The either-or question was a weapon of his century, not a description.

Tchaikovsky was the conservatory's prize product and therefore the circle's necessary target. He had entered the St Petersburg Conservatory in its first years, studied composition under Anton Rubinstein himself, graduated in 1865 with the institution's silver medal, and taken a professorship at Moscow. Everything about his formation announced which side of the institutional war he belonged to, and the circle's reviewers, above all Cui in a long campaign of notices, treated his works as dispatches from the enemy camp: correct, fluent, eclectic, and empty of the national substance that alone could justify a Russian composer. The dispute, in other words, was never an innocent inquiry into the sources of his style. It was a border war, and he was the border.

The dispute had critics on the inside of both camps, which is worth noticing before the cases are argued. Hermann Laroche, the composer's conservatory classmate and the most literate musical critic of his generation in Russia, championed him from the enemy camp's own premises, praising exactly the schooled craftsmanship the circle derided, and his praise shows that the conservatory side was not a monolith of routine any more than the circle was a monolith of inspiration. The question Russian or Western was being asked in the press, in those terms, while the composer was still alive to read it, and he read it, and answered it in letters that both camps later mined. A dispute conducted in the subject's hearing, about the subject's essence, by parties who needed his answer to come out their way, is the raw material of this whole article in its purest form.

What counted as Russian, in the circle's usage, was itself a recent invention, and the invention can be dated. Stasov's criticism of the 1860s assembled the criteria: fidelity to folk material in substance rather than decoration, freedom from German developmental routine, subjects from Russian history and legend, a suspicion of the Italianate and the academic alike. The criteria were not foolish; they described real qualities in Glinka, the composer both camps claimed as a father, and they powered genuine masterpieces in Mussorgsky and Borodin. But they were a programme, written by partisans, and like all programmes they confused a strategy with an essence. The next two sections give each side of the dispute its strongest form, because the dispute is still running and still doing work, and a reader is owed both cases before any weighing.

## The case that he is a Western composer in Russia

State the Western case as its best advocates state it, and it is formidable. The genres he worked in are the European set, taken over entire: the symphony, the string quartet, the piano concerto, the violin concerto, the opera on literary subjects drawn from Shakespeare, Dante, Schiller and Byron as readily as from Pushkin. His harmony is the conservatory's harmony, his voice-leading the German classroom's, his orchestration learned from the European repertory he conducted and studied all his life. His ambitions were frankly European: he toured as a conductor to Leipzig, Paris, London and New York, he cared intensely about his standing in Vienna, and he measured his operas against the European stage. A composer is what his methods make him, and by method he belongs to the international, German-centred profession of his century, a Russian practitioner of a Western art.

The folk material, on this reading, is costume. He quotes the folk songs, a birch-tree song in the Fourth's finale, Ukrainian tunes in the Second Symphony, the way a historical novelist dresses a modern plot in period clothes: the melodies are genuine and the treatment is imported, harmonised, developed and orchestrated by procedures no folk musician would recognise. Where Mussorgsky let the speech rhythms of Russian peasants bend his vocal lines out of all classical shape, Tchaikovsky kept the shapes and applied the tunes to them. Cui's recurring verdict on the early works, that they might have been written by any competent graduate of any conservatory in Europe, was cruel, partisan and not groundless, and its modern form, that he is the most Western of the great Russians, is offered in reference books as neutral description. Even his defenders concede the description and argue only about its value.

The operas sharpen the prosecution's point and then complicate it, which is why both sides cite them. The subjects are Pushkin, the fountainhead of Russian literature, in Eugene Onegin and The Queen of Spades, and a competent Western reading notes the manner instead: Onegin composed not as a grand opera but as lyrical scenes, an intimate, almost French restraint applied to the most Russian of texts. Then the prosecution produces its favourite exhibit, The Maid of Orleans, a Russian composer setting Schiller's play about a French heroine in the manner of French grand opera, and asks what further proof of allegiance is needed. The defence answers that a man's reading is not his identity and that no one audits Shakespeare for his Italian plots. The exchange is recorded here because it shows the dispute's real unit of account: not the notes, but the choices that can be narrated as loyalty or betrayal.

His own testimony can be quoted for the prosecution, carefully and in paraphrase. He described himself, in his letters, as a man in whom two formations warred, the Russian saturated in childhood with the songs of the countryside and the professional trained to European order, and he sometimes wrote as though the professional were the truer self, the one that paid for the house at Klin and stood on the podium at Carnegie Hall. The Western case does not need to invent its composer. It needs only to take his credentials, his catalogue and his career at face value and decline to be distracted by the accent of his melodies. On the evidence of the curriculum vitae, the case is closed, and it is exactly this appearance of closure that the other side exists to contest.

## The case that he is a Russian composer in a Western idiom

The counter-case begins where the Western case is strongest and turns its own evidence. The conservatory training is real, but a training is a set of tools, and what a composer builds with the tools is the question. What Tchaikovsky built, from the start, was a melodic world whose fingerprints are Russian in ways that have nothing to do with quotation: the long, breathing cantabile line learned from Russian song and from the chant of the church he attended as a child at Votkinsk; the descending lament figures that structure whole themes; the habit of letting a melody's phrase lengths breathe asymmetrically instead of squaring them to German fours. Analysts can find these fingerprints in works with no folk source at all, which is the test that separates an idiom from a costume. A costume comes off. This does not.

The folk usage, examined closely, also refuses the decorative verdict. In the finale of the Fourth Symphony the birch-tree song is not a postcard pasted onto a European movement; by the account the composer himself gave his patroness of the symphony's programme, the folk tune is the movement's substance and its argument, the people at their festival as the answer to the fate that crushes the individual in the first movement, and the tune is made to survive the fate fanfare's interruptions. In the Second Symphony the Ukrainian songs are the themes, worked with all the conservatory's machinery, yes, but worked the way a native speaker works his own language, from inside. And beyond the orchestral works stand the liturgical ones, the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom and the All-Night Vigil, in which the conservatory graduate set himself to reform Russian church music from within its own chant tradition, an undertaking no merely Western composer in Russia would have conceived, let alone spent years defending against the ecclesiastical censors.

One chamber movement carries the counter-case almost by itself. The Andante cantabile of the First String Quartet, the most German genre in the catalogue, is built on a folk song the composer heard sung by a house painter at work and wrote down, and it treats that song not as a quotation to be framed but as the movement's entire substance, varied, respaced and harmonised until the tune's own character dictates the form. Played around the world, it became the piece by which audiences who knew nothing of the Russian dispute judged him, and they judged him, in effect, a melodist of folk descent. The movement is the two inheritances fused past separation, which is why both camps claimed it and neither could have it whole.

The deepest move in the counter-case belongs to the historians of the category itself. Taruskin's work on the period argues that Russianness in music was never a substance waiting to be measured but a claim staked by parties, and that by the circle's folk criterion, honestly applied, Tchaikovsky passes more often than the polemic admitted, since the circle's own heroes were meanwhile building their Russian idiom out of imports, oriental colour, Lisztian form, the whole European toolkit. Marina Frolova-Walker, whose 2007 history of Russian musical nationalism is the fullest institutional account, draws the conclusion the evidence supports: the categories sorted organisations, patrons and careers, not sounds. A Western composer in Russia is a phrase that describes a man's passport and his schooling. A Russian composer in a Western idiom describes his music. The dispute persists because the two phrases are answers to different questions, each true, each wielded as though it refuted the other.

## What the Balakirev circle actually objected to

Strip the polemic of its vocabulary and the circle's objection had a balance sheet. The conservatory was an institution with state recognition, salaries, examinations and a pipeline to posts; the circle was a voluntary association of gifted men, several of them employed in offices and barracks, whose school, the Free School of Music, ran on air. Every success of the conservatory's prize graduate was an argument that the pipeline worked and the association was unnecessary. This is the fourth rung of the ladder: the Balakirev circle's objection was institutional as much as aesthetic, because he was the conservatory's product and they were against conservatories, against what conservatories did to music and to musicians, against the conversion of a calling into a credential. Cui's reviews were the sound of that objection being enforced in public, week by week, premiere by premiere.

The personal record complicates the enmity in a way that helps the truth. Balakirev himself, the circle's head, did not treat the young Tchaikovsky as an enemy to be destroyed. He treated him as a talent to be captured. Romeo and Juliet was written in 1869 under Balakirev's close and hectoring supervision, dedicated to him, revised under his detailed criticism; years later it was Balakirev who pressed on the composer the programme for Manfred, the Byronic symphony the circle wanted a Russian to write. The circle's objection, in practice, was not that this man lacked gifts. It was that his gifts had been formed by the wrong institution and were being spent in the wrong service, and the remedy was recruitment, not exile. A purely aesthetic objection does not behave like that. An institutional one does, because institutions fight over people.

The policing extended to his tastes, and taste is where an institutional objection shows its hand most clearly. He adored Carmen, openly, at a time when Bizet's opera was the circle's idea of everything slick and foreign; he revered Mozart, whose quartets he treated as scripture, with a devotion the nationalists found embarrassing in a Russian; he learned from Italian melody and made no apology for it. None of these enthusiasms cost a conservatory man anything, since cosmopolitan taste was the conservatory's own creed. To the circle they were defections, evidence that the definer's border ran not between techniques but between loyalties, and that a composer could fail the test by what he loved at the opera house on his night off. An objection that reaches a man's pleasures has stopped describing his music.

The sequel confirms the reading. In 1871 Rimsky-Korsakov, a founding member of the circle, accepted a professorship at the St Petersburg Conservatory, the enemy institution, and spent years secretly remedying his own technical education while teaching there, a defection that scandalised the faithful and that history has judged the making of him. The border the polemic policed so fiercely turned out to be permeable to everyone. What the circle actually objected to, beneath the folk-song criteria and the anti-German rhetoric, was the loss of the power to define what Russian music was, a power they had held briefly, by right of invention, and that the conservatory's success was taking away. The objection to Tchaikovsky was the sound of custody changing hands, and it is the first appearance, inside his lifetime, of the pattern this article has been tracking: parties contesting the right to define him, with the definition always serving the definer.

## Who the dispute served then

Follow the benefits and the dispute's energy becomes legible. The circle gained the most obvious prize, an identity. A movement defines itself by its opposite, and the conservatory's golden graduate, famous, fluent and foreign-trained, was the opposite ready-made; every review Cui wrote against him was also a manifesto for the handful, and the handful's place in history is partly built from those reviews. Stasov, their publicist, gained a narrative, the heroic band of Russians against the German machine, which he told so well that histories repeated it for a century, usually without noticing that the band's members took conservatory salaries, state commissions and European tours as readily as their supposed opposite did.

The conservatory gained in the opposite direction. Persecution by the nationalists certified its product as dangerous, which is to say significant, and the institution could present itself as the party of craft and Europe against the party of rhetoric. The press gained serial content; the Petersburg journals ran the polemic the way later media would run a feud, and both camps wrote for the coverage. The publishers gained a controversy attached to scores. And over both camps stood the imperial state, which had its own use for a Russian composer of European standing and in 1888 granted him a pension from the Tsar's own funds, an honour that announced, decades before the Soviet emblem-making, that the custody of this name was a matter of state. The dispute served the throne too, as a demonstration that Russian culture could beat Europe at Europe's own art, and that demonstration required exactly the Western formation the nationalists were denouncing. Every party to the quarrel needed him to be something. The quarrel was the negotiation among their needs. And the composer, it should be said plainly, gained from both sides in turn: Balakirev's bullying mentorship made Romeo and Juliet, the conservatory network made his posts, his pupils and his European career, and his own instinct for survival made him polite to everyone, dedicating, corresponding, accepting criticism in letters of extravagant gratitude and then revising as he pleased. The dispute served every participant. The only party it did not serve was the listener who wanted to know what the music was, because the dispute's whole function was to answer that question in advance, from outside.

## Who the dispute serves now

The dispute did not die with its institutions, because new institutions found uses for it. In the state that succeeded the empire, the categories were nationalised: the composer was installed as the Russian classic, his Western formation softened in the telling, his folk connections emphasised, and the old circle-versus-conservatory war was rewritten as a progress narrative ending in him. After that state's collapse, the categories were inherited again, by a cultural politics that markets a Russian essence to the world and by a competition, the one founded in 1958 that bears his name, whose function as a national showcase depends on there being a definable Russian article to showcase. The dispute serves the branding, and the branding is worth money and prestige, which is why a musicological quarrel of the 1860s still has a budget.

It serves scholarship too, in a more honourable currency. The quarrel became the worked example through which historians of nationalism, Frolova-Walker's institutional history and Taruskin's category criticism at their head, taught the profession to distrust national essences in music altogether, a lesson that has since been applied to every national school in Europe. The dispute is now, in the seminar room, a machine for dismantling disputes. And it serves the concert business in its simplest form, because a Russian soul can be printed on a poster and a deconstruction cannot. The pattern is the one the longer history of states and composers always shows, and the series survey of [music and politics across the centuries](/2010/07/21/music-and-politics-through-history/) traces the same custody battle over other men's names: the argument is never only about the notes, because the notes cannot defend themselves and the name is worth defending them for.

The performance world keeps its own version of the franchise. A Russian manner in this music, weighty, dark, unhurried, is marketed by orchestras and record labels as authenticity, the sound of the soil, although the manner descends in large part from the same twentieth-century performance tradition examined earlier in this article, which the scores do not mandate and which conductors of every nationality now learn from recordings. The brand sells the dispute's Western pole as its Russian pole, German-trained weight rebadged as national essence, and the transaction is so smooth that nobody involved experiences it as a contradiction. It is the dispute's most successful modern product: the sound of an argument, sold as the sound of a country.

What neither the branding nor the scholarship can do is settle the original question, because the original question was malformed. Russian or Western presumes that a composer's music carries a nationality the way a passport does, one stamp, inspectable at the border. The record shows a man formed by two inheritances and honest about both, whose music was fought over by the parties that owned the stamps. The parties are the subject. He is the territory. With that, the aesthetic and national arguments have both been heard, and the article turns to the third, in which the custody was exercised not by critics or movements but by editors, with scissors.

## His sexuality and the documentary record

The third argument differs from the other two in one respect that changes everything about how it must be handled: its subject is not a matter of interpretation at all. Tchaikovsky's homosexuality is a documented biographical fact, documented in the first instance by himself. His letters to his brothers Modest and Anatoly discuss his desires, his temptations, his resolutions and his lapses in the plain language of a man writing to the two people from whom he hid nothing. His diaries record the same life in shorthand. There is no code to crack and no inference to draw; there is a record to read, and for most of a century the record was kept from readers by the people who published it. This article reports the fact the way the documents state it, neither as scandal nor as key to the music, because both of those treatments are forms of the same custody.

Alexander Poznansky, the scholar whose archival work at Klin restored this record to print, established its contours beyond serious dispute. The composer understood himself from youth, struggled against his nature in the terms his upbringing supplied, attempted in 1877 to cure himself by marriage, to Antonina Miliukova, and produced a catastrophe that collapsed within weeks and shadowed both lives permanently. His deepest documented attachment of his last years was to his nephew Vladimir Davydov, the Bob of the letters, to whom the Sixth Symphony is dedicated and whose place in the correspondence is that of the central person of the composer's emotional world. The inner circle knew: the family knew, his publisher knew, the household knew. What the record shows is not a secret life in the melodramatic sense but a private one, lived in the open among intimates and closed only to print.

The marriage of 1877 is the record's severest test, because it is where the private fact became a public event and had to be narrated by somebody. The letters of that summer survive in his hand: the decision taken in a spirit of experiment and desperation that he analysed on paper with terrible clarity, the wedding, the immediate collapse of his nerves, the flight abroad, the family's management of a separation that was never a divorce. Read whole, the episode is not a mystery. It is a documented disaster, explicable at every step by the pressure a man of his nature was under to be other than he was. Read in the redacted form in which the world received it for ninety years, it is a gothic blank, a bride, a breakdown, a flight, no cause given, and into that blank the mythologies poured. The difference between the two readings is the entire subject of the next three sections, and it is why the restorers of this record describe their work not as interpretation but as repair. One detail completes the section's irony. Modest, the brother who received the frankest of the letters and who knew everything they contain, is the same Modest who then wrote the biography that closed the door. The first custodian was not an enemy or a bureaucrat. He was the confidant, protecting his brother's name with the same hand that had received his brother's truth, and every later custodian inherited both halves of that gesture.

Why does a legacy debate have to carry this section at all? Because the erasure of the record is one of the three arguments, and because claims about the music were built for decades on the edited life. The symphonies were read as the overflow of a suffering that criticism could not name and therefore mythologised, into nerves, into fate, into a doomed marriage, into anything the printed page allowed. When the record was restored, a second mythology rushed in to replace the first, in which the sexuality became the single explanation of everything, the key that turns every lock in the music. Both mythologies are custodial. The first protected an emblem; the second sells a revelation. The documents support neither. They support a man, professionally formidable and privately divided, whose letters about money, deadlines, dinners and despair sit on the same pages as his reflections on his nature, and whose music has to be argued about on musical evidence, which is what the rest of this article does.

## How Soviet editing handled it

### Did Soviet censors alter Tchaikovsky's biography?

Yes, systematically and over decades. The collected editions of his letters and the published diaries were cut against the surviving manuscripts, removing passages about his private life without marking the omissions, and the standard biography inherited a portrait from which the centre had been removed. The manuscripts survived intact at Klin, which is what later made restoration possible.

The template was set before the Soviet Union existed, by the first custodian. Modest Tchaikovsky's biography of his brother, published in 1900 to 1902, is a work of devotion and of genuine documentary value that nonetheless performs the original act of custody: it presents the marriage as an inexplicable error, the suffering as constitutional, and the private life as a closed door, and its authority as the brother's own account made the closed door official. Every later editor worked inside Modest's architecture. The Soviet apparatus did not invent the redaction; it inherited a redacted brother and industrialised him.

The industrial stage had three instruments. The first was the published diary: the diaries appeared in 1923, already cut, with the entries touching his nature and his attachments omitted or thinned past recognition. The second was the great collected edition of his writings, whose volumes of letters appeared across the middle decades of the century under state editorship and state rules, and in which passages were removed silently, sentences vanishing between manuscript and page with no ellipsis to confess the surgery, and some letters not printed at all. The third was the law's climate: after 1934 the Soviet state had recriminalised the very thing the letters discussed, and no editor in that jurisdiction could have printed the passages whatever his private wishes. The result was a documentary record with a man-shaped hole in it, and a musicology obliged to write around the hole, explaining the suffering it could see by causes it was permitted to name: the marriage, the nerves, the sensitivity, the fate programme of the Fourth Symphony, anything with a permit.

The custody, it must be added, was exported. The Western record of the life was built downstream of the Russian one and inherited its shape without knowing it had one. The first English life, Rosa Newmarch's of 1906, was written with the family's cooperation and Modest's blessing and observed the family discretion as a condition of its access. The diaries reached English readers in 1945 in a translation made from the already cut Russian edition, so that an American student of that year read the redaction twice removed and took it for a voice. Biographers in the West were not censors; they were customers of a censored supply, and the distinction matters for the moral bookkeeping of this history. The Soviet editors cut the texts. The rest of the world merely failed, for most of a century, to ask whether the texts it had were the texts there were, a failure the more striking because the manuscripts' existence at Klin was never a secret. Access was the border, and access was granted rarely, late, and usually to scholars prepared to be told what the archive was for.

The technique matters as much as the fact. A censored text that confesses its cuts can still be used, carefully, as evidence; the reader discounts and reads around the marks. The Soviet editions' deeper offence against scholarship was the silence of the cuts, which converted an edited record into a false one, since generations of honest scholars abroad built arguments on texts they believed complete. When Western writers of the middle century speculated wildly about the composer's inner life, they were speculating into a vacuum that had been manufactured for them, and some of the wildest pages of the older literature are the vacuum's fault rather than the writers'. Custody, exercised thoroughly enough, falsifies not only its own country's record but every record that trusts it.

## What was removed and from where

Here the shareable fact of this article lands, and it is stated plainly because it deserves to be repeated: through much of the twentieth century Soviet editions of Tchaikovsky's letters and diaries were published with material about his private life removed, while the same cultural apparatus promoted him as a national emblem. This is the fifth rung of the ladder, and the detail behind it is a matter of collation, of laying the printed page beside the manuscript page and watching the sentences disappear. Poznansky's work in the Klin archive did that laying side by side, and the pattern of the cuts is itself a document, a map of exactly what the emblem could not be allowed to contain.

The removals cluster where a reader of this article would predict. From the letters to Modest and Anatoly went the passages in which the composer discusses his desires and his conduct in his own words, the self-reproaches, the coded and then increasingly uncoded reports, the accounts of attachments to particular men. From the diaries went entries of the same character, the private register of a life whose public register was being simultaneously monumentalised. From the correspondence of the marriage crisis went the full measure of what he understood himself to have done and why. What remained was copious, and that was the technique's elegance: the editions were fat, learned, annotated, apparently exhaustive, and the hole was invisible precisely because so much surrounded it. A reader could spend a year in the published letters and emerge knowing the price of every score and the menu of every tour, and nothing of the centre.

The letters to his nephew show the system's finest grain, because they were too important to suppress whole and too compromising to print whole. The correspondence with Vladimir Davydov contains the record of the composer's last creative years, the progress of the ballets and the Sixth Symphony, material no collected edition could omit and remain credible. It also contains the emotional centre of the life, in terms no Soviet editor could pass. The solution, visible in the collation, was to print the letters with their hearts removed: premieres, publishers and travel plans intact, endearments, confidences and self-knowledge gone, so that the most important correspondence of the life reads in the Soviet text as the business file of an uncle. A falsification that preserves the facts and deletes the meaning is harder to detect than a crude one, and it shaped the scholarly picture for longer, because specialists who checked the edition against their own knowledge found the facts correct and stopped checking. The collation, when it came, had to be done by a man willing to distrust a text that was accurate everywhere it was safe to be accurate.

Two features of the removal deserve emphasis because they determine what could later be done about it. The first is that the manuscripts survived. The scissors worked on print, not on paper: the archive at Klin, the house museum founded from his last home, preserved the letters and diaries whole, so that the record was suppressed but never destroyed, and the falsification was always, in principle, reversible by anyone granted the boxes and the patience. The second is that the cuts were systematic rather than thorough in the manner of panic. Material of merely personal embarrassment, money troubles, professional spite, the composer's complaints about his digestion and his rivals, passed into print freely. The editors were not protecting a reputation in general. They were removing one subject, with a thoroughness that tells the modern reader exactly where the danger was judged to lie, and the judgement was the state's, renewed by each generation of editors as faithfully as if it had been written into the edition's charter, which in effect it had been.

## When and how the record was restored

Restoration came from the archive outward, and its turning points can be dated. Poznansky's research at Klin through the 1980s produced first the scholarly detonation of his 1988 essay on the death, which treated the documentary record whole, and then his biography of 1991, The Quest for the Inner Man, the first life built openly on the unexpurgated letters and diaries. As the Soviet Union ended, access widened from a privilege negotiated by one persistent scholar into a general scholarly condition: the manuscripts could be consulted, the cuts could be catalogued in print, and new editions, in Russia and in translation, began the long work of replacing the doctored texts with complete ones. The process was neither instant nor total, since a century of print is not recalled like a defective part, and doctored volumes still sit on the world's shelves doing their quiet work. But the direction reversed for good. After Poznansky, a biographer writes the life whole or explains why not.

Collation on that scale generates a mountain of private working paper, variants, transcriptions, lists of cuts keyed to manuscripts, and scholars doing it have practical reasons to keep such notes both ordered and confidential, which is the straightforward use case for the offline encrypted notes app obtained from [the VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/). The tool matters here only as an example of what restoration actually consists of at the desk: not a revelation but an accumulation of small, checkable, securely kept facts, each one a sentence restored to its letter.

The lag between the restoration and its absorption is itself part of the record now. Poznansky's biography appeared in 1991; the reference works, school texts and programme notes of the world went on retailing the redacted life for years afterwards, because print culture moves at the speed of its slowest shelf. Wiley's biography of 2009 is instructive precisely because it is unremarkable about the private record: it treats the letters as letters, the marriage as a documented mistake, the attachments as facts of the life with consequences for the work's circumstances, and it reserves its severity for the places where evidence runs out. That tone, impossible in a Soviet edition and startling in an English one of fifty years earlier, is what a restored record sounds like once it has been absorbed: not revelation, not scandal, but the ordinary texture of a life fully on the table. The distance between the 1906 life written under family licence and the 2009 life written after the archive is the distance this whole argument has travelled.

The restoration's effect on the other arguments is the point the legacy debate keeps missing. It did not merely add a biographical chapter. It changed the evidence base under the aesthetic argument, dissolving the confessional portrait on which the sentimentality charge had leaned, and it changed the evidence base under the death dispute, since the allegation there depends for its motive on the very private life the editions had hidden. A restored record can be used by myth-makers of the new school as readily as the redacted one was used by the old, and it has been: the suffering composer of the Soviet editions became, in some of the writing of the 1990s, the martyr composer of the restored ones, with the same symphonies re-read as documents of the same life under new management. The documents resist both managements, for readers willing to let them. That willingness is what the death dispute, taken up next, tests most severely, because there the documents are thinnest and the temptation to let a theory of the man outrun them is strongest.

## The contested account of his death

### Why is Tchaikovsky's death still debated?

Because the contemporary documents and a later allegation cannot both be true, and no procedure exists for generating new evidence. The physicians of 1893 left a clear account; the alternative rests on a chain of retelling first printed in 1981. The dispute persists because it carries the weight of the whole argument about who owns his story.

The settled facts are few and should be stated with the plainness they deserve. He conducted the first performance of his Sixth Symphony in St Petersburg on 28 October 1893. Within days he was ill. He died on 6 November 1893, and the cause announced by his attending physicians was cholera, in a city where cholera was epidemic that season. He was fifty-three. The funeral was a state occasion, the burial place of honour, the grief public and enormous. Nothing in that paragraph is contested by any party, and nothing in it will be embellished here: the circumstances of a death are documents, not material, and this article's practice throughout is to report the record and stop.

The premiere's own record matters to what followed, because the farewell reading of the Sixth was manufactured in the days after the death, not at the premiere itself. The audience on 28 October received the new symphony respectfully and with puzzlement, particularly at a finale that dies away instead of blazing; the critics were divided in the ordinary way. The second performance, given under Eduard Nápravník a fortnight after the funeral, was received as a memorial rite, and the puzzlement reorganised itself as prophecy: the symphony became a requiem the composer had written for himself, its ending a foreknown exit. Rimsky-Korsakov's memoirs preserve the profession's genuine shock and its encounters with the composer in his last days, documents written by a colleague with no theory to sell, and they record a working musician at the height of his fame making plans. Plans are not proof of anything about a death, and this article draws no inference from them beyond the one they support: the farewell symphony was an interpretation imposed by bereavement on a puzzled first hearing, and interpretations imposed in grief are data about the grievers. That is the soil in which, nine decades later, a far stronger claim could grow.

What is contested is whether the announced cause is the true one. In 1981 there appeared in print, in the West, an alternative account, brought forward by Alexandra Orlova, a Soviet musicologist who had emigrated two years earlier and who said she had carried the story for years after hearing it from a source she traced to the composer's own generation. The alternative account alleges a concealed ending arranged by men acting to protect an institution and a throne from scandal. It has been answered in detail by Poznansky and weighed, with different conclusions about its status, by other scholars. The two positions and the evidence are the business of the next two sections. Here, at the opening of the dispute, the third open question of this article is posed, and it is not what happened, because that may be unknowable. It is why the argument matters so much more than its evidence can support: why does a dispute about a cause of death carry so much weight? It opens here and closes after the evidence has been weighed, when the answer, that the death dispute is the other three disputes in factual dress, can be stated as a finding rather than as a suspicion.

## What the two principal positions claim

The first position is the documented one. It holds that the composer died of cholera, contracted in the ordinary way in a city in the grip of the disease, and that the announcement made at the time by the physicians who attended him is what it appears to be: a medical statement by named men, published, uncontradicted by any contemporary document, and consistent with the accounts of the household and with his brother's biography. Its most detailed modern defender is Poznansky, whose examination of the record argues that the timeline, the symptoms as reported in the press and in correspondence, and the conduct of everyone involved are coherent as they stand, and that nothing in the archives he spent years reading requires or rewards a second story. On this position the matter is not open. It is a settled fact with a loud afterlife.

The second position is the allegation. Orlova reported that she had been told, through a chain she described as reaching back to a fellow graduate of the Imperial School of Jurisprudence, the composer's own school, that in the autumn of 1893 a complaint concerning the composer's private conduct was on its way towards the Tsar; that a group of his old schoolfellows, convened as a self-appointed court of honour, judged that the scandal had to be stopped; and that the composer, under that judgement, ended his own life, after which the cholera account was agreed among the family, the doctors and the authorities as the story the public would receive. Let the status of every element be exact. This was alleged, decades after the event, by a chain of retelling. It was never charged in any proceeding, never tested in any court or inquiry, never proved by any document, and it is denied by the defenders of the record as a fabrication built on gossip. David Brown, the standard English biographer, gave the allegation the most serious hearing it has received from a scholar of his standing, and in his final volume declined to close the question, treating the composer's despairing state of mind in his last year as part of the evidence and the official account as less complete than it presents itself. Roland John Wiley, whose 2009 biography is the most recent full weighing by a specialist, reviewed the whole file and found the allegation unproven, a structure of hearsay that collapses at its load-bearing points, leaving the documented account standing by default of any rival with documents of its own.

The two positions are therefore not symmetrical, and the article does the reader no service by pretending otherwise. One is a contemporary record, multiply attested, published at the time. The other is a single chain of hearsay, first printed eighty-eight years after the death, whose transmission cannot be audited because its links are dead and its sources unnamed or unverifiable. Symmetry of tone is owed to both; symmetry of weight is not, and confusing the two is how allegations harden into legends. What can fairly be said for the allegation is that it was brought forward in good faith by a scholar who believed the official culture had lied about this man as a matter of routine, and that the official culture had, demonstrably, lied about his life in print for decades. The redaction is the allegation's best friend. A state that cut the letters taught the world to discount its announcements, and the discount, once learned, was applied to the death certificate too.

## What the evidence supports and what it cannot settle

Weigh the file as an evidence problem and its shape is quickly seen. For the cholera account there are the attending physicians' statements, made at the time and in public; the context of an epidemic, independently documented; the household's correspondence; the absence of any contemporary document, in a capital that lived on diaries and gossip, recording suspicion of the kind the allegation requires. That absence is an argument from silence and should be labelled as one, but silence has weight when the alleged event is a conspiracy that would have had to include doctors, family, schoolfellows, police and palace, each of whom would have had to keep the secret not for a season but for good, in a society whose elites wrote everything down. For the allegation there is the chain itself, and Poznansky's examination of it enumerated the faults a hearsay structure accumulates: persons placed at meetings the records put elsewhere, dates that drift between tellings, a source at the origin who cannot be questioned and whose own route to the knowledge is never explained. A story can survive one such fault. It cannot survive being composed of them.

What the evidence cannot settle must be stated with equal care, because the honesty of this article depends on the boundary being drawn in the right place. The allegation is constructed in a way that makes it unfalsifiable: every missing document is absorbed as proof of the suppression it alleges, so no archive, however open, can ever refute it to a committed believer's satisfaction. Unfalsifiability is not a mark of truth; in evidence work it is a mark of a claim that has left the jurisdiction of evidence altogether. No exhumation has been performed and none is proposed by any party with standing; no independent medical examination was made in 1893 beyond the attending physicians; the physical question is closed forever. What remains open, and will remain open, is only the interpretive question of how much credit a hearsay chain deserves against a contemporary record, and that question is answered not by new facts but by the reader's standard of proof. This article's standard has been stated throughout: alleged is alleged, proved is proved, and the distance between them is not a technicality.

One more distinction, because the dispute's literature keeps blurring it. It does not follow from the allegation's weakness that the composer's last years were untroubled, or that the despair documented in his letters of the period is an invention of myth-makers. The record of his state of mind is real and it belongs to his biography, where the life article in this cluster treats it in full; the [documented life and career](/2011/06/07/pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky-life-and-career/) is the place for that narrative in its proper proportion. What the despair cannot do is serve as evidence for a specific account of a death. Unhappiness is not a mechanism. The defenders of the allegation ask the reader to move from a documented inner state to an undocumented event, and the move is precisely the one the stance of this series forbids: it converts a fact about a life into a verdict about a death, with the conversion performed by sympathy. Sympathy is not evidence, in either direction.

## Why this dispute became proxy for the others

The third open question now closes. Why does a dispute about a cause of death carry so much weight? Because it is the only one of the three great arguments that can be phrased as a question of fact with a yes or a no, and every party to the interpretive wars migrated into it, bringing their wars with them. This is the sixth and last rung of the ladder: the argument over his death became the place where the other disputes turned into a factual claim, which is why it has outlasted the evidence available to settle it. A factual claim can be won. Custody can be seized by it. If the allegation were proved, the entire official inheritance, the emblem, the editions, the schoolroom portrait, would stand convicted of a foundational lie, and the party that proved it would own the composer outright. If the record holds, the custodians keep him. Both sides understood the stakes in exactly those terms, which is why a question with an eighty-eight-year-old hearsay chain on one side and a death certificate on the other has generated libraries.

Notice the dependence that locks the disputes together. The allegation's engine is the private life: there is no scandal to suppress, no court to convene, without the documented fact of his nature and the documented terror his society attached to its exposure. For as long as the editions hid the life, the allegation lacked its motive in public and could not be fully told; the restoration of the record, Poznansky's work, is therefore the unlikely godparent of the very theory Poznansky spent his authority refuting. He printed the life whole and thereby made the death story tellable, and then had to fight the story with the documents he had freed. There is no neater demonstration in this whole debate that evidence does not control its own uses. Custodians come in pairs: the editor who hides a record and the theorist who builds on its return both stand between the reader and the man, and both must be read through, not obeyed.

The proxy structure also explains the dispute's peculiar emotional register, which everyone who has written on it remarks and few explain. To defend the cholera record feels, to its defenders, like defending the possibility that documents mean anything; to press the allegation feels, to its pressers, like refusing to let the state that censored a man's life have the last word on his death as well. Both feelings are honourable and both are beside the evidential point, and they are the same feelings that powered the formalist demotion and the nationalist border war, the feeling that this composer's definition is a prize worth a fight. The fights are the continuity. The ostensible subjects, structure, nation, biography, death, are the occasions. A reader who has learned to hear the custody claim underneath each occasion has learned the single skill this article exists to teach, and can now be given the last task the debate sets: the attempt, made honestly and on declared criteria, to say what his standing actually is.

## The Tchaikovsky overrated debate in its sharpest form: the greatest-composer claim stated on criteria

Every argument so far has been, at bottom, a quarrel about measurement conducted without declaring the instrument. The greatest-composer claim is where the habit does its worst damage, because the claim is absolute and the instruments are never mentioned at all. Is he one of the greatest composers? Greatest at what, measured how, against which comparison class, on which evidence, with whose performance tradition included in the sample? Asked in that form, the question dissolves into its criteria, and the dissolution is the useful part: a listener who has watched the criteria being chosen knows exactly what any answer is worth. This section and the three that follow therefore do the thing the whole tradition declined to do. They name the criteria in advance, three of them, melodic invention, formal command and cultural reach, and apply each one to the composer with the same evidence rules used for his rivals, before any conclusion is allowed to form.

The criteria are not arbitrary, and their selection is itself stated so it can be challenged. Melodic invention is included because it is the criterion his enemies never denied him, the floor of the debate. Formal command is included because it is the criterion his enemies always cited, the ceiling they set. Cultural reach is included because it is the criterion the public applies and the critics distrust, and a fair assessment has no business excluding the public's evidence merely because the public supplied it. A criterion conspicuously missing is influence on later composers, missing because it belongs to a different article's custody, the cluster's account of [his influence on music](/2011/07/21/pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky-influence-on-music/), and conflating it with standing is one of the ways rankings are rigged. The wider comparison across all the candidates, conducted on declared criteria of the same kind, is maintained in the series survey of the [greatest composers of all time](/2025/04/07/greatest-composers-of-all-time/), against which the single-figure application here can be checked.

One more piece of hygiene before the criteria are applied. The comparison class must be held still. It does no work to measure him against Bach on counterpoint, against Wagner on scale and against Schubert on song, choosing each rival for the ground he is strongest on; that is the anthology method of the hostile textbook, and it convicts every composer in the pantheon in turn, since nobody is strongest on all grounds. The class for what follows is the small set of composers whose names fill the same sentence as his when the greatest are listed, and each criterion will be applied to the set, not to a champion picked for the occasion. With the rules declared, the first criterion is the one on which the debate, uniquely, is nearly over.

## Melodic invention as the criterion

On melodic invention the hostile and friendly traditions agree, and in a debate where agreement is this rare its presence is evidence in itself. Hanslick, who found the Violin Concerto's finale an offence, never pretended the man could not invent a tune. Cui, who spent decades prosecuting him for the nationalists, granted the melodic gift while deploring its employment. The textbook that demoted his forms let his melodies pass as a fact of nature. When every custodian in this article, the formalist, the nationalist and the state that packaged him for export, concedes the same point, the point may be taken as settled: as an inventor of melody he stands in the first rank of his century, and the argument is only about what that rank is worth.

What the melodies are, technically, explains why the concession was forced. They are not folk tunes transcribed, though he could do that supremely, and they are not Italianate arias with Russian captions. Their signature is a long span sustained by sequence that behaves like breathing rather than machinery, periodic phrases stretched asymmetrically so that the expected return arrives late and lands the harder, and a gift for the second subject that redefines the movement around it: the horn theme of the Fifth's slow movement, the love theme of Romeo and Juliet, the second theme of the Sixth's first movement are the passages by which listeners who cannot read a score navigate the works, and each is constructed, not found. The construction can be audited in the scores, which is why this criterion, unlike the formal one, never generated a war of standards: there is no rival gauge on which the tunes measure small.

The transmission confirms the rank from the receiving end. Rachmaninoff, the next generation's supreme melodist in the Russian line, built his spans openly in this school, and his Second Piano Concerto's slow movement is unthinkable without the example of the older man's cantabile. Stravinsky, whose aesthetic was in most respects a rebellion against everything the nineteenth century held dear, made a documented exception here: he championed the ballets, worked on The Sleeping Beauty's materials for Diaghilev, praised the music's Russianness in terms the Balakirev circle would have envied, and built his own ballet manner partly on ground this composer had surveyed. When the melodists of the next age, including its revolutionaries, queue to acknowledge a debt, the criterion can be closed. On melodic invention, stated plainly: first rank, by the testimony of the only jury fully qualified to judge it, his peers and successors in the same craft.

## Formal command as the criterion

Formal command is the criterion on which the verdict is standard-dependent, and the honest treatment is to report both readings rather than to launder one of them into a fact. Under the inherited standard, development-weighted, academic, the standard of the syllabus that produced the structural objection, he places below the German masters of the sonata and knows it: the transitions vamp, the climaxes accumulate, the recapitulations restore. Under the corrected standard, the one that asks whether a work's parts are proportioned to its own stated design, he places high: the Sixth's trajectory is exact, the Fifth's frame is load-bearing, Romeo and Juliet compresses a five-act tragedy into a single span whose three materials collide and resolve with an economy any opera composer would envy, and the ballets construct across whole evenings. A ranking that gives one number for formal command without declaring which standard produced it is not a measurement. It is the custody dispute wearing a lab coat.

The ballets are the evidence both standards must face, because they cannot be explained away as charm. The Sleeping Beauty is a three-act structure in which opposed thematic materials, the Lilac Fairy's world against Carabosse's, are stated, developed through the drama and resolved in the apotheosis, with the whole held at the scale of a full evening in the theatre. That is formal command by any definition that is not rigged in advance, and the fact that it was exercised in a genre the academy held cheap says more about the academy's exchange rates than about the composer's capacities. The academy price of a symphony was high and of a ballet low, so command displayed in a ballet was discounted at the border. Exchange rates are set by institutions. They are not properties of the goods.

The verdict on this criterion, then, is a finding about criteria. He is a formal master of the second kind beyond serious dispute and a formal apprentice of the first kind by his own occasional confession, and the two statements together describe a composer whose forms are his own and must be judged as his own or not judged at all. Brown's life work on the scores stands as the demonstration that the second-kind judgement can be made rigorous, passage by passage, and the objection's survival in casual writing stands as the demonstration that rigour is not what keeps a verdict alive. What keeps it alive is usefulness to the verdict's custodians, and the reader has met those.

## Cultural reach as the criterion

Cultural reach is the criterion nobody in the academy will defend in print and everybody in the concert business applies in private. By it, the measurement is not close. The symphonies, concertos and ballets hold a share of the world's orchestral and theatrical life that only a handful of composers in any century have matched: the Nutcracker alone, in its annual occupation of stages from Moscow to Chicago, may put more listeners in seats in a single December than most canonical figures draw in a decade. His name is on the conservatory of his country's capital and on the competition by which young pianists and violinists are still certified to the world. The melodies have passed out of the repertory into the furniture of the culture, quoted, arranged and half-recognised by millions who could not name their source, which is the furthest reach music can have.

The honesty clause for this criterion is that reach is partly manufactured, and the manufacturing has been documented in this article at length. The Soviet emblem machine promoted him with a state's resources; the competition and the conservatory name are institutions, not spontaneous tributes; the ballet's seasonal empire is a triumph of programming as much as it is of composition. But the contamination cuts both ways, and the academy that discounts his reach for being promoted never discounts the reach of composers whose promotion was performed by the academy itself, through the syllabus, the examination and the subsidised canon concert. Beethoven's ubiquity is also an institutional achievement. It is simply an older one, whose machinery has had longer to become invisible. Reach measures the joint product of the music and its custodians, for every composer in the class, and applied evenly it places Tchaikovsky in the smallest company: the composers a civilisation uses to recognise itself.

There is a final datum under this criterion that the penalty literature never quotes. His reach was achieved against the critical weather, not with it. The works conquered the halls while the textbooks were demoting them, which means the reach cannot be explained as the academy's gift or the state's alone; the state's own grip failed in half of Europe and all of America, where no ministry programmed him and the audiences came anyway, decade after decade, dragging the institutions behind them. Whatever cultural reach is evidence of, in this case it is evidence that survived cross-examination by two hostile establishments. On this criterion the verdict is first rank, and the only remaining question is why the criterion is so seldom allowed into the courtroom, a question the reader can now answer unaided.

## What a fair assessment would require

A fair assessment of this composer is possible, and its requirements can be listed in prose, each one the negation of a failure documented in the preceding sections. It would require the criteria to be named before the evidence is heard, and applied identically to every member of the comparison class, so that no rival is measured on his best ground against the subject's worst. It would require the performance tradition to be subtracted, the scores heard as their markings direct, so that a century of other men's rubato is not entered as the composer's excess. It would require the documentary record to be used whole, the restored letters and diaries, neither hidden by custodians nor wielded as a key that supposedly unlocks the music, since a life is evidence about a life and only the scores are evidence about the scores. It would require the national categories to be declared as the constructions they are, so that a man is not convicted of failing an essence invented by his opponents. It would require the popularity datum to be quarantined: an audience's love is a fact about audiences and about the reach criterion, and it is not a solvent to be poured over the other criteria in either direction, neither as proof of greatness nor as proof of shallowness. And it would require the dispute about his death to be excluded from the assessment entire, because a composer's standing is a property of his work and its reception, and the manner of a death, however it is resolved, is not a premise in any argument about either.

The requirements are easier to keep visible when they are set side by side, which is why teachers who take this debate into a seminar room tend to reduce it to a comparative table in [the browser-based presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), criteria down one side and evidence across the top, where a hidden standard has nowhere to hide. The device is humble and the effect is not: half the verdicts in this article's history could not have survived being tabulated, because tabulation forces the criterion into the open, and the open is where custody goes to die.

What such an assessment would find has been stated criterion by criterion, and it will not be aggregated here into a single rank, because the aggregation is the original sin of the genre. It would find a melodist of the first rank by the concession of his enemies, a formal intelligence of a high and different order whose measurement exposes the measurer, a reach matched by a handful of composers in the history of the art, and a body of work whose worst pages are the occasional ones every prolific professional produces and whose best are load-bearing walls of the repertory. It would also find that none of this was ever really in dispute among people who worked with the evidence. The dispute was among the custodians, about custody. The last section returns the composer to the only custody that has ever improved him: the reader's own, exercised on the scores and the documents directly.

## Three claims of ownership, one composer

The close of a debate article in this series is structural, and this one can now be built, because every element of it has been laid out in evidence. Three claims of ownership have been made over this composer, and each can be stated as what would have to be true for the claim to be just. For the aesthetic claim to be just, the German formal standard would have to be a law of music rather than a tradition's preference, and the performance tradition would have to be innocent of the excess charged to the scores; the first is a claim about history that the history refutes, and the second is a claim about fact that the markings refute. For the national claim to be just, Russianness would have to be a substance that exists independently of the parties who defined it, measurable in a score the way key is measurable; the institutional record shows a definition manufactured for a border war and inherited by states and brands, which is not a substance but a flag. For the biographical claim to be just, in its first form, the custody of a dead man's private life would have to be a legitimate act of care even when it falsifies the scholarly record of the world; and in its second, factual form, a chain of hearsay first printed eighty-eight years after a death would have to outrank the contemporary documents of that death. Neither form survives the standards the claimants themselves use on every other subject.

What survives the setting aside of all three claims is not a smaller composer but a freer one. There remain the scores, whose evidence any listener can audit, and the documents, whose restoration any reader can verify against the manuscripts at Klin, and a standing that no longer needs to be issued by a custodian because it can be re-derived, criterion by criterion, from those two sources. The state that named the conservatory for him could not protect him from the formalists. The formalists could not protect the syllabus from the audiences. The audiences could not protect the biography from the editors. And the editors, in the end, could not protect their edition from the archive, because the paper outlasted the permission. That is the one cheerful law in this whole history: documents wait. The composer's definition, fought over for a century and a half by parties who needed it for their own wars, was in the boxes at Klin the entire time, next to the scores, in the house he lived in, indifferent to which custodian was winning. It is still there. The arguments will go on, because the custody is still valuable. But no reader who has followed them to this point need ever again mistake one for the man.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the main criticisms of Tchaikovsky?

Three criticisms have organised the debate for over a century. The first calls the music sentimental, emotion demanded rather than earned. The second calls the forms structurally weak, episodes strung together where a German-trained ear expects development. The third, made by the Balakirev circle in his lifetime, calls him insufficiently Russian, a conservatory product in European dress. Around these orbits a fourth, the popularity penalty, the suspicion that an audience's love is itself evidence against a composer. Each criticism has a custodian, a critical tradition, a national movement or an editorial apparatus, and each survives partly because it serves its custodian. Weighed passage by passage, the descriptive parts of all three are partly true, and the verdicts built on them depend on standards their makers rarely declared.

### Q: Why is Tchaikovsky called sentimental?

The word descends from the German formalist tradition, for which Hanslick's doctrine made feeling legitimate only when embodied in structure, and it hardened into a textbook verdict repeated long after the argument behind it was forgotten. Two accidents fed it. His music was read as confession, the overflow of a suffering man's inner life, and a confession can always be accused of excess in a way a construction cannot. And the performance tradition that grew around the symphonies, dragging tempi, milked cadences, lunging climaxes, manufactured a lachrymose surface the scores do not require, which critics then described as the music itself. Set against the strict definition of sentimentality, feeling unearned by its object, the charge fits some salon pieces and fails the major scores, whose climaxes are built by processes a listener can audit.

### Q: Do the scores ask for an emotional performance style?

They ask for emotional intensity inside strict proportion, which is a different request from the one the tradition obeyed. His tempo markings warn against dragging: the Sixth Symphony's first movement is marked Allegro non troppo, an instruction to keep moving, and its tender second theme is marked to be played sweetly in tempo, not outside it. In the Fifth's slow movement he wrote the permission to linger into the score and bounded it, Andante cantabile con alcuna licenza, with some licence, the licence measured and local. Preparing The Sleeping Beauty he sent the theatre timings for the numbers, the act of a composer engineering effect through proportion. Conductors who play the markings, as Mravinsky did, get intensity without indulgence, which indicates where the indulgence was coming from.

### Q: Who first made the structural criticism of Tchaikovsky?

No single critic owns it, because it has two independent sources that later merged. From the Russian side, César Cui and the Balakirev circle attacked the young composer in the 1860s and 1870s for routine, academic workmanship, the opposite crime from looseness but an early form of the competence charge. From the German side, the formalist tradition descending from Hanslick and codified in the conservatory syllabus supplied the durable version: themes stated rather than developed, transitions filled with sequence, climaxes reached by repetition. That version passed into the textbooks and became the default verdict in classrooms for a century. Gerald Abraham, the English scholar sympathetic to the composer, repeated the concession about episodic construction so honestly that hostile critics quoted him ever after, which is how the criticism acquired its respectable passport.

### Q: Is the structural criticism accepted today?

As a description of method, largely yes; as a verdict, largely no. Analysts grant the seams the criticism points to: developments that are short against their expositions, transitions that work by sequence, a finale in the Fifth whose motto returns by imposition as much as by logic. What modern scholarship disputes is the standard that converts those observations into a conviction. David Brown's analytical work followed the symphonies' actual procedures, recurrence, framing and trajectory, and found coherent architectures that the development gauge cannot register. The ballets, above all The Sleeping Beauty, demonstrate large-scale command that no reading of the evidence can deny him. The criticism survives in casual writing and programme notes; in the scholarly literature it has been demoted from a finding to a position, one whose national origin and institutional history are part of the record.

### Q: Does popularity count against a composer?

Only by a logical sleight of hand. To hold an audience's love against a composer is to make a claim about audiences, that they are poor or corruptible judges, and then enter that claim as a finding about the music, which cannot be true or false depending on who is listening. Popularity is real evidence on exactly one question, cultural reach, where it is the whole measurement, and on that criterion his standing is in the smallest class. On every other criterion it is contamination, whether it is used to convict him of shallowness, as the textbook tradition used it, or to acquit him of the structural charge, as his box office is sometimes asked to do. The fair procedure quarantines the datum: count the audiences under reach, and keep them out of the analysis of the notes.

### Q: Why did the Balakirev circle object to Tchaikovsky?

The objection was institutional before it was aesthetic. The circle, Balakirev, Cui, Mussorgsky, Borodin and Rimsky-Korsakov with Stasov as publicist, was a voluntary association defining Russian music against the German-trained conservatory, and Tchaikovsky was the conservatory's prize product, Anton Rubinstein's pupil, a professor at Moscow, fluent in every European procedure the circle distrusted. Cui's reviews policed the border premiere by premiere. Yet the circle's head behaved like a recruiter rather than an executioner: Balakirev supervised Romeo and Juliet in detail in 1869 and later pressed the Manfred programme on the composer, wanting the gifts captured for the national cause. When Rimsky-Korsakov took a conservatory professorship in 1871 the border proved permeable to everyone. What the circle was defending was the power to define Russianness, and he was the territory it was defended on.

### Q: Was Tchaikovsky considered Russian enough by his contemporaries?

His contemporaries returned a split verdict, and the split ran along institutional lines. The Balakirev circle's press said no, finding him eclectic, academic and European in substance whatever tunes he quoted. The conservatory world said yes, and Hermann Laroche, his classmate and the most literate Russian critic of the day, championed the craftsmanship as a national asset. The public settled its own question at the box office, and the state settled its own in 1888, when the Tsar granted him a pension, an official finding that this composer represented Russia. His folk usage, examined closely, supports the yes more often than the polemic admitted: the birch-tree song that builds the Fourth Symphony's finale and the folk melody at the heart of the First Quartet's Andante cantabile are substance, not costume. Russian enough depended on who was counting.

### Q: What does the record say about Tchaikovsky's private life?

It says, in the composer's own words, that he was homosexual, and it says so without code or ambiguity. His letters to his brothers Modest and Anatoly discuss his nature, his struggles against it and his conduct across his whole adult life, and his diaries keep the same register in private shorthand. The record also documents the attempt of 1877 to cure himself by marriage, to Antonina Miliukova, its collapse within weeks and the permanent separation that followed, and it documents the central attachment of his last years, to his nephew Vladimir Davydov, the dedicatee of the Sixth Symphony. His family, his publisher and his household knew. The fact was closed only to print, by editors, which is a statement about the editions and not about the life. Nothing in the record requires speculation, and nothing in it licenses scandal.

### Q: What did Soviet editors remove from his letters?

They removed the private life, silently and systematically. From the letters to his brothers went the passages discussing his desires and attachments in his own words. From the diaries, published in cut form in 1923, went the entries of the same character. From the correspondence with his nephew Vladimir Davydov went the emotional substance, leaving the business of premieres and publishers intact, so that the most important letters of the life read as an uncle's office file. Some letters were withheld entire. The collected edition of his writings, appearing across the middle decades of the century, made no marks where the cuts fell, so readers could not know what they were missing. The manuscripts survived whole at the Klin archive, which is why every removal could later be identified by collation and the record restored.

### Q: When was the full record restored?

Restoration was a process with a datable turning point rather than a single day. Alexander Poznansky's years of work in the Klin archive produced his essay on the death in 1988 and his biography The Quest for the Inner Man in 1991, the first life built openly on the unexpurgated letters and diaries, and the point after which no scholar could plead the old record in good faith. As the Soviet Union ended, archive access widened and complete texts began to replace the doctored ones in new editions and translations, a replacement that proceeded shelf by shelf rather than by decree. By the time of Roland John Wiley's biography in 2009, a life written whole from the record had become the scholarly norm rather than a provocation. Older doctored volumes remain in circulation, so the restoration is complete in scholarship and still catching up in the world's libraries.

### Q: What are the two positions on Tchaikovsky's death?

The first is the documented record: that he died of cholera on 6 November 1893, contracted during an epidemic in St Petersburg, as his attending physicians announced at the time and as the household accounts and his brother's biography consistently report. Alexander Poznansky is its most detailed modern defender, arguing that the timeline and the documents cohere without remainder. The second is an allegation published by Alexandra Orlova in 1981, on a chain of retelling she traced to the composer's school generation: that former schoolfellows of the Imperial School of Jurisprudence, fearing a scandal on its way to the Tsar, sat as a court of honour and required him to end his own life, after which the cholera account was agreed as a cover. Its status is exact: alleged, never charged, never tested, never proved, and rejected by the record's defenders as hearsay.

### Q: Can the dispute about Tchaikovsky's death be settled?

Not by evidence, because no new evidence can come into existence. No exhumation has been made or proposed by any party with standing, no independent medical examination was performed in 1893, and the allegation's chain of transmission runs through witnesses who died before it reached print. The allegation is also built to be unfalsifiable, since every missing document can be absorbed as proof of the suppression it alleges, and unfalsifiable claims sit outside the jurisdiction of evidence altogether. What readers can settle is their own standard: the contemporary documents, multiply attested and published at the time, against a single hearsay chain printed eighty-eight years later. On any standard applied consistently to other historical questions, the documented account stands and the allegation remains what it has always been, an unproved story whose persistence measures the stakes of the larger argument about who owns this composer's definition.

### Q: Should a listener care about a composer's private life?

A listener should care about it as history and refuse it as a key. The private life of this composer is documented fact, restored after a century of editorial custody, and knowing it corrects the record: the marriage, the attachments and the inner division are part of what happened, and happened to the man who wrote the music. What the life cannot do is adjudicate the music. The symphonies' worth is a property of their notes and of what listeners and analysts can demonstrate from them, and both of the great abuses of the biography make the same error in opposite directions: the Soviet editions hid the life so the music could be an emblem, and the revelation literature sells the life as the explanation of the music. Scores are evidence about scores. Letters are evidence about a life. Keeping the two jurisdictions separate is the whole discipline.

### Q: How do I evaluate a censored primary source?

Begin by asking what stands behind the printed page. A censored edition usually descends from a manuscript tradition, so establish whether the manuscripts survive, where they are held and who has been allowed to collate them. Check whether the edition marks its omissions; silent cuts are the dangerous kind, because they convert an edited text into a false one. Compare editions across jurisdictions and across decades, since censors in different places cut different things and the overlaps reveal the shape of each regime's anxiety. Attend to clustering: removals that concentrate on one subject are a map of what the custodian feared, which is evidence in itself. Prefer editions built on published collation, where the restored passages can be checked, and treat fat, smooth, apparently exhaustive volumes with the most suspicion of all, since completeness is the easiest quality to counterfeit.

### Q: How do I tell a Tchaikovsky marking from a performance habit?

Go to the score and read what is actually printed. His tempo words are the first witness: a movement marked Allegro non troppo that is being played as a dirge is being played against its marking. His metronome-consciousness is the second: this is a composer who sent a theatre the timings of a ballet's numbers, so proportion belongs to the text. Learn the fingerprints of the tradition so you can see them being applied: the unwritten ritardando at every cadence, the tempo lunge into climaxes, the continuous upholstered string sound, the slow movement stretched past its architecture. Those are habits, and their presence in a performance tells you about the conductor's inheritance, not the composer's instructions. A strict recording, Mravinsky's of the last symphonies, set against a traditional one of the same work, makes the boundary audible in a single sitting.

### Q: Which works make the strongest case for Tchaikovsky?

Choose the works that answer each criticism on its own ground. For construction, Romeo and Juliet, where three dramatic materials collide and resolve inside a single span of symphonic logic, and the Sixth Symphony taken whole, whose first movement's trajectory and quiet ending are exact architecture rather than episode. For melodic invention, the Fifth Symphony's slow movement and the second theme of the Sixth's first movement, passages conceded even by his prosecutors. For large-scale command, The Sleeping Beauty, a full evening held together by a thematic system no miniaturist could have built. For the Russian idiom at its most fused, the First String Quartet, whose Andante cantabile grows an entire movement from a song he heard a workman sing. A case assembled across those works is the case the custodians of the demotion never had to face whole.

### Q: Where should a skeptical listener start with Tchaikovsky?

Start where the evidence is strongest and the tradition's interference is easiest to hear. Romeo and Juliet first, in a complete performance rather than an excerpt, because its design is audible to an untrained ear and its famous love theme earns its apotheosis in context. Then the Sixth Symphony played straight, a recording that respects the tempo markings, so that the farewell mythology can be tested against the notes and found to be an addition of the audience. Then a full act of The Sleeping Beauty rather than the suite, since the suite is an editor's selection of charms and the act is the composer's architecture. Leave the 1812 Overture for last, not because it is beneath notice but because it is the one work its composer shared the skeptics' low opinion of, and a verdict formed on it is a verdict on the wrong exhibit.

### Q: How did Tchaikovsky's standing among critics change?

It has described a long arc with a manufactured dip in the middle. In his lifetime he was Russia's most successful composer and a divided critical question, prosecuted by the nationalists and honoured by the state. His death converted the music into a biographical legend, and the legend carried the sentimentality charge into the twentieth century's classrooms, where the textbook tradition demoted him to a gifted populariser with defective forms. The rehabilitation came from evidence: analytical work, David Brown's above all, that followed the scores' actual procedures; historical work by Taruskin and Frolova-Walker that exposed the national categories as constructions; and the restoration of the documentary record by Poznansky, which dissolved the confessional portrait the charges had fed on. The standing that results is not the old one restored but a new one built criterion by criterion, which is why it is harder to reverse.

### Q: What would change the verdict on Tchaikovsky?

The verdict changes whenever one of its inputs changes, and naming the inputs is the answer. It would change if the criteria changed: a musical culture that stopped treating German development as the definition of form would retire the structural verdict without hearing another note, as the analytical literature has largely done. It would change if the performance sample changed, as it does whenever a generation of conductors plays the markings and the manufactured excess drains out of the evidence. It changed already when the documents changed, in the years around 1991, because the confessional portrait underpinning the sentimentality charge could not survive the restored record. What would not change it is a new verdict in the old form, an authority announcing a rank. Ranks issued by custodians are what the whole history warns against; verdicts derived from declared criteria, on the scores and the documents, are the only kind built to last.
