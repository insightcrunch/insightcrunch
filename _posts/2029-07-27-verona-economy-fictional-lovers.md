Verona runs an economy on Romeo and Juliet that no balance sheet in the city fully records, and the parts that can be counted point the same way: the lovers generate enormous volume at small yield per visitor, the scarcest asset in the system is a balcony the city itself attached to a medieval house in the 1930s, and the value the legend creates is captured unevenly, by the municipality that prices the sites, by the hoteliers and restaurateurs who absorb the spillover, and by unpaid volunteers whose labour subsidizes the whole structure. This article audits the money system whole: the visitor counts, the ticket prices, the wedding fees, the letter trade, the souvenir trade, the maintenance bills and the brand. Its verdict, defended against the commodification critics and against the city's own promotional arithmetic alike, is that Verona operates a devotion economy. The product is not deception, because the fabrication is openly documented and the visitors arrive informed. The product is participation in a fiction at the place the fiction names, and the play itself announced that product in its closing scene, when Montague promised to raise Juliet's statue in pure gold while Verona bore the name (5.3). The city has spent a century building the fulfilment of that promise into ticket offices, tariff schedules and festival programming.

## What Verona Sells When It Sells Romeo and Juliet

The first economic fact about the lovers' industry is that nobody owns it. Shakespeare's text sits in the public domain, beyond copyright in every jurisdiction, so no estate collects a royalty when Verona prints the names on a tea towel, stages a wedding under the balcony or licenses a guided walk through the courtyard. The playwright's unprotectable words are the raw material, and the raw material is free. What Verona sells, therefore, cannot be the story. Rivals in Stratford, in London, in any town with a printer can sell the story at the same zero input cost. What Verona sells is location-specific access to the story's named ground: a courtyard on Via Cappello, a sarcophagus in a former convent, a wall for messages, a desk where letters addressed to a dead girl are answered by hand. The commodity is co-presence with the fiction at its fictional address.

Dean MacCannell's sociology of tourism supplies the cleanest model of that commodity. In The Tourist (1976), MacCannell separated every attraction into a sight, the thing itself, and a marker, the information that points at the thing and certifies it as worth seeing. Most heritage economics runs sight-first: the Roman Arena stands in Verona as a genuine sight, two thousand years of masonry that would justify a journey without a word of commentary. The lovers' sites invert the ratio. The balcony is nearly pure marker, a piece of stonework assembled and attached during Antonio Avena's restoration of the house in the late 1930s, as the museum's own documentation and the Smithsonian's account of the site record. The sight, the event the marker commemorates, never occurred, because the girl never existed. Verona's lovers' economy is thus the limiting case of MacCannell's model, an attraction system in which the marker carries the entire commercial weight and the sight contributes nothing at all. The sections that follow test that model against the figures, because the city publishes more numbers about the lovers than the romance of the subject suggests, and the numbers are stranger than the myth.

Francesco Marcorin, the architectural historian whose paper Crafted Identities: Renaissance Verona as a Case-Study was delivered at the Italian Academy of Columbia University in 2017, stated the municipal logic without embarrassment. Marcorin described the house museum as created to launch, in his words, "a new brand in the world: Verona, the city of lovers." That sentence is the industry's founding charter, written by a scholar rather than an advertiser. A brand, unlike a shrine, is managed: priced, segmented, refreshed and defended against substitutes. Verona's conduct across a century, from the 1905 purchase of the house from the Dal Cappello heirs to the courtyard tariff of 2026, reads as brand management conducted with unusual patience. The brand's equity rests on an association no competitor can copy, because Shakespeare fixed the scene with a surveyor's precision in the Prologue: "In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue). Other cities can stage the play. Only one city is named in the second line of it.

The rival model stands in Stratford-upon-Avon, where the Shakespeare economy sells the author's genuine traces: a birthplace house, a grave, a documented life assembled by a charitable trust into ticketed properties. Stratford monetizes biography; Verona monetizes fiction, and the comparison exposes why Verona's asset, for all its fabrication, is the hardier of the two. A biography has one birthplace and one grave, and their appeal is bounded by the author's fame. A fiction's named ground multiplies its market to everyone the story has ever moved, including the far larger public that loves the lovers without curiosity about the man who wrote them, and the fiction can be re-performed, re-filmed and re-read into each new generation without wearing out the address it advertises. Verona did not merely choose the weaker evidence. It chose, or lucked into, the stronger asset class, the one whose demand renews itself in every schoolroom and cinema on earth while asking the city only to keep a courtyard swept and a balcony standing.

## The Founding Investment: Buying a Fiction a House

The public-domain point cuts deeper than a missing royalty. Because the text belongs to everyone, the only way to build a defensible business on it is to own something the text points at, and Verona's founding economic act was exactly that purchase. In 1905 the city bought the house on Via Cappello from the heirs of the Dal Cappello family, the transaction recorded by the Smithsonian's account and by the museum's own history alike. The building was a medieval tower house with a documented past as an inn, the Albergo del Cappello in the records of its working life, and a carved hat, a cappello, on the courtyard arch that let a hopeful etymology marry the address to the Capulets. The city did not buy a sight in MacCannell's sense. The city bought an option: a piece of ground inside the UNESCO-to-be historic centre whose fictional value depended entirely on future investment in markers. Options of that kind are cheap precisely because their value is unbuilt, and the purchase price of an unfashionable inn in 1905 bears no relation to the yield of the asset the city then spent three decades constructing.

The construction was directed by Antonio Avena, director of the civic museums, whose restoration of the house across the late 1930s supplied the marker set the fiction required: the balcony assembled and mounted on the courtyard wall, the Gothic dressing of windows and interiors, the arrangement of rooms as a heroine's residence. Avena's works belonged to the same moment as the first great cinematic demand shock, Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer's Romeo and Juliet of 1936, directed by George Cukor with Norma Shearer and Leslie Howard, which carried the balcony image to a worldwide audience in the very years the balcony itself was being built onto the house. The sequence, documented in the site's chronology and in the Smithsonian's dating of the balcony to 1936, shows municipal investment behaving like any well-timed capital project: supply constructed at the moment demand announced itself. Marcorin's thesis that the museum was created to launch a brand is sometimes read as an accusation of cynicism. Read as economic history, it is a description of competence. The city identified an underpriced association, bought the ground under it for an inn's price, built the missing scenery with a museum director's budget and then collected admissions on the result for eighty years before repricing the courtyard in 2026.

The investment logic extended to the product's props. The house displays the bed identified with Zeffirelli's 1968 film, a piece of cinema furniture that the Smithsonian's account singled out among the museum's holdings, and the room it occupies teaches the asset structure of the whole system: a prop from an adaptation, itself an interpretation of a fiction, installed as a relic inside a house assigned to a girl who never lived, and ticketed as part of a €6 interior. Each layer of mediation adds authenticity of a different kind, the film's material authenticity standing in for the biography that cannot exist. Avena's successors also understood maintenance as investment. The scrubbing of the courtyard walls and the house's reopening, reported by Italy Magazine in 2009 alongside the launch of the wedding programme, preceded the sale of ceremonies in the building: the shrine was cleaned so that it could be hired. Capital expenditure, product preparation and price revision, the ordinary grammar of any attraction business, run through the lovers' economy from its first municipal act, and the romance of the subject has never exempted the city from speaking that grammar fluently.

## How Many Visitors Does the Legend Draw to Verona?

The honest answer begins with a confession about attribution. No instrument separates the visitor who came to Verona for the lovers from the visitor who came for the Arena, the Roman theatre, the wine country or a trade fair, and then stood in the courtyard because the courtyard stood on the route. Tourism economists call this the attribution problem, and Verona's published figures display it in layers. The top layer, the city's total visitor volume, is the best documented: Marcorin, drawing on the municipal record, reported that in 2016 the city of Verona was visited by two million tourists, and the Smithsonian's Folklife account of the city, published in 2019, used the same round measure, "some two million visitors" descending each year. A local press figure, repeated in Verona's River News in 2023, credited the Shakespearean icon with attracting an average of 1.5 million tourists a year, a number that attributes three quarters of the city's entire traffic to the lovers and that no municipal series supports. The disciplined reading is that two million people a year entered a city whose single most famous product was a fiction, and that the lovers' measurable share must be found one layer down, where the counting is done by turnstile rather than by slogan.

The turnstile layer is Marcorin's second gift to the audit. Citing the annual report of the Direzione Musei d'Arte Monumenti, the city's museums directorate, for 2016, he gave the distribution of museum visits across the civic system: of every hundred tourists who entered a Verona museum in that year, 49 visited the Arena, 20 visited Juliet's House, 10 visited the Castelvecchio museum, 6 visited the Roman theatre and its archaeological museum, and 1 visited the Scaliger tombs. The lovers' house, a building whose claim rests on a surname's resemblance to a fictional family's, took one fifth of all museum traffic in a city holding a Roman amphitheatre, a medieval castle gallery and two millennia of fabric. Marcorin also gave the house's paid admissions for the same year: 329,000 people bought a ticket to enter Juliet's House in 2016. That figure counts only the paying entrants to the museum interior. It excludes the courtyard crowd, which in 2016 paid nothing and was counted by no gate, and it excludes the VeronaCard admissions bundled into the city's pass. The most reliable single number in the lovers' economy is therefore a floor under the phenomenon it measures, and the gap between 329,000 ticketed devotees and the uncounted courtyard mass is the economy's central measurement problem in miniature.

### Why did the courtyard stay free for so long?

The courtyard stayed free because it served as the funnel of the whole system rather than as a product: a visitor drawn in from Via Cappello at no charge might pay for the museum, buy the VeronaCard, book the hotel night and write the letter, so the unpriced passage earned its keep as advertising for every priced asset behind it.

Charging for the funnel, on that theory, risked narrowing the flow that fed the rest of the ledger, and for decades no administration cared to test the risk against the goodwill the free passage bought.

The measurement problem deserves one more layer, because it explains the shape of every later section. A devotion economy monetizes presence, and presence is hard to meter. The Arena sells a seat for a dated performance, a transaction with a natural count. The lovers sell proximity, which visitors consume by standing, photographing, touching a statue and leaving a note, most of it, for most of the site's history, outside any till. Verona's two million annual visitors at the top of the funnel, 329,000 paying house entrants in the best-documented year, and an unmetered courtyard in between: the lovers' economy is a wide river measured only at one narrow bridge. Any verdict on its size must carry that caveat, and the verdict offered here does. What can be stated without caveat is the comparative result inside the museum system: in 2016 the fictional house outdrew every authentic museum in the city except the amphitheatre, beating the Castelvecchio's genuine masterpieces by two to one. Fabrication, priced at six euros, beat authenticity, priced the same, in every gallery contest except the one against Rome's largest surviving provincial arena.

## Why Nobody Can State the Legend's Total

The audit must state plainly why no total for the legend's contribution appears in its profile table: the quantity does not exist in any record, and the reasons are structural rather than accidental. Tourism economics distinguishes the overnight visitor, whose spending spreads across lodging, dinners and second days, from the excursionist, the day-tripper whose entire local expenditure may be a ticket, a coffee and a magnet. Verona's position, an hour from Venice and beside the Lake Garda resorts, loads its visitor mix heavily toward excursionists, and the lovers' sites sit at the excursionist end of the market: a courtyard visit fits inside a morning, asks no booking until the reforms of 2026 and pairs naturally with a train home. A million excursionists and a million overnighters produce identical headlines and radically different economies. The two-million figure that Marcorin and the Smithsonian both carry counts heads, not nights, and no published Verona series allocates those heads between the two classes at the level of the legend, so every derived total for the lovers' worth, whether a promoter's fortune or a sceptic's footnote, is arithmetic performed on a missing variable.

The multiplier literature compounds the problem symmetrically, and symmetry is the audit's protection against favouring either Verona industry. The Arena's commissioned Nomisma study reported a return of €6.30 for every euro invested and a €2 billion generation for the national economy; such figures rest on social accounting matrices that trace a euro through suppliers and wages until the tracing convention, not the evidence, decides where to stop. Devotion economics has its own version of the same instrument in the unsourced claims that credit the lovers with three quarters of the city's traffic. Both numbers function identically in their ecosystems: they convert a real, countable core, €35 million of box office in the one case, 329,000 ticketed admissions in the other, into a penumbra of impact sized to the needs of the institution quoting it. The profile table in this article therefore prints the cores and grades the penumbras, and the verdict rests on the cores alone. What survives the discipline is still remarkable: a fictional address outdrawing every real museum in its city but one, a wedding tariff graduated like an airline's fare classes, and a mail service to a dead girl that has operated, through volunteers, for half a century. The legend's total is unknowable. The legend's structure is not, and structure, priced and distributed as the following sections show, is where the economic interest lies.

## What the Balcony Earns: Ticket Economics at Casa di Giulietta

For most of the museum's modern life, the price of devotion was modest and stable: the house ticket stood at €6, with the tomb museum at the former convent of San Francesco al Corso priced at €4.50 and a combined ticket sold for the pair, a tariff reproduced in the municipal listings and visitor guides of the period. Six euros bought the rooms, the film costumes, the bed displayed from Zeffirelli's 1968 film and the right to stand on the balcony itself. The courtyard, the statue and the view of the balcony from below remained free, open to the street through the arched passage on Via Cappello. The pricing encoded a theory of the visitor: the photograph from the courtyard was the advertisement, distributed worldwide at no cost to the city, and the €6 interior was the souvenir a fraction of the crowd chose to buy. The theory held for decades. It also produced the crowding that eventually broke it, as the free passage filled beyond comfort and beyond the fire officer's patience, a pressure the city documented in its own deliberations before acting in the winter of 2025.

The break came in two steps, both dated and both instructive. For the Christmas season, from 6 December 2025 to 6 January 2026, the city charged €12 for entry to the courtyard and house together, capped the courtyard at one hundred people at a time, down from a previous informal level of about one hundred and thirty, and required online booking. The stated grounds were safety and crowd management, and the trial was framed as exceptional. Then, from 1 April 2026, the exception became the system: entry was rerouted through the Teatro Nuovo on Piazzetta Navona, booking became mandatory for every visitor including VeronaCard holders, and the tariff settled into two routes, €5 for the courtyard alone and €12 for the courtyard and the house. The fabricated balcony had acquired what every mature attraction acquires, a price ladder, in which the marker itself, the courtyard view that had been free for generations, was unbundled and sold separately from the museum behind it.

The results of the first six months under the new system, which the city released on 30 October 2026, are the most remarkable figures in this entire audit. Between 1 April and 30 September 2026, the site recorded 586,742 visits. Fifty-one per cent of visitors chose the courtyard route and 49 per cent continued into the house, and house visits rose by about half against the same period of 2022. More than 40,000 of the entries were made with a VeronaCard, roughly 7 per cent of the total, which means the overwhelming majority of devotees paid cash prices for a sight they had previously received free. Charging for the courtyard did not empty it. The demand that economists would call inelastic revealed itself at the till: visitors who had crossed continents to stand under a balcony assembled in the 1930s did not turn away at a €5 gate. The funnel theory of the free courtyard, plausible for a generation, was refuted by the city's own takings. The marker had been underpriced at zero.

### Why did charging for the courtyard not cut the crowd?

Charging failed to cut the crowd because the purchase was never really the courtyard: the ticket's €5 or €12 was trivial beside the travel sunk into reaching Verona, so the price ratified a pilgrimage rather than deterring a diversion, and booking spread arrivals across the day, letting the season absorb 586,742 visits with less crushing than the free regime produced.

The reform converted a congestion problem into a revenue line without surrendering volume, which is why a measure announced as temporary crowd management survived its trial season and became the permanent system.

The ticket economics carry one further lesson about who the lovers' customer is. The VeronaCard share of roughly 7 per cent shows that the bundle, the instrument designed for the systematic sightseer working through the Arena, Castelvecchio and the Roman theatre, barely touches this market. The courtyard crowd is a single-purpose crowd, arriving for one address and paying the single-purpose price. In the language of the trade, Juliet's House is a destination product wearing the clothes of a museum, and its €12 full route in 2026 finally priced it as one. Set against the 329,000 paid house admissions of 2016, the 586,742 total visits of a single half-year under priced entry cannot be read as decline or growth without care, because the two figures count different things, ticketed interiors against routed visits. Read together with the reported rise of about half in house visits against 2022, however, they establish the direction: monetization and volume rose together. Verona discovered, late, that devotion is not a fragile good that pricing spoils. Devotion is a good whose price had simply never been asked.

## The Second Site: How the Tomb Is Bundled

Every portfolio holds a weaker asset, and in the lovers' portfolio the weaker asset is the tomb. The sarcophagus at San Francesco al Corso sits a twenty-minute walk from the courtyard's magnetic centre, inside a former Capuchin convent whose G.B. Cavalcaselle Fresco Museum holds detached frescoes of genuine art-historical weight, and the pairing defines the site's economics: the tomb draws the crowd, the frescoes receive it. The municipal tariff of the pre-2026 period priced the relationship honestly, €4.50 for the tomb museum against €6 for the house, with a combined ticket at €10 that discounted the pair by half a euro against separate purchase, a small, deliberate bribe to move a fraction of the courtyard's river southward across the centre. Bundling of that kind is the standard instrument for redistributing demand inside a museum system, and Verona applied it to a fictional grave and a real fresco collection without embarrassment: the invented asset at the top of the funnel subsidizes the authentic asset at the bottom, reversing the usual direction of heritage subsidy, in which masterpieces are expected to carry the curiosities.

The tomb's second monetization is cleverer. The Fresco Museum's Sala delle Muse, a ceremonial hall inside the same complex, entered the Sposami a Verona venue list, so the quiet site earns wedding fees at municipal rates while the balcony takes the proposals and the grand ceremonies. The arrangement spreads the wedding economy across the map, gives the tomb a revenue line independent of its modest gate and converts the circuit's weakest draw into a bookable room with a heroine in the basement. Marcorin's 2016 distribution, which assigns named shares to the Arena, the house, Castelvecchio, the Roman theatre and the Scaliger tombs, leaves a residual of fourteen in every hundred museum visits for the rest of the civic system, the fresco museum among it, and no published figure in the sources consulted separates the tomb's own admissions from that residual. The audit records the gap rather than filling it: the second site of the world's most famous love story does not publish its gate, and its economics must be read from its prices, its bundle and its wedding diary instead of from a count.

The tomb holds one further economic distinction that the house cannot match: its central exhibit costs almost nothing to conserve, because the exhibit is an absence. The sarcophagus shown in the crypt is a stone coffin, empty and lidless in the guides' plain description, with no remains to insure, no relic to climate-control and no provenance to defend against rival claimants, since a fiction cannot be exhumed. Pilgrims file past a vacancy and pay €4.50 for the filing, and the vacancy obliges by never deteriorating, never requiring authentication and never disappointing an expectation it never raised. In a portfolio of fabricated assets, the empty tomb is the perfect holding: zero carrying cost, infinite patience, and a meaning supplied entirely by the visitor, who arrives carrying the body the box lacks.

## The Circuit: How Three Sites Share One Wallet

The lovers' sites are not scattered shrines but a spaced itinerary, and the spacing is an economic variable in its own right. The Casa stands in the commercial heart, steps from Piazza delle Erbe, on the natural pedestrian route between the Arena and the shops, perfectly placed to intercept flows it did not create. The tomb lies south, at the former convent of San Francesco al Corso, far enough from the centre that reaching it is a decision rather than a drift, and its gate has always reflected the distance: visitors vote with their feet for the balcony and against the sarcophagus, which is why the combined ticket existed, to tax the strong site's gravity for the weak site's benefit. Romeo's house completes the triangle to the east, privately held and closed to the interior, contributing an exterior, a photograph and a restaurant's turnover to the circuit while costing the municipality nothing. A designed itinerary binds the three into a half-day product that guides sell as a single walk in the lovers' footsteps, and the product's economics are those of any bundle: the strong asset anchors the price, the weaker assets extend the dwell time, and every extra hour inside the municipal boundary is an extra hour in which coffee, lunch and retail can happen.

Distance decay, the tendency of visitation to fall with every minute of walking, explains the circuit's internal hierarchy better than any difference in the sites' claims, since the tomb's tradition is older than the house's and its sarcophagus at least pretends to an antiquity the balcony cannot. What the balcony has is position. The audit's figures encode the geography without naming it: the house took a fifth of all museum visits in 2016 while the tomb hid inside an unitemized residual of fourteen in a hundred, and the wedding office monetized the tomb not through its gate but through a rentable hall in its museum, bypassing the weak footfall entirely. Verona's managers have behaved, across a century, as if they understood the map's lesson, investing the marker budget where the flows already ran and letting the bundle, the wedding diary and the festival carry value outward to the quieter addresses. The circuit is the brand rendered as urban form, and its lesson generalizes bleakly for heritage policy: in a devotion economy, location compounds faster than authenticity, and a fabricated balcony on the main street will out-earn a genuine antiquity in a side street for as long as feet follow streets.

## The Arena Comparison: What Real Heritage Earns

Every audit of the lovers' economy must pass the Arena on its way, because the amphitheatre is the control case: Verona's authentic asset, monetized by the most professional instrument in the city's cultural portfolio, the Fondazione Arena di Verona and its summer opera festival. The foundation's own published results give the comparison its spine. For the 2025 season the festival reported 404,715 spectators from 130 countries, 61 per cent of them foreign, and total revenue above €35 million, €35,619,000 in the figure carried by OperaWire's report of the foundation's announcement. The 2024 season, the festival's 101st, had set the previous records with 417,354 spectators and €33,619,825 in revenue. A Nomisma study of the foundation's impact, presented in May 2026 and commissioned by the foundation itself, raised the stakes further, calculating that the festival generated €2 billion for the Italian economy, close to 6,000 jobs, €206 million in taxes and a return of €6.30 for every euro invested, with 71 per cent of the foundation's production value coming from tickets, sponsorships and private contributions rather than public subsidy. Those impact figures carry a commissioner's health warning, and this article applies it: an impact study ordered by the institution it measures is an advocacy document with a methodology attached, and the €2 billion belongs in the profile table graded as such. The box-office figures need no such discount. Forty thousand euros short of €36 million changed hands at one Roman amphitheatre in one summer, for performances of works as fictional as any tragedy, staged inside a genuine monument.

The comparison clarifies the lovers' economics by contrast. The Arena converts authenticity into yield: a scarce seat, a dated night, a premium price, a high-spending international audience staying several days. The lovers convert fiction into volume: an abundant courtyard, an undated visit, a €5 or €6 or €12 price, a crowd measured in hundreds of thousands. In the 2016 museum distribution, the Arena took 49 of every hundred museum visits and the house took 20, yet the revenue relationship is far more lopsided than the visitor relationship, because the Arena's visitor buys a performance at opera prices while the house's visitor bought, for most of the site's history, a €6 museum ticket after a free courtyard. Verona thus operates two heritage economies side by side: a yield economy on the genuine article and a volume economy on the fabricated one, and the city's genius, or its luck, is that the two economies feed the same hotels. The tourist who pays €100 for an Arena seat and the tourist who pays €5 for the courtyard sleep in the same beds, eat in the same osterie and appear in the same lodging-tax returns. The lovers' low yield is subsidized, in effect, by the Arena's high yield, and the Arena's audience is warmed, in part, by a city brand the lovers built.

### Why does the Arena not settle the argument about the lovers' value?

The Arena cannot settle the argument because the two assets sell goods to crowds sharing one stock of hotels and restaurants: the box office measures a monument's yield, while the lovers' contribution hides inside the spillover of beds and meals, so the comparison proves the city monetizes authenticity at high yield and fiction at volume, not which asset matters more.

## Demand Shocks Verona Never Paid For: The Films

A peculiarity of the lovers' economy is that its largest marketing expenditures have always been made by other people. The Cukor film of 1936 arrived with the balcony works and taught the world the visual the city was building. Zeffirelli's 1968 film, shot with teenage leads and a Nino Rota score, renewed the legend for the postwar generations and left the city its most portable relic, the bed installed in the house museum, so that the film's furniture earns admission money inside the fiction's address in a closed loop of mutual promotion. Baz Luhrmann's William Shakespeare's Romeo + Juliet of 1996 reset the lovers for a teenage audience raised on music video, and Verona's courtyard filled with the cohort the film created, although not a frame of it was shot within a thousand miles of Via Cappello. The pattern completes itself with Gary Winick's Letters to Juliet in 2010, a modest Hollywood romance that functioned, for the Club di Giulietta, as a global recruitment film: press accounts from the film's release period reported the secretaries' post swollen by viewers writing to an address they had learned in a cinema. In each case the demand shock was financed by a studio, distributed by a studio and harvested by a city that paid for none of it.

Economists have a term for such transfers, a positive externality, and the lovers' economy may be the purest case in cultural tourism of an externality with a permanent address. A film studio cannot internalize the courtyard value its adaptation creates, because the value accrues to ground the studio does not own, in a country whose laws give it no claim; the studio's rational response is to ignore the spillover, and Verona's rational response is to keep the courtyard in perfect readiness for shocks it cannot schedule. A fifth shock arrived without a studio behind it. The city's own reporting on the courtyard crisis of 2025 named social media among the forces that had intensified the pressure on the site, as the platforms taught a generation of visitors to treat the courtyard as a photographic set whose image certifies a journey, and the rite of the statue and the balcony portrait circulated as reproducible poses detached from any film. The platform shock differs from the cinematic ones in rhythm rather than in kind: a film delivers its audience in a wave across a few seasons, while the feed delivers a permanent, self-renewing trickle of pilgrims who arrive pre-instructed in the gestures the site expects, the hand on the bronze breast, the gaze up at the balcony, the note composed for the wall. Verona's response, the priced and booked entry of 2026, is best understood as the moment the city stopped treating that instruction as free advertising and started metering the set on which it is performed. The externality had grown large enough to need a gate, and the gate, once built, discovered that the photographed devotion on the far side of it was willing to pay for the privilege of the backdrop.

The arrangement also explains the city's curatorial choices inside the house, where film costumes and the 1968 bed hold the place of honour that a documentary museum would give to manuscripts: the museum's true subject is not the Verona of the Cappello family but the accumulated screen image of the lovers, because the screen image is what the visitors arrive carrying. The house sells admission to the inside of a picture the visitor already owns, and the picture's producers, from MGM in 1936 to the makers of the 2010 romance, replenish the stock of that picture at their own expense in every generation. No line in any Verona budget pays for this. It is the economy's largest single subsidy after the volunteers' labour, and the least acknowledged, because acknowledging it would require the brand to credit its rivals in the storytelling trade with its own footfall.

## Weddings at Juliet's House and the Price of a Vow

If the ticket ladder prices proximity, the wedding tariff prices exclusivity, and the wedding tariff is the most candid document the lovers' economy has produced. Verona launched its Sposami a Verona programme, weddings in the city's romantic sites, in 2009, and Italy Magazine's report of the launch preserved the schedule in full: a marriage at Juliet's House cost €600 for Verona residents, €700 for couples from the city's catchment, €800 for citizens of the European Union and €1,000 for couples from outside it, against the €50 then charged for an ordinary Italian civil marriage licence. The tourism councillor who launched the scheme, Daniele Polato, announced that Verona intended to become a wedding capital offering, in his quoted words, "tourist packages, the whole shebang, just like Las Vegas does," and Mayor Flavio Tosi defended the graduated tariff as covering higher administrative costs for foreign paperwork. The defence was polite fiction of an administrative kind. The graduation by residence is price discrimination in its textbook form, charging each segment what the segment's devotion will bear, and the segment willing to bear most is the couple for whom Verona is a once-in-a-lifetime destination rather than a municipality.

The later schedules refined the product rather than abandoning it. The municipal offer as summarized for couples in 2019 priced a sixty-minute civil ceremony at the house at €500 for Italian citizens and €800 for foreigners, and sold the balcony itself in ten-minute units: a marriage proposal on the balcony, with the courtyard's attention as a backdrop, cost €50. In April 2023 the local press reported that the proposal fee had doubled, from €50 to €100 for the same ten minutes of exclusive use, and the report noted, without surprise, that demand had given the city no reason to hesitate. The product line repays study because it sells the scarcest thing the site owns. The courtyard can absorb a hundred visitors at once; the balcony holds a couple. By pricing the balcony in ten-minute slices, the city manufactures scarcity inside abundance, converting a piece of 1930s stonework into a time-share of devotion, and the doubling of the price in 2023 is the market's verdict that the manufactured scarcity was still undervalued. Spillover inventory followed the same logic: the Sposami a Verona venues extended to the Sala delle Muse in the Fresco Museum beside Juliet's Tomb, to the Sala degli Arazzi in Palazzo Barbieri and to the Cappella dei Notai, so that the overflow of couples who could not have the balcony could still buy a municipal room with a romantic warrant.

### What does a wedding fee buy that a ticket cannot?

A wedding fee buys exclusion: the ticket admits a visitor to a courtyard, while the fee empties the balcony for ten minutes or the room for an hour, reserves the fiction's centre for one couple, and attaches the city's legal authority to the vow, so the couple purchases not a view of the legend but a licensed place inside it.

The weddings expose the economy's double standard with unusual clarity, and the exposure matters for the adjudication later in this article. A ticket buyer purchases a look; a marrying couple purchases a transformation, and the transformation is real even though the warrant is fictional. The marriage registered at Juliet's House binds in Italian law exactly as the €50 registry-office marriage binds. Verona sells a genuine legal act staged inside an invented room, and the premium of €800 or €1,000 over the standard fee measures, with embarrassing precision, the market value of staging. No part of the product is counterfeit. The room is invented, the balcony is assembled, the heroine is fictional, and the marriage is enforceable in every court in Europe. The wedding tariff is therefore the devotion economy in its purest published form: a price list for belief, graduated by distance, doubled without protest, and attached to the one transaction in the entire system whose value no participant describes as a deception.

One figure in the wedding economy is missing, and its absence belongs in the audit beside the missing souvenir totals: the city publishes its wedding tariffs to the euro yet releases no annual count of ceremonies at the house in the sources this article could consult, so the revenue of its most candid price list cannot be computed. The gap is characteristic. Verona prices devotion precisely and counts it selectively, publishing the numbers that describe crowds, since crowds justify management, and leaving unpublished the numbers that would describe income per rite, since those would invite a different argument about what a municipality is for. The tariff schedules remain the best evidence of the wedding economy's scale that the record offers: municipalities do not double a fee, as the proposal fee was doubled in 2023, for a product that is failing, and they do not maintain a four-tier residence graduation for a handful of ceremonies a year.

## The Letters: An Economy Paid in Feeling

The strangest balance sheet in Verona belongs to an organization that sells nothing. Letters addressed to Juliet have arrived in the city since the 1930s, when Ettore Solimani, custodian of the tomb and a director of the civic museums, began answering the notes visitors left at the sarcophagus, a practice documented from 1937 in the Club's own tradition and in Italy Magazine's account of the custom. In 1972 Giulio Tamassia founded the Club di Giulietta, the nonprofit cultural association that institutionalized the answering, and the Club, later directed by Tamassia's daughter Giovanna, built the practice into a standing service: volunteers styled Juliet's secretaries read every letter and answer by hand, in the writer's own language where the volunteer bench allows, signing as secretaries of a girl who never lived. Lise Friedman and Ceil Friedman's book Letters to Juliet (2006) documented the system for an international readership, and the Friedmans' account, read alongside [The Juliet Club and Letters to Juliet](/2029/06/29/juliet-club-letters-to-juliet/), records the essential economic facts: the service charges nothing, promises an answer to every letter that carries a return address, and runs on donated time.

The volume of the mail is the one number in this economy that refuses to settle, and its refusal is itself evidence. Press profiles of the Club have put the annual inflow at figures ranging from roughly five thousand letters to roughly ten thousand, with festival-era coverage circulated by Tourism Verona favouring the higher figure and at least one popular account inflating the flow to fifty thousand by folding in the notes, postcards and emails left at the house. No audited series exists. The Club does not publish accounts of its mail with a statistician's apparatus, and the spread of the estimates, fivefold between the careful and the careless versions, marks the boundary of the documented economy: where devotion can be priced, Verona counts precisely, down to the last of 586,742 visits; where devotion is given away, the counting dissolves into round numbers and warm approximations. The 2010 Hollywood film Letters to Juliet, directed by Gary Winick and built around the secretaries, pushed the unpriced sector into visibility, and press accounts of the film's release period reported the Club's post swollen with letters from viewers who had learned the service existed from a cinema screen. Hollywood captured the film's box office abroad. Verona captured the mail.

The letters matter to an economic audit because they are an input, not an output. Every answered letter manufactures the brand's scarcest quality, sincerity, at a cost borne almost entirely by volunteers. A correspondent in São Paulo or Seoul who receives a handwritten reply on Club stationery has received proof that the fiction is tended by people who take it seriously, and that proof circulates: the Friedmans built a book on it, the filmmakers built a feature on it, the festival builds its Premio Cara Giulietta, the annual prize for the finest letter, on it. The municipality's contribution to this production line has historically been modest beside its take from tickets and weddings, premises and goodwill rather than a payroll, so the letters sector operates as a subsidy flowing from the volunteers to the brand. The secretaries are paid in the coin the whole economy runs on, participation in the fiction, and the arrangement is stable precisely because the wage is the work. Any honest distribution of the lovers' value must therefore record a transfer running in the unexpected direction: the most devoted participants in the system give value to it, unpaid, and the priced sectors downstream harvest the sincerity the volunteers produce.

The mail's prehistory shows that the devotion economy predates the municipality's management of it, and the prehistory matters because it identifies what the city actually acquired in 1905: not an invention but a going concern. Visitors were leaving letters at the tomb across the nineteenth century, drawn by a tradition that attached the sarcophagus at San Francesco al Corso to the heroine long before the Casa had a balcony, and Charles Dickens, describing the tomb in Pictures from Italy in 1846, recorded the shrine and its custodian with a traveller's scepticism that did nothing to slow the pilgrimage he was documenting. The tomb economy of that century ran on custodian gratuities and guide fees, unpriced devotion of the kind Solimani inherited when he began answering the letters in 1937. The Club di Giulietta's foundation in 1972 thus regularized a century-old flow rather than creating one, and the Friedmans' 2006 documentation of the secretaries at work describes an institution whose antiquity is measured against the post that reaches it: writers addressing a fictional girl in the confidence of an answer, a confidence the nineteenth-century custodians first underwrote. Verona's modern managers did not manufacture belief. They bought, priced and serviced a belief that had been arriving by post and on foot since before the Italian state existed, which is why the brand's sincerity, the asset the volunteers maintain, has roots no competitor's start-up could replicate on any budget.

### What do the letter-writers contribute that no ticket buys?

The letter-writers contribute the testimony that the fiction is alive: each envelope addressed to Juliet in Verona is scattered proof of belief that no advertising budget could purchase, and the secretaries' answers convert it into a reputation for sincerity which the priced sectors monetize, making the mail the one line in the economy whose workers pay to supply it.

## Souvenirs, Walls and the Maintenance Ledger

Below the ticketed economy runs a retail economy that no Verona publication has ever isolated, and the gap deserves emphasis because popular accounts fill it with invention. No official series separates the lovers' merchandise from Verona's general tourist retail: the balcony magnets, miniature statues, heart-shaped pasta, love-lock novelties and Verona-branded confectionery sold along Via Cappello and around Piazza delle Erbe enter the statistics, when they enter at all, as undifferentiated turnover of shops in the historic centre. The souvenir trade is real, visible and completely unquantified at the level of the legend, and the disciplined conclusion is that any total offered for it, in journalism or in promotion, is an estimate wearing the costume of a count. What can be documented is the trade's position in the system: it clusters within a few hundred metres of the courtyard, it prices at the small-change level of the devotion economy, €2 magnets and €15 statuettes in the shop windows of the period, and it pays its rents and taxes to private landlords and the fisc rather than to the museum directorate, which is why the municipality's own accounts understate the legend's retail wake so severely.

The maintenance ledger is better documented, because devotion at this scale damages its object, and damage generates paperwork. The bronze statue of Juliet placed in the courtyard in 1972, the work of the sculptor Nereo Costantini in the standard accounts, became the economy's most touched asset: the custom of rubbing the statue's right breast for luck in love wore and stressed the bronze until, in 2014, the statue's right arm fractured, and the city withdrew the original into the house and installed a replica in the courtyard, as the Smithsonian's account records. The original artwork was consumed by the devotion it attracted, and the site adapted by substituting a copy for the daily rite, reserving the authentic object for the museum interior. The same ledger records the walls: visitors wrote on the courtyard passage, stuck notes with chewing gum and adhesive bandages, and covered every reachable surface with declarations, until the city moved to protect the fabric with replaceable panels, prohibitions and fines, and routed the impulse toward post boxes and digital screens. Each of these measures is a cost of running a shrine whose visitors insist on leaving a deposit of themselves, and each reappears in the civic accounts as cleaning, restoration and enforcement, the negative line that promotional totals for the legend's contribution rarely subtract.

Read together, the statue and the walls teach the audit's most portable lesson. The devotion economy does not merely sell access to its assets; it spends its assets, at a rate set by the visitors' hands. A Roman amphitheatre amortizes across two thousand years of weather. A bronze Juliet amortizes across forty-two years of caresses. Verona's managers responded with the classic instruments of a mature attraction, substitution of the consumable part, channeling of the deposit impulse into sanctioned containers, and finally the priced and booked entry of 2026, which rations the touching itself. The maintenance ledger is where the romance of the subject ends and the facilities management of belief begins, and the city's willingness to keep paying it, decade after decade, is the quietest proof in this article that the gross figures, whatever their imprecision, clear the costs by a margin no council has ever doubted in public.

## The Hotel Layer: Where the Wider Money Sleeps

The largest cheques in the lovers' economy are written in buildings the legend does not own, and the lodging trade is where the fiction's value concentrates after dark. Verona's hoteliers have never needed a licence to trade on the association, since names and themes drawn from a public-domain text are free to every signwriter: a hotel trading as Giulietta e Romeo stands near the Arena, suites named for Juliet are marketed in a fifteenth-century building a minute from Piazza delle Erbe in the trade's own descriptions, and wedding packages sold by private operators wrap the municipal ceremony, the only part of the event the city sells, inside receptions, rooms and photography priced by the market. The structure deserves emphasis because it inverts the public story of the industry. The city's €800 foreign-couple ceremony, the figure that draws the headlines about commodification, is the smallest component of a destination wedding in Verona; the couple that pays it will spend a multiple of it within the week on the private layer that the ceremony anchors. The municipality sells the warrant, and the warrant is cheap. The celebration the warrant authorizes is the expensive part, and private Verona collects it.

The lodging layer also explains the industry's tolerance of the courtyard's historically low prices. A €6 ticket looks like underpricing only if the gate is mistaken for the business; from the city's wider accounts, the gate was the invitation and the bed was the business, and every instrument of policy, the free courtyard, the bundled card, the February festival, points the same way, toward converting the legend's global fame into occupied rooms inside the municipal boundary, where the lodging tax and the restaurant tills can reach it. Verona's day-trippers complicate the model without breaking it. The excursionist who stands in the courtyard and sleeps at Lake Garda exports the lodging value to another tax jurisdiction, and the reforms of 2026, which put a price on the excursionist's previously free devotion, can be read as the city's adjustment to exactly that leakage: a visitor who will not buy a bed can at least be asked €5 for the courtyard. The hotel layer thus completes the audit's map of capture. The legend manufactures desire at global scale for no royalty; the municipality prices the desire's physical points of contact; and the private trade in rooms, meals and celebrations, unmeasured at the level of the legend and probably larger than every priced line in the profile combined, collects the remainder in the dark, which is where, in this economy as in the play, the decisive transactions were always scheduled.

## The Wages of Devotion: Labour in the Lovers' Economy

The lovers employ Verona at every grade of the labour market, and the grades map the economy's values with uncomfortable fidelity. At the professional summit stand the museum staff of the Direzione Musei, the wedding office that administers the Sposami a Verona calendar, the festival organizers and the licensed guides whose lovers' walks are a standing product of the city's tour operators, a standard itinerary sold in a dozen languages at per-head prices the operators set. Below them runs the seasonal workforce of the courtyard economy, ticket sellers, attendants, cleaners and the retail staff of Via Cappello, whose employment swells with the same calendar as the crowds. The Arena's foundation, by contrast, publishes its labour aristocracy in detail, 1,450 people working a season in the foundation's own account, from singers to seamstresses, a reminder that the authentic sector's economy can be described job by job while the devotion sector's cannot: no Verona publication in the sources consulted counts the employees of the lovers' industry, because the industry has no single employer to count them. Its payroll is smeared across a directorate, a wedding office, a hundred shops and a volunteer association.

The base of the pyramid is the Club's bench of secretaries, the only workers in the system whose wage is zero and whose product, sincerity, the rest of the pyramid sells. Labour economics would classify the arrangement as a gift economy nested inside a market economy, and the nesting is stable for a reason the Friedmans' documentation makes plain: the secretaries receive the one payment the market cannot supply, which is the experience of answering as Juliet, a role with a waiting list of volunteers in the Club's own accounts of its recruitment. The contrast with the Arena's 1,450 contracted professionals is not an embarrassment to be explained away but the devotion economy's defining feature rendered as a staffing table. Where the product is a performance, labour is hired; where the product is belief, labour volunteers, and the boundary between the two workforces is the exact boundary between Verona's two heritage economies. The lovers' industry runs, at its emotional core, on donated time, and every euro in its profile table is downstream of that donation.

## Verona, City of Love: The Brand as Municipal Strategy

Marcorin's word was brand, and the city's conduct fits the word with unusual completeness. Verona layered the lovers' identity over an already formidable heritage position, a Roman amphitheatre, a medieval and Renaissance centre inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 2000, and wine country at the gates, and then promoted the compound under a single emotional trademark: Verona, the city of love, in the Italian phrase Verona città dell'amore that the municipal portal and the tourist board carry. The brand's slogan compresses the offer into an instruction: "Se ami qualcuno, portalo a Verona," rendered by the tourist office as "If you love someone, bring them to Verona." The slogan deserves a cold reading. It does not invite the world to read a play. It designates Verona as the mandatory venue for a universal experience, annexing every love affair everywhere as the brand's potential market, and it converts the city's name into the answer to a question lovers ask in every country on earth.

The festival calendar operationalizes the brand. Verona in Love, the multi-day festival staged around Valentine's Day, was established in the middle of the 2000s, its 2011 edition billed as the seventh, and it fills the February trough of the tourist year with heart-shaped markets in Piazza dei Signori, concerts, guided walks in the lovers' footsteps and the Club's letter prize, under the same slogan. The economic intelligence of the festival is its timing: the Arena sleeps in February, the city's hotels face their emptiest weeks, and the lovers, who ask no season and no performance schedule, are deployed to fill the gap. A brand that monetizes February in a northern Italian city has earned its keep before a single courtyard ticket is counted. Around the festival, the brand extends into product lines the city does not have to manufacture: hotels sell Juliet packages, restaurants sell lovers' menus, the house identified as Romeo's, which [Romeo's House and Verona's Tourist Geography](/2029/07/20/romeos-house-verona-tourism/) maps as a privately held counterweight to the municipal Casa, supports a restaurant within its walls, and the province's wine trade bottles romance for the Valpolicella shelf. The municipality's role in this wider market is closer to a franchisor's than an operator's: it maintains the flagship sites, polices the trademark's physical plant and lets private capital harvest the surrounding demand.

The brand's deepest economic feature is its defensibility, and the defence is geographic rather than legal. Because the text is public property, Verona cannot stop a rival from selling the lovers; it can only rely on the fact that the play names Verona, that the Dal Cappello house stands on Via Cappello, that the tomb tradition settled at San Francesco al Corso and that a century of visitor habit has compounded into expectation. Shakespeare's Juliet asks, "What's in a name?" (2.2), and the brand is the city's four-century answer: everything, provided the name is attached to ground. The moat is not intellectual property. The moat is accumulated association, the one asset class that cannot be printed by a competitor's mint, and Verona's century of maintenance spending, festival programming and tariff management is best understood as the continuous renewal of that association against entropy.

## The Calendar Is the Strategy: Seasonality and the Portfolio

The lovers' least visible economic service is performed on the calendar. The Arena festival concentrates Verona's high-yield tourism into a summer season of roughly fifty evenings between June and September, leaving the city's hotels to face the northern Italian winter with a Roman monument and a business-travel base. The devotion economy asks no season. The courtyard draws in every month, the Christmas trial of paid entry in December 2025 was viable precisely because the holiday crowd at the Casa rivalled the summer's, and Verona in Love was planted in February, the year's emptiest lodging month, where a four-day festival of hearts, markets and letter prizes converts the brand into occupancy at the moment occupancy is worth most to a hotelier. Portfolio language fits the arrangement exactly: the Arena is the city's high-yield, high-variance asset, a summer blockbuster exposed to weather, casts and fashion; the lovers are its low-yield, low-variance bond, paying a modest coupon of tickets and fees in every month of the year, through festival years and fallow ones, because devotion does not check the programme before travelling.

The smoothing function explains municipal behaviour that pure gate arithmetic cannot. A city maximizing ticket revenue alone might neglect the lovers beside the Arena's €35 million summers; a city managing a portfolio defends the asset that fills February, employs the guides in November and gives the Christmas market a second pole, and Verona's century of small, steady investments in the legend, a restoration here, a festival there, a tariff reform when crowding demanded it, reads as portfolio management by instinct. The 2026 pricing reform even improved the bond's coupon without disturbing its character, raising yield at the gate while the booking system steadied the flow, the financial signature of an asset being professionalized rather than exploited. Verona's two heritage economies are sometimes described as rivals for the tourist's day. On the calendar they are complements, and the lovers' contribution to the city's year, February hearts against August arias, may be worth more to the lodging trade than any figure the courtyard itself has ever collected.

## Who Captures the Value of Two Fictional Lovers?

The distribution question has a short answer and a long one. The short answer is that the municipality captures the priced centre, private hospitality captures the unpriced circumference, the volunteers capture nothing but meaning, and the global culture industry captures the story's largest single cheques while paying Verona nothing. The long answer runs channel by channel. The municipality takes the ticket revenue at the house and the tomb, where [The Tomb of Juliet in Verona](/2029/07/13/tomb-of-juliet-verona/) anchors the second site of the circuit, the wedding fees graduated by residence, the proposal fees for the balcony, and the per-night lodging tax that Italian law has allowed cities to levy on paid accommodation since the early 2010s, a tax whose Verona yield rises with every lover who stays the night. The Fondazione Arena, a separate foundation with the city among its founders, takes the festival's €35 million and change in strong years, a capture adjacent to the lovers' economy rather than inside it, though the brand the lovers built helps fill the city's rooms on the festival's dark nights. Hoteliers, restaurateurs, landlords of the Via Cappello shops and the souvenir trade take the spillover, which no series measures and which, on the evidence of two million annual visitors against a few hundred thousand ticketed site visits, dwarfs the priced centre by an unknowable factor.

The volunteers of the Club di Giulietta capture the value in its least convertible form. Their wages are the work itself, the standing to answer as Juliet's secretaries, and the record of the Friedmans' book shows the secretaries describing the labour in the language of gift rather than employment. In the distribution table of a normal industry, unpaid labour that produces the brand's sincerity would appear as an anomaly. In a devotion economy it appears as the load-bearing wall. Remove the priced centre and the brand survives on the letters; remove the letters and the priced centre becomes a prettier, emptier courtyard that a sceptical age would eventually price at its masonry value. The global industry's capture is the mirror image: the 2010 film's producers monetized the secretaries' story at the world's box offices and owed Verona no royalty, because sincerity, like Shakespeare, is not ownable. The city's defence against that leakage is the same as its defence against rival towns: the film sent its audience to Verona, converting Hollywood's capture into Verona's footfall, a reflux the city has learned to welcome rather than resent.

Two further claimants take their share before any surplus settles. The transport layer, railways, coaches and the municipal buses that move the excursionist mass between Porta Nuova station, the courtyard and the tomb, collects fares that no Verona account attributes to the legend, although the legend schedules a good portion of the journeys. The public purse also spends before it collects: the festival's programming, the courtyard's staffing, security and cleaning, and the museums directorate's overheads are costs carried by the municipality and its partners in advance of the ticket and tax returns, and no published account nets them against the legend's gross, which is why this article speaks of capture and structure rather than of profit. A municipal attraction is not a business unit with a bottom line; it is a fiscal ecosystem in which the same authority that sells the €12 ticket also pays for the police officer at the archway, and the lovers' contribution to that ecosystem is real precisely to the extent that it cannot be isolated.

The VeronaCard deserves its own line in the distribution, because a bundle is a redistribution mechanism disguised as a ticket. Sold in 24-hour and 48-hour forms, the card admits its holder across the civic system, Arena, house, tomb and museums at one flat price, which means the systematic sightseer who buys it for the amphitheatre receives the lovers at a marginal cost of zero, while the card's revenue is the city's to allocate. The 2026 figures expose the mechanism's limit at the Casa: only about 7 per cent of the site's 586,742 visits in the first priced season arrived on the card, so the courtyard's crowd overwhelmingly paid its own way and the bundle's cross-subsidy flowed elsewhere in the system. The lovers, in short, are net contributors to the card's value rather than dependents on it: their presence in the bundle helps sell the card to the romantic day-tripper, while their own gate stands on cash. Few assets in a municipal portfolio occupy that position, famous enough to sell the pass, independent enough to scarcely need it.

One claimant is missing from the distribution, and the absence is the system's foundation. The author takes nothing. No Shakespeare estate invoices the Casa for the use of the names, no descendant licenses the balcony, and the text that generates the entire flow entered the public domain before any living institution in the chain existed. Verona's lovers' economy is what a cultural asset looks like when its creator's claim has fully expired: the rents accrue to whoever holds the ground, tends the rite and prices the gate, and the playwright's share is the immortality the Prologue promised his characters rather than any portion of the €12 ticket. The final claimant is the visitor, who captures the experience the whole apparatus exists to deliver and whose surplus, the difference between the €5 paid and the pricelessness claimed in a thousand guest-book phrases, is the one figure in the economy that every participant agrees is large and no participant can state.

## The Play Set the Price First: Gold, Ducats and the Tragedy's Own Ledger

The modern economy's deepest warrant sits inside the text it exploits, and an audit of Verona's money should close its evidence with the play's own transactions. Shakespeare's Verona is a city that prices everything it touches. The feud's principals keep households whose costs the play itemizes in feasts, servants and wedding preparations; the marriage market prices Juliet against Paris's rank in open negotiation between fathers; and the catastrophe itself is executed through a purchase, when Romeo, banished and desperate, finds in Mantua an apothecary whose poverty has already priced his conscience. The scene (5.1) is the tragedy's only cash transaction and its most explicit economic statement: Romeo offers forty ducats for poison the law forbids the sale of, observes that the gold is "worse poison to men's souls" than the compound, and closes the bargain by separating the seller's will from his need, "I pay thy poverty and not thy will." The play understands, with a clarity its tourist afterlife rarely matches, that money in Verona moves people to acts their judgement refuses, and it builds its ending out of that understanding.

The ending then reverses the polarity, and the reversal is the founding document of the modern industry. Confronted with the bodies, the fathers do not renounce wealth; they compete in it, converting grief into commemorative capital in a public bidding of statues. Montague pledges Juliet's figure in pure gold, reasoning in the same breath that the monument and the city's fame are one asset, "that whiles Verona by that name is known, / There shall no figure at such rate be set / As that of true and faithful Juliet" (5.3). Capulet matches the bid for Romeo, "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie" (5.3), and the Prince closes the play over the transaction. The first Verona-economy profile is therefore a pair of gold statues, commissioned as civic advertising by the two houses whose feud the statues memorialize, explicitly justified by the fame they will bring the city's name. Avena's balcony, the 1972 bronze in the courtyard, the wedding tariff and the €12 gate are footnotes to a valuation the text performed in its final minute: the lovers, dead, are worth more to Verona as monuments than they ever were to their families alive, and the city that Shakespeare imagined spending gold on their images merely waited three centuries for a municipal successor willing to take the commission seriously.

## Commerce Against the Play: The Commodification Case Adjudicated

The indictment of Verona's industry was written before the industry existed, in the work of Theodor W. Adorno and Max Horkheimer, whose chapter on the culture industry in Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947) described mass culture as a factory of standardized pleasures whose consumers are cheated of the very experience sold to them. Applied to Via Cappello, the indictment writes itself: a fabricated balcony, a statue rubbed for luck, a heroine who never lived, ten-minute proposals at €100, a city slogan annexing the world's love affairs. On Adorno's account, the courtyard is the culture industry at its most honest, a site that has dispensed even with the pretext of an original work, and the visitor shuffling toward the statue is the administered consumer performing a scripted emotion on schedule. The commodification critic's case deserves its strongest form, because the verdict of this article accepts half of it.

A second prosecution speaks not for the play but for the city, and it has grown louder inside Verona itself. The civic version of the commodification case holds that the brand hollows the centre it celebrates: that a historic core reorganized around a fictional address becomes a stage set for excursionists, that residents are priced and crowded out of their own streets, and that the lovers function as Verona's version of the Venetian disease, a monoculture of sentiment flattening a complex city into one saleable emotion. The reports surrounding the courtyard reforms of 2025 recorded the pressure in practical form, with businesses near Via Cappello complaining of the chaotic spill of the free crowd into neighbouring streets and the administration pleading public safety for its caps and gates. The civic critics and the administration agree on the diagnosis of congestion while prescribing opposite medicines, the critics asking for the brand to be loosened and the city choosing instead to price it, and the sequel belongs to the figures: the priced season of 2026 absorbed more visits with less crushing, which suggests that the disease was never devotion itself but devotion unpriced and unbooked, a queue mistaken for a culture. The play survives the civic case for the same reason it survives Adorno: the tragedy's subject is a city that fails its young through the management of appearances, and Verona's argument about its own streets is one more performance of the theme, conducted in council deliberations rather than in verse.

The strongest answer comes from the sociology of the visitors rather than the philosophy of the producers. Ning Wang's rethinking of authenticity in tourism experience, published in the Annals of Tourism Research in 1999, separated object authenticity, the genuineness of the thing seen, from existential authenticity, the genuineness of what the visitor does and feels, and argued that tourists routinely achieve the second in settings where the first is absent or irrelevant. The courtyard is Wang's thesis with a cash register. The visitors of 2026 did not behave as the dupes of a fabrication: Verona's own museum documentation states openly that the balcony was assembled in the 1930s, the record examined in [Casa di Giulietta: The Balcony That Lies](/2029/06/22/casa-di-giulietta-balcony-myth/), and the guidebooks repeat the fact, and still 586,742 visits were recorded in six months of priced entry, and still couples paid €100 for ten minutes on the fabricated stones to propose marriage on them. Knowledge did not dissolve the rite. The fabrication was never the secret of the product; the rite was the product, and the rite is performed by the visitors themselves, which is why its authenticity survives the exposure of its scenery. Between MacCannell's marker with no sight behind it and Adorno's cheated consumer, Wang's pilgrim fits the evidence: the crowd knows, and comes, and pays, and the paying rose when the city finally asked.

The adjudication, then, divides the honours with intent. Adorno wins on structure: the lovers' industry is standardized, segmented by willingness to pay, graduated by nationality in its wedding tariff and innocent of any interest in the play's text, and a visitor can pass through the entire Verona apparatus, courtyard, statue, balcony, gift shop and wedding room, without encountering a single line of Shakespeare beyond a name. MacCannell wins on mechanism: the economy runs on markers, and its history is the history of marker manufacture, from Avena's restoration to the festival slogan. Wang wins on the transaction itself: what changes hands is not a counterfeit antiquity but a licensed occasion for genuine feeling, and the proof is that exposure has never once dented the demand. The play, finally, declines to take the prosecution's side in its own case. Shakespeare's Verona already runs on transactions: an apothecary in Mantua sells poison against the law because, in his words, "My poverty, but not my will, consents," and hears the economy's whole defence and condemnation in the reply, "I pay thy poverty and not thy will" (5.1). Romeo carries gold into that scene and calls it "worse poison to men's souls" than the compound it buys (5.1), and the tragedy ends with the fathers competing in commemorative expenditure, Montague's promised statue of Juliet in pure gold answered by Capulet's matching pledge, "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie" (5.3). Commerce did not descend on an innocent text in 1905. The text closes with a bidding war in gold statues, and Verona's industry is that closing gesture, institutionalized.

Read the profile as a geologist reads strata and the economy's history stands in order: a 1905 purchase at an inn's price at the bottom, a 1930s building campaign above it, a half-century of €6 stability, a wedding tariff that learned price discrimination in 2009, a proposal fee doubled in 2023, and a priced courtyard at the top, each layer a more confident monetization of the same devotion. The strata also mark what never changed across the sequence: the volunteers answered letters in every period at the same wage of nothing, the visitors arrived in every period already knowing the balcony's date, and the play sat underneath the entire deposit, unowned and unroyaltied, generating value it could not collect. An economy that behaves so consistently across nine decades of management is not an accident of promotion. It is a structure with rules, and the rules are the ones the profile prices.

## The Counterfactual: Verona Without the Lovers

The cleanest test of the legend's economic weight is subtraction. Remove the lovers from Verona and the city's skeleton remains formidable: the Arena would still fill its fifty summer evenings with the opera audience that produced €35 million in 2025, the UNESCO-listed centre would still draw the heritage traveller, the wine country of Valpolicella would still bottle its vintages at the gates, and Veronafiere, the city's exhibition corporation, would still stage Vinitaly, among the largest wine trade fairs in the world, filling hotels with business travellers who have never stood in the courtyard. Verona without Romeo and Juliet is a prosperous provincial city with a world-class summer festival and a serious fair trade. The counterfactual therefore disciplines the promoters' arithmetic: the lovers did not create Verona's tourism economy, and the two million annual visitors would not fall to zero in their absence, since the Arena's 404,715 spectators of 2025 alone testify to a draw that owes the balcony nothing.

What would vanish is more specific and, for the city's strategy, more precious. The February occupancy built by Verona in Love would vanish, and with it the smoothing of the lodging year. The wedding trade would vanish, every tier of it, from the €1,000 municipal licence to the private receptions it anchors, because no couple crosses an ocean to marry in a trade-fair hall. The mail would vanish, and with it the sincerity machine that no other Italian city operates. The emotional trademark would vanish: Verona would remain the city of the Arena in the trade's shorthand, a summer address, one monumental brand among Italy's many, instead of the city of love, a phrase that markets the destination in every month and every language to the largest travelling constituency on earth, people in love or hoping to be. The courtyard economy of Via Cappello, the guides' standing itinerary, the souvenir cluster and the circuit's whole half-day product would vanish into an ordinary shopping street. Subtraction thus locates the legend's true contribution with a precision the gross figures never achieve: not the largest share of Verona's tourism, but its only counter-seasonal, globally legible, emotionally trademarked layer, the layer that converts a heritage city into a destination with a promise. Cities can buy festivals and restore monuments. The promise that a place will ratify a visitor's own love story is the one asset in the portfolio that cannot be commissioned, which is why its defence has justified a century of municipal patience and why its pricing, when it finally came, was set at €5 rather than at a sum that might test the congregation's faith.

## The Verdict on Verona's Economy of Two Fictional Lovers

The verdict follows from the figures rather than from the romance. Verona's lovers' economy is a low-yield, high-volume devotion economy whose scarcest asset is a fabricated balcony, and its accounts divide into three certainties and one permanent unknown. The first certainty is volume: two million visitors a year to the city in the documented counts, a fifth of all museum traffic to the fictional house in 2016, 329,000 paid admissions that year, and 586,742 routed visits in the first six months of priced entry, a demand that survived both exposure of the fabrication and the end of free access. The second certainty is the smallness of the unit price through most of the system's history: €6 tickets, €4.50 tombs, €50 proposals, sums that mark the lovers as a mass devotion rather than a luxury trade, with the wedding tariff's €1,000 ceiling and the 2023 doubling of the proposal fee showing the city learning, slowly, where the willingness to pay actually sits. The third certainty is the asymmetry of capture: the municipality prices the centre and takes the lodging tax, private hospitality absorbs the unmeasured circumference, Hollywood took the letters' story for nothing, and the volunteers who manufacture the brand's sincerity are paid in meaning alone.

The structure carries one strategic risk that the pricing reforms of 2025 and 2026 brought into the open. A devotion economy fed by excursionists lives on goodwill, and the courtyard's conversion from a free civic passage into a booked and ticketed route was debated in Verona precisely as a question of what the city owes its own residents and its poorer pilgrims against what it may charge its richer ones. The six-month figures suggest the city judged the politics correctly, since volume held and house visits rose, but the reform also changed the product's moral basis in a way the wedding tariff never did: the vow was always a luxury purchase, while the courtyard had been the one element of the legend that money could not improve, the free square metre where any lover on earth could stand. Pricing it aligned the lovers with every other managed attraction in Europe and surrendered a distinction that had cost the city nothing but congestion. Whether the €5 gate is remembered as the moment the devotion economy matured or the moment it began taxing its own congregation is a question the next decade of turnstile data will answer; the audit records the trade without pretending the figures of a single season settle it.

The permanent unknown is the legend's true share of the two million, and this article leaves it unknown on purpose, because the discipline of the audit matters more than the size of the claim. Verona's promoters have every incentive to call the whole city the lovers' product; its sceptics have an equal and opposite incentive to call the courtyard a rounding error beside the Arena's €35 million summers. The graded figures support neither slogan. They support a structure: a fiction with no owner, anchored to real ground by a century of municipal investment, monetized at devotional prices by the city that holds the ground, subsidized by unpaid tenderness, and shadowed at every point by the authentic colossus in Piazza Bra, whose audited millions prove what Verona's heritage earns when the monument is real, and whose audience sleeps in rooms the lovers' brand helps to fill. The economy's final defence against the commodification charge is the one the play supplied in advance. Verona did not invent the conversion of these deaths into gold. It read the last scene, where the fathers promise statues in pure gold "that whiles Verona by that name is known" (5.3), and it built the statues, the house, the balcony and the tariff schedule that the promise described. The city is not the tragedy's parasite. The city is the tragedy's longest-running production, and its box office, at last, is learning to charge what the house will bear.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does Verona actually sell to visitors who come for Romeo and Juliet?

Verona sells access to the ground the fiction names, not the fiction itself. The play is public property, free to every printer and producer on earth, so the city's product is co-presence: entry to the courtyard and house on Via Cappello, a place before the sarcophagus called Juliet's tomb, ten private minutes on the balcony, a legally binding wedding staged inside the legend, and a handwritten answer to a letter addressed to a heroine who never lived. Every priced item in the system is a form of proximity to a story the buyer already owns for nothing, which is why the scarcest assets, the balcony's ten minutes and the courtyard's square metres, command the system's most instructive prices.

### Q: How many visitors a year does the legend draw to Verona?

No instrument counts the legend's visitors separately, and the honest answer is a layered one. Francesco Marcorin, citing the municipal record, reported two million tourists visiting Verona in 2016, and the Smithsonian's 2019 account of the city used the same figure of some two million a year. A Verona press figure from 2023 credited the Shakespearean icon with attracting 1.5 million tourists annually, which would assign three quarters of the city's entire traffic to the lovers and which no municipal series supports. The defensible statement is that two million people a year entered a city whose most famous product was the lovers, and that the lovers' measurable share appears only at the museum gates, where the counting is exact.

### Q: How many paid admissions did Juliet's House record in 2016?

Juliet's House recorded 329,000 paid admissions in 2016. The figure comes from the annual report of the Direzione Musei d'Arte Monumenti, Verona's museums directorate, as cited by Francesco Marcorin in his 2017 paper on the city's crafted identities. It counts only the visitors who bought entry to the museum interior. The courtyard crowd of that year paid nothing and passed no gate, so the number is a floor under the site's true footfall rather than a measure of it, and VeronaCard admissions bundled into the city pass sit outside the count as well. For a shrine to a fictional girl, a third of a million paid entrances in a single year is the hardest number in the entire economy.

### Q: What share of Verona's museum visitors chose Juliet's House in 2016?

One fifth of them did: of every hundred tourists who entered a Verona museum in 2016, twenty visited Juliet's House. Marcorin's citation of the museums directorate's annual report gives the full distribution, with the Arena taking 49 of every hundred museum visits, Castelvecchio 10, the Roman theatre and its archaeological museum 6, and the Scaliger tombs 1. The comparison is the finding. A house whose claim rests on a surname's resemblance to a fictional family outdrew every authentic museum in a city of Roman and medieval monuments, beaten only by the amphitheatre, and it did so at the same €6 price as the genuine galleries it defeated.

### Q: How much did a ticket to Juliet's House cost before the courtyard was priced?

The house ticket stood at €6 under the long-standing municipal tariff that preceded the reforms of 2025 and 2026. That price bought the museum rooms, the film costumes, the bed displayed from the 1968 Zeffirelli production and the right to stand on the balcony itself, while the courtyard, the statue and the view of the balcony from below remained free to anyone entering from Via Cappello. The companion prices of the same schedule set the tomb museum at €4.50 and offered a combined ticket for house and tomb at €10. Six euros was a devotional price rather than a commercial one, set for a mass public and held steady while the crowds it admitted grew beyond the courtyard's comfort.

### Q: When did Verona start charging for the courtyard, and what did entry cost?

Verona first charged for the courtyard in the Christmas season of 2025, when entry to courtyard and house together cost €12 from 6 December 2025 to 6 January 2026. The measure was framed as a safety trial: the courtyard's capacity was cut to one hundred people at a time and online booking became compulsory. From 1 April 2026 the exception hardened into a system, with entry rerouted through the Teatro Nuovo, booking required of every visitor including VeronaCard holders, and a two-route tariff of €5 for the courtyard alone or €12 for courtyard and house together. The free courtyard, the funnel of the lovers' economy for generations, ended as a priced and rationed space.

### Q: How many visits did Juliet's House record in the first six months of priced entry?

The site recorded 586,742 visits between 1 April and 30 September 2026, in the figures the city released on 30 October 2026. Fifty-one per cent of visitors chose the courtyard route and 49 per cent continued into the house, and house visits rose by about half against the same period of 2022, an increase achieved after free entry ended. More than 40,000 entries were made with a VeronaCard, roughly 7 per cent of the total, which means the great majority of devotees paid the cash tariff. The season refuted the funnel theory that had kept the courtyard free: charging for the marker did not shrink the crowd, and the booking system spread the same devotion more evenly across the day.

### Q: How much revenue did the Arena Opera Festival report for 2025?

The Fondazione Arena di Verona reported total revenue above €35 million for its 2025 season, €35,619,000 in the figure carried by OperaWire's report of the foundation's announcement. The season drew 404,715 spectators from 130 countries, 61 per cent of them foreign, across some fifty evenings in the Roman amphitheatre. The comparison disciplines every claim made for the lovers: the Arena's single summer of ticketed performances, at opera prices, in a genuine monument, produced a countable fortune of a size the devotion economy, with its €5 and €6 and €12 price points, approaches only across far longer periods and far larger crowds. Verona's authentic asset is its yield engine; the lovers are its volume engine.

### Q: What did the Nomisma study claim the Arena festival generates?

The Nomisma study presented in May 2026 calculated that the Fondazione Arena and its festival generated €2 billion for the Italian economy, close to 6,000 jobs and €206 million in taxes, returning €6.30 for every euro invested. It also found that 71 per cent of the foundation's production value came from tickets, sponsorships and private contributions rather than public subsidy. The study was commissioned by the Fondazione Arena itself, and this article grades it accordingly, as an advocacy document with a methodology attached: impact multipliers trace a euro through suppliers and wages until a convention decides where the tracing stops. The festival's audited box office needs no such discount, and the contrast between the countable €35 million and the claimed €2 billion is the authentic sector's version of the same inflation that devotion's promoters practise.

### Q: How much did Verona charge foreign couples to marry at Juliet's House in 2009?

A couple from outside the European Union paid €1,000 under the schedule published at the launch of Sposami a Verona in 2009. The tariff graduated by residence with textbook precision: €600 for Verona residents, €700 for couples from the surrounding catchment, €800 for citizens of the European Union and €1,000 for the rest of the world, against the €50 then charged for an ordinary Italian civil marriage licence. Tourism councillor Daniele Polato launched the scheme with the declared ambition of making Verona a wedding capital rivalling Las Vegas, and Mayor Flavio Tosi defended the graduation as covering foreign paperwork. The schedule is price discrimination in its candid form, each segment charged what its devotion would bear.

### Q: How much does ten minutes on the balcony for a proposal cost?

Ten minutes of exclusive use of the balcony for a marriage proposal cost €100 under the schedule reported in April 2023, when the local press recorded that Verona had doubled the fee from its earlier €50. The product repays attention because it manufactures scarcity inside abundance: the courtyard absorbs a hundred visitors at once, while the balcony holds one couple, and the city sells that couple a rationed slice of the fiction at the exact moment their own lives change direction. The doubling met no reported resistance, which makes the proposal fee the cleanest market signal in the lovers' economy, a price raised on a fabricated monument and paid without protest by buyers who knew its history perfectly well.

### Q: Why does Verona charge non-residents more than residents for a wedding at the house?

The graduation is price discrimination, the standard practice of charging each market segment in proportion to its willingness to pay. A Verona couple marrying at the house buys a municipal service with a romantic room attached, and the 2009 schedule charged them €600; a couple flown in from another continent buys a once-in-a-lifetime staging that anchors an entire destination wedding, and the same schedule charged them €1,000, with the intermediate tiers priced between. The official defence offered at the launch, higher administrative costs for foreign paperwork, explains a surcharge but not a two-thirds premium scaled so neatly by distance. Distance measures devotion in this market, and the tariff prices the measurement: the farther a couple travels to marry under the balcony, the more the balcony is worth to them, and the schedule collects the difference.

### Q: How many letters to Juliet arrive in Verona each year?

The published estimates range from about five thousand to about ten thousand letters a year, and no audited figure exists. Profiles of the Club di Giulietta across the 2000s and 2010s, including the record in Lise and Ceil Friedman's 2006 book Letters to Juliet, support the lower part of the range, while festival-era coverage circulated by Tourism Verona favoured ten thousand, and at least one popular account reached fifty thousand by folding in the notes and emails left at the house. The Club answers every letter that carries a return address, by hand, without charge. The fivefold spread between careful and careless counts is itself a finding of the audit: where devotion is priced, Verona counts to the last visitor, and where devotion is given away, the counting dissolves into round numbers.

### Q: Who answers the letters to Juliet, and are they paid?

The letters are answered by the volunteers of the Club di Giulietta, who style themselves Juliet's secretaries and are not paid. Giulio Tamassia founded the Club as a nonprofit cultural association in 1972, institutionalizing a practice that began in 1937 when Ettore Solimani, custodian of the tomb, started replying to the notes visitors left at the sarcophagus; Tamassia's daughter Giovanna later directed the work. The secretaries answer by hand, in the writer's language where the volunteer bench allows, and sign as secretaries of Juliet. Their unpaid labour manufactures the brand's sincerity, the quality the ticket office and the wedding tariff then monetize, which makes the Club the load-bearing subsidy of the entire economy: the one production line whose workers are compensated in participation alone.

### Q: What did the 2010 film Letters to Juliet do to Verona's letter trade?

The film turned the secretaries into a global advertisement that Verona did not pay for. Gary Winick's 2010 romance, built around the Club di Giulietta and its answering practice, carried the address of a fictional girl's mail service into cinemas worldwide, and press accounts from the film's release period reported the Club's post swollen with letters from viewers who had learned from a screen that Juliet answers. The pattern is the devotion economy's standard subsidy: a studio financed the storytelling, the storytelling manufactured devotion, and the devotion arrived by post in Verona, where volunteers converted it into reputation at no cost to the city. Hollywood kept the box office. Verona kept the mail, and the mail, answered by hand, kept the brand sincere for another generation of visitors.

### Q: Why does nobody collect a royalty on the Romeo and Juliet brand?

No royalty exists because Shakespeare's text sits in the public domain in every jurisdiction, beyond copyright, so no estate, publisher or descendant holds a claim that a licence could attach to. The consequence shapes the whole economy: since the story itself cannot be owned, value migrates to the things that can be, the ground on Via Cappello, the sarcophagus at San Francesco al Corso, the municipal tariff, the festival calendar and the accumulated association between a city's name and a pair of lovers. Rivals may print the names on any merchandise at the same zero input cost, and Verona's defence is not legal but geographic and historical, a century of purchased ground, built scenery and visitor habit that a competitor cannot download. The playwright's share of the €12 ticket is the fame the Prologue promised, payable in reputation only.

### Q: Who pays for the upkeep of the courtyard, the statue and the walls?

The municipality pays, through the civic museums budget that maintains the Casa as part of Verona's Direzione Musei system, and the bills are the devotion economy's negative line. The bronze statue placed in the courtyard in 1972 was worn and stressed by the custom of rubbing its right breast for luck until the right arm fractured in 2014, when the city withdrew the original into the house and installed a replica for the daily rite. The walls of the entrance passage required panels, prohibitions and cleaning as visitors covered them with notes, gum and declarations. Substitution, channeling and, from 2026, priced rationing of the touching itself are the city's answers, and its willingness to keep paying them is quiet evidence that the gross returns clear the costs by a margin no council has publicly doubted.

### Q: Does Verona publish a total for what the lovers are worth to the city?

No audited total exists, and the absence is structural. The city's instruments count heads at gates, not motives in minds: the two million annual visitors of the documented counts mix overnight guests with day-trippers, Arena audiences with courtyard pilgrims, and no municipal series separates the lovers' share or the spending it causes. Promotional arithmetic fills the gap from both directions, with local press crediting the icon with 1.5 million tourists a year and commissioned impact studies multiplying countable cores into billions. The graded record supports a structure rather than a sum: a fifth of museum traffic in 2016, 329,000 paid admissions, a wedding tariff graduated by nationality and 586,742 visits in the first priced season. Any single figure offered for the legend's worth, by promoter or sceptic, exceeds what the evidence can carry.
