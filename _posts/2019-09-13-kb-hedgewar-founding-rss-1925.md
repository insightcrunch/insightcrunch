On 27 September 1925, the festival of Vijayadashami, Keshav Baliram Hedgewar gathered a small group of associates at his residence in Nagpur and announced the founding of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the organisation known by its initials as the RSS. No charter was read out, no manifesto was released to the press, and no public meeting marked the occasion. What Hedgewar unveiled that day was not a programme but a machine for producing organised men: a daily local gathering called the shakha, a cadre rather than a membership roll, a training hour that joined drill to discussion, and a strict refusal to contest elections or to merge with any political party. The argument of this article is that the near-century durability of the RSS follows from those four architectural choices of 1925, and that the standard biographical treatment of Hedgewar, which reads the Sangh as the consummation of one man's life, cannot explain why the design outlived its designer, crossed every political era since independence, and survived three separate bans with its form intact.

## The RSS Founding at Nagpur on 27 September 1925

The founding scene survives in two kinds of record, and the difference between them matters for everything that follows. The first is the Sangh's own published chronology, carried in its official milestones accounts and repeated in M.S. Golwalkar's Hedgewar Charit of 1957, the biography written by Hedgewar's successor. The second is the reconstruction assembled by Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle in The Brotherhood in Saffron (1987), built from interviews with surviving early swayamsevaks and from the organisational papers that families and local units had kept. The two records agree on the core facts: the date, 27 September 1925; the place, Hedgewar's residence in Nagpur, the capital of the Central Provinces; the scale, a room of associates rather than a rally; and the content of the announcement, which concerned training rather than agitation. In the Sangh's own chronology, his words at the gathering are preserved as a summons: those present must train themselves physically, intellectually and in every way so as to be capable of achieving their cherished goal. The vocabulary is the vocabulary of preparation. A political founding announces demands, grievances and a programme of action. Hedgewar's announcement specified a regimen.

The associates in the room came from the two public worlds Hedgewar had worked in for a decade, the Nagpur Congress organisation and the Hindu Mahasabha circles around B.S. Moonje. Most were young, many still students, and several would remain inside the Sangh's leadership for decades. Appaji Joshi, a close associate from Hedgewar's Congress years, stood inside that first circle. Marthandrao Jog, a retired military man in the same circle, would take charge of the physical training that gave the early shakha its drill-square character. What the gathering did not produce is as telling as what it did. There was no founding resolution addressed to any government, no list of office-bearers announced to newspapers, no subscription collected and no name adopted. The organisation that would be counted among the largest volunteer bodies in the world began without a name, and it acquired one only on 17 April 1926, when sixteen swayamsevaks met at Hedgewar's residence and chose Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh by deliberation and vote from a shortlist that included Jaripatka Mandal, Bharat Uddharak Mandal and Hindu Swayamsevak Sangh. A founder indifferent to design does not leave his organisation unnamed for seven months and then let its name be settled by a vote among alternatives. The delay and the procedure both belong to a man building a form he intended to last longer than his own direction of it.

### How many men were present when Hedgewar announced the Sangh in 1925?

Between fifteen and twenty associates were present, by the count carried in the standard accounts, Andersen and Damle's The Brotherhood in Saffron (1987) and the Sangh's published chronology. Most were young Nagpur men from Hedgewar's Congress and Hindu Mahasabha circles. No attendance register or minutes book survives in the Hedgewar Papers.

The choice of day carried its own statement. Vijayadashami, the tenth day of the autumn Navaratri cycle, marks in the Ramayana tradition the victory of Rama over Ravana and, in Maharashtra's public memory, the season when Shivaji's armies traditionally opened a campaign after the monsoon. The festival joins religious observance to martial renewal: weapons and tools are worshipped, and new ventures are begun under an auspicious sign. Hedgewar, whose biographers record a mind stocked with the Maratha past, chose the one day of the year on which beginning an organisation dedicated to disciplined collective strength required no explanation to any Hindu audience in Nagpur. Yet the founding was staged with an almost aggressive modesty. There was no procession, no public shastra puja, no attempt to convert the festival crowd into a membership. The contrast between the symbolic weight of the date and the privacy of the event is the first evidence for the architectural reading: Hedgewar wanted the date's meaning inside the cadre's memory, and he wanted nothing that resembled a launch in the political market of rallies, resolutions and press coverage.

Nagpur's position completes the scene. The city was a provincial capital with a strong Congress organisation, a significant Hindu Mahasabha presence, a Marathi-speaking Hindu majority in a province that also held large Hindi-speaking districts, and a recent scar: the communal rioting of 1923, in which Hindu processions and Muslim neighbourhoods had clashed and in which, by the account Andersen and Damle record from Hedgewar's own retelling, the Hindus of the city had been caught disorganised and panicky. Hedgewar drew from 1923 a conclusion about organisation rather than about any other community: a population without standing habits of collective discipline would panic under pressure regardless of its numbers or its grievances. The Sangh was designed as the standing habit. Every architectural choice examined in this article answers that diagnosis, and the diagnosis itself came from an event, dated and local, that a biographical reading tends to pass over as background colour.

## Three Organisations Before the Sangh: What Hedgewar Had Already Tried

The architectural reading gains its force from a simple observation: by 1925 Hedgewar had served an apprenticeship inside three distinct organisational forms, and the Sangh negates the failure mode of each. He had been a revolutionary conspirator in Calcutta, a Congress campaigner in Vidarbha, and a Hindu Mahasabha associate in Nagpur. Each form taught him a capability, and each form failed in a way he had watched at close quarters. The standard biographical treatment narrates these years as a pilgrim's progress toward the founding. Read architecturally, they are a designer's survey of available templates, conducted from inside the templates themselves.

The first template was the revolutionary secret society. Hedgewar was born on 1 April 1889 in Nagpur, lost both parents to plague at thirteen, and was sent in 1910 to study medicine at Calcutta Medical College, in biographical accounts at the instance of the Tilakite leadership in Maharashtra, who wanted a trusted young man inside Bengal's revolutionary networks. Calcutta in those years held the Anushilan Samiti, the most extensive of the revolutionary organisations, with its gymnasiums, its cells and its programme of physical and moral preparation for armed action. Hedgewar joined the Samiti and, on the testimony of Trailokyanath Chakravarty, an Anushilan leader whose memoir Jele Trish Bachhar (Thirty Years in Prison) remains a primary source for the movement, he reached its inner circle. The Samiti's capabilities were real: discipline, secrecy, physical training, an ethic of self-sacrifice, and a cadre bound by personal loyalty. Its failure mode was structural. A secret society cannot grow beyond the reach of personal trust, and the colonial police state could decapitate it with a single round of arrests, as the Alipore and later conspiracy cases demonstrated against the Bengal revolutionaries between 1908 and 1918. Hedgewar took the gymnasium and the ethic from Anushilan. He discarded the secrecy and the bomb.

The second template was the Congress mass organisation. Back in Nagpur by the middle of the following decade, Hedgewar threw himself into Congress work in the Tilakite tradition, organised volunteers for the Congress session held at Nagpur in December 1920, and served the provincial Congress organisation in secretarial roles. When Gandhi's Non-Cooperation movement reached the Central Provinces, Hedgewar campaigned for it across Vidarbha, and the campaign ended in a courtroom. In May 1921 he was arrested for seditious speeches delivered at Katol and Bharatwada, defended himself in a statement that the presiding judge, in the Sangh's oft-retold account, called more seditious than the speeches themselves, and received a sentence of one year's rigorous imprisonment, which he served at Ajani jail in Nagpur until his release in July 1922. The episode matters architecturally because of what a year inside a colonial prison teaches an organiser: a campaign organisation lives and dies by the calendar of its agitations. Between movements, the Congress machine thinned to committees and notables; its membership drives surged and ebbed with each campaign; and its alliances, above all the Khilafat alliance with which Gandhi had bound the movement to a religious cause in 1920 and 1921, exposed it to shocks it could not control, as the Moplah violence in Malabar in August 1921 demonstrated to contemporaries. Hedgewar's disagreement with the Khilafat strategy, recorded by Andersen and Damle and by Jaffrelot alike, was at bottom an organisational judgment: a body that borrows its energy from causes outside itself will be deserted when those causes collapse.

The third template was the Hindu Mahasabha, the political organisation of Hindu interests founded in 1915 and led in the Central Provinces by B.S. Moonje, a physician, former associate of Tilak, and the senior figure of Nagpur's Hindu politics. From 1923 Hedgewar worked in the Mahasabha's orbit, and Moonje became his mentor in Hindu organisational politics, a relationship documented in the correspondence preserved in the Hedgewar Papers. The Mahasabha's capability was representation: it could pass resolutions, petition governments and contest the representative institutions the colonial state offered. Its failure mode was the obverse of the Congress failure. Where Congress dissolved between campaigns, the Mahasabha never condensed at all: it was an organisation of conference platforms and elected notables, with no daily presence in any locality, no cadre below its leaders, and no formative hold on the young. It could speak for Hindus in a council chamber. It could not manufacture an organised Hindu in a neighbourhood. Hedgewar had also read, in 1923, the text that gave the Mahasabha's politics its ideological vocabulary, Vinayak Damodar Savarkar's Essentials of Hindutva, composed during Savarkar's internment at Ratnagiri and published under the pen name A Mahratta; Sangh biographical accounts record that Hedgewar visited Savarkar at Ratnagiri in 1925, before the founding, to discuss the organisation he intended to build. Doctrine, therefore, was available off the shelf. What did not exist anywhere in India in 1925 was an organisational form that combined the revolutionary's cadre depth, the mass movement's public presence and the political party's permanence, while shedding secrecy, campaign dependence and notable-thinness. The Sangh was Hedgewar's answer to that combined specification, and its four founding choices map, one by one, onto the failure modes he had served inside.

## Reading a Founder Through His Design: The Personality-as-Institution Method

The scholarly disagreement this article adjudicates can be stated plainly. The biographical-narrative reading, set by Hedgewar Charit in 1957 and reproduced in most popular treatments, explains the RSS as Hedgewar's life consummated: the boy who refused the sweets distributed for Queen Victoria's jubilee becomes the student conspirator, the Congress prisoner, and finally the founder, with the organisation appearing as the outer form of an inner trajectory. The institutional-architectural reading, which this article defends, explains the RSS as a set of design decisions taken in and after 1925, whose consequences can be traced independently of the designer's personality, in the organisation's survival profile across a century. The two readings use much of the same evidence. They differ in what they take the evidence to explain.

The method applied here is the one this series developed for an earlier founder-figure of a different kind, [the personality-as-institution framework](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/), which asks of any dominant political personality not what the person intended but what institutional residue the person's choices deposited, and whether the residue operated after the person left the scene. Applied to Hedgewar, the method has an unusual advantage: the residue is almost entirely formal. Hedgewar left no major book, no collected speeches of consequence, no doctrine that bears his name. He left a format, a cadre system, a training curriculum and a rule of political abstention. Where Indira Gandhi's institutional residue has to be excavated from decisions taken inside a pre-existing state, Hedgewar's is the whole of his legacy, standing in plain sight. The man is unusually hard to separate from the design because the design is very nearly all there is.

A second instrument from this series sharpens the adjudication. The analysis of Hindutva as three strands that polemic habitually fuses, [the tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/), separates Savarkar's doctrinal definition, the Sangh's organisational practice and the electoral Hindutva of party politics into distinct objects with distinct histories. The separation matters here because the biographical reading silently borrows the doctrine's history to explain the organisation's durability, as if the RSS lasted because Hindutva persuaded. Once the strands are separated, the durability question relocates: doctrines of comparable content, carried by the Hindu Mahasabha's electoral form, failed to produce a durable organisation in the same decades and the same towns. The variable that differs is architecture. Jaffrelot's The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics (1996), in its chapters on the early Sangh, supplies much of the empirical spine for this relocation, since Jaffrelot reconstructs the early shakha network, the pracharak system and the Mahasabha relationship from organisational records and interviews rather than from the founder's legend. Tapan Basu and his co-authors, in Khaki Shorts and Saffron Flags (1993), reach the organisation from the opposite political direction and still place its format, its daily discipline and its cellular spread at the centre of their explanation of its strength. When scholars who celebrate the Sangh, scholars who study it neutrally and scholars who oppose it all converge on the organisation's form as the thing to be explained, the biographical reading is left explaining the wrong variable.

The primary evidence has a specific address. The K.B. Hedgewar Papers, preserved at the archives of the Hedgewar Smarak Samiti in Nagpur, hold the founder's correspondence, including the early internal correspondence of 1925 to 1930 between Hedgewar and his first cadre, in which the shakha format was specified and the cadre-only membership practice was settled. These letters are the least-used stratum of the RSS archive, and they are architectural documents in the strict sense: they concern timings, programmes, instructor standards and the terms on which a swayamsevak was to be made. Researchers working through the scanned sets of this correspondence, comparing the format specifications year by year, can lay the documents side by side with tools as ordinary as [a browser-based PowerPoint viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) when the sets circulate as presentation decks, and what the comparison shows is standardisation proceeding month by month under the founder's hand. Hedgewar Charit, by contrast, must be handled as what it is: an act of institutional memory by the successor who inherited the machine, invaluable for the founder's image inside the Sangh and treacherous as a record of fact wherever reverence and record diverge. The adjudication that follows relies, wherever the two conflict, on the correspondence and on the convergent secondary literature, and treats the biography as evidence about the Sangh's memory of its founder rather than about the founder himself.


## The Archive of a Design: What the Hedgewar Papers Preserve

The architectural reading stands or falls on documents, and the documents repay a closer description than they usually receive. The Hedgewar Papers at the Hedgewar Smarak Samiti archives in Nagpur are not the papers of a public man in the usual sense: there are no diaries of self-explanation, no drafts of a testament, no literary remains. What survives in bulk is working correspondence, the letters of an organiser running a young organisation by post across a province and then a country, and the correspondence's subjects are relentlessly practical. Timings of shakhas are fixed and refixed. The sequence of the daily programme is argued and settled. Instructors are assessed, posted and recalled. The terms on which a young man might be taken up as a full-time worker are weighed case by case. Read against the devotional literature, the letters are almost shocking in their administrative dryness, and the dryness is the point: the founding of the RSS generated specifications, not scriptures, and the specifications are the primary evidence that the architecture was designed rather than improvised.

Three strata of the correspondence carry particular weight. The first is the run of letters from 1925 to 1930 between Hedgewar and his initial cadre, in which the shakha format's specifications were established: the shift from the twice-weekly pattern of the first months to the daily shakha of May 1926, the introduction of the lathi and of standardised drill commands, the content expected in the discussion segment. These letters show standardisation as a process conducted by argument, with local organisers proposing variations and the centre ruling on them, which is to say they show a constitution being drafted in correspondence a quarter-century before the constitution of 1949 gave the practice documentary form. The second stratum is the Moonje correspondence, in which the relationship between the Sangh and the Hindu Mahasabha was negotiated in real time: the letters record respect, cooperation and a steadily clarifying divergence over method, as Hedgewar declined, in courteous and repeated terms, to let the young cadre body be absorbed into the Mahasabha's political programme. The third stratum concerns the cadre itself: letters touching the first full-time workers, their maintenance, their transfers and the standards expected of them, in which the pracharak system can be watched assembling itself out of individual decisions about individual men.

The archive's silences are as architectural as its contents. There are no membership returns, because there was no membership to return. There are no substantial financial ledgers of the kind a subscription body keeps, because the guru dakshina's anonymous envelopes generated devotion rather than accounts. There is no founding charter, no minute book of the Vijayadashami gathering, no attendance list. A historian of the Congress or the Mahasabha works from printed proceedings and can take the paper trail for granted; a historian of the early Sangh works from letters, from the intelligence reports of a colonial state watching from outside, and from testimony gathered decades later, which is the evidentiary base Andersen and Damle assembled through their interviews with first-generation swayamsevaks for The Brotherhood in Saffron. The thinness of the paper record is usually lamented as an obstacle to scholarship. For the question this article asks, the thinness is itself data: an organisation that declined to generate the documents by which organisations are usually known was an organisation whose substance lay elsewhere, in routine and relationship, and the archive's very gaps trace the outline of the design. Access to the Smarak Samiti collections has been mediated by the memorial trust, and the secondary literature's dependence on interviews reflects that mediation; the correspondence stratum remains, as the discipline's practitioners acknowledge, under-worked relative to its importance, and it is the stratum on which any future revision of the founding's history will have to be built.

## Choice One: The Shakha as the Basic Unit of the Whole Design

The first and most consequential of the four choices was the unit. Every organisation Hedgewar had served was built from the top down: the Samiti from its secret central leadership, the Congress from its annual session and provincial committees, the Mahasabha from its conferences. Hedgewar inverted the construction. The RSS would be built from a single repeating cell, the shakha, a gathering of swayamsevaks in one locality, meeting for one hour, running one fixed programme, requiring no building, no funds beyond a ground and a flag, and no permission from any headquarters to exist. Headquarters, in the Sangh's mature vocabulary, does not create shakhas so much as recognise them. The organisation's growth would be counted in shakhas opened, not in members enrolled, and that accounting choice, visible already in the internal documents of the late 1920s, tells the reader where the designer located the organisation's substance.

The shakha programme settled quickly into the form it retained for a century. The gathering opens with a salute to the Bhagwa Dhwaj, the saffron flag that Hedgewar placed at the centre of the ground as the organisation's guru, its teacher-object. There follow physical exercises, drill movements performed in formation and on command, and practice with the lathi, the long staff that served as the shakha's training weapon. Games occupy a middle portion of the hour. Songs, patriotic in content and sung in unison, carry the programme's emotional register. A discussion segment, the bauddhik, delivers the formative content: a talk by an instructor, a passage read and explained, a question taken up in common. The hour closes with the Sangh prayer and a final salute to the flag. The standardisation was deliberate and dated. In the first months the gatherings met twice a week, on Sundays for drill and on Thursdays and Sundays for discourse, in the pattern the Sangh's chronology records. The full daily form, the nitya shakha, began on 28 May 1926 at the Mohite Wada ground in Nagpur, with the lathi drills and the drill commands introduced at the same stage. The first route march, the path sanchalan in which swayamsevaks parade in uniform through public streets, followed within the year with thirty participants.

### Where did the first daily shakha meet in Nagpur?

The first daily shakha met from 28 May 1926 on the ground at Mohite Wada in Nagpur, where the lathi entered the programme and the drill commands were standardised. Before that date the founding circle met twice a week near Hedgewar's residence, dividing the meetings between drill on Sundays and discussion of national affairs.

The design properties of the cell repay attention, because they explain the survival profile traced later in this article. The shakha is public but not provocative: it meets on open ground, in view of any passer-by, performing activities that a watching policeman would record as exercise, games and singing. It is daily, which converts membership from a status declared once into a habit renewed each morning or evening, and habits survive the arrest of leaders in a way that statuses cannot. It is cheap to the point of invisibility: a flag, a ground, a set of staffs, no rent, no press, no fund that a hostile administration could freeze. It is self-similar at every scale, since a town with one shakha and a province with five hundred run the same hour, and a swayamsevak transferred by work or marriage can walk into a shakha anywhere in India and take his place in the ranks without introduction. And it is leader-light: the local instructor, the shikshak, runs the programme from a standard format, so the cell's quality does not depend on charisma. Andersen and Damle, whose interviews with first-generation swayamsevaks give the fullest account of the early shakhas, emphasise the deliberate tedium of the design: Hedgewar was not building a stage for inspiration but a routine robust enough to survive boredom, weather, police attention and the founder's own absence. The shakha is the architectural answer to the Anushilan failure mode. Where the secret cell multiplied risk as it multiplied members, the public cell multiplied resilience.

Comparison with the alternatives available in 1925 fixes the choice's originality. The gymnasium and akhara tradition of Maharashtra and Bengal offered physical training without ideological curriculum or national organisation. The Congress volunteer corps, of which Hedgewar himself had organised the Nagpur contingent in 1920, offered public drill subordinated to campaign calendars. The Mahasabha offered doctrine without any local routine at all. The shakha fused gymnasium, volunteer corps and study circle into a single daily institution owned by no campaign and directed at no immediate political object, and it is precisely this refusal of an immediate object that puzzled contemporaries and that the biographical reading, with its search for the founder's intentions toward events, consistently undervalues. The intention was the routine itself.

## Choice Two: A Cadre, Not a Membership Roll

The second choice governed who belonged, and it was as radical as the first. The RSS kept no membership register, issued no card, collected no subscription and conducted no enrolment ceremony that created a legal or documentary fact of membership. A boy or man who came to the shakha ground and took part was a swayamsevak; a man who stopped coming had, in organisational terms, simply stopped, and might return without formality. Recruitment proceeded by personal contact: an existing swayamsevak brought a friend, a student, a younger brother, and the newcomer was absorbed into the daily routine over weeks rather than signed up in a moment. The cadre identity was performative, created and recreated by attendance, and the organisation's centre held no list on which that identity depended.

Each element of the practice answers a failure Hedgewar had observed. The Congress membership drive, with its nominal fee and its paper rolls, produced paper members whose allegiance expired with the campaign that had recruited them. The secret societies produced the opposite defect, a cadre so tightly screened that it could not grow. Hedgewar's practice split the difference by relocating the screening into time: the shakha itself was the filter, since only the genuinely committed would sustain daily attendance at drill for months without pay, office or public recognition, and the insincere filtered themselves out without any expulsion procedure. The design also carried a defensive property that later decades would make precious. An organisation with no membership list offers a banning state nothing to seize. There is no roll to confiscate, no card index from which to compile arrest lists, no subscription account to freeze. The membership exists in the relationships between the men on the ground, and relationships cannot be impounded by a district magistrate.

Above the local cadre Hedgewar built the system's second element, the corps of full-time organisers known as pracharaks. The pracharak gives his working life to the Sangh, remains unmarried in the classical pattern, lives on the organisation's modest support, and accepts transfer to any district or province where shakhas are to be opened or strengthened. The system took shape under Hedgewar's hand in the 1930s, as the first generation of Nagpur swayamsevaks matured into men willing to be sent out: associates such as Babasaheb Apte travelled on the founder's instructions to open ground in new towns, carrying nothing but the format and the authority of the centre's confidence. The pracharak is the connective tissue that the shakha's localism requires. A federation of autonomous daily cells would drift into a thousand local variants within a decade; the travelling full-timer, rotated between postings and formed in the founder's own camps, carries the standard from cell to cell and reports the condition of each district back to the centre. The mature Sangh's famed uniformity, the same hour run the same way in Kerala and in Nagpur, is the pracharak system's product, and its origins lie in Hedgewar's refusal to let the local cell stand alone.

The pracharak corps also solved, quietly, the leadership problem that kills founder-built bodies at the second generation. A party recruits its future leaders from public life and inherits their ambitions and factions ready-made; the Sangh grew its leaders inside the format, young men formed by the daily shakha, tested as instructors, sent out as pracharaks to districts where they were strangers, and promoted on the evidence of organisational work done far from any spotlight. Golwalkar's own path ran through this pipeline's early form: a university lecturer drawn in through a provincial shakha, recalled by Hedgewar from a Bengal ashram in the late 1930s when he had turned toward renunciation, and raised through organisational responsibility until the succession of 1940 could fall to him as a matter of course. The corps was, in effect, a standing solution to succession at every level simultaneously, since at any moment the organisation held hundreds of men whose entire formation fitted them to replace any officer above them. No document of 1925 announces this consequence, and the biographical literature rarely names it, but the correspondence on postings and standards shows the pipeline being built deliberately, one transfer at a time.

The cadre's finance completes the design. In place of subscriptions the Sangh instituted the guru dakshina, an annual offering in which each swayamsevak places before the flag, as guru, a sum according to his means, in an envelope that records no name against the amount. The first guru dakshina collection, held in 1928, raised a total of eighty-four rupees, a figure preserved in the Sangh's chronology precisely because its smallness measures the distance travelled. The doctrinal choice beneath the practice is architectural, not merely financial. By installing the saffron flag, an object, as the guru to whom offering and obedience are rendered, Hedgewar removed the person of the founder from the organisation's devotional centre. No swayamsevak makes his offering to Hedgewar, swears by Hedgewar or salutes Hedgewar's portrait at the shakha's opening. The loyalty the format generates attaches to the flag and through the flag to the nation the flag represents, which is to say it attaches to something that does not die, retire or disgrace itself. Critics of the Sangh, Basu and his co-authors among them, read the no-paper cadre and the flag-centred devotion as instruments of insulation, a structure designed to maximise deniability and to bind the cadre by ties the outside world cannot audit. The reading is attributed here at its strongest because it identifies real properties of the design. What it does not explain is why those properties should have produced durability rather than the stagnation that insulated bodies usually earn, and the missing variable is the daily public routine of the shakha itself, which kept the cadre's formation open to any observer even as its formal membership stayed undocumented. Insulation and openness were combined, and the combination was the design.

## Choice Three: Drill, Song and Discussion Inside a Single Hour

The third choice concerned the hour's content, and specifically the decision to weld physical training to ideological formation inside one daily programme. Hedgewar's Calcutta years had taught him the gymnasium's power to bind young men; his Congress years had taught him the meeting's power to inform them; neither, separately, could form a cadre. The shakha hour assigned the body and the mind to the same sixty minutes: drill and exercise to produce discipline and collective confidence, song to produce shared emotion under control, discussion to produce a common account of the nation's condition and the Hindu's place in it. The uniform, displayed first at the Ram Navami festival of 1926, khaki shorts, white shirt, black cap and staff, turned the individual participant into a visible unit of a larger body each time he put it on. The format's texture was military without being an army, devotional without being a congregation, and educational without being a school, and each of those proximities was calibrated.

The calibration had a defensive dimension under colonial rule. Physical exercise by young men on a public ground was an activity the colonial administration could observe but could not readily suppress as politics; there was no sedition in a drill square, no offence in a song sung in unison, no conspiracy in a discussion held in the open. The Central Provinces administration watched the young organisation with alternating suspicion and dismissal through the late 1920s and 1930s, and official discussion of the Sangh's military-style drill, recorded in the intelligence literature that Jaffrelot draws upon, repeatedly returned to the same frustration: the thing watched was real, organised and growing, yet no single suppressible political act could be found inside the shakha hour itself. Hedgewar understood the protective value of the format precisely. The ideological content travelled inside a vessel that the law of the period had no category for, and the vessel travelled daily, in public, in hundreds of localities, beneath the threshold of prohibition.

Formation, however, and not camouflage, was the content's primary purpose, and the evidence is the training superstructure Hedgewar built above the daily hour. In May 1927 the Sangh held its first Officers' Training Camp, the month-long intensive course in which selected swayamsevaks were trained as instructors, with seventeen participants in the first cohort. The camps became annual and then proliferated by province and by level, creating a ladder of qualification through which the ordinary swayamsevak could rise as shikshak, as mukhya shikshak, as camp officer, carrying the standard programme with standardised commands to every new ground. The content carried inside the hour deserves the same specificity as its form. The discussion segment drew on a settled repertoire: narratives of Indian history with the Maratha past at its centre, biographies of national heroes read as studies in character, the geography and civilisation of the country presented as a single sacred inheritance, and the condition of Hindu society examined under heads of unity, discipline and self-respect. Songs supplied the repertoire's emotional memory, and the prayer that closed the hour fixed its terms in verse every swayamsevak carried away verbatim. None of the content was esoteric, and its very publishedness was part of the design: a colonial prosecutor reading the bauddhik syllabus would find history, biography and exhortation, the stock of any nationalist schoolroom, while a swayamsevak absorbing the same syllabus daily across years underwent a formation no schoolroom matched for duration. A political party trains its workers to win the next contest; the camp system trained the Sangh's workers to reproduce the Sangh. The distinction is the whole of the third choice. The curriculum's centre of gravity was never the coming election or the current agitation, neither of which the organisation contested or joined, but the reproduction of the format itself in the person of the trainee. Curriculum, camps and commands together amount to a written constitution in practice, a standard operating form fixed a quarter-century before the Sangh possessed a constitution on paper, and they fixed the programme so firmly that Golwalkar, Deoras and their successors inherited a machine whose daily operation none of them needed to redesign.

## Choice Four: Separation from Electoral Politics

The fourth choice was a refusal, maintained with a consistency that contemporaries found perverse: the RSS would not operate as a political party, would not contest elections, would not affiliate with any party, and would not permit its organisational machinery to be placed at any campaign's disposal. Swayamsevaks remained free as individual citizens to join parties, vote, campaign and stand for office; the Sangh as a body stood outside. Hedgewar's correspondence and instructions return to the rule whenever pressure tested it, and the pressure was constant, because an expanding network of disciplined young men is the single asset every political entrepreneur of the period wished to borrow.

The rule's sharpest test came from Hedgewar's own mentor's direction. The Hindu Mahasabha, in whose orbit the young founder had worked, was precisely an electoral and representative body, and its national politics was being supplied in these years with doctrine by Savarkar, whose territorial account of the Hindu nation, [Savarkar's territorial-cultural framework as immediate antecedent](/2019/09/07/savarkar-hindutva-1923-reanalysed/), gave Hedgewar's generation its vocabulary of pitribhumi and punyabhumi, fatherland and holy land. Hedgewar took the vocabulary and declined the vehicle. When Savarkar assumed the Mahasabha's presidency in 1937 and led it toward electoral competition and wartime coalition ministries, the Sangh's separation stood out in still higher relief: the doctrine's author commanded a party that contested and bargained, while the doctrine's organisational heir commanded a cadre that would not contest at all. The divergence is the cleanest natural experiment the pillar offers. Two vehicles carried substantially the same ideological content through the same decades in overlapping towns; the electoral vehicle wasted to a shell, and the cadre vehicle grew without interruption. Ideology is the constant in that comparison and architecture is the variable, which is why this article locates the durability in the architecture and not in the doctrine.

The individual-capacity doctrine was the rule's operating mechanism, and Hedgewar demonstrated it on his own person. When Gandhi launched the Civil Disobedience movement in 1930, the Sangh's position, settled at a meeting of its organisers in November 1929, was that the organisation would take no part as a body, while swayamsevaks who wished to join the struggle were free to do so as individuals. Hedgewar, who wished to join it, refused to blur the line he had drawn: he handed the charge of Sarsanghchalak to his trusted colleague L.V. Paranjape, marched with the jungle satyagraha in Vidarbha in July 1930 as a private citizen, and accepted nine months of rigorous imprisonment at Akola as a private citizen, resuming the chief's office only after his release in February 1931. Eight years later the same correspondence files show him writing to the organisers of the 1938 Bhagyanagar satyagraha, who had credited the RSS in their press statements as a participating body, instructing them to stop: swayamsevaks might join the Hyderabad struggle individually with permission, but the Sangh's name was not theirs to use. A rule enforced against one's own mentor's movement, against one's own participation in a beloved national struggle, and against friendly movements eager for the Sangh's endorsement is not a posture adopted for convenience. It is a load-bearing wall of the design, and its function becomes visible in the survival record: no election defeat has ever befallen the RSS, no coalition collapse has ever dissolved it, no party split has ever divided its cadre, because the organisation staked its existence on none of the contests whose outcomes destroy political bodies. The cost was real and should be recorded with the benefit. For its first quarter-century the Sangh exercised no direct influence over legislation or government, and its critics inside the Hindu movement, Mahasabha men above all, charged Hedgewar with hoarding strength he would not spend. The charge is part of the record, attributed to its holders. The defence the record supports is that the strength existed to be inherited at all only because it had never been spent in the currencies that bankrupt organisations.


## The Mentor's Other Path: Moonje, Militarisation and the Road Not Taken

The separation choice gains definition from the road Hedgewar's own mentor took in full view of him. B.S. Moonje travelled to Italy in 1931, inspected the fascist state's youth and military organisations, met Mussolini, and returned to India an open admirer of institutionalised militarisation; Jaffrelot's account of the Hindu nationalist movement gives the visit and Moonje's impressions their full documentary weight. The admiration bore institutional fruit in the Bhonsala Military School at Nashik, founded in 1937 under Moonje's Central Hindu Military Education Society, a cadet institution designed to feed Hindu officers into the Indian Army and to school a generation in arms. Here was the alternative architecture for the same diagnosis, offered by the man Hedgewar most respected: where Hedgewar saw Hindu weakness as a failure of organisation to be repaired by a daily civic routine, Moonje's mature project repaired it by military schooling, a cadre formed in barracks rather than on open grounds, selected rather than open to every neighbourhood boy, and aimed frankly at the capture of military capacity within the state.

Hedgewar's Sangh cooperated with Moonje's world, sent no obstacle into its path, and adopted none of it. The shakha kept its lathi and its drill square and remained stubbornly a civic routine, its physical training pitched at the level of the schoolboy and the clerk rather than the cadet, its grounds open in every town rather than concentrated in a residential academy, its products instructors and organisers rather than aspirant officers. The divergence matters to the adjudication because it eliminates the easiest objection to the architectural reading, the objection that Hedgewar's design was merely the period's common sense, what any organiser of 1925 would have built. The period's common sense, in the mouth of his own mentor, pointed toward militarisation on the European model; the revolutionary tradition pointed toward secrecy; the Congress pointed toward campaigns; the Mahasabha pointed toward councils. Hedgewar's four choices were made against every one of those currents, several of them embodied in men he loved, and they were remade year after year, in episodes like the Bhagyanagar letter and the refusal of the Mahasabha's embrace, long after the founding moment had passed. A design chosen once might be luck. A design chosen continuously, against respected alternatives and at visible cost, is a position, and positions of that durability in a founder's conduct are the strongest internal evidence that the architecture was understood by its author as the substance of his life's work.

The Moonje divergence also disciplines a familiar charge in the literature, and the adjudication should not pass it by. Critics of the Sangh, in a line running from mid-century polemic through some readings of Jaffrelot's evidence, hold that the early RSS borrowed its form from European fascist models: the uniform, the drill, the salute, the cult of discipline, transmitted through Moonje's 1931 Italian journey. The charge is attributed here at its strongest because the resemblances of surface are genuine and Moonje's admiration is documented. The chronology, however, will not carry the weight the charge requires. The shakha's format, its drill, its flag salute, its camp system and its cadre practice were all fixed between 1925 and 1927, half a decade before Moonje boarded ship for Italy; the drill instructor was a retired Indian soldier, the training idiom descended from the akhara and the Congress volunteer corps, and the organisation Moonje returned to influence in 1931 was already running the design he is supposed to have inspired. Influence claims must run forward in time or confess themselves as atmosphere, and the scholarly consensus, in Andersen and Damle's documentary work and in Jaffrelot's own careful chapters, treats fascist influence on Hedgewar's Sangh as at most a matter of the milieu's general currency, not of transmission into the design. The point matters beyond polemic: a borrowed form would date with its model, as the Khaksars' more theatrical imitations dated, while the Sangh's form, drawn from Indian gymnasiums, volunteer corps and revolutionary cells, belonged to the society it had to survive in. Originality of combination, not novelty of parts, is the architectural claim, and the chronology of 1925 to 1927 is its proof.

## The Four Choices as One Architecture: Table 1

The four choices interlock, and the interlocking is the design. The shakha supplies the daily local routine; the cadre practice supplies a membership that routine can form and no document can betray; the training content supplies the standard that camps and pracharaks carry from cell to cell; the electoral separation supplies the immunity that lets the other three operate across decades without being consumed by any political season. Remove any one element and the remainder degenerates toward a form Hedgewar had already watched fail: a shakha network contesting elections becomes the Mahasabha with drill; a cadre without the daily routine becomes a secret society awaiting decapitation; a training curriculum without separation becomes a party's volunteer wing, dissolved and re-formed with each campaign. The interlock is summarised in Table 1, the four-architectural-choice map, which sets each choice beside the mechanism it created and a named consequence from the record between 1925 and 2019, so that the architectural reading can be cited choice by choice rather than merely asserted as a whole.

The map also disciplines the argument against exaggeration. Architecture explains a survival profile; it does not explain growth rates in any single decade, the content of the doctrine the cadre carried, or the moral standing of the enterprise, on which the scholarly literature remains divided and on which this article's claim is silent. What the map asserts is comparative and causal in a narrow sense: of the Hindu organisations founded in the 1920s, the one that survived intact into a second century is the one whose founder built a cadre architecture instead of a party, a campaign or a conspiracy, and the survival tracks the architecture's stress points with a precision the following sections test against the hardest cases the record offers.

## The Build-Out of the 1930s: One Ground Becomes a Network

Between the specification and the stress tests stands a decade of construction that the architectural reading must account for in detail, since a design that cannot be built is merely a drawing. The build-out proceeded on three tracks, each visible in the dated record. The first track was standardisation. The training camp that began with seventeen trainees in May 1927 became an annual fixture at Nagpur and then a graded system, with provincial camps preparing instructors for the all-India course and the course returning them to their districts carrying one programme, one set of commands and one conception of the hour. The uniform, the prayer, the flag salute and the discussion format settled into their durable shapes in these years, not by proclamation but by the camp system's quiet power to make one version of the routine the version every instructor in India had personally been taught. The second track was geographic. Pracharaks and travelling organisers carried the shakha out of the Central Provinces into the Bombay Presidency, the United Provinces, Delhi, Punjab and Bengal; students formed at Nagpur's colleges opened grounds in their home towns; and Hedgewar himself toured the provinces year after year, inspecting, appointing and exhorting, doing in person the work the centre's correspondence could not. The Varanasi shakha of the early 1930s, which drew in the young Banaras Hindu University lecturer M.S. Golwalkar, stands in the record as the type of hundreds of such plantings: a local ground, a handful of students, an instructor formed in the standard, and a cell that outlasted its founders' presence in the town.

The third track was the hardening of the centre. In 1929 Hedgewar formally assumed the title of Sarsanghchalak, general chief, converting a founder's natural authority into a named office that could in principle be vacated, transferred and inherited, a small bureaucratic act whose significance the succession of 1940 would prove. The guru dakshina, first collected in 1928 at eighty-four rupees, regularised the organisation's maintenance on the envelope-before-the-flag principle. Relations with the surrounding Hindu movement were managed without merger: the Mahasabha's leaders remained honoured interlocutors, Savarkar's doctrine remained the milieu's common vocabulary, and the Sangh's separateness was preserved through episodes, the 1938 Bhagyanagar correspondence among them, in which friendlier courses were pressed on the founder and declined. Estimates of the network's size at the decade's end vary by source and by sympathy, and the figures should be handled as estimates: the literature places shakhas in the hundreds and swayamsevak strength in the tens of thousands by 1940, with a presence in most provinces of British India. The precise count matters less than the distribution. By the founder's death the design had been instantiated in enough localities, by enough independently formed instructors, that no event in Nagpur, whether a death, an arrest or a prohibition, could reach more than a fraction of the whole. The architecture had achieved what engineers call redundancy, and redundancy, not fervour, is the property the bans of the following decades would test.

## The First Stress Test: The Founder's Death in 1940

The earliest and in some ways the purest test of the architecture came fifteen years after the founding, when the designer died. Hedgewar's health, never robust after his prison years, declined through the late 1930s even as he drove the organisation's expansion with punishing tours of the provinces, inspecting shakhas, addressing training camps and appointing pracharaks to new districts. He died at Nagpur on 21 June 1940, aged fifty-one, with the organisation present in most provinces of British India and a second generation of full-time workers already carrying its format beyond his personal reach. M.S. Golwalkar, a former lecturer at Banaras Hindu University who had come into the Sangh through its Varanasi shakha in the early 1930s and risen as Hedgewar's chosen lieutenant, assumed the office of Sarsanghchalak in July 1940, designated by the dying founder. The succession, the moment at which founder-built organisations most commonly fracture into personality factions, produced no schism, no rival claimant and no interruption of the daily routine on any recorded ground.

### When did Hedgewar die, and who led the RSS immediately after him?

Hedgewar died at Nagpur on 21 June 1940, aged fifty-one, after months of declining health during which he continued touring the provinces to inspect shakhas and camps. M.S. Golwalkar, designated by Hedgewar as his successor, assumed the office of Sarsanghchalak in July 1940, and the succession took effect across the network without contest or split.

The founder's final months supply the coda the design required. Through the winter of 1939 and 1940 Hedgewar toured against medical advice, addressing camps and inspecting shakhas across the provinces while his strength visibly failed, and the last great gathering he addressed, the Nagpur training camp of the summer of 1940, weeks before his death, is remembered in the Sangh's accounts for the burden of its message: the work must not depend on any person, the organisation must be so built that any worker can be replaced and the routine continue. Whether every sentence attributed to the dying founder in the devotional record was spoken as recorded, the historian cannot finally certify, since the record passes through the successor's own Hedgewar Charit and the memories of men formed by reverence. The conduct, however, is independently documented in the correspondence and in the succession's unruffled mechanics: a founder who spent his last strength on inspection tours rather than on anointing a court, who left the office's powers defined by practice rather than by his person, and whose organisation registered his death as a bereavement and not as an emergency. Movements are commonly said to be tested by their founder's exit. The test is usually administered once and failed permanently. The Sangh passed it in 1940 and, in doing so, retired the question of whether its continuity was personal: from July 1940 onward, continuity was demonstrably a property of the form.

The smoothness of 1940 is evidence, and the architectural reading claims it. Charismatic founders typically hold their organisations together through personal loyalty, and the loyalty dies with them; the history of India's sects, parties and movements is littered with successions that became schisms. Hedgewar had spent fifteen years systematically de-personalising the loyalty his cadre felt. The guru of the shakha was a flag, not the founder. The offering was made to the flag. The programme a swayamsevak followed was fixed by camp-taught standard, not by the founder's presence or instruction of the day. The full-time workers who held the network together owed their postings and formation to an institution, the pracharak system, rather than to household service in a leader's entourage. When the man died, every object of the cadre's attachment survived him untouched: the flag still stood at the centre of the ground, the hour still ran, the camps still convened, the successor's authority ran through the same channels the founder had built for it. Golwalkar would lead the Sangh for thirty-three years and reshape much, but the fact that there was an undivided Sangh for him to lead, on the morning after the founder's cremation, is the architecture's first proven consequence. A biographical reading, which locates the organisation's meaning in the founder's person, predicts a crisis in 1940. The record shows a routine. The prediction that fails belongs to the reading this article argues against.

## Stress Test Two: Colonial Constraint and the Civil Disobedience Years

The colonial decades tested the design from the opposite direction: not the loss of the founder but the pressure of a hostile and watchful state during the most intense mass mobilisation of the century. The test's centre is the Civil Disobedience movement of 1930 to 1932, when the Congress called the country into open law-breaking and every organisation in India had to declare itself. The Sangh's declaration, settled in advance at the organisers' meeting of November 1929, was the separation rule applied under maximum strain: the body abstains, the individual is free. Hedgewar's own conduct gave the rule its demonstration. On 21 July 1930 he joined the jungle satyagraha at Pusad in Vidarbha, where crowds courted arrest by cutting grass and timber in the reserved forests in defiance of the forest laws, having first transferred the Sarsanghchalak's charge to L.V. Paranjape so that the organisation he led would not be the organisation that marched. He was arrested, sentenced to nine months of rigorous imprisonment and held at Akola jail, where Sangh accounts describe him conducting the Sangh's routine among fellow prisoners and drawing satyagrahis into the cadre, an appropriation of the prison itself to the format that the biographical literature treats as colour and the architectural reading treats as the design running on its own logic inside a cell block.

### Did the British ban the RSS before independence?

No. The colonial state never prohibited the RSS between the founding and independence. British administrations watched the Sangh, compiled intelligence reports on its drill and growth, and debated its military character, yet no ban followed, because the shakha presented authority with exercise and song rather than any suppressible political act. The first prohibition came from independent India's government in 1948.

The movement years also fix, in dated episodes, the terms of an argument that has run ever since. Critics of the Sangh, a line of scholarship and polemic that Basu and his co-authors represent in organised form, read the organisational abstention of 1930 to 1932, repeated during the Quit India movement of 1942, as abstention from the freedom struggle itself, and note that the Sangh as a body courted no confrontation with the Raj in its founding decades. Defenders answer from the same record: the founder twice imprisoned, swayamsevaks participating across every movement in their individual capacity, medical volunteers of the Sangh serving the satyagraha crowds of 1930 in uniform, and a standing rule whose purpose was to keep a permanent organisation from being spent in a single campaign, however righteous. The disagreement is marked here as disputed, since it turns on whether a nation's struggle is owed by organisations or by citizens, and the evidence sustains both descriptions of the same facts. The pattern held through the war years and the century's last great colonial confrontation. Under Golwalkar, who succeeded in 1940, the Sangh maintained the separation rule through the Quit India movement of August 1942: the organisation as a body stood aside from the uprising, swayamsevaks again acting as individuals where they acted at all, and the colonial state, which imprisoned the Congress leadership entire and governed India by ordinance for three years, left the shakha network substantially unmolested because the network had given it no organisational act to answer. The official mind of the Raj returned to the Sangh periodically in these years, in assessments Jaffrelot and others have drawn from the home department records, and the assessments circle the same paradox the 1930s had taught: an avowedly non-political body of drilled Hindu men, growing steadily, declining every invitation to become legible as a political actor. Hedgewar's last service to the design was to have settled the question before the test arrived; the rule he left behind required no fresh decision from his successor in 1942, and institutions, like armies, are never stronger than when the correct response to a crisis has been decided in advance. What is not disputed is the architectural datum underneath the quarrel: the Sangh entered the 1940s with its network intact and growing, holding trained cadre in reserve through the very years when the colonial state imprisoned the leadership of every political formation in the country, and it did so because the state could find nothing in the shakha hour to prohibit and no campaign headquarters to raid. Constraint had been designed for. The colonial state was the environment the format was built to survive, and survive it the format did, without martyrdom on any scale that touched the network's continuity.

## Stress Test Three: Partition, Assassination and the Ban of 1948

Independence brought the design into a harsher element than the colonial state had ever provided: a sovereign Indian government, commanded by men who knew the Sangh intimately, prepared to ban it, and possessed of a provocation of the first magnitude. The sequence is dated and plain. Through the Partition violence of 1947, swayamsevaks in Punjab and Delhi performed relief and protection work among Hindu and Sikh refugees on a scale that Sangh accounts describe as extensive and that historians of the period, Jaffrelot among them, record as real, while differing sharply over its character and its place inside the communal catastrophe of those months. On 30 January 1948 Mohandas Gandhi was assassinated in Delhi by Nathuram Godse, whose past association with the Sangh became an immediate matter of state: the government of India held that Godse had been a swayamsevak, the Sangh maintained that he had left its ranks years before, and the extent and currency of his membership remains disputed in the record, examined later by the Kapoor Commission of inquiry. On 4 February 1948 the government banned the RSS outright, arrested Golwalkar and thousands of swayamsevaks, sealed offices and prohibited the shakha itself.

The ban letters of those months expose the new state's own divided reading of the organisation it had prohibited. Home Minister Sardar Patel, who administered the prohibition, wrote to Golwalkar on 11 September 1948 in terms Andersen and Damle reproduce: he acknowledged the discipline of the Sangh's men and the patriotic relief work its workers had performed during the Partition emergency, and in the same correspondence charged that the organisation's speeches and atmosphere had spread a communal bitterness whose fruit the assassination had been. The duality is [the religion-state founding tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) of the Constituent Assembly years translated into administration: a constitutionally secular republic, whose founding settlement was being drafted in the same city in the same months, deciding what a Hindu cadre organisation might lawfully be. Patel stood inside that settlement as one of its principal architects, a position this series has examined through [the founding triangulation](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) of Patel, Nehru and Ambedkar, and his handling of the ban, prohibition administered with an open channel to the prohibited, reflects a statesman suppressing an atmosphere rather than extirpating an enemy.

### Did the RSS have a written constitution when it was founded?

No. The RSS of 1925 had no charter, written constitution or codified rules; its order was carried in the shakha's fixed practice, in camp-taught standards and in the founder's correspondence. The organisation adopted its first written constitution only in 1949, under the settlement by which the government of India lifted the 1948 ban.

The negotiation that ended the ban is the architecture's finest hour in the strict analytical sense. Golwalkar, released from detention in August 1948 under restrictions, conducted a correspondence with the government while swayamsevaks courted arrest in a satyagraha against the prohibition through the winter of 1948 and 1949. The government's conditions centred on a written constitution, an elected internal order and a declared loyalty to the Indian state and flag. The Sangh adopted its first written constitution in 1949, and on 11 July 1949 the ban was lifted. Two features of the settlement deserve emphasis. The constitution codified existing practice rather than importing a new design; the shakha, the pracharak, the flag and the separation rule all passed into the document from the routine, so the state had, in effect, required the architecture to describe itself, not to change itself. And throughout the seventeen months of prohibition, the cadre network whose destruction the ban was supposed to accomplish had persisted through the very medium the design prescribed: personal relationships among men formed by daily attendance, sustained by correspondence and local contact when the public ground was closed to them. The state seized offices, funds and printing presses. It could not seize a habit practised in ten thousand localities by men whose names it largely did not know, because the founder had declined, in 1925, to write the names down.

The pressure that converted persistence into settlement deserves its own paragraph, because it too ran on the architecture. In December 1948 the Sangh launched a nationwide satyagraha against the prohibition, and swayamsevaks courted arrest in batches across the provinces; Sangh accounts place the number who went to jail in the tens of thousands, with some eighty thousand commonly claimed in the organisation's own record, a figure the historian should attribute rather than adopt, while noting that the government of India's own management of the episode confirms arrests on a scale that filled provincial jails through the winter. A membership that exists on paper can be demobilised by seizing the paper; a membership that exists as a habit can be mobilised by a word passed along the cadre line even while the organisation is unlawful, and the satyagraha demonstrated the second kind to the very ministry charged with suppressing it. The constitution that closed the episode was drafted with the assistance of T.R. Venkatarama Sastri, the former Advocate-General of Madras, whose involvement Andersen and Damle record and whose presence in the drafting signals the settlement's character: the document had to satisfy a Home Ministry examining it clause by clause, and it did so by codifying, in the language of a lawful voluntary association, the practice the shakhas had run for twenty-four years, complete with the declarations of loyalty to the Indian state and its flag that Patel's ministry required. The state got its document. The Sangh kept its design. The transaction is unintelligible on the biographical reading, under which a movement of devotion ought either to have defied the state into martyrdom or dissolved under its pressure; on the architectural reading it is the design behaving exactly as designed, absorbing a documentary requirement the way the format had absorbed every previous constraint, as an addition to practice rather than a replacement of it.

## Stress Test Four: The Emergency Ban and the Ban of 1992

The second prohibition arrived with the Emergency. On 4 July 1975, nine days after Indira Gandhi's government proclaimed the Emergency, the RSS was banned for the second time, one of twenty-six organisations prohibited in a single sweep, and its third Sarsanghchalak, Balasaheb Deoras, was arrested along with thousands of swayamsevaks held under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act. The episode's organisational history has been reconstructed in [the Emergency-era RSS ban context](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/) in this series, and its relevance here is specific. Of the forces that sustained the underground resistance to the Emergency, the printing of forbidden literature, the courier lines between jailed leaders and free workers, the relief of prisoners' families, a substantial share ran through Sangh cadre networks, as Andersen and Damle record in The RSS: A View to the Inside (2018) and as Jayaprakash Narayan, the resistance's own leader, acknowledged in his reliance on the Sangh's organisation during those months. A network built to survive prohibition did the thing it was built for, under a prohibition, for twenty-one months. When the Emergency ended in March 1977 the ban ended with it, and the Sangh emerged holding a form of national legitimacy it had never previously possessed, having been certified by suffering in a common cause with the entire democratic opposition. The legitimacy, note, accrued to an organisation that had contested nothing and held no office: the separation rule had once again converted political catastrophe into institutional credit.

The sequel inside party politics proves the same point from the other side. The Sangh's political alumni, organised since 1951 in the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, entered the Janata Party merger of 1977 as one constituent among several, and when the Janata experiment fractured in 1979 and 1980 on the so-called dual membership question, the demand that former Jana Sangh men renounce their Sangh membership as the price of remaining in the party, the quarrel consumed the party and not the Sangh. The former Jana Sangh men walked out and founded the Bharatiya Janata Party in April 1980; the Sangh, whose membership was the quarrel's subject, was not a party to it, lost nothing in it, and continued its daily routine while two national parties were born and buried around the question of their members' relation to it. An organisation that can be the stake of a party split without being a party to the split has achieved a separation so complete that the political system's convulsions register in its life as weather rather than as wounds.

The third ban was the briefest and the most legally instructive. On 10 December 1992, four days after the demolition of the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya, P.V. Narasimha Rao's Congress government prohibited the RSS under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act. The prohibition was referred, as the statute requires, to a tribunal, and in June 1993 the tribunal headed by Justice P.K. Bahri of the Delhi High Court held that the ban was not justified on the material the government had placed before it. The government did not renew the prohibition. The Sangh's public activity, suspended for roughly six months, resumed with its structure untouched. Set the three bans side by side and a pattern emerges that no reading of the century can avoid. In 1948 the prohibiting state was led by the founders of the republic and armed with the assassination of Gandhi. In 1975 the prohibiting state commanded the full apparatus of a declared Emergency. In 1992 the prohibiting state acted in the aftermath of the most polarising act of demolition in independent India's history. Three maximum provocations, three prohibitions, and in each case the same result: activity suspended, organisation preserved, prohibition lifted, network intact. The sample is small and the provocations differ, but the constant across them is not the politics of any decade. The constant is the design of the thing prohibited.


## Disruption Is Not Termination: The Three-Bans Objection Answered

The strongest objection to the architectural reading must be given its full weight before it is answered, because it is a good objection. The RSS has been banned three times, and each ban bit hard. In 1948 the shakha was driven from public grounds, the leadership was jailed, the organisation's name could not lawfully be printed in its own cause, and thousands of families carried the costs of arrests. In 1975 swayamsevaks filled the Emergency's prisons while the underground consumed the cadre's energies for twenty-one months. In 1992 offices were sealed again and public activity suspended again. If the architecture was designed to protect the organisation from political disruption, the objection runs, it failed three times: the disruption arrived on schedule whenever the state chose to inflict it. Defenders of the biographical reading add that the Sangh survived its bans on the strength of its members' devotion to the founder's memory and mission, a quality of persons, not of plans.

The answer begins with a distinction the objection blurs, between disruption and termination. Disruption is the suspension of an organisation's visible activity; termination is the destruction of its capacity to resume. The bans achieved the first completely and the second not at all, and the difference between the two outcomes is precisely architectural. A terminated organisation does not merely pause. Its assets are dissipated, its membership disperses into other allegiances, its leadership fragments into exile factions, and when the prohibition lifts there is nothing left to resume under the old name except the name. The comparative record of the same decades supplies terminated organisations in every category Hedgewar had declined to copy. The revolutionary societies of Bengal, decapitated by the conspiracy trials of the 1910s, never reconstituted themselves after their prohibitions and imprisonments, because their substance was held in secret networks that arrests physically dismantled. The Hindu Mahasabha, never banned at all, terminated itself by the slower route of electoral irrelevance, its conferences thinning decade by decade as its notables died without cadre beneath them. Registered parties and campaign organisations across the century have died of single defeats, single splits, single successions. Against that record the Sangh's three resumptions, each time with the same programme, the same offices, the same local units and an enlarged cadre, stand as the anomaly requiring explanation, and devotion alone cannot carry the explanation, since the revolutionaries of Anushilan were devoted unto death and their organisation died anyway.

What the architecture contributed to each resumption can be specified ban by ban, and the specification is the adjudication. In 1948 the state could not compile a membership list because none existed; it could not decapitate the local units because each unit's leadership was a camp-trained instructor replaceable from the ranks; it could not starve the organisation of funds because the organisation's finance was a once-a-year envelope placed before a flag. In 1975 the underground ran on pracharak lines, full-time workers with no families to hostage, no property to seize and a lifetime's practice in unglamorous organisational routine, exactly the human material a clandestine logistics network requires and exactly the material a campaign organisation does not produce. In 1992 the prohibition collapsed in law within six months, and an organisation whose public life was one daily hour on open ground resumed that hour the week the tribunal's ruling cleared the way. In every case the feature that defeated termination was one of the four choices of 1925, operating mechanically, without any swayamsevak needing to remember the founder at all. Devotion explains why men were willing to suffer for the Sangh. Architecture explains why their suffering sufficed, and why the suffering of equally devoted men in differently designed organisations, in the same country in the same century, did not.

The Khaksar movement of Punjab supplies the control case in its purest form, since it resembled the Sangh in almost every surface particular and differed in the architecture underneath. Founded at Lahore in 1931 by Allama Inayatullah Khan Mashriqi, the Khaksars were a uniformed volunteer body of drilled men, organised in ranks, devoted to discipline, service and the regeneration of their community, and numbering at their height a strength contemporaries estimated in the tens of thousands. What the Khaksars lacked was the Sangh's separation of the organisation from its founder's person and from spectacular public action: the movement's substance was its massed parades and its leader's theatrical generalship. When the Punjab government prohibited the movement in 1940 after its armed clash with the Lahore police and imprisoned Mashriqi, the Khaksars never recovered their former strength, and the organisation that briefly rivalled the Sangh as a model of uniformed voluntarism faded into a footnote of the Pakistan movement's prehistory. Prohibition terminated the Khaksars and merely disrupted the Sangh, in the same subcontinent within the same decade, against volunteer bodies of comparable devotion. The variable that separates the two outcomes is the one this article has tracked throughout: the Sangh's cadre habit was distributed, daily, local and undocumented, while the Khaksar spectacle was centralised, episodic and embodied in a leader, and a state can ban a spectacle and jail a leader far more completely than it can ban a habit.

## What the Biographical Reading Cannot Explain

Andersen and Damle's return to the subject in The RSS: A View to the Inside (2018), thirty-one years after The Brotherhood in Saffron, functions as an unintended longitudinal test of the two readings. The scholars who had interviewed the first generation in the 1980s interviewed the fourth and fifth generations in the 2010s, and the organisation those later interviews describe runs on the founding specifications: the daily shakha remains the unit of account and of belonging, the pracharak remains the connective officer, the flag remains the devotional centre, the centre still holds no membership roll in the documentary sense the founders had refused. Between the two books lie the third ban, the demolition at Ayodhya, the rise of a Sangh-formed party to national power and the transformation of India's media and economy, and through all of it the format's descriptions in the scholarly record change less than the descriptions of any comparable Indian institution over the same span. A biographical variable cannot produce that constancy, because the biographies ended: Hedgewar in 1940, Golwalkar in 1973, Deoras in 1996. What persists across the interviews of 1987 and 2018 is not a memory, however tended, but a working machine whose parts are replaced without altering its operation. The later book's authors, no devotees of the founder's legend, record the fact with something like professional wonder, and their record is the biographical reading's quietest and most damaging refutation.

Three facts from the record sit awkwardly on the biographical reading's shoulders, and its treatments tend to hurry past all three. The first is the succession of 1940, examined earlier: a founder-centred organisation that does not fracture when the founder dies is a contradiction the reading must explain away each time it is raised. The second is format invariance. A shakha programme recognisable in every particular, its sequence of salute, drill, game, song, discussion and prayer, runs in towns Hedgewar never visited, in languages he did not speak, taught by instructors separated from him by four generations of camp training. Biography produces influence; it does not produce invariance across a century and a subcontinent. Only a standardised format, deliberately fixed and institutionally reproduced, produces invariance, and the fixing is documented in the founder's own correspondence of 1925 to 1930, where the specifications are argued over and settled like clauses in a code. The third fact is the survival profile across political eras so different that they share almost nothing else: the late colonial state, the Congress-dominant republic, the Emergency, the coalition decades and the post-2014 period in which a party led by Sangh-formed men holds the Union government. Biographies age with their subjects' eras. The RSS has outlasted every era that formed it, every opponent who defined himself against it and every ally who borrowed from it, and the thread of continuity through those eras is not a text, a family or a fortune. It is a format.

None of this requires the founder to be diminished, and the architectural reading, properly stated, enlarges him. Hedgewar Charit and the devotional literature make him a saint of the organisation; the standard academic treatments make him a vehicle of his context, of Moonje's mentorship, of Savarkar's doctrine, of Maharashtra's gymnasium tradition and Bengal's revolutionary inheritance. The architectural reading restores to him the one thing both literatures, for opposite reasons, take away: authorship of a consequential design. The elements were all available in 1925, in Nagpur, to many men. The gymnasium tradition was common property. Savarkar's doctrine was in print. The revolutionary ethic had a generation of martyrs to advertise it. Dozens of gifted organisers worked the same city in the same years and founded organisations that are footnotes. Hedgewar alone combined the elements into the four choices, refused the three available templates, and enforced the refusals against his own mentor, his own movement and his own instincts as a twice-jailed agitator who plainly loved a fight. Jaffrelot's account, the most influential in the academy, positions the Sangh's organisation as the transmission mechanism by which an ideology entered Indian society durably; the formulation concedes the architectural point in scholarly language, since a transmission mechanism is a piece of engineering, and engineering has an engineer. The disagreement this article set out to adjudicate therefore resolves, on the primary evidence of the archive and the comparative evidence of the survival record, in favour of the institutional-architectural reading: the RSS is best explained not as Hedgewar's biography consummated but as Hedgewar's design operating, and the biography is the story of how the designer acquired, at first hand and at personal cost, the specification his design had to meet.

## The Claim Restated: Architecture as the Cause of Durability

The namable claim of this article can be carried in one sentence. The RSS's near-century durability follows from Hedgewar's 1925 architectural choices; the cadre architecture was deliberate and consequential. Deliberate, because each choice answers a dated failure the founder had served inside: the shakha answering the secret society's fragility and the campaign's impermanence, the cadre answering the paper membership of mass politics, the training hour answering the notable's empty platform, the electoral separation answering the party's mortality by defeat and split. Consequential, because the survival record runs through the choices like a proof: the succession of 1940 survived by the flag-as-guru de-personalisation, the prohibition of 1948 defeated as termination by the list that did not exist, the Emergency underground carried by the pracharak corps, the prohibition of 1992 outlasted by a format that needed one open ground and one hour to resume. The claim has limits, and they belong in its statement. Architecture does not certify the doctrine the cadre carried, does not acquit or convict the organisation in the moral arguments of the century, and does not predict the Sangh's second century, which will be governed by choices its present custodians have not yet had to make. What the architecture explains is why there is a second century for the argument to be about. Fifteen to twenty men in a Nagpur residence on Vijayadashami 1925 signed nothing, published nothing and founded, in the political sense, nothing at all. They adopted a routine. The routine is still running, and the running of it, morning and evening, on open grounds across India, is the most consequential political fact ever produced by a meeting that issued no document.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who founded the RSS?

The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh was founded by Dr Keshav Baliram Hedgewar, a Nagpur physician and former Congress organiser, who announced its creation to a small circle of associates on 27 September 1925. Hedgewar, born in Nagpur in 1889, had worked inside the Anushilan Samiti revolutionary network in Calcutta, the Congress organisation in Vidarbha and the Hindu Mahasabha's circles before concluding that none of those forms could build the disciplined cadre he believed Hindu society required. He led the Sangh as its first Sarsanghchalak until his death on 21 June 1940, when M.S. Golwalkar succeeded him in an uncontested succession. Hedgewar's historical significance lies less in any doctrine he authored, since he published no major text, than in the organisational design he imposed: the shakha system, the cadre-only membership and the refusal of electoral politics, all of which survived him intact.

### Q: When was the RSS founded?

The RSS was founded on 27 September 1925, the festival of Vijayadashami, at K.B. Hedgewar's residence in Nagpur. The founding gathering was deliberately small and private, with no public proclamation, no founding charter and no name yet adopted for the organisation. The name Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh was settled only on 17 April 1926, when sixteen swayamsevaks chose it by deliberation and vote from alternatives that included Jaripatka Mandal, Bharat Uddharak Mandal and Hindu Swayamsevak Sangh. The daily form of the shakha, the organisation's basic unit, began on 28 May 1926 at the Mohite Wada ground in Nagpur, and the first officers' training camp followed in May 1927. The Sangh's own chronology, Golwalkar's Hedgewar Charit of 1957 and Andersen and Damle's reconstruction in The Brotherhood in Saffron (1987) agree on the founding date and its Vijayadashami setting, which Hedgewar chose for the festival's association with righteous victory and auspicious new beginnings.

### Q: What is a shakha?

A shakha is the basic organisational unit of the RSS: a daily gathering of swayamsevaks from one locality, meeting for one hour on an open ground. The programme follows a fixed sequence that Hedgewar standardised in 1926: a salute to the saffron flag, physical exercises and formation drill including lathi practice, games, patriotic songs sung in unison, a discussion segment known as bauddhik in which an instructor delivers formative teaching, and a closing prayer and salute. Attendance at the shakha, rather than any enrolment or subscription, constitutes membership of the Sangh, which keeps no membership register. Because the cell requires no building, office or funds, and because its programme is identical everywhere, the shakha can be opened in any town and can persist through conditions, including the three national bans, that close down formally constituted organisations. The organisation counts its strength in shakhas held, not in members enrolled.

### Q: Why was the RSS founded on Vijayadashami?

Vijayadashami, the tenth day of the autumn Navaratri festival, commemorates in the Ramayana tradition Rama's victory over Ravana and is associated across India with the triumph of dharma and with auspicious new undertakings; in Maharashtra's public memory it is also the season when Shivaji's armies traditionally took the field after the monsoon, and weapons and tools are worshipped on the day. Sangh accounts, from Hedgewar Charit onward, present the choice as Hedgewar's deliberate alignment of his new organisation with that complex of meanings: righteous victory, martial renewal and a beginning made under blessing. The founding itself was staged with notable modesty, a private gathering at Hedgewar's residence rather than a public festival event, which suggests the date was chosen for the meaning it would carry inside the cadre's own calendar. The Sangh continues to treat Vijayadashami as its founding festival, and its annual Vijayadashami observances mark the organisation's year.

### Q: How is the RSS different from the Hindu Mahasabha?

The RSS and the Hindu Mahasabha shared personnel, milieu and much ideological vocabulary, but they were built as opposite organisational forms. The Mahasabha, founded in 1915, was a political and representative body: it held conferences, passed resolutions, petitioned governments and contested elections, and under Savarkar's presidency from 1937 it pursued electoral competition and coalition ministries. The RSS, founded in 1925 by a man who had worked in the Mahasabha's Nagpur orbit under B.S. Moonje, refused every one of those activities: it contested no elections, affiliated with no party and built instead a daily cadre routine with no political programme. The Mahasabha spoke for Hindus in legislatures and shrank as its notable leadership aged; the Sangh manufactured organised Hindus in neighbourhoods and grew without interruption. Hedgewar took from the Mahasabha's world the doctrine of Hindu organisation and declined its vehicle, and the divergent fates of the two bodies supply the standard comparison for students of the period.

### Q: Has the RSS been banned in India?

Yes, three times, each prohibition imposed by the government of India at a moment of national crisis. The first ban ran from 4 February 1948, imposed days after Gandhi's assassination by Nathuram Godse, until 11 July 1949, when it was lifted after the Sangh adopted its first written constitution and accepted conditions on its internal order. The second accompanied the Emergency: the Sangh was prohibited on 4 July 1975 and its leaders and thousands of swayamsevaks were imprisoned, until the Emergency and the ban ended together in March 1977. The third was imposed on 10 December 1992, after the demolition of the Babri Masjid, and collapsed in law when a tribunal headed by Justice P.K. Bahri held in June 1993 that the prohibition was unjustified on the government's material. No colonial administration had banned the organisation before 1947.

### Q: What is the RSS organisational structure?

The structure rests on the shakha, the daily local gathering, as its base unit, and rises through territorial tiers of town, district, province and the all-India centre at Nagpur. Local shakhas are run by instructors trained in the Sangh's camp system, and coordination at each tier is held by a sanghachalak, an organiser figure paired with executive officers. At the apex stands the Sarsanghchalak, the chief, an office Hedgewar held until 1940 and which has since been held by M.S. Golwalkar, Balasaheb Deoras and their successors, the succession passing by the incumbent's designation. The system's distinctive element is the pracharak corps: full-time organisers, classically unmarried and living on the organisation's support, who are transferred between districts and provinces to open shakhas and carry the standard format, binding the autonomous local cells into one uniform national body without a membership register beneath them.

### Q: Who was B.S. Moonje, and what was his role in Hedgewar's path to the RSS?

Balakrishna Shivram Moonje was a Nagpur physician, a former associate of Bal Gangadhar Tilak and the leading figure of the Hindu Mahasabha in the Central Provinces, and he functioned in Hedgewar's life as mentor and patron across two decades. Biographical accounts credit the Tilakite circle around Moonje with sending the young Hedgewar to Calcutta in 1910, to study medicine and to make contact with Bengal's revolutionary networks. From 1923, after Hedgewar's release from his Non-Cooperation imprisonment, Moonje drew him into the Mahasabha's organisational work in Nagpur, giving him his apprenticeship in Hindu politics. The relationship is documented in the correspondence preserved in the Hedgewar Papers at the Hedgewar Smarak Samiti archives. Its historical interest lies in its limit: Hedgewar absorbed Moonje's commitment to Hindu organisation while declining the Mahasabha's electoral form, and the disciple's cadre body eventually eclipsed the mentor's party entirely.

### Q: What was Hedgewar's connection with the Anushilan Samiti?

Hedgewar joined the Anushilan Samiti, the revolutionary society centred in Bengal, during his medical studies at Calcutta between 1910 and 1914, and on the testimony of the Anushilan leader Trailokyanath Chakravarty, recorded in his memoir Jele Trish Bachhar, he reached the society's inner circle. The Samiti combined secret political organisation with gymnasiums, physical training and an ethic of disciplined self-sacrifice, and its imprint on the young Hedgewar is visible in the RSS design: the value placed on bodily discipline, on cadre over crowd, and on long preparation over immediate action. The divergence is equally instructive. Hedgewar concluded from the Samiti's fate under colonial repression, its cells dismantled by conspiracy trials between 1908 and 1918, that secrecy capped an organisation's growth and exposed it to decapitation, and he built the Sangh as a public body whose daily activity no police report could construe as conspiracy.

### Q: Why was Hedgewar imprisoned in 1921?

Hedgewar was imprisoned for sedition arising from his campaign speeches during the Non-Cooperation movement. Working as a Congress organiser in Vidarbha, he addressed meetings at Katol and Bharatwada in the spring of 1921 in language the colonial authorities prosecuted as seditious. Arrested in May 1921, he defended himself at trial with a statement challenging the moral right of British rule, which Sangh accounts record the presiding judge as calling more seditious than the speeches themselves, and he was sentenced to one year's rigorous imprisonment. He served the sentence at Ajani jail in Nagpur and was released in July 1922, receiving a public welcome at which Motilal Nehru and Hakim Ajmal Khan spoke, by the Sangh's account. The year in prison, four years before the founding, gave Hedgewar his formative lesson in the impermanence of campaign organisations, which dissolved between agitations while their leaders sat in jail.

### Q: What is a pracharak in the RSS?

A pracharak is a full-time organiser of the RSS who gives his working life to the Sangh's expansion and coordination. In the classical pattern established under Hedgewar in the 1930s, the pracharak remains unmarried, lives simply on the organisation's support, holds no private career, and accepts posting and transfer to any district or province where shakhas are to be opened, strengthened or inspected. Pracharaks carry the standard shakha format from locality to locality, train local instructors, and report the condition of each territory to the centre, which is how a federation of autonomous daily cells maintains a single uniform practice across India. The institution grew from Hedgewar's practice of sending trusted first-generation swayamsevaks, associates such as Babasaheb Apte among them, out from Nagpur with nothing but the format and the centre's confidence. Many of the Sangh's later leaders, and the founders of its affiliated organisations, entered public life through the pracharak corps.

### Q: What were the Officers' Training Camps Hedgewar introduced?

The Officers' Training Camps were intensive residential courses through which Hedgewar standardised the shakha's instruction across the growing organisation. The first camp was held in May 1927 with seventeen participants, selected swayamsevaks trained over the course as instructors in the drill, commands, songs and discussion programme of the daily shakha. Camps became an annual institution and developed into a graded ladder: local and provincial camps feeding an all-India course, with trainees rising through qualifications as shikshak and mukhya shikshak, instructor and chief instructor. Their function was constitutional in effect long before the Sangh had a written constitution, since they fixed one national standard for the daily hour and reproduced it in the person of every trainee who returned to his home ground. A swayamsevak could thus walk into a shakha in any province and find the same programme run to the same commands, a uniformity that distinguishes the RSS from looser movements of the period.

### Q: Why did Hedgewar make the saffron flag, rather than any person, the guru of the RSS?

Hedgewar placed the Bhagwa Dhwaj, the saffron flag, at the centre of the shakha ground and assigned it the role of guru, the teacher to whom the swayamsevak's reverence and annual offering are directed. Sangh tradition explains the decision as a deliberate guard against the cult of personality: a human guru dies, errs or becomes an object of faction, whereas the flag, representing the nation and its civilisational memory, endures unchanged and belongs to no individual. The guru dakshina ceremony, in which each swayamsevak lays his annual offering before the flag in an unmarked envelope, institutionalises the choice, funding the organisation through devotion to a symbol rather than subscription to a leader. The design carried a measurable consequence at Hedgewar's death in 1940, when the cadre's devotional centre survived the founder untouched and the succession of Golwalkar proceeded without the fracture that personality-centred movements commonly suffer at a founder's passing.

### Q: What did Hedgewar study in Calcutta?

Hedgewar studied medicine at Calcutta Medical College after travelling to Bengal in 1910, in biographical accounts at the instance of the Tilakite leadership of Maharashtra, which wanted a trusted young man placed inside the city's revolutionary circles as well as trained as a physician. His Calcutta years, from 1910 to about 1914 on the standard chronology, joined formal medical study to clandestine political education: he joined the Anushilan Samiti and reached its inner circle, and he observed at close quarters the gymnasium culture through which Bengal's revolutionaries formed their cadre. Accounts differ on whether he completed the final medical qualification before returning to Nagpur, and the discrepancy is one reason historians treat the devotional biographies cautiously. What is not in doubt is the double formation the Calcutta years produced, medical and revolutionary, and the use he made of both: the physician's standing in Nagpur society and the conspirator's understanding of cadre discipline.

### Q: How did the RSS expand beyond Nagpur during the 1930s?

The expansion ran on the pracharak mechanism and the shakha's portability. Hedgewar sent trusted first-generation swayamsevaks out from Nagpur to open grounds in new towns, beginning within Maharashtra and reaching across the provinces during the 1930s; a shakha at Varanasi in the early years of the decade brought M.S. Golwalkar, then a young lecturer at Banaras Hindu University, into the Sangh's orbit. Students who encountered the shakha at Nagpur's colleges carried the format home to their own districts in vacations and after graduation, and the annual training camps at Nagpur standardised each returning instructor in the same programme. Hedgewar toured relentlessly through the decade, inspecting shakhas and appointing organisers despite declining health. Because a new shakha needed only an open ground, a flag and a trained instructor, expansion cost the centre almost nothing, and by Hedgewar's death in 1940 the organisation stood in most provinces of British India.

### Q: Why was the name Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh chosen in 1926?

The organisation founded on Vijayadashami 1925 worked without a name for its first seven months, a delay that itself reflects Hedgewar's order of priorities, format before label. When newcomers' questions made a name necessary, the choice was made collectively: on 17 April 1926 sixteen swayamsevaks met at Hedgewar's residence and selected Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh from a shortlist that also included Jaripatka Mandal, Bharat Uddharak Mandal and Hindu Swayamsevak Sangh, by the Sangh's published account. The chosen name is a programme in three words: rashtriya, national, fixing the body's object as the nation rather than a party or a cause of the season; swayamsevak, volunteer or self-server, defining the member by service rendered rather than by subscription paid; and sangh, union or association, describing a consolidated body. Notably absent from the name is any reference to Hindu identity, doctrine or leadership, a restraint consistent with the cadre architecture's preference for substance over declaration.

### Q: What role do historians assign to the RSS in the Partition violence and relief of 1947?

Historians record two facts together and interpret them in opposed directions. Sangh accounts describe extensive relief and protection work by swayamsevaks among Hindu and Sikh refugees in Punjab and Delhi during the Partition massacres of 1947, including refugee camps, escorts and defence of threatened localities, and Home Minister Sardar Patel acknowledged the Sangh's relief work in his correspondence with Golwalkar in September 1948. Critical historians situate the same activity inside the communal violence of Partition, treating the Sangh's armed self-defence formations as participants in the cycle of attack and reprisal rather than as neutral relief workers, and the Sangh's own role in the violence remains contested in the scholarship. The question acquired its political charge from Gandhi's assassination in January 1948 and the ban that followed, and it is best approached through the primary correspondence of the ban period, in which Patel simultaneously credited the relief work and condemned the communal atmosphere he held the Sangh responsible for spreading.

### Q: Where are Hedgewar's papers and the early RSS records preserved?

The K.B. Hedgewar Papers are preserved at the archives of the Hedgewar Smarak Samiti, the memorial trust at Nagpur, where the founder's correspondence, including his exchanges with B.S. Moonje and with the first generation of swayamsevaks, forms the core collection for the Sangh's founding decades. The stratum of the archive most valuable to historians, and the least exhausted by the literature, is the early internal correspondence of 1925 to 1930 between Hedgewar and his initial cadre, in which the specifications of the shakha format and the terms of cadre-only membership were settled in writing. These documents matter disproportionately because the young organisation published almost nothing and kept no membership records by design, so the correspondence carries evidence that in other movements would sit in minute books and registers. Access has historically been mediated by the memorial trust, one reason the archival base of RSS historiography remains thinner than its interview base.

### Q: What is Hedgewar Charit, and why do historians treat it with caution?

Hedgewar Charit is the biography of the founder published in 1957 under the authority of M.S. Golwalkar, Hedgewar's successor as Sarsanghchalak, and it is the fountainhead of the Sangh's internal account of its own origins: the boy who scorned the jubilee sweets, the Calcutta conspirator, the prisoner of 1921, the founder of 1925. Historians treat it with caution because of its genre and its authorship. It is an act of institutional memory by the man who inherited the organisation, composed to form the devotion of swayamsevaks, and its episodes are arranged for edification rather than for verification; dates, attendances and conversations cannot always be checked against independent records, and where the correspondence in the Hedgewar Papers diverges from the biography, scholars privilege the correspondence. The caution is not dismissal. Andersen and Damle drew heavily on the same first-generation testimony the biography codified, and Hedgewar Charit remains primary evidence of how the Sangh wished its founder to be remembered, which is itself a fact about the organisation's design.

### Q: What did Hedgewar do when the 1938 Bhagyanagar satyagraha organisers named the RSS as a participant?

He wrote to the organisers and instructed them to stop. The Bhagyanagar, or Hyderabad, satyagraha of 1938, mounted by the Hindu Mahasabha and the Arya Samaj against the Nizam's government, drew in swayamsevaks in their individual capacity with Hedgewar's permission, as the Sangh's standing rule allowed. When the organisers' press statements began crediting the RSS as a participating organisation, Hedgewar intervened in writing to have the Sangh's name withdrawn, on the ground that the body itself had taken no part and could not be named as a party to any campaign. Andersen and Damle treat the episode as documentary proof of the separation rule's sincerity, since the rule was here enforced against a friendly movement, in a cause Hedgewar supported, at the cost of public credit his organisation would gladly have banked. The letter survives as one of the clearest statements of the fourth architectural choice in operation.
