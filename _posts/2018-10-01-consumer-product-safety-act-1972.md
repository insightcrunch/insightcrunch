## Introduction

What should happen when a household product injures people in a pattern, and no single existing law names the product, assigns the hazard to a regulator, or gives anyone power to pull the product from sale? The Consumer Product Safety Act answered that question by building an institution rather than by writing a rule for one product. Congress decided that the federal government needed a permanent, independent commission able to count injuries across the whole consumer marketplace, to set safety standards where counting revealed unreasonable risk, to force dangerous products out of homes and stores, and to do each of those things on a public record that courts could review.

![Consumer Product Safety Act: the five statutes, the reporting duty, and the disclosure restriction - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-34.webp)

The problem behind the statute had a measurable shape and a structural cause. The National Commission on Product Safety reported in 1970 that consumer products were associated with an estimated 20 million injuries a year, 30,000 deaths a year, and 110,000 permanent disabilities a year, at an annual cost placed at 5.5 billion dollars. Those figures described burns from flammable fabrics, poisonings from household chemicals that children could open, suffocations inside latched refrigerators, and injuries from powered equipment, furniture, and toys that no federal standard governed at all. The structural cause was fragmentation. Authority sat inside departments built for other missions, enforcement followed the category into which a product happened to fall, and each new hazard pattern waited for a separate statute before a federal requirement could attach to it.

The answer Congress enacted in October 1972 joined consolidation to new authority. The new commission received the older federal laws on hazardous substances, flammable fabrics, poison prevention packaging, and refrigerator safety, and administered those laws alongside the new act itself, so that one agency held five statutes with five distinct tests, labels, and remedies. Onto that inherited base the act layered powers none of the older laws had supplied in general form: authority to write consumer product safety standards, authority to ban a product class where no feasible standard would protect the public, authority to require reports from firms that learned of defects, and authority to order notice, repair, replacement, or refund for products already sold. The jurisdiction was vast by design, reaching a product universe that published descriptions placed in a range from roughly 10,000 types to more than 15,000 types.

The design also carried deliberate restraints, and those restraints shaped the agency as deeply as the powers did. Congress gave the commission no general power to approve products before sale, so the system operated after distribution, dependent on injury surveillance, import inspection, and reports that firms filed about their own products. Congress surrounded mandatory rulemaking with advance notices, regulatory analyses, and findings on necessity, public interest, cost, burden, and voluntary alternatives, procedures that made a finished rule slow to build and hard to defend. Congress also conditioned public disclosure of information identifying a manufacturer on advance notice, a summary, a comment opportunity, and documented steps toward accuracy and fairness. Each restraint answered a real fear, of arbitrary bans, of careless rules, and of reputational destruction by unverified official statements, and each restraint exacted a price in speed.

Amendment tested the settlement repeatedly without replacing it. The revisions of 1976 redrew jurisdictional boundaries and set funding schedules. The amendments of 1981 ordered deference to adequate voluntary standards with likely substantial compliance, redirecting agency energy toward consensus committees and recalls. The amendments of 2008, passed after a wave of recalls of imported toys bearing lead paint, imposed mandatory limits and third party testing for children's products, raised penalties, enlarged the agency, and created a public database of harm reports. Litigation policed the edges throughout, setting aside rules built on thin evidence and sustaining the disclosure restriction even against requests under the Freedom of Information Act.

The account that follows treats the statute as a piece of institutional architecture and examines each load bearing choice on its own terms: the injury ledger and the case for an independent commission, the five statute portfolio and the exclusions that bounded it, the preference for borrowed standards over written rules, the reporting duty that powered the recall system, the disclosure restriction that set the agency apart from every peer regulator, and the five decades of amendment and litigation that left the original structure standing in altered form. Read as a whole, the act emerges as neither a simple grant of power nor a simple set of shackles, but as a negotiated machine whose strengths and failures traced back, again and again, to decisions written into the text in 1972 and revised, deliberately, in the years after.

## The injury ledger that made a regulator necessary

Before the Consumer Product Safety Act entered the statute books, federal protection against dangerous household goods rested on a patchwork assembled one crisis at a time. Each earlier law answered a narrow class of harm, each assigned enforcement to an agency whose principal mission lay elsewhere, and each left large categories of ordinary products outside any system of mandatory federal safety standards. A burn, a poisoning, a suffocation, or a laceration might fall under federal law or might fall under no federal law at all, depending on the material involved, the wording on a label, or the room of the house in which the injury occurred. The structure produced gaps that manufacturers, physicians, and injured families encountered repeatedly during the postwar expansion of consumer manufacturing, when powered tools, synthetic fabrics, chemical cleaners, pressurized containers, and mass produced toys entered American homes in volumes that older regulatory tools had never been designed to govern.

The oldest strand in the patchwork was the Flammable Fabrics Act of 1953, enacted after highly flammable brushed rayon garments, including the so called torch sweaters and long pile cowboy chaps worn by children, caused severe burns. The 1953 statute prohibited the marketing of wearing apparel and apparel fabrics so highly flammable as to be dangerous when worn, and enforcement rested with the Federal Trade Commission, an agency built principally for competition and trade practice work rather than for laboratory based product engineering. Congress broadened the flammable fabrics law in 1967 to reach interior furnishings and to authorize the Secretary of Commerce to issue flammability standards, yet the statute remained a fabric statute. A standard for clothing textiles said nothing about a space heater with an unstable base, a crib with slats spaced to trap an infant head, or a power mower that threw debris, and the division between Commerce standard setting and Commission enforcement illustrated the fragmentation that later reformers cited as a defect in itself.

A second strand was the Federal Hazardous Substances Act of 1960, which addressed toxic, corrosive, flammable, and otherwise hazardous substances intended for household use and required cautionary labeling, with authority to ban substances so dangerous that labeling could not protect the public. Administration ran through the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare and its Food and Drug Administration, placing household chemicals alongside the far larger federal responsibilities for food, drugs, and medical products. The hazardous substances framework depended heavily on classification and labels, and a label could warn an adult reader while doing little for a child, a person unable to read the language printed on the container, or a household that stored a solvent in an unmarked bottle. Amendments during the 1960s extended the framework to toys and other articles intended for children that presented electrical, mechanical, or thermal hazards, a recognition that dangerous goods could not be sorted neatly by whether the danger came from a chemical property or from physical design.

Two narrower statutes completed the prehistory that Congress surveyed in the late 1960s. The Refrigerator Safety Act of 1956 required that refrigerator doors be openable from the inside, a direct response to children who climbed into abandoned or stored refrigerators, pulled the door closed, and suffocated in airtight cabinets that latched from the outside only. The statute solved a specific mechanical problem with a specific performance requirement, and its very precision showed the limits of the method, since each new entrapment, ingestion, or burn hazard awaited a separate act of Congress before a comparable federal requirement could attach. The Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970 moved in a broader direction by authorizing standards for child resistant packaging for specified household substances, including certain medicines and chemicals that children had swallowed in large numbers. That law, also administered within the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare orbit, treated the container rather than the full range of product design, and its enactment two years before the comprehensive statute showed both momentum toward federal action and the piecemeal character of the action taken.

Administration in fragments compounded the statutory gaps. The Food and Drug Administration carried product related duties inside the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare while also regulating food additives, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics under separate authorities. The Federal Trade Commission policed flammable fabrics as a trade matter. The Department of Commerce held standard setting functions tied to fabrics. State and local governments filled portions of the remaining space with rules that varied by jurisdiction, so a manufacturer selling nationally faced conflicting requirements in some product lines and no requirement at all in others. No single federal body kept a comprehensive count of product related injuries, matched injury patterns to product designs, and held authority to set a mandatory standard, order a dangerous item out of commerce, and require notice of a defect across the whole consumer marketplace. The absence of a central injury surveillance function mattered as much as the absence of standard setting power, because scattered hospital records and individual lawsuits revealed harm case by case without assembling the pattern that would justify a general rule.

### What problem was Congress trying to solve in consumer product safety?

Congress sought to replace fragmented, product by product federal authority and uneven state control with a single regulator able to identify unreasonable risks across the entire consumer marketplace, set uniform safety standards, remove dangerous products from commerce, and generate reliable injury data for prevention.

Congress created the institution that supplied the missing evidence in 1967. By joint resolution approved on November 20, 1967, as Public Law 90-146, Congress established the National Commission on Product Safety and directed a comprehensive study of the scope and adequacy of the measures then employed to protect consumers against unreasonable risks of injury associated with household products. The temporary commission held hearings, gathered testimony from manufacturers, insurers, physicians, engineers, and consumer representatives, and examined whether the existing mixture of state law, voluntary industry standards, common law litigation, and narrow federal statutes matched the scale of the harm. The choice of a study commission before a regulatory commission reflected a legislative judgment that jurisdiction should follow evidence, and that a permanent agency with power over thousands of product categories required a factual record capable of surviving opposition from industries that disputed both the size of the injury problem and the need for mandatory standards.

The National Commission on Product Safety delivered its Final Report to the President and to Congress in June 1970, and the figures in that report became the foundation on which the later statute built its findings. The Final Report estimated that consumer products were associated with 20 million injuries a year serious enough to require medical attention or to restrict ordinary activity, with 30,000 deaths a year and 110,000 persons permanently disabled each year, and placed the annual cost to the nation at 5.5 billion dollars in medical expense, lost earnings, property damage, and related losses. Later summaries of the report, including the account in Teresa M. Schwartz, The Consumer Product Safety Commission: A Flawed Product of the Consumer Decade, published in the George Washington Law Review in 1982, repeated the 20 million injury figure and the cost exceeding 5.5 billion dollars as the numbers that framed congressional debate. The report also warned that the dollar figure understated the full burden because pain, suffering, and family disruption resisted conversion into an accounting total.

The numbers carried force because of the method behind the argument attached to them. The commission treated the injury total not as a collection of isolated accidents caused solely by careless use, but as evidence that design, construction, packaging, warnings, and directions for use could each contribute to harm, and that foreseeable misuse by ordinary households belonged inside any serious safety analysis. A product used in a home would be handled by children, by elderly persons, by tired workers, and by guests with no familiarity with its mechanism, and a standard written only for an attentive adult reading every instruction missed the conditions under which injuries accumulated. Steven Kelman, in Regulation by the Numbers, a report on the Consumer Product Safety Commission published in The Public Interest, recorded that the 20 million and 30,000 figures drew criticism as estimates built in part on limited Public Health Service survey material from 1960, and that a large share of the injuries involved falls, sports, and kitchen implements that federal rules could influence only at the margins. That criticism entered the historical record alongside the figures rather than displacing them, and Congress legislated with awareness that the estimates were approximations of a burden whose exact size exceeded the capacity of the fragmented system to measure.

The Final Report paired the injury ledger with an institutional recommendation. The commission concluded that consumers faced excessive exposure to unreasonable product hazards and recommended creation of a new independent federal agency charged with product safety, rather than assignment of the new duties to an existing department as a subordinate bureau. The recommendation addressed the diagnosis directly, since the study had found authority divided among agencies for which product safety ranked as a secondary assignment, standards that depended on voluntary compliance in many categories, and no federal office responsible for the whole field. The report supplied draft legislative concepts as well, and the ensuing bills in the Senate and the House drew on its structure even as committees altered definitions, procedures, and enforcement details during the two years of legislative work that followed.

The legislative path ran through the 92nd Congress. The Senate bill, S. 3419, was introduced on March 24, 1972, passed the Senate on June 21, 1972, and passed the House on September 20, 1972, after conference work reconciled differences between the chambers over the design of the new agency and the procedures for issuing mandatory standards. President Richard Nixon signed the measure on October 27, 1972, and at signing President Nixon described the act as the most significant consumer protection legislation passed by the 92nd Congress. The statute took effect on the sixtieth day after enactment, on December 26, 1972, under its effective date provision, although the commission created by the act required presidential appointments, Senate confirmations, staff, and transferred functions before operations could begin as a working regulator.

The formal identity of the statute can be stated plainly. Section 1 provided that the act may be cited as the Consumer Product Safety Act. The measure was enacted as Public Law 92-573, printed at 86 Statutes at Large 1207, signed by President Richard Nixon on October 27, 1972, and codified at title 15, United States Code, sections 2051 and following. Section 2, codified at 15 United States Code section 2051 and headed as congressional findings and declaration of purpose, recorded the findings and purposes on which the grant of authority rested. In paraphrase, section 2 found that an unacceptable number of consumer products presenting unreasonable risks of injury were distributed in commerce, that the complexity of consumer products and the diverse nature and abilities of the persons using them frequently left users unable to anticipate risks and to safeguard against them adequately, that the public should be protected against unreasonable risks of injury associated with consumer products, that control by state and local governments was inadequate and could burden manufacturers, that existing federal authority was inadequate, and that regulation of products whose distribution or use affected interstate or foreign commerce was necessary to carry out the chapter. Section 2 then declared four purposes, namely protection of the public against unreasonable risks of injury, assistance to consumers in evaluating the comparative safety of consumer products, development of uniform safety standards with a reduction in conflicting state and local regulations, and promotion of research and investigation into the causes and prevention of product related deaths, illnesses, and injuries.

The findings did legal work beyond reciting concern. The finding on complexity answered the argument that an injured person had simply failed to take care, by placing limited foreseeability inside the statutory premise. The finding on state and local inadequacy answered a federalism objection by recording that uneven local control both underprotected the public and burdened manufacturers who sold across state lines, so uniformity appeared in the statute as a safety goal and as a commercial goal at the same time. The finding on inadequate federal authority connected the new act to the patchwork described in the study record, and the commerce finding tied the regulatory program to the constitutional basis on which federal power over goods moving in interstate and foreign commerce rested. Read together, the findings converted the National Commission on Product Safety estimates into a legislative premise, and the four purposes gave the new commission a charter broad enough to cover standard setting, information for consumers, and injury research under a single roof.

Section 4 of the act, codified at 15 United States Code section 2053, created the United States Consumer Product Safety Commission as an independent regulatory commission. The commission consisted of five commissioners appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, chosen with attention to background and experience in areas related to consumer products and protection of the public from risks to safety. The President designated one commissioner as chairman, and the statute protected commissioners against removal except for neglect of duty or malfeasance in office. Terms were staggered at the start by design, with the first commissioners appointed for terms ending three, four, five, six, and seven years after October 27, 1972, and successors appointed for seven year terms from the expiration of the term of a predecessor. A separate provision capped partisan concentration by barring more than three commissioners from affiliation with a single political party. The structure placed policy in a multimember body whose members would outlast a single presidential term in the ordinary course, whose decisions required collegial votes, and whose leadership could not be replaced at pleasure after a disputed rule or recall.

The commission began operations on May 14, 1973, the activation date recorded in Consumer Product Safety Commission releases from the founding period. Richard O. Simpson, formerly Assistant Secretary for Science and Technology at the Department of Commerce, served as the first chairman, and Barbara H. Franklin, who had served as a staff assistant to President Nixon, was elected vice chairman by the commissioners. The other founding commissioners included Lawrence M. Kushner and Constance B. Newman, each beginning service in May 1973, with R. David Pittle joining in October 1973. The transfers that gave the new body a working inheritance under the older statutes are mapped in full in the five statute account that follows, which sets out each transferred function, its source agency, and the distinct test each transferred law carried with it.

Why Congress chose an independent commission rather than a bureau inside an existing department followed from the record the study commission had assembled. A bureau inside the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare would have competed for budget, leadership attention, and legal resources against food, drug, and public health programs of far greater size, repeating the subordination that had marked administration of the hazardous substances and packaging laws. A bureau inside the Department of Commerce would have placed the safety regulator inside the department charged with promotion of industry and commerce, creating an institutional conflict whenever a mandatory standard raised production costs or forced a product withdrawal. A bureau inside the Federal Trade Commission would have inherited an agency organized around case by case trade practice adjudication rather than engineering analysis and prospective standard setting. Independence answered each defect by giving product safety a governing body with no competing departmental mission, a direct relationship with Congress through appropriations and oversight, and statutory protection for commissioners who voted for a rule opposed by an administration or by a regulated industry.

Independence also served a second function tied to the breadth of the jurisdiction granted. The act reached thousands of categories of consumer products, from toys and household appliances to lawn equipment, furniture, and recreational goods, while expressly leaving products assigned to other federal regimes, including food, drugs, cosmetics, motor vehicles, pesticides, aircraft, and firearms, under their separate statutes. A regulator with that breadth would make decisions affecting nearly every manufacturing sector that sold to households, and Congress structured the decision maker as a bipartisan, staggered, multimember commission to distribute that power and to slow its capture by any single interest. Fixed terms and removal protection gave commissioners room to follow injury data across election cycles. The party cap forced a measure of cross party agreement in appointments over time. Collegial voting meant that a ban, a mandatory standard, or a corrective action rested on a majority of a body rather than on the direction of a single administrator serving at pleasure.

The design carried costs that legislators accepted with the structure. A multimember commission decides more slowly than a single administrator, vacancies can stall action when nominations lag, and staggered terms can leave a commission divided between appointees of different presidents during a contested rulemaking. The debates of the 92nd Congress treated those costs as the price of insulation for a regulator whose central task involved judgments about acceptable risk, judgments that mixed engineering evidence with economic consequence and public tolerance. A standard that removed a hazard at trivial cost presented an easy case, while a standard that raised prices, eliminated a product line, or restricted a familiar household practice demanded a record that could withstand judicial review and public scrutiny. Congress placed those judgments in a commission built to deliberate in public, to hear industry and consumer evidence through advisory structures that included a Product Safety Advisory Council under section 28, and to answer to Congress without taking direction from a cabinet secretary whose department carried other loyalties.

The origins of the act therefore explain its form. A patchwork of narrow statutes had produced protection that depended on the category into which a dangerous object happened to fall. A temporary study commission converted scattered injuries into a national ledger of 20 million injuries, 30,000 deaths, and 5.5 billion dollars in annual costs, and tied that ledger to design, packaging, warnings, and foreseeable use. A two year legislative effort in the 92nd Congress converted the recommendation into Public Law 92-573, with findings and purposes in section 2 that named unreasonable risk as the enemy, uniformity as a goal, and research as a continuing duty. An independent five member commission, activated on May 14, 1973, under Chairman Richard O. Simpson, received the consolidated inheritance of the older laws and the new authority to act across the consumer marketplace. The statute that emerged in October 1972 was thus less a single rule for a single product than a decision about institutional architecture, a judgment that the injury ledger would keep growing under fragmented administration, and that only a regulator built for the whole field could count the harm accurately, set standards before injuries accumulated, and answer for the result.

## One commission, five statutes

The Consumer Product Safety Commission entered the federal regulatory structure in 1973 as an independent agency with a portfolio rather than a single mandate. Congress created the commission in the Consumer Product Safety Act, enacted on October 27, 1972, and in the same enactment Congress handed the new agency four older statutes that had previously been scattered among departments with broader and very different missions. The result was a deliberate consolidation, gathering hazards from paints and solvents, from fabrics that burned too fast, from household chemicals that children could open, and from refrigerator doors that trapped children inside. Consolidation, however, did not produce uniformity. Each of the five statutes kept its own definitions, its own operative prohibitions, its own rulemaking procedure, and its own remedies, and the commission administered the five as distinct bodies of law that happened to share an administrator. Understanding that architecture is a precondition for understanding the act of 1972, because the governing statute determines the test a product must pass, the label the product must bear, and the sanction available when a firm fails to comply.

Section 30 of the act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2079, performed the transfers in three subsections. Subsection (a) transferred the functions of the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare under the Federal Hazardous Substances Act and the Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970, together with functions under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act to the extent that those functions related to the packaging statute. Subsection (b) transferred functions under the Flammable Fabrics Act from three sources at once, from the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare, from the Secretary of Commerce, and from the Federal Trade Commission, together with functions under the Federal Trade Commission Act to the extent that those functions related to the fabrics statute. Subsection (c) transferred the functions of the Secretary of Commerce and the Federal Trade Commission under the act of August 2, 1956, the Refrigerator Safety Act. Later provisions of section 30 moved the personnel, property, records, and obligations attached to the transferred functions. Congress transferred functions rather than repealing those statutes and reenacting their substance inside the new act, preserving decades of definitions, regulations, and administrative practice.

The practical consequence for any practitioner, importer, manufacturer, or retailer begins with a threshold question of identification. Guidance on [how to read a federal statute](/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/) stresses the discipline of starting with the governing text, and the multi-statute design makes that discipline concrete, because the first task in a product safety matter is to identify which of the five statutes governs the product and the hazard before any standard is consulted or any filing is made. A chemical cleaner that lacked adequate warnings presented a question under the hazardous substances statute and its labeling regime. A garment that burned with dangerous speed presented a question under the fabrics statute and its flammability classifications. A prescription drug in a closure a child could defeat presented a question under the packaging statute, even though the drug itself belonged to another regulator entirely. A household refrigerator presented, alongside the general law of the act of 1972, a specific door requirement under the refrigerator statute. Each answer selected a different statutory vocabulary. Terms such as misbranded hazardous substance, banned hazardous substance, dangerously flammable fabric, and special packaging have precise meanings inside their home statutes and no operative meaning outside them.

The Federal Hazardous Substances Act, enacted in 1960 and codified at 15 U.S.C. sections 1261 to 1278, supplied the oldest and most elaborate labeling regime in the portfolio. The statute addressed substances that were toxic, corrosive, flammable, or combustible, that were irritants or strong sensitizers, or that generated pressure through decomposition, heat, or other means, where the substance was intended for household use or packaged in a form suitable for household use and could cause substantial personal injury or illness during customary handling or use. Its central device was the category of the misbranded hazardous substance. A covered substance had to bear a label carrying the name and address of the manufacturer, packer, distributor, or seller, the common or usual name of each component that contributed substantially to the hazard, a signal word such as danger or warning or caution, an affirmative statement of the principal hazard, precautionary measures describing action to follow or avoid, instructions for first aid treatment where necessary, the word poison for substances defined as highly toxic, handling and storage instructions where special care was required, and the statement to keep the container out of the reach of children or its practical equivalent. A substance missing a required element, or bearing the elements without the required prominence and legibility, entered commerce as a misbranded product, and section 4 prohibited the introduction of a misbranded hazardous substance into interstate commerce. Beside the labeling regime stood a more severe category, the banned hazardous substance, which covered toys and articles intended for children that bore a hazardous substance accessible to a child, and household substances classified by regulation as banned because cautionary labeling could not adequately protect public health and safety. The distinction embodied a judgment that some hazards could be managed by information and others only by exclusion from the market.

The Flammable Fabrics Act, enacted in 1953 and codified at 15 U.S.C. sections 1191 to 1204, grew out of a narrower catastrophe and retained a test based structure quite unlike the labeling logic of the hazardous substances statute. Congress acted in 1953 after deaths and severe burns associated with brushed rayon garments, including high pile sweaters that ignited readily and children in rayon cowboy chaps, and the original statute prohibited the manufacture and sale of wearing apparel and apparel fabrics that were so highly flammable as to be dangerous when worn. The standard of flammability turned on laboratory testing under commercial standards promulgated by the Secretary of Commerce, and the regime that developed under the statute classified clothing textiles by speed of burning, with the most rapid and intense burning class treated as dangerously flammable and unsuitable for use in clothing. Amendments in 1967 widened the statute beyond apparel to reach interior furnishings, and standards issued under the statute came to address vinyl plastic film, carpets and rugs, children in sleepwear, and mattresses and mattress pads. The authority transferred in 1972 therefore differed from the hazardous substances authority in both object and method. The question under the fabrics statute was rarely whether a warning had been given, since a warning pinned to a garment could not slow a flame front, and the question was instead whether the fabric itself, tested under the prescribed method, fell inside a permissible class. Enforcement also carried a distinctive character, because violations were framed in part as unfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce, a legacy of Federal Trade Commission administration. A practitioner who treated a flammability failure as a labeling defect misread the governing law.

The Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970, codified at 15 U.S.C. sections 1471 to 1477, addressed a hazard located neither in a substance alone nor in a fabric alone but in the interaction between a container and a child. The statute authorized the commission to establish standards for special packaging of household substances where the commission found that the degree or nature of the hazard to children in the availability of the substance, by reason of the packaging, was such that special packaging was required to protect children from serious personal injury or serious illness resulting from handling, using, or ingesting the substance, and where the special packaging to be required was technically feasible, practicable, and appropriate for the substance. Special packaging meant packaging designed or constructed to be significantly difficult for children under five years of age to open within a reasonable time, and not difficult for normal adults to use properly, without defeating the protective purpose through a design that adults would abandon or circumvent. Regulations issued under the statute applied child resistant requirements to specified categories that included aspirin and other medications, certain solvents, turpentine, methanol, sulfuric acid, and furniture polish, and the list grew through rulemaking as evidence about childhood poisonings accumulated. The statute also preserved a measure of consumer choice, providing for noncomplying packages in defined circumstances, including packages for elderly persons or handicapped persons unable to use special packaging, subject to labeling that disclosed the absence of child resistant protection. A packaging standard rested on findings about children, feasibility, and practicability, was developed after consultation with the technical advisory committee contemplated by the statute, and took effect within a timetable the statute itself prescribed, and a violation could also render a household substance a misbranded hazardous substance under the hazardous substances statute, an example of the five statutes interlocking without merging.

The Refrigerator Safety Act, enacted on August 2, 1956, and codified at 15 U.S.C. sections 1211 to 1214, was the smallest statute in the portfolio and the most specific. Congress acted after deaths of children who climbed inside abandoned refrigerators and could not reopen the doors from within, because the latching mechanisms of the period held fast against pressure from inside the cabinet. The statute made it unlawful to introduce into commerce a refrigerator having a door that could not be opened easily from the inside, and it directed the prescription and publication in the Federal Register of commercial standards for devices that, when used in or on household refrigerators, would enable the doors to be opened easily from the inside, with the first standards to be prescribed within one year after August 2, 1956. The transfer in section 30(c) moved that function from the Secretary of Commerce and the Federal Trade Commission to the commission, and the requirement survived as a design standard of unusual clarity. No labeling could satisfy it, no warning to parents or to children discarding appliances stood in its place, and compliance was a physical property of the door and its latch or magnetic gasket. The refrigerator statute therefore illustrated a principle that ran across the whole portfolio. Where Congress judged that a hazard yielded to information, the governing statute regulated words. Where Congress judged that a hazard yielded only to design, the governing statute regulated the object.

Differences of remedy and procedure reinforced the need to keep the five statutes distinct. The Consumer Product Safety Act itself supplied consumer product safety rules, substantial product hazard reporting, corrective action, import surveillance, and civil and criminal penalties in a framework built around the concept of unreasonable risk of injury. The hazardous substances statute operated through its own prohibited acts, its own provisions for seizure and for embargoed merchandise, and a penalty structure that Congress amended over time, including the addition of civil penalty authority by the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 1990. The fabrics statute carried its own guaranty provisions protecting retailers who relied in good faith on a guaranty from a supplier, its own inspection and record provisions, and penalties tied to its classification as trade regulation. The packaging statute channeled disputes into findings about feasibility and child testing protocols. None of these schemes was interchangeable. A recall negotiated under the act of 1972 rested on different findings from a ban declared under the hazardous substances statute, and a firm that reported a defect under one statute had not thereby satisfied a duty arising under another. Later amendments, including the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008, adjusted the portfolio statute by statute, confirming that the five laws remained five laws.

### What products does the Consumer Product Safety Commission not regulate?

The commission does not regulate motor vehicles, food, drugs, devices, cosmetics, firearms, ammunition, aircraft, pesticides, tobacco, or boats subject to Coast Guard regulation. Section 3 excludes those categories from the definition of consumer product, leaving them to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, the Food and Drug Administration, and other designated federal regulators.

The exclusions sit in section 3 of the act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2052, which defines consumer product as any article, or component part of an article, produced or distributed for sale to a consumer for use in or around a permanent or temporary household or residence, a school, in recreation, or otherwise, or for the personal use, consumption, or enjoyment of a consumer in or around such a household or residence, a school, in recreation, or otherwise. The definition is broad in its affirmative reach and exact in its carve outs, and the paragraph then lists the categories that the term does not include. The first carve out removes any article that is not customarily produced or distributed for sale to, or use or consumption by, or enjoyment of, a consumer, a limitation that kept industrial and purely commercial equipment outside the definition even where an individual might encounter the equipment at work. The second removes tobacco and tobacco products. The third removes motor vehicles and motor vehicle equipment, as defined in title 49, and the discussion in [the motor vehicle safety statute](/2016/05/15/motor-vehicle-safety-act-1966-guide/) traces the separate federal regime that Congress had built for highway safety beginning in 1966. Authority over motor vehicles and motor vehicle equipment rests with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration in the Department of Transportation, which administers federal motor vehicle safety standards, defect investigations, and vehicle recalls under a statute written for the particular risks of highway travel. The placement reflected a decision against overlapping defect regimes, so a defect in a factory installed vehicle component belonged to the highway safety system, while a home product with a similar hazard could belong to the commission.

The remaining carve outs follow the same pattern of assignment to a regulator with a specialized statute. The fourth removes pesticides as defined by the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act, leaving pesticide registration, labeling, and use restrictions to the Environmental Protection Agency, which evaluates pesticides through a licensing system built around efficacy and environmental effects as well as human exposure. The fifth removes any article that, if sold by the manufacturer, producer, or importer, would be subject to the tax imposed by section 4181 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986, together with any component of such an article. Section 4181 imposes the manufacturers excise tax on pistols, revolvers, other firearms, shells, and cartridges, so the tax cross reference performs the work of a firearms and ammunition exclusion without naming a product list, and regulatory authority over firearms commerce rests with the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives. The sixth removes aircraft, aircraft engines, propellers, and appliances as defined in title 49, leaving airworthiness certification and aviation safety to the Federal Aviation Administration. The seventh removes boats that could be subjected to safety regulation under chapter 43 of title 46, together with vessels and appurtenances subject to marine safety statutes administered by the department in which the Coast Guard operates, and associated equipment to the extent that a risk from its use could be eliminated or reduced under those marine statutes. The recreational boat carve out is therefore partial rather than absolute, and a marine adjacent product outside the Coast Guard system could remain a consumer product. The eighth removes drugs, devices, and cosmetics as defined in sections 201(g), (h), and (i) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, and the ninth removes food as defined in section 201(f) of that act, including poultry and poultry products, meat and meat food products, and eggs and egg products under the respective inspection statutes. Authority over food, drugs, devices, and cosmetics rests with the Food and Drug Administration, with meat, poultry, and egg inspection administered through the Department of Agriculture under the inspection acts that the definition names. A boundary consequence followed from the packaging statute. The commission could require child resistant packaging for a drug that the Food and Drug Administration regulated as to safety and efficacy, because the packaging authority arrived through the transferred statute rather than through the definition of consumer product, and the coexistence of the two authorities on a single medicine bottle demonstrated that the exclusions allocated subjects rather than sealing products inside impermeable compartments.

What remained after the exclusions was still an extraordinary breadth of commerce. Commission descriptions of the jurisdiction have commonly placed the figure at more than 15,000 types of consumer products, a figure that ranges across toys and nursery products, bicycles and helmets, lawn mowers, power tools, appliances, furniture, candles, cigarette lighters, all terrain vehicles, swimming pools, and thousands of other articles of household and recreational life, while the Congressional Research Service, in a legal analysis published in 2018, described the jurisdiction as covering approximately 10,000 types of products. The two figures differ because counting methods differ, and the safer statement is the attributed one, that published descriptions have put the reach in the range from approximately 10,000 types to more than 15,000 types. Either figure conveys the structural fact. Congress withheld the product categories that carried mature regulatory systems of their own and assigned the commission the long remainder, the vast residue of manufactured goods for which no specialized regulator existed in 1972. The definition also reaches component parts and imported articles, so a foreign manufacturer shipping into the United States market stood inside the statutory scheme even though the manufacturer had not designed the product for that market alone.

State law formed the final boundary of the design, and the act treated the states as participants rather than as displaced rivals. Section 25 of the act, at 15 U.S.C. section 2074, provided that compliance with a consumer product safety rule, or with other rules and orders under the act, did not relieve any person from liability at common law or under the law of any state in a damages action, so a federal standard functioned as a floor for private liability purposes rather than as a shield. Section 26, at 15 U.S.C. section 2075, drew the preemption line in a more qualified form. Where a consumer product safety standard under the act was in effect and applied to a risk of injury associated with a consumer product, a state or political subdivision could not establish or continue in effect a requirement as to performance, composition, contents, design, finish, construction, packaging, or labeling aimed at the same risk unless the state requirement was identical to the federal requirement. The same section preserved room for stricter state protection through an exemption mechanism, under which the commission could exempt a state requirement that imposed a higher degree of protection than the federal standard without unduly burdening interstate commerce. States also retained authority to enforce identical requirements and to act against risks that no federal standard addressed, and the transferred statutes carried preemption and savings provisions of their own, including section 18 of the hazardous substances statute, section 16 of the fabrics statute, and section 7 of the packaging statute, each of which had to be read on its own terms. The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 added that the preemption provisions of the several statutes could not be expanded or contracted by rule or by preamble, and that the commission could not construe any of the acts as preempting a cause of action under state law regarding damage claims. A stricter state rule therefore survived where the governing statute saved it, where an exemption had been granted, or where the state rule addressed a risk outside the federal standard. The five statute architecture, the definitional exclusions, and the treatment of state law together describe a jurisdiction built by allocation.

The five-statute map below sets out each law in the portfolio, the ground each law covered, and the authority each law supplied, together with the product categories that sat outside the commission altogether.

## Standards the Commission must usually borrow

Section 7 of the Consumer Product Safety Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2056, granted the Consumer Product Safety Commission authority to promulgate consumer product safety standards, and the text confined that authority to two kinds of requirement. A standard could impose requirements expressed in terms of performance, meaning a measurable result that a product had to achieve under a stated test rather than a prescribed design, material, or construction method. A standard could also require that a product be marked with or accompanied by clear and adequate warnings or instructions, and could regulate the form of those warnings or instructions. Every requirement had to be reasonably necessary to prevent or reduce an unreasonable risk of injury associated with the product. The performance orientation left manufacturers free to reach a required result by different engineering routes, while the labeling orientation treated information as a legitimate regulatory instrument for hazards that resisted elimination by redesign at tolerable cost.

Section 8, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2057, supplied the sharper instrument. Under section 8, the Commission could declare a consumer product a banned hazardous product by rule where the product presented an unreasonable risk of injury and no feasible consumer product safety standard would adequately protect the public from that risk. The statutory logic placed a ban at the end of a hierarchy. A standard assumed that a product could be made acceptably safe and then sold in compliant form. A ban assumed the opposite conclusion, so distribution in commerce had to stop, and Congress made the Commission prove the negative proposition about feasibility on a reviewable record.

Both instruments depended on section 9, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2058, and section 9 functioned as the price of using either one. Section 9 prescribed the procedure for consumer product safety rules, a term that covered both standards under section 7 and bans under section 8. The procedure exceeded the notice and comment model of the Administrative Procedure Act at every stage. A proceeding began with an advance notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register, inviting comment on the risk, on alternatives, and on any voluntary standard addressing the hazard. Only after that round could the Commission publish a proposed rule, and the proposal had to carry a preliminary regulatory analysis describing the potential benefits and costs of the proposal and of alternatives, with an explanation for any alternative declined. A final rule required a final regulatory analysis of the same character. The statute ordinarily capped the interval between promulgation and effectiveness at 180 days absent a finding of good cause.

### Why does the Commission so rarely write its own safety rules?

Congress made mandatory rulemaking slow, record heavy, and easy to challenge in court, and in 1981 Congress ordered deference to adequate voluntary standards with likely substantial compliance. Rational agency leadership therefore invested scarce staff in consensus committees, monitoring, recalls, and enforcement instead of multi-year rule proceedings.

The depth of the burden became visible in the findings that section 9 attached to every final rule. Under 15 U.S.C. section 2058(f)(3), the Commission could not promulgate a consumer product safety rule unless the rule itself contained findings on a series of distinct propositions. The Commission had to find that the rule, including its effective date, was reasonably necessary to eliminate or reduce an unreasonable risk of injury associated with the product. The Commission had to find that promulgation was in the public interest. For a rule declaring a product a banned hazardous product, the Commission had to find that no feasible consumer product safety standard would adequately protect the public from the risk. Where persons subject to the rule had adopted and implemented a voluntary standard addressing the same risk, the Commission had to find either that compliance with the voluntary standard was not likely to eliminate or adequately reduce the risk, or that substantial compliance with the voluntary standard was unlikely. The Commission had to find that the benefits expected from the rule bore a reasonable relationship to its costs. The Commission also had to find that the rule imposed the least burdensome requirement that prevented or adequately reduced the risk. Each finding depended on evidence about injury patterns, exposure, engineering feasibility, market behavior, and compliance rates that the Commission had to gather and defend.

Chronic hazards added a further institutional layer. Section 28 of the Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2077, established Chronic Hazard Advisory Panels drawn from lists of nominees maintained with the involvement of the National Academy of Sciences. Before the Commission proposed a rule addressing a risk of cancer, birth defects, gene mutations, or another chronic illness, a panel of independent scientists had to examine the hazard and report, and the Commission then had to build its proposal on a record that included the panel conclusions. The panel requirement reflected congressional distrust of agency improvisation on toxicology and long latency disease, where exposure data tended to be thin and expert disagreement wide. The practical consequence was delay measured in years. On September 20, 2017, the Commission voted to grant a petition for a ban on organohalogen flame retardants covering four product categories and directed staff to convene a Chronic Hazard Advisory Panel as an early step, years before any final prohibition could emerge.

Judicial review exposure completed the design. Section 11, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2060, allowed a person adversely affected by a rule to petition a court of appeals within 60 days after promulgation, and a rule could not be affirmed unless the findings under section 2058(f)(1) and section 2058(f)(3) rested on substantial evidence on the record taken as a whole. Substantial evidence review forced the Commission to litigate from the rulemaking record rather than from post hoc rationalization, and the prospect of vacatur after a proceeding lasting half a decade shaped internal choices long before any petition reached a court. Commissioner Robert S. Adler quantified the behavioral result in a July 7, 2015 statement published by the Commission, counting 24 safety rules drafted from 1973 to 1981, roughly three rules per year, and only ten rules during the 34 years after 1981. A Congressional Research Service legal analysis published on April 24, 2018 recorded approximately 40 mandatory safety rules and product bans under Consumer Product Safety Act authorities since 1972, with most completed before the 1981 amendments.

The decisive change arrived with the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35, signed on August 13, 1981, with the relevant amendments applicable to rulemaking notices issued after August 14, 1981. The 1981 amendments rewrote section 7. The earlier text had organized standard setting around an offeror process, under which outside persons could be invited or accepted to develop proposed standards for the Commission. Public Law 97-35 struck that machinery and substituted a rule of deference. The new subsection (b) of 15 U.S.C. section 2056 provided that the Commission shall rely upon voluntary consumer product safety standards rather than promulgate a consumer product safety standard whenever compliance with such voluntary standards would eliminate or adequately reduce the risk of injury addressed and substantial compliance with such voluntary standards was likely. A 1990 amendment, Public Law 101-608, added a duty to devise procedures for monitoring compliance with voluntary standards on which the Commission had relied. The preference therefore operated as a threshold test. Agency staff first asked whether a voluntary standard existed, whether the standard was technically adequate against the injury data, and whether the marketplace was likely to comply at a substantial level.

The preference also redirected agency labor rather than eliminating agency influence. Commission engineers and health scientists served on consensus committees, supplied injury data from the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System and from incident report files, proposed test methods, and pressed committees to tighten weak provisions. That posture gave the Commission leverage without the section 9 record, because a committee facing credible agency data and a latent threat of mandatory rulemaking had reason to act. The leverage had limits. A voluntary standard bound no importer who chose to ignore the standard, created no federal violation by itself, and depended on retailer purchasing policies, insurer pressure, certification marks, and liability litigation for practical force. Where compliance faltered, the Commission had to return to section 9 or shift to the recall tools supplied by section 15.

The consensus bodies that absorbed the displaced work had distinct structures. ASTM International, founded in 1898 as the American Society for Testing and Materials and renamed in 2001, operated through technical committees that balanced producer, user, consumer, and general interest members and adopted standards by ballot under consensus rules. Committee F15 on Consumer Products became the principal ASTM home for juvenile products, toys, and furniture hazards. Underwriters Laboratories, founded in 1894, combined standards development with testing and certification, so a UL standard carried market force through the certification mark that retailers and insurers demanded. The American National Standards Institute, founded in 1918, functioned mainly as an accreditor and coordinator rather than as a drafting body, accrediting developers and auditing adherence to ANSI Essential Requirements for due process, openness, balance, and consensus. Federal policy reinforced the same architecture outside the Act, as the National Technology Transfer and Advancement Act of 1995 directed federal agencies generally to use voluntary consensus standards in place of government unique standards where consistent with agency missions.

A concrete illustration came from infant walkers. ASTM F977, the Standard Consumer Safety Specification for Infant Walkers, addressed a hazard pattern in which a child in a wheeled walker reached a stairway edge and fell down the stairs while seated in the device. The specification pushed manufacturers toward two solutions that Don Mays, chair of ASTM Committee F15, described in ASTM publications. A walker could carry friction pads positioned to stop the walker abruptly if a wheel passed over a stair edge, or a walker could be built wide enough that passage through a conventional doorway became physically impossible. The Commission worked inside that voluntary process rather than through a section 9 standard, and ASTM accounts recorded a considerable reduction in walker associated injuries after manufacturers redesigned products to the specification. Congress ratified the model in the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008, Public Law 110-314, when section 104 directed mandatory standards for durable infant and toddler products based substantially on existing voluntary standards, and section 106 converted ASTM F963, the Standard Consumer Safety Specification for Toy Safety, into a mandatory standard by operation of law.

Against that headwind, the Commission did complete mandatory rules, and the completed examples showed which conditions overcame the preference. Lead paint supplied the earliest major case, although precision about the legal route matters. In 16 CFR part 1303, issued at 42 Federal Register 44199 on September 1, 1977 and applicable to covered products manufactured after February 27, 1978, the Commission declared paint and similar surface coating materials containing lead in excess of 0.06 percent by weight to be banned hazardous products, and extended the declaration to toys and other articles intended for children and to furniture articles bearing such paint. The regulation rested on sections 8 and 9 of the Consumer Product Safety Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. sections 2057 and 2058, and the Commission found an unreasonable risk of lead poisoning in children and found that no feasible consumer product safety standard would adequately protect the public. The same regulation stated that a ban under part 1303 counted as a regulation under section 2(q) of the Federal Hazardous Substances Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 1261(q). Congress later reduced the threshold to 0.009 percent effective August 14, 2009 under section 101(f) of the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008. The episode predated the 1981 preference, and the Commission acted during the brief window when section 9 procedure existed without a statutory instruction to defer.

The bicycle requirements showed a transferred statute doing mandatory work beside the Act rather than under section 9. The Requirements for Bicycles at 16 CFR part 1512, published in amended form at 43 Federal Register 60034 on December 22, 1978, sat within the Federal Hazardous Substances Act regulations that Congress had transferred to the new Commission in 1973. Part 1512 imposed detailed requirements covering braking systems, including a stopping distance of no more than 15 feet under the prescribed handbrake test for a rider of at least 150 pounds, fork and frame strength tests, wheel hub retention, seat post insertion markings, and reflector requirements. A bicycle failing the requirements qualified as a banned hazardous product under the Federal Hazardous Substances Act framework. Regulation flowed more readily through authorities handed over in nearly finished form, while new standards built from the ground under sections 7 and 9 faced the full procedural tariff.

The bunk bed standard demonstrated that the tariff could be paid after 1981 where injury data showed entrapment deaths and voluntary compliance left lethal gaps. The Safety Standard for Entrapment Hazards in Bunk Beds at 16 CFR part 1213, issued on December 22, 1999 and effective June 19, 2000, rested on 15 U.S.C. sections 2056 and 2058 and carried the section 2058(f)(3) findings in an appendix. The findings recorded 57 deaths of children under age 15 during a 9.6 year period from entrapment between the upper bunk and a wall or within openings in the bed structure, with more than 96 percent of the children who died aged three or younger. The rule required guardrails on both sides of the upper bunk, required one guardrail to run continuously between the end structures, and subjected openings to wedge block and template tests designed to prevent a small child torso from passing into a space where the head could not follow. The appendix estimated a benefit with a present value of about 175 dollars to 350 dollars for each bed that otherwise would have failed one or more requirements. The rule closely tracked the ASTM bunk bed specification in substance. Consensus drafting supplied the engineering content, and a mandatory rule followed only after recalls of more than 500,000 bunk beds during a four year period showed that voluntary adoption had not reached the dangerous tail of the market.

The lawn dart ban showed section 8 operating at full extension. In 16 CFR part 1306, sourced to 53 Federal Register 46839 on November 18, 1988 and effective December 19, 1988, the Commission declared any lawn dart a banned hazardous product and stated the purpose as prohibiting sale of a product found to present an unreasonable risk of skull puncture injury to children. The findings in section 1306.4 described the mechanism with unusual specificity. Weight, a narrow elongated shaft, travel speed at impact, and the thickness of a child skull at the point of impact combined to create penetration potential that the dart tips did not make obvious to purchasers. The findings recorded that the Commission considered performance requirements designed to separate dangerous lawn darts from other lawn darts and determined that such requirements were not feasible. The statutory findings then followed the section 2058(f)(3) template, including reasonable necessity, public interest, absence of a feasible protective standard, a reasonable relationship between benefits and costs, and the least burdensome adequate requirement. Michelle Snow, aged seven, died in 1987 in Riverside, California after a lawn dart struck her head, and her father, David Snow, campaigned in Congress for a total prohibition. The Commission cited records of three deaths associated with lawn darts since 1970 and an estimated 700 emergency room treated injuries per year at the time of the ban.

Drawstrings on children upper outerwear followed a different legal path, and the difference clarified the boundary between a standard and the section 15 system. The Commission issued drawstring guidelines in February 1996, and reports covering January 1985 through January 1999 eventually reached 22 deaths and 48 nonfatal entanglement incidents on playground equipment, bus doors, cribs, and similar catch points. ASTM converted the substance into a voluntary consensus standard in 1997 as ASTM F1816-97, prohibiting drawstrings around the hood and neck in sizes 2T to 12 and limiting waist and bottom drawstrings in sizes 2T to 16. In May 2006 the Commission Office of Compliance informed manufacturers and retailers that upper outerwear with hood or neck drawstrings was defective and presented a substantial risk of injury. In July 2011 the Commission approved a final rule at 16 CFR section 1120.3(b) deeming nonconforming children upper outerwear in sizes 2T to 16 a substantial product hazard under section 15(a)(2) of the Act, using listing authority granted by the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008. No section 9 standard emerged for drawstrings. A voluntary specification supplied the technical content, and a section 15 listing converted nonconformance into a reporting and recall problem for individual firms.

That sequence framed the three way distinction that organized Commission practice. A consumer product safety standard under section 7 set prospective, class wide requirements for performance or labeling. A ban under section 8 also operated class wide and prospectively, but the legal conclusion differed, because the Commission determined that the class itself could not remain in commerce. A substantial product hazard determination under section 15, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2064, operated on distributed products and on defects. Section 15(a) defined the term to cover a failure to comply with an applicable consumer product safety rule that created a substantial risk of injury to the public, and a product defect that, because of the pattern of defect, the number of defective products distributed in commerce, the severity of the risk, or otherwise, created a substantial risk of injury to the public. Section 15(b) required manufacturers, distributors, and retailers obtaining information reasonably supporting a reportable conclusion to inform the Commission immediately, and later subsections empowered the Commission, after notice and hearing, to order public notice and repair, replacement, or refund for products already sold. A standard governed how products could be made, a ban governed whether a class could be sold at all, and section 15 governed units already moving through warehouses, stores, and homes.

The cost and benefit findings tied the entire structure together. The preliminary and final regulatory analyses under section 2058(c) and section 2058(f)(2) forced the Commission to describe the market, estimate the number of products affected, project injury reduction, price compliance through redesign, testing, labeling, or withdrawal, and compare the chosen rule against alternatives that included a narrower standard, a broader ban, reliance on a voluntary standard, and no action. The finding that expected benefits bore a reasonable relationship to costs did not impose a strict test that monetized benefits exceed monetized costs in every case, and injury data involving children often resisted complete monetization. The finding nevertheless required a reasoned proportionality judgment on the record, open to substantial evidence review. The least burdensome finding obliged the Commission to search for a requirement that achieved adequate risk reduction with a smaller imposition. The voluntary standard findings obliged the Commission to explain why private ordering had failed or would fail before public ordering displaced private ordering. Read together, the findings converted every major policy argument against a proposed rule into a legal element of the rule. Congress had created an agency to write national product safety rules and then drafted provisions that made rule writing the slowest and most resource intensive path available to the agency, while consensus committees, section 15 recalls, and statutory incorporation of voluntary standards offered faster routes to many of the same engineering outcomes.

## A recall system built on companies reporting themselves

The Consumer Product Safety Act placed the first move in hazard correction in private hands. The Commission created in 1972 received authority over thousands of categories of consumer products, but Congress gave the agency no general staff of inspectors stationed inside factories and no general power to approve a product before sale. A dangerous design or a defective production run therefore tended to become visible first to the firm that made, imported, distributed, or sold the product, through warranty claims, retailer returns, quality control testing, injury reports, and lawsuits. Section 15 of the act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2064, converted that private knowledge into a legal duty. A firm that learned enough was required to tell the Commission, and the telling set in motion the evaluation that could end in repair, replacement, refund, or a contested order. Without a report, the agency often had no file, no engineering assessment, and no corrective action plan to negotiate. With a report, a small compliance staff could focus on products that firms themselves had flagged as presenting a credible risk.

Section 15(b), codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2064(b), imposed the duty on every manufacturer, importer, distributor, and retailer of a consumer product distributed in commerce. The trigger was information that reasonably supported a conclusion, a threshold set below proof and below certainty. One category covered a failure to comply with an applicable consumer product safety rule. A second covered a failure to comply with a voluntary consumer product safety standard on which the Commission had relied under section 9, and with another rule, regulation, standard, or ban under the act or under another statute enforced by the Commission. A third covered a product that contained a defect that could create a substantial product hazard. A fourth covered a product that created an unreasonable risk of serious injury or death, even apart from a proven defect in a particular unit. The duty arose unless the firm had actual knowledge that the Commission had already been adequately informed of the failure, defect, or risk. A firm did not need a laboratory finding of root cause, a count of injuries large enough to satisfy a court, or an admission that a recall was warranted. Complaints, incident reports, engineering data, and quality control results could each supply the information, and a report was not treated as an admission that a substantial product hazard existed.

The reporting regulation at 16 C.F.R. Part 1115 gave the duty an operational shape. Part 1115 defined the subject firms covered, described the information that should be reported, set the contents of an initial report and a full report, and supplied the timetable applied in penalty cases. Section 1115.12 instructed firms not to delay reporting in order to determine a reportable noncompliance, defect, or unreasonable risk to a certainty. The same section identified information patterns that demanded particular attention, including information indicating that a noncompliance or defect had caused, may have caused, or could cause a death or a grievous bodily injury such as mutilation, amputation, disfigurement, severe burns, severe electrical shock, or an injury likely to require extended hospitalization. Section 1115.11 provided that a firm would be deemed to know what a reasonable and prudent person in the same circumstances would know, including knowledge obtainable through due care in checking complaints and representations.

### How quickly must a company tell the Commission about a dangerous defect?

A firm must inform the Commission immediately, interpreted by regulation as within 24 hours after obtaining information that reasonably supports a reportable conclusion. A firm uncertain about reportability may investigate in a reasonably expeditious manner, ordinarily within 10 days, and a longer period requires a demonstration that the extra time was reasonable.

The 24 hour rule appeared in section 1115.14(e) of the regulation, which translated the statutory word immediately into a one day period running from the point at which a firm obtained reportable information. The investigation allowance appeared in section 1115.14(d), and the verified text allowed a reasonably expeditious investigation that should not exceed 10 days unless a firm could demonstrate that a longer period was reasonable. The Commission stated that at the end of 10 days a firm would be deemed to have received and considered all information that a reasonable, expeditious, and diligent investigation would have produced. The Commission Recall Handbook applied the same structure in working day terms, treating 10 working days as the ordinary outer limit and placing the burden of justification on a firm that took longer. Section 1115.14(b) added a second clock inside the firm. Knowledge was imputed when information reached an official or employee capable of appreciating the significance of the information, and under ordinary circumstances five days was the maximum reasonable time for that information to reach the chief executive officer or the official responsible for compliance with section 15(b). A complaint buried in a sales office, a service department file, or a foreign parent company inbox therefore did not stop the clock merely because headquarters had not assembled the file.

A separate reporting channel covered child choking incidents. The Child Safety Protection Act added a duty, implemented at 16 C.F.R. Part 1117, for a manufacturer, distributor, retailer, or importer to report within 24 hours after obtaining information that reasonably supported the conclusion that a child had choked on a marble, a small ball, a latex balloon, or another small part contained in a toy or game and, as a result, had died, suffered serious injury, ceased breathing for any length of time, or received medical treatment. Part 1117 operated in addition to section 15(b) rather than in place of that duty, so a firm could owe both reports on related facts. The pattern based duties also extended beyond single incidents. A firm evaluating a defect was directed to weigh the pattern of the defect, the number of defective products distributed, the severity of the risk, and any other relevant consideration, so a series of minor failures with a common cause could become reportable even when no single failure had produced a grave injury.

The concept that a report could set in motion was the substantial product hazard defined in section 15(a), codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2064(a). The definition had two branches. The first branch covered a failure to comply with an applicable consumer product safety rule, where the failure created a substantial risk of injury to the public. The second branch covered a product defect that, because of the pattern of the defect, the number of defective products distributed in commerce, the severity of the risk, or otherwise, created a substantial risk of injury to the public. A defect could arise from a design choice shared by every unit, from a manufacturing error confined to one production lot, or from inadequate warnings or instructions, and the statutory test asked about risk rather than about harm already completed. Section 15(a) thus linked the reporting duty to the corrective powers that followed. A report under section 15(b) invited Commission staff to make a preliminary determination whether the reported product presented a substantial product hazard, drawing on information from the reporting firm and from any other source.

Section 15(c) supplied the public notice side of the mandatory route. After affording interested persons an opportunity to submit views, the Commission could order a manufacturer, distributor, or retailer to give notice of a substantial product hazard to purchasers and to publicize the hazard. Section 15(d) supplied the remedy side. If the Commission determined, after affording interested parties including consumers and consumer organizations an opportunity for a hearing under section 15(f), that a product presented a substantial product hazard and that action was in the public interest, the Commission could order the manufacturer, distributor, or retailer to repair the product, to replace the product with a like or equivalent product that lacked the hazard, or to refund the purchase price, less a reasonable allowance for use where a consumer had possessed the product for one year or more. The hearing requirement gave the mandatory route its defining character. A firm could contest whether a defect existed, whether the risk was substantial, whether the proposed remedy matched the risk, and whether the order served the public interest, in proceedings governed by section 554 of title 5 and by the Commission Rules of Practice in 16 C.F.R. Part 1025.

That contested structure explained why orders under section 15(c) and section 15(d) remained exceptional while negotiated corrections became routine. An adjudicated case demanded staff engineers, lawyers, hearing time, and a record capable of surviving judicial review, and each contested element multiplied delay while units remained in homes and in retail channels. A firm facing a mandatory proceeding also faced incentives to litigate the preliminary determination itself, because a formal finding of substantial product hazard could be cited by private plaintiffs and could damage a brand beyond the cost of the remedy. Negotiation reversed those incentives. A firm that reported promptly and offered a corrective action plan acceptable to staff could obtain an announced recall, a repair kit, a replacement program, or a refund program without the same adjudicated finding, and the Commission could obtain removal of the product on a timetable measured in weeks rather than in the longer periods that contested proceedings consumed. Commission statements across several decades described nearly all recalls as voluntary in that negotiated sense, meaning that the firm agreed to conduct the correction in consultation with the Commission. The reporting duty, the penalty exposure for silence, and the prospect of a section 15 order formed the background against which the agreement was reached.

The Fast Track program formalized the bargain. The Commission began a pilot version in August 1995, and the Commission voted on March 24, 1997 to make the program permanent, with a Federal Register notice at 62 Fed. Reg. 39827 in July 1997. Under Fast Track, a firm that filed a full report under section 15(b) and implemented a voluntary recall acceptable to staff within 20 working days of the report avoided a preliminary determination by staff that the product contained a defect creating a substantial product hazard. The firm supplied the complaint and injury information required for a full report under 16 C.F.R. section 1115.13(d), proposed a corrective action plan, and prepared joint notice, direct notification, and point of purchase posters in time for the plan to be announced within the 20 working day window. Staff review moved in parallel rather than in sequence, and the absence of a preliminary determination removed the finding that firms most often sought to avoid. Commission figures for fiscal year 1999, published in the Commission annual report for that year, recorded about 140 firms participating, 164 corrective actions, and approximately 16 million product units covered.

A statement issued by Commissioner Peter A. Feldman on November 1, 2023 supplied a later measure of the same pattern. The statement tabulated 312 total recalls in fiscal year 2023, of which 167 were Fast Track recalls, against 260 total recalls in fiscal year 2022 and 265 in fiscal year 2018. Testimony by Commissioner Feldman to a House committee gave a closely related figure of 313 voluntary recalls negotiated in fiscal year 2023, with the small difference reflecting the counting basis used in each document. Speed depended on the firm arriving with a report and a workable plan, so the fastest channel in the system was open only after self disclosure had already occurred.

Section 12 of the act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2061, gave the Commission a sharper instrument for a narrower class of danger. The statute defined an imminently hazardous consumer product as a consumer product that presented an imminent and unreasonable risk of death, serious illness, or severe personal injury. The Commission could file an action in a United States district court against the product for seizure, against a manufacturer, distributor, or retailer of the product, or against both, even when a consumer product safety rule applied and even while other proceedings were pending. A district court that declared a product imminently hazardous could grant temporary or permanent relief necessary to protect the public, including orders requiring notification of purchasers, public notice, recall, repair, replacement, or refund, and a product proceeded against directly could be seized and condemned. Section 12 also directed the Commission, where appropriate, to initiate a proceeding for a consumer product safety rule for the product in connection with the court action.

Use of section 12 remained rare. The Congressional Research Service report titled The Consumer Product Safety Act: A Legal Analysis, in the version updated in 2018, stated that the exact number of attempts to exercise the imminent hazard authority was unclear and that use appeared to be rare. The report identified two structural reasons. Proof in court of an imminent and unreasonable risk was harder than proof of a substantial product hazard in the section 15 process, and the relief available for a substantial product hazard overlapped heavily with the relief a court could grant under section 12, so the harder route added little in most cases. A negotiated recall was faster than either route when a firm cooperated. Section 12 therefore functioned as a reserve power for a product whose risk was both grave and immediate and whose firm would not act.

Penalties supplied the enforcement lever that made the reporting duty credible, and late reporting became the violation the Commission pursued most consistently. Section 19 of the act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2068, listed prohibited acts, including a failure to furnish information required by section 15(b). Section 20, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2069, authorized civil penalties against a person who knowingly violated a prohibited act, and section 20(d) defined knowingly to include knowledge that a reasonable person acting in the same circumstances would have possessed, including knowledge obtainable through due care. Section 21, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2070, authorized criminal penalties for a knowing and willful violation. A civil penalty case therefore did not require proof that a product had injured anyone. The case turned on the information the firm held, the point at which that information reasonably supported a reportable conclusion, and the gap between that point and the report the firm eventually filed. That focus made late reporting cases comparatively provable from company records, correspondence, and incident logs.

The Graco case set the early benchmark. On March 22, 2005 the Commission announced a provisional settlement imposing a 4 million dollar civil penalty on Graco Children Products Inc., described in the Commission announcement as the largest civil penalty in Commission history at that date. The Commission stated that Graco and Century, a brand Graco had acquired in 1998, had failed between 1991 and 2002 to report defects in juvenile products that could create substantial product hazards or unreasonable risks to young children. The Commission counted hundreds of unreported incidents and injuries involving 16 different products and more than 12 million products in total, including infant carriers, high chairs, infant swings, strollers, and toddler beds, with injuries ranging from contusions and fractures to strangulation, including some fatalities. The announcement accompanied a recall of about 1.2 million toddler beds sold between February 1994 and March 2001.

The Office Depot settlement showed the same theory applied to a retailer. On May 27, 2015 the Commission announced that Office Depot Inc. had agreed to pay a 3.4 million dollar civil penalty to settle charges that the company had knowingly failed to report defects and an unreasonable risk of serious injury in two office chair models, the Quantum and the Gibson, whose seatbacks failed in use. The Commission stated that Office Depot had never reported the Quantum hazard and had reported the Gibson hazard only after Commission staff requested a report. By the time of the recalls, Office Depot had received 33 reports of seatback detachment for the Quantum and 153 reports for the Gibson, with 14 and 25 reports of injury respectively. The Commission record of action dated May 22, 2015 grounded the charge in section 15(b) and in section 20(a)(1). About 150,000 Quantum chairs had been sold between 2006 and 2009 and about 1.4 million Gibson chairs between 2003 and 2012.

The Gree case carried the penalty scale to a different level and added a criminal sequel. On March 25, 2016 the Commission announced that Gree Electric Appliances Inc. of Zhuhai, Hong Kong Gree Electric Appliances Sales Co. Ltd., and Gree USA Inc. had agreed to pay a 15.45 million dollar civil penalty, described by the Commission as a record at that date, to resolve allegations that the companies had knowingly failed to report a defect in dehumidifiers that overheated and caught fire, had misrepresented the dehumidifiers as compliant with a safety standard, and had used a certification mark improperly. The dehumidifiers had been sold under 13 brand names, and a first recall in September 2013 covered about 2.2 million units in the United States. A Department of Justice resolution announced on October 29, 2021 then produced a deferred prosecution agreement for the Zhuhai and Hong Kong companies and a guilty plea by Gree USA to willfully failing to report, with 91 million dollars in total monetary penalties in the criminal resolution and the 2016 civil penalty credited within that figure.

Dependence on reports followed from the limits on every alternative source of knowledge. The act created no premarket approval program for consumer products generally, and no general requirement that a new product pass a Commission test before sale, although later statutes imposed third party testing and certification duties for defined categories such as children products. The Congressional Research Service analysis recorded that the Commission lacked the resources to inspect each of the millions of consumer products manufactured domestically or imported into the United States before those products reached consumers. Import surveillance operated by selection rather than by comprehensive examination. The Commission obtained samples of products offered for import through the Secretary of the Treasury, used a risk based assessment methodology and data from United States Customs and Border Protection to choose shipments for inspection, and could ask the Treasury to refuse entry to a product that failed a safety rule or presented a substantial product hazard. A Government Accountability Office report cited in the Congressional Research Service analysis found that four of five product recalls in the United States in the period examined involved an imported product. Establishment inspections, state and local compliance work, consumer complaints filed through the SaferProducts.gov database created under later amendments, and injury surveillance through hospital emergency departments each supplied leads, and none supplied the design history, production records, or internal test results that a reporting firm possessed.

The result was a system whose strength and weakness came from the same source. A report delivered within 24 hours of a reportable conclusion let a small agency move a hazardous product toward repair, replacement, or refund at national scale, and the Fast Track program rewarded that speed by sparing the reporting firm a preliminary hazard determination. A report delayed while a firm investigated beyond the period the regulation allowed, discounted complaints as isolated events, or waited for proof of root cause left the agency blind to a hazard that the firm alone could see in full. Sections 15(c) and 15(d) gave the Commission a mandatory remedy after a hearing, section 12 gave a court a seizure and injunction remedy for an imminent risk, and sections 19, 20, and 21 supplied the penalty provisions that enforced the duty that started the process. The penalty cases against Graco in 2005, Office Depot in 2015, and Gree in 2016 recorded the price set for firms that made that decision late.

## The Consumer Product Safety Act and the disclosure restriction

Only one major federal safety regulator operates under a statute that conditions release of information on advance notice to the firm, a summary of the material, an opportunity to comment, and a judicially enforceable duty to take reasonable steps on accuracy and fairness before speaking. That regulator is the Consumer Product Safety Commission, and the condition is section 6(b) of the Consumer Product Safety Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2055(b). Consumer Federation of America materials distilled the arrangement into one sentence, stating that section 6(b) required the Commission to essentially ask permission from manufacturers before communicating information about a product to the public. The sentence was advocacy shorthand rather than statutory text, because the statute grants no formal veto and reserves the disclosure decision to the Commission. The shorthand endured because a manufacturer received warning before the public received warning, held a right to answer the agency in advance, and held a right to ask a federal judge to stop the disclosure altogether.

The provision applies before public disclosure of information obtained under the Act when the manner in which a consumer product is designated or described will permit the public to ascertain readily the identity of a manufacturer or private labeler. Once the trigger is met, the Commission must act not less than 15 days before disclosure, to the extent practicable. The Commission must notify each affected manufacturer or private labeler, provide a summary, and provide a reasonable opportunity to submit comments. Before disclosure, the Commission must also take reasonable steps to assure that information from which an identity may be readily ascertained is accurate, that disclosure is fair in the circumstances, and that disclosure is reasonably related to effectuating the purposes of the Act, a term that subsection (d) extended to three other statutes administered by the Commission. Upon request, the Commission was required to include comments submitted by the manufacturer or private labeler with the disclosure. Section 6(b)(1) permitted a lesser period when the Commission published a finding that public health and safety required the shorter period, so the statute contemplated emergencies without freeing an emergency disclosure from the accuracy, fairness, and purpose requirements. Section 6(b)(7) added a remedy after error, requiring publication of a retraction, in a manner equivalent to the original disclosure, after a finding that inaccurate or misleading information reflecting adversely on product safety or on a firm had been disclosed.

The 15 day period represented a shortening of an older restraint. As enacted on October 27, 1972, section 6(b)(1) required notice not less than 30 days before disclosure, and the House Report described a 30 day comment opportunity as the safeguard paired with the broad information gathering powers granted elsewhere in the bill. The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008, Public Law 110-314, enacted on August 14, 2008, amended section 6 through section 211 of that law and reduced the period from 30 days to 15 days. A second stage converted comment into litigation leverage. Under section 6(b)(2), when a manufacturer or private labeler claimed that a document was inaccurate and the Commission determined that the document should be disclosed because the requirements of paragraph (1) had been satisfied, the Commission was required to notify the firm that disclosure would occur at a date not less than 5 days after receipt of the notification. Under section 6(b)(3), before the date set for release, the recipient of the paragraph (2) notice could bring an action in a United States district court where the complainant resided or held a principal place of business, where the documents were located, or in the District of Columbia, to enjoin disclosure. The district court could enjoin disclosure if the Commission had failed to take the reasonable steps prescribed in paragraph (1), and an expedited track allowed the Commission to request expedited consideration, with a decision on the injunction due within 30 days after the request. An industry fact sheet titled Section 6(b): Myth versus Fact, dated January 20, 2020, traced the court challenge to 1981 amendments responding to complaints that the Commission was regulating by press release.

The restriction contained boundaries. Section 6(b)(4) provided that paragraphs (1) through (3) did not apply to information about a product subject to an action under 15 U.S.C. section 2061 concerning imminently hazardous products, or a product the Commission had reasonable cause to believe violated a consumer product safety rule or another provision enforced by the Commission. The same paragraph exempted information in the course of or concerning a rulemaking proceeding, an adjudicatory proceeding, or another administrative or judicial proceeding under the Act. Section 6(b)(5) imposed a stricter gate for reports submitted under section 15(b) of the Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2064(b), through which firms reported defects that could create a substantial product hazard, unreasonable risks of serious injury or death, and failures to comply with applicable rules. The Commission could not disclose section 15(b) information unless the Commission had issued a complaint under section 2064(c) or (d) alleging a substantial product hazard, had accepted a written remedial settlement agreement, had obtained agreement from the submitter, or had published a finding that public health and safety required disclosure on shorter notice. A separate confidentiality track in subsection (a) governed trade secrets and matter protected under 18 U.S.C. section 1905 and 5 U.S.C. section 552(b)(4), with a 15 calendar day opportunity to mark information as confidential, 10 days of written notice before disclosure of material the Commission determined was not confidential, and a district court action to restrain disclosure.

### Why must the Commission warn a manufacturer before it warns the public?

Section 6(b) conditions any Commission disclosure identifying a manufacturer on advance notice, a summary, an opportunity to comment, and reasonable steps to assure accuracy and fairness. Congress imposed the sequence in 1972 to prevent reputational harm from unverified agency statements, and the Supreme Court sustained the condition against Freedom of Information Act requests in 1980.

The longer answer lies in the 1972 bargain. The 1972 Congress granted the new agency broad authority to obtain reports, records, and accident data that firms had no general duty to publish, much of the material relating to trade secrets or other sensitive competitive information. Section 6 answered that grant with detailed limitations on disclosure authority, directed at the reputational interest rather than at secrecy alone. A false statement about a named product differed from a false statement about an unnamed category, because buyers, retailers, lenders, and insurers could act on the identification within hours and a later retraction rarely reconstructed a market position. Nancy A. Nord, a Commissioner from 2005 and Acting Chairman from 2006 to 2009, and Nathan S. Cardon, senior counsel to Commissioner Nord, argued in The Regulatory Review on February 6, 2013, that section 6(b) codified an expectation of accuracy checks before release, described the duty as consultation rather than a gag, and contended that the provision gave the Commission more information and earlier response.

The contrast with peer regulators sharpened the anomaly. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration administered defect law under the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act without a duty to give a manufacturer 15 days of notice, a summary, and a comment opportunity before public identification, and without a manufacturer action to enjoin disclosure. The Food and Drug Administration administered food, drug, and device recall publicity under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act without an equivalent notice and objection regime. Consumer Reports quoted Peter Cassell, a press officer for the Food and Drug Administration, explaining that the Administration coordinated with a company to ensure that a recall was effective and added agency notices where company information was insufficient, and Consumer Reports reported that the Administration had no obligation to obtain permission before publicly disclosing company names in alerts or recalls. David Friedman, vice president of advocacy at Consumer Reports and a former acting administrator of the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, called the restriction a dangerous anomaly among federal safety agencies and stated that the highway agency had possessed freedom to share available knowledge during the Takata air bag recall campaign that the Commission lacked.

A parallel constraint governed the other consumer agency housed outside the safety field. The Federal Trade Commission received expanded rulemaking authority under the Magnuson Moss Warranty Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act of 1975 only together with hybrid procedures exceeding the Administrative Procedure Act baseline, including cross examination on disputed issues of material fact and a substantial evidence standard for judicial review. The procedural price attached to [the Federal Trade Commission's consumer protection rulemaking](/2019/01/15/ftc-consumer-protection-rulemaking/) differed from the procedural price attached to Commission disclosure, because the trade commission procedure slowed the making of generally applicable rules while section 6(b) slowed the release of particular facts about particular products. In each setting Congress answered distrust of broad delegated power with a pre action hearing right for the regulated party. The difference lay in the cost of delay, because a delayed trade regulation left an existing market rule in place, while a delayed hazard disclosure left an existing product in homes.

The Supreme Court fixed the legal reach of the restriction in Consumer Product Safety Commission v. GTE Sylvania, Inc., 447 U.S. 102 (1980), in an opinion by Justice Rehnquist decided on June 9, 1980. The dispute began when the Commission obtained accident reports from television manufacturers, most accompanied by confidentiality claims, and decided, after Freedom of Information Act requests from Consumers Union and the Public Citizen Health Research Group, to release the reports without complying with section 6(b)(1), on the theory that the paragraph covered only affirmative Commission disclosures. The District Court for the District of Delaware permanently enjoined disclosure, at 443 F. Supp. 1152 (1977), and the Third Circuit affirmed at 598 F.2d 790 (1979). The Supreme Court granted certiorari because of a conflict with the Second Circuit decision in Pierce and Stevens Chemical Corp. v. Consumer Product Safety Commission, 585 F.2d 1382 (1978), on the question whether section 6(b)(1) governed disclosure pursuant to a Freedom of Information Act request.

The Court answered affirmatively and affirmed. On text, the Court held that section 6(b)(1) applied to the public disclosure of any information obtained under the Act, that no language limited the paragraph to Commission initiated disclosures, and that a Freedom of Information Act disclosure was a public disclosure within the plain meaning of the paragraph. On structure, the Court observed that the exceptions paragraph included no exception for Freedom of Information Act disclosures, and that section 25(c) of the Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2074(c), made disclosure of designated accident and investigation reports subject to sections 6(a)(2) and 6(b) whether release was affirmative or pursuant to a request. On exemption, the Court held that the applicability of section 6(b)(1) was not precluded by the time requirements of the Freedom of Information Act, because Exemption 3 of that Act, 5 U.S.C. section 552(b)(3), removed matters specifically exempted from disclosure by another statute that established particular criteria for withholding, and section 6(b)(1) set forth sufficiently definite standards to fall within Exemption 3. On burden, the Court held that claims of insurmountable administrative burden were speculative and that increased burdens represented a balance struck by Congress, so that a complaint about burden was properly addressed to Congress. A Freedom of Information Act suit could therefore compel only disclosure consistent with section 6(b).

Disclosure to Congress and to other agencies occupied separate statutory ground. Section 6(a)(2), as reprinted in the opinion, stated that nothing in the Act authorized withholding of information from the duly authorized committees of Congress. Section 2055(a)(7) provides that paragraphs (2) through (6) of subsection (a) did not apply to disclosures to duly authorized committees or subcommittees of Congress, with immediate notice to the firm of a request for confidential material. Sharing with other regulators rested on section 29(e) of the Act, a provision added in 1976 that the GTE Sylvania opinion discussed in connection with accident and investigation reports. Section 29(e) permitted sharing of accident or investigation reports with another federal, state, or local health, safety, or consumer protection authority only if confidential information under section 6(a)(2) was excluded and the receiving agency gave satisfactory assurances protecting the identity of injured persons, and section 29(e) barred public disclosure by the receiving agency unless the Commission had complied with section 6(b). The holding governed public disclosure and left the congressional access rule and the agency sharing rule to the provisions written for those channels.

Named critics attributed three consequences to the provision, delay in warning, chilling of staff communication, and litigation leverage. Elliot Kaye, a Commissioner and former Chairman, stated at an April 2019 House Energy and Commerce subcommittee hearing that section 6(b) required elimination, summarizing the delay claim in a sentence quoted by Consumer Reports, that people die because of section 6(b). Robert Adler, a former Acting Chairman, stated at the 2021 conference of the International Consumer Product Health and Safety Organization that the agency needed release from the information muzzle in section 6(b), according to a Crowell and Moring LLP analysis published in April 2021. Teresa Murray of the United States Public Interest Research Group, author of the Safe at Home report, identified the litigation mechanism as the operative delay device, writing that the 15 day wait gave companies enough time to file a lawsuit to block disclosure, and Murray connected the mechanism to the Peloton Tread Plus recall of May 5, 2021, preceded by reports of a child death and other injuries and by a Commission warning issued over company objection. Representative Jan Schakowsky of Illinois stated on April 22, 2021, that section 6(b) had severely restricted prompt warning, leading to unnecessary deaths and injuries from products including the Fisher Price Rock and Play sleeper and the Peloton Tread Plus treadmill. Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut, a repeal sponsor with Schakowsky and Representative Bobby Rush of Illinois, stated that the Commission required power to warn without giving a manufacturer a virtual veto over disclosure. The Fisher Price episode supplied a chronology for the delay claim, because the inclined sleeper was recalled in April 2019, congressional investigation material described internal Commission awareness of at least a dozen deaths before the recall, and testimony placed the eventual death toll above 90. Consumer Reports reported that companies often restricted information released in alerts and recalls and negotiated the language used. The chilling claim operated at staff level, because a regime attaching a comment file and a potential injunction action to every identifying statement rewarded negotiated language over unilateral warning, and section 2055(d)(2) extended the provision to disclosure by any member, employee, agent, or representative of the Commission acting in an official capacity.

Named defenders answered each consequence with a competing account of error, cooperation, and legal reality. Nord and Cardon argued in the February 6, 2013 Regulatory Review essay that release of every item received through voluntary submission would dry up information sources, because firms supplied safety information beyond legal compulsion in reliance on fair treatment, and that consumers received more and more useful information with the provision than without the provision. The January 20, 2020 industry fact sheet answered the delay claim by asserting that no evidence showed that section 6(b) had prevented or delayed disclosure of important safety information, that the Commission could disclose verified information quickly through acceleration and emergency authorities, and that slow disclosure reflected agency process rather than statutory requirements. The fact sheet answered the veto claim by noting that release decisions rested with the Commission, and answered the litigation claim by reporting that court challenges were rare, produced mixed outcomes, and tended to require disclosure of the very information a company sought to protect. The accuracy defense rested on erroneous identification, because a Commission statement carried a governmental imprimatur that a newspaper allegation lacked, retailers delisted products on agency language alone, and a retraction could not restore shelf space or sales lost after a mistaken identification. The 1981 amendments embodied that defense, because Congress answered complaints about regulation by press release with a judicial check rather than repeal.

The 2008 database supplied a partial workaround for the restriction. SaferProducts.gov, created under section 212 of the 2008 law, published reports of harm in the voice of the submitter rather than in the voice of the Commission, and the full operation and litigation history of that database are assessed in the historical account that follows.

The 2008 Congress that shortened the period considered and removed a provision that would have eliminated section 6(b), as later reporting recorded. A 2014 notice of proposed rulemaking to revise the interpretive regulation at 16 C.F.R. Part 1101, which would have expanded exempt categories including information already publicly available, according to a Lexology analysis published in 2014, produced no change to the statutory text. Representative Rush introduced the SHARE Act in the preceding Congress, a bill that, according to the Crowell and Moring analysis of April 2021, targeted the judicial challenge feature while leaving the remaining protections intact, and the bill did not become law. On April 22, 2021, Senator Blumenthal and Representatives Schakowsky and Rush introduced the Sunshine in Product Safety Act, to repeal section 6(b) in full. Reporting on the campaign recorded that no Republican lawmaker signed onto either bill, and the Act did not reach enactment. The core sequence survived the 1981, 1990, 2008, and 2011 amendment cycles intact, because Congress in each cycle chose revision of the clocks and procedures over removal of the gate.

## What five decades of amendment and litigation left standing

The statute that survived to its sixth decade bore only a partial resemblance to the statute enacted in October 1972, and the distance between the two versions measured the recurring argument over what kind of agency Congress had created. Each major amendment answered a failure that the preceding settlement had exposed. The first revision narrowed jurisdiction drawn too broadly for the politics of the moment. The second revision, enacted under pressure to shrink the federal establishment, preserved the Commission while stripping away parts of its supporting structure. The third revision, enacted after a year of recalls of imported toys, rebuilt the agency with more money, more staff, higher penalties, and mandatory duties for children's products that the original Act had never imposed. Litigation ran alongside legislation, testing how much evidence the Commission needed before a rule could stand and how much control a manufacturer retained over information the agency wished to publish. The measured record supported neither complete vindication nor complete repudiation.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission Improvements Act of 1976, enacted as Public Law 94-284 and signed on May 11, 1976, performed the first recalibration. Congress authorized appropriations of 51 million dollars for fiscal year 1976, 60 million dollars for fiscal year 1977, and 68 million dollars for fiscal year 1978, with an additional 14 million dollars for the transition period ending September 30, 1976, placing the young agency on a defined funding schedule. The same Act withdrew territory. Jurisdiction over pesticides under the Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970 passed out of Commission hands. Authority to ban cigarettes under the Federal Hazardous Substances Act ended, as did any regulatory authority over tobacco products, and authority over firearms ammunition suffered the same fate. The exclusions settled questions that the open language of 1972 had left available for agency ambition, and the settlement endured because each excluded product carried a constituency and a competing regulatory home. The 1976 Act also barred any consumer product safety standard from requiring, incorporating, or referencing a sampling plan, subject to exceptions for flammability and glass containers. The pattern established in 1976 repeated across the following decades, in which expansion of enforcement tools arrived packaged with explicit boundaries drawn in statutory text.

### Did the Consumer Product Safety Act require products to be approved before sale?

No. The Act created a post-market system for ordinary consumer products. Manufacturers could sell without Commission approval, certification in advance, or a government safety license. The Commission set standards, collected injury reports, inspected imports, and pursued recalls or bans after products reached the market and hazards emerged in use.

That answer framed every later debate about the agency's performance, because a post-market design allocated the first move to manufacturers and assigned the government the harder task of detection after distribution had begun. The Commission received reports from manufacturers under Section 15, gathered emergency department data through the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System, investigated deaths with medical examiners and coroners, and tested products purchased from store shelves or seized at ports. None of that machinery operated as a gate through which a product passed before a family encountered the product at retail. Consumer advocates at the Consumer Federation of America and Consumers Union returned to the distinction repeatedly in oversight testimony, arguing that public expectation assumed a screening function the statute had withheld. Congress revisited the question at each reauthorization and declined each time to convert the system, preferring instead to strengthen reporting duties, import surveillance, and penalties after harm. The 2008 amendments narrowed the gap for children's products through mandatory third-party testing, and even that reform stopped short of approval, since a certificate of conformity attested that testing had occurred rather than recording a discretionary government judgment that a product deserved a place in commerce.

The near abolition episode of the early 1980s tested whether the agency would survive at all. In 1981 David Stockman, director of the Office of Management and Budget under President Ronald Reagan, sought to end the authorization for the agency and to move the Commission inside the Department of Commerce, a transfer that would have extinguished independent status and folded product safety into a department charged with promoting American business. The proposal failed only after senators reached agreement that preserved a separate Commission. Congress disagreed with abolition, and the budget was cut instead. Survival came with structural losses under the 1981 amendments, whose rulemaking consequences are treated in full in the standards account above. The 1981 amendments eliminated the Product Safety Advisory Council, the National Advisory Committee for the Flammable Fabrics Act, and the technical advisory committee authorized under the Poison Prevention Packaging Act, removing the standing bodies through which outside expertise and public constituencies had held a formal seat in agency deliberations. The amendments established a congressional veto over consumer product safety rules promulgated under the three statutes the Commission administered, inserting a legislative check that reflected distrust of agency discretion in the same statute that kept the agency alive. Authorization after 1981 proceeded in short and contested increments. In 1986 Congress restricted funds for commissioners' salaries, and from that year forward the Commission functioned with three commissioners rather than five, a reduction accomplished through appropriations language rather than through a redesign of the organic statute. The last reauthorization before 2008 arrived in 1990 and covered only two years, after which the agency operated for seventeen years on appropriations without a renewed authorization.

The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 reversed the direction of travel. Enacted as Public Law 110-314 and signed by President George W. Bush on August 14, 2008, the Act responded to the recall wave of 2007, in which lead paint on toys manufactured in China exposed an agency with one employee assigned to testing toys. The House of Representatives had approved the underlying bill, H.R. 4040, sponsored by Representative Bobby Rush of Illinois, by a vote of 407 to 0 on December 19, 2007, and the Senate approved the measure by a vote of 79 to 13 on March 6, 2008. Title I rebuilt the law of children's products, defined as products designed or intended primarily for children aged twelve years or younger. Section 101 treated any children's product containing lead above statutory limits as a banned hazardous substance, with total lead content falling from 600 parts per million to 300 parts per million and then to 100 parts per million on August 14, 2011, where technological feasibility permitted. The limit for lead in paint fell to 90 parts per million. Section 108 prohibited children's toys and child care articles containing more than 0.1 percent, or 1,000 parts per million, of the phthalates DEHP, DBP, or BBP, and imposed an interim prohibition at the same concentration for DINP, DIDP, and DnOP in toys that could be placed in a child's mouth and in child care articles, pending study by a Chronic Hazard Advisory Panel.

The testing architecture of the 2008 Act mattered as much as the substance limits. Section 102 required manufacturers and importers of children's products to have production samples tested by an independent laboratory accepted by the Commission and to issue a Children's Product Certificate based on passing results, converting conformity from a private assurance into a documented duty that customs officials and retailers could demand. Section 103 required permanent tracking labels on children's products and packaging, to the extent practicable, identifying the manufacturer or private labeler, the production location, the date of manufacture, and cohort information such as a batch or run number, so that a recall could reach a defined population of goods. Section 104 directed mandatory standards for durable infant and toddler products, including cribs, play yards, bath seats, and walkers, on a rolling schedule tied to voluntary standards maintained by ASTM International. Section 105 made the ASTM F963 toy safety standard mandatory, transforming a voluntary specification into federal law that updated as ASTM revised the specification under Commission oversight. Taken together, the provisions moved children's products closer to a certification regime than any other part of the consumer product universe had approached, while preserving the distinction between testing by accredited private laboratories and approval by the government itself.

Enforcement and institutional provisions completed the reconstruction. The Act raised the maximum civil penalty to 100,000 dollars for each violation and 15 million dollars for any related series of violations, replacing a cap of 1,825,000 dollars for a related series, and criminal exposure expanded to include asset forfeiture and imprisonment of up to five years for knowing and willful violations. Authorization levels rose from 80 million dollars in 2008 toward 136 million dollars in 2014, and the Act directed the Commission to reach at least 500 full-time personnel by October 1, 2013, with a portion assigned to ports of entry and to inspection of overseas manufacturing facilities. The Act restored a path back to five commissioners by repealing the funding limitation that had held the Commission at three, and it supplied a temporary two-member quorum rule after the quorum collapse of 2006 and 2007, when the departure of Chairman Hal Stratton left an agency unable, after a six-month grace period expired, to authorize suits or mandatory recall proceedings. Section 219 protected whistleblowers employed by manufacturers, importers, distributors, and retailers who provided information about product hazards, with complaints routed to the Occupational Safety and Health Administration under a 180-day filing period. Section 212 ordered creation of a publicly accessible, searchable database of reports of harm, and the Commission launched that database as SaferProducts.gov on March 11, 2011, publishing reports after notice to the named manufacturer and an opportunity to claim that a report contained materially inaccurate or confidential information.

Litigation defined the boundaries within which the rebuilt statute operated, beginning long before 2008. In Aqua Slide 'N' Dive Corp. v. Consumer Product Safety Commission, 569 F.2d 831, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit in 1978, the court reviewed the Commission's swimming pool slide standard, published on January 19, 1976, under the substantial evidence test that Section 11 of the Act prescribed for judicial review. The standard had addressed an injury pattern in which swimmers entering head first struck the bottom of a pool and suffered quadriplegia or paraplegia, together with leg fractures and collisions with other swimmers. The Fifth Circuit set aside portions of the standard, holding that the Commission had failed to supply substantial evidence for requirements governing warning signs, since the agency had never tested the signs and had offered little evidence about whether graphic depictions of paralysis risk would deter purchases so sharply as to reduce product availability, a factor the statute required the Commission to weigh. Slide sales had fallen by 42 percent from 1973 to 1974, and the record attributed part of that decline to uncertainty about the coming standard. The Commission published revisions reflecting the decision on December 18, 1978, and the case stood as a warning that the Act demanded an evidentiary record built through testing and quantified findings. The rulemaking caution that agency officials lamented in the 1980s and 1990s owed part of its origin to the knowledge that an inadequately supported finding invited vacatur after years of proceedings.

The database generated the defining litigation of the following decade. In Company Doe v. Tenenbaum, decided by the United States District Court for the District of Maryland in October 2012, a manufacturer sued under a pseudonym to block publication of a report of harm submitted by a local government agency and linking a company product to an infant death. The district court granted summary judgment to the company, found the decision to publish arbitrary and capricious, and permanently enjoined publication, concluding that the report failed to describe a harm related to use of the product and remained materially inaccurate through successive agency redactions. The Commission chose not to appeal the merits. Public Citizen, the Consumer Federation of America, and Consumers Union intervened to challenge the sealing of the case, and a panel of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit held in April 2014 that the sealing order violated the public right of access under the First Amendment and remanded with instructions to unseal the case in its entirety. The combined outcome preserved a manufacturer's right to defeat a materially inaccurate report while denying anonymity. Congressional correction supplied a second form of review. After libraries, thrift stores, and makers of youth all-terrain vehicles documented that lead limits and testing duties reached ordinary books and vehicle components whose lead content posed no ingestion pathway for a child, Congress enacted H.R. 2715 as Public Law 112-28 on August 12, 2011, exempting ordinary books and paper-based printed materials, granting relief for small batch manufacturers, and directing the Commission to seek public comment on reducing third-party testing burdens.

Measured effects resisted any single summary figure, and the most defensible account proceeded hazard by hazard from named sources over named periods. The Commission's fiscal year 1999 annual report stated that agency work had contributed significantly to a 30 percent decline in the rates of death and injury related to consumer products since the agency began operating, while recording an average of 22,000 deaths and more than 29 million injuries per year associated with consumer products at the close of the 1990s. The founding comparison supplied the scale of the original problem, since early Commission materials had estimated roughly 20 million injuries and approximately 30,000 deaths per year at the start of the 1970s. Electrical hazards produced one of the clearest trend lines. The Electrical Safety Foundation International reported that electrocutions fell by 83 percent after the introduction of ground fault circuit interrupters in 1973, and the Commission estimated that ground fault protection had prevented 50 percent of home electrocutions. Crib hazards produced a second documented decline. Consumer Reports, summarizing Commission data, reported that crib deaths decreased by nearly 80 percent from 1973 to 2018, a period spanning the early crib standards, the ban on traditional drop-side cribs, and the mandatory durable nursery product standards that Section 104 required. A Pacific Legal Foundation analysis of Commission nursery product reports added a necessary qualification, finding that the share of crib-related deaths attributed to product defects fell from 38 percent in the period from 2004 to 2006 to 9 percent in the period from 2019 to 2021, while deaths associated with cluttered sleep environments and hazardous surroundings came to dominate the residual toll. Attribution shifted as design defects receded.

Contested readings of the same record divided along institutional lines, and each reading arrived with a named author. Rosario Palmieri, vice president of the National Association of Manufacturers, warned during the database debate that publication of unverified reports would expose companies to inaccurate and malicious submissions by competitors and aggrieved consumers, a concern the Consumer Specialty Products Association extended by arguing that the database scope invited publication of consumer dissatisfaction rather than safety information. Representative Mike Pompeo of Kansas carried the objection into appropriations, proposing to withhold funding for the database on the ground that the structure made false information too easy to upload. Compliance cost arguments acquired a concrete illustration from the American Library Association, whose spokeswoman reported in 2009 that testing a single book for lead would cost about 300 dollars, a figure librarians used to demonstrate that a children's product definition drawn around age swept in objects whose risk profile Congress had never examined. Small manufacturers of handmade toys and children's apparel pressed a parallel case through the 2011 amendment campaign, documenting testing charges that consumed the margin on short production runs and reporting product withdrawals where a firm discontinued a line rather than finance laboratory certification for each variation. Consumer advocates answered with an institutional argument. Rachel Weintraub of the Consumer Federation of America testified that Commission staffing had fallen from 786 full-time employees in 1974 and a high of 978 in 1980 toward roughly 420 full-time positions in 2007, describing the trajectory as death by a thousand cuts, and Janell Mayo Duncan of Consumers Union testified that inadequate funding represented the single greatest challenge inhibiting fulfillment of the Commission's mission. Kids In Danger and the Consumer Federation of America, in an April 2012 study of SaferProducts.gov covering the first ten months of operation, reported 6,080 reports filed, with consumers supplying 97 percent of reports and manufacturers exercising the right to comment on 53 percent of reports, evidence the organizations offered against predictions that the database would consist mainly of fabricated submissions.

Recall performance supplied the sharpest point of disagreement. Consumer advocates at Kids In Danger documented across repeated reports that only a small fraction of recalled children's products reached repair, replacement, or destruction, with most units remaining in homes after announcement publicity faded, and the organization attributed the gap to a system dependent on voluntary corrective action plans negotiated firm by firm. Agency officials answered that the Fast Track program, begun in the 1990s, allowed a firm reporting a hazard to begin a recall within twenty business days without a preliminary hazard determination, trading formal compulsion for speed where a manufacturer preferred cooperation to litigation. Both accounts described the same statutory fact. Section 15 empowered the Commission, after notice and an opportunity for a hearing, to order repair, replacement, refund, or public notice for a product presenting a substantial product hazard, and Section 12 permitted suit against an imminently hazardous product, yet neither provision gave the agency the summary mandatory recall authority over ordinary products that the Food and Drug Administration received for food in 2011. A contested recall therefore required process, and process consumed months during which distribution continued unless a firm agreed to act. The 2008 Act strengthened the surrounding leverage through higher penalties, a prohibition on sale of recalled products, and authority for state attorneys general to seek injunctive relief, while leaving the central negotiation structure intact.

The honest limits of the statute followed from design choices that amendments adjusted without reversing. The resource arithmetic never matched the jurisdictional claim. Congressional Research Service reporting recorded a fiscal year 2007 budget of 62.27 million dollars and a fiscal year 2008 appropriation of 80 million dollars, against responsibility for more than 15,000 types of consumer products, and the Center for Public Integrity reported in 2008 that the agency employed about 90 field investigators, with about 15 staff assigned to ports of entry and one person responsible for toy testing at the moment the imported toy recalls of 2007 reached their peak. Even after the 2008 Act raised authorizations and set a floor of 500 personnel, the ratio of inspectors to product categories and import volume left sampling as the only available method. The second limit concerned information flow. Section 6(b), preserved through every amendment cycle in modified form, required the Commission to give a manufacturer notice and an opportunity to comment before disclosing information identifying a product, a procedure that litigation showed could consume years when a firm contested a single report. The third limit concerned scope. Tobacco, firearms ammunition, pesticides, motor vehicles, food, drugs, and cosmetics sat outside Commission jurisdiction by statute, so the phrase consumer product in the Act never described the whole material environment of an American household. The statute after five decades of amendment stood as a durable post-market enforcement system with a transformed children's product regime layered onto an original structure of negotiated recalls, evidentiary rulemaking, and chronic resource scarcity, and the distance between the transformation and the structure explained why assessments diverged depending on whether the assessor began with a crib, a toy, or a port of entry.

## The pillar and its cluster

The honest assessment of the Consumer Product Safety Act begins with a recognition that the statute solved the problem Congress diagnosed and created fresh problems in the solving. The diagnosis was fragmentation: hazards sorted by accident of category, authority buried inside departments with larger missions, and no office charged with counting injuries across the consumer marketplace. Against that record, consolidation worked. One independent commission inherited four older statutes, administered five bodies of law from a single address, built a national injury surveillance system, and acquired power to set standards, ban product classes, compel reports, and order remedies for goods already in homes. The injury ledger that justified creation became, after creation, a permanent instrument of government rather than a one time argument, and that institutional permanence stands as the clearest achievement of the design.

The limits were written into the same text. A post market system detects harm after distribution, so the first injuries in any pattern function as the evidence that triggers correction, and dependence on firm reporting means the agency sees many hazards first through the records of the party with the strongest incentive to read those records narrowly. Penalty law for late reporting supplied a counterweight, and the major late reporting settlements showed the counterweight in use, yet enforcement after delay cannot restore the interval during which a defective product sold without interruption. The rulemaking procedure produced a parallel tension. Findings on necessity, cost, burden, and voluntary alternatives disciplined agency judgment and gave courts a record to review, and the same findings consumed years, pushed the commission toward consensus committees it did not control, and left the mandatory rule as the instrument of last resort rather than the centerpiece that early supporters imagined. Section 6(b) completed the pattern, protecting firms against unverified official identification at the price of delayed warning in contested cases.

Later amendment confirmed the architecture by rebuilding around it instead of through it. The children's product regime of 2008 approached certification more closely than any other part of the system, through third party testing, certificates, tracking labels, and incorporated voluntary standards, while leaving the general product universe under the older post market settlement. Recall law kept its negotiated character, with speed purchased through cooperation and formal orders reserved for firms that refused to agree. Resource arithmetic pressed on every function, because a jurisdiction described in ranges from roughly 10,000 to more than 15,000 product types rested on appropriations and staffing that never matched the breadth of the claim. The statute therefore rewarded a particular kind of reading, one that followed the text section by section, asked which of the five statutes governed a given product, and treated procedure not as decoration on power but as a substantive part of what Congress granted and withheld.

Placed inside the wider literature of the site, the act rewards study beside [the history of United States consumer protection law](/2019/02/15/us-consumer-protection-law-history/), which traces the longer movement from caveat emptor through agency government, and beside [the federal and state consumer protection comparison](/2019/02/01/federal-vs-state-consumer-protection/), which examines how national standards and state liability divided the work of protection between levels of government. Serious study of a statute with five interlocking texts, a half century of amendment, and a dense case record benefits from a single organised place for the material, and readers assembling that record can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html). The pillar holds because Congress built an institution able to learn from injury data and to survive political cycles that cut its budget and narrowed its authority. The cluster of weaknesses around the pillar holds for the same reason: each weakness was a choice, recorded in statutory language, defended by named interests, and preserved by legislatures that revised the clocks and the penalties while leaving the underlying bargain intact.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Consumer Product Safety Act create?

The act created the United States Consumer Product Safety Commission, an independent regulatory commission of five commissioners appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate. Section 4 of the act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2053, gave the commission staggered terms, protection against removal except for neglect of duty or malfeasance in office, and a cap barring more than three commissioners from a single political party. The commission began operations on May 14, 1973, under Chairman Richard O. Simpson, and received both the new authority of the 1972 act and the transferred functions of four older product safety statutes.

### Q: Which president signed the Consumer Product Safety Act?

President Richard Nixon signed the Consumer Product Safety Act on October 27, 1972, at the close of the 92nd Congress, and described the measure at signing as the most significant consumer protection legislation passed by that Congress. The Senate bill, S. 3419, had passed the Senate in June 1972 and the House in September 1972 after conference work reconciled differences over the design of the new agency and the procedures for mandatory standards. The statute took effect on December 26, 1972, was enacted as Public Law 92-573, printed at 86 Statutes at Large 1207, and was codified at title 15, United States Code, sections 2051 and following.

### Q: What products are excluded from the Consumer Product Safety Act?

Section 3 of the act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2052, defined consumer product broadly and then carved out categories assigned to other federal regimes. The exclusions covered tobacco and tobacco products, motor vehicles and motor vehicle equipment, pesticides, firearms and ammunition reached through a cross reference to the manufacturers excise tax in section 4181 of the Internal Revenue Code, aircraft and related appliances, boats subject to Coast Guard regulation, and drugs, devices, cosmetics, and food as defined in the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Authority over the excluded categories rested with regulators including the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, the Environmental Protection Agency, the Federal Aviation Administration, and the Food and Drug Administration.

### Q: What is section 6(b) of the Consumer Product Safety Act?

Section 6(b), codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2055(b), conditioned public disclosure of information that identified a manufacturer or private labeler on a sequence of safeguards. The Commission had to notify each affected firm in advance, provide a summary of the material, allow a reasonable opportunity to comment, and take reasonable steps to assure that the information was accurate, that disclosure was fair in the circumstances, and that disclosure was reasonably related to the purposes of the act. As enacted in 1972 the notice period was 30 days, and the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 reduced the period to 15 days. A firm also gained a right to sue in federal district court to enjoin a disclosure that failed the statutory steps.

### Q: When must a company report a hazard under the Consumer Product Safety Act?

Section 15(b), codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2064(b), required every manufacturer, importer, distributor, and retailer to inform the Commission immediately upon obtaining information that reasonably supported a conclusion that a product failed to comply with an applicable rule, contained a defect that could create a substantial product hazard, or created an unreasonable risk of serious injury or death. The regulation at 16 C.F.R. Part 1115 translated immediately into a report within 24 hours of obtaining reportable information. A firm uncertain about reportability could investigate in a reasonably expeditious manner, and section 1115.14(d) set that investigation window at 10 days unless the firm demonstrated that a longer period was reasonable.

### Q: Why does the Consumer Product Safety Act prefer voluntary standards?

The preference rested on a judgment Congress wrote into the statute through the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, which amended section 7 at 15 U.S.C. section 2056. The amended text directed the Commission to rely upon voluntary consumer product safety standards rather than promulgate a mandatory standard whenever compliance with a voluntary standard would eliminate or adequately reduce the risk of injury and substantial compliance with that standard was likely. A 1990 amendment added a duty to monitor compliance with voluntary standards on which the Commission had relied. The preference reflected the cost of section 9 rulemaking, with its advance notices, regulatory analyses, and multiple findings exposed to substantial evidence review, and it redirected agency staff toward consensus committees run by bodies such as ASTM International and Underwriters Laboratories.

### Q: How many statutes does the Consumer Product Safety Act commission administer?

The commission administered five statutes as distinct bodies of law sharing a single administrator. The portfolio consisted of the Consumer Product Safety Act itself and four transferred laws: the Federal Hazardous Substances Act, the Flammable Fabrics Act, the Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970, and the Refrigerator Safety Act of 1956. Section 30 of the 1972 act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2079, transferred functions under the older statutes from the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, the Department of Commerce, and the Federal Trade Commission. Each statute kept separate definitions, prohibitions, rulemaking procedures, and remedies, so identifying the governing statute was the first task in any product safety matter.

### Q: What did the 2008 amendments add to the Consumer Product Safety Act?

The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008, Public Law 110-314, rebuilt the law of children's products and enlarged the agency. The amendments set declining limits on total lead content in children's products, cut the lead paint limit to 90 parts per million, restricted specified phthalates in toys and child care articles, required third party testing by accepted laboratories with Children's Product Certificates, mandated tracking labels, converted the ASTM F963 toy safety standard into a mandatory standard, and directed mandatory standards for durable infant and toddler products. The same law raised civil penalty caps to 100,000 dollars per violation and 15 million dollars for a related series, ordered creation of the SaferProducts.gov database, added whistleblower protection, and shortened the section 6(b) notice period from 30 days to 15 days.

### Q: Does the Consumer Product Safety Commission test products before they are sold?

For ordinary consumer products, the answer is no, because the act created a post market system rather than a premarket approval regime. Manufacturers could sell without Commission approval, advance certification, or a government safety license, and the Commission instead set standards, gathered injury data through hospital surveillance, inspected selected imports at ports of entry, and pursued recalls or bans after hazards emerged in use. The 2008 amendments narrowed the gap for children's products by requiring testing by independent laboratories accepted by the Commission and a Children's Product Certificate based on passing results, yet that certificate attested that required testing had occurred rather than recording a discretionary government judgment approving the product for commerce.

### Q: Can the Consumer Product Safety Commission order a mandatory recall?

Yes, through the contested procedure of section 15 rather than through summary authority. Under sections 15(c) and 15(d), codified at 15 U.S.C. sections 2064(c) and 2064(d), the Commission could, after notice and an opportunity for a hearing, order a manufacturer, distributor, or retailer to give public notice of a substantial product hazard and to repair the product, replace the product with a like or equivalent product, or refund the purchase price. Section 12 supplied a separate judicial route against an imminently hazardous product, allowing a district court to order seizure, notice, recall, repair, replacement, or refund. In practice, nearly all recalls proceeded as negotiated voluntary corrections, because adjudicated orders demanded staff time, a hearing record, and exposure to judicial review.

### Q: What is a substantial product hazard under the Consumer Product Safety Act?

Section 15(a), codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2064(a), defined a substantial product hazard through two branches. The first branch covered a failure to comply with an applicable consumer product safety rule where the failure created a substantial risk of injury to the public. The second branch covered a product defect that, because of the pattern of the defect, the number of defective products distributed in commerce, the severity of the risk, or otherwise, created a substantial risk of injury to the public. A defect could arise from a design shared by every unit, from a manufacturing error confined to one production lot, or from inadequate warnings or instructions, and the statutory test asked about risk rather than about harm already completed.

### Q: What penalties apply for failing to report a hazard under the Consumer Product Safety Act?

Section 19, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2068, listed failure to furnish information required by section 15(b) among the prohibited acts under the act. Section 20, at 15 U.S.C. section 2069, authorized civil penalties against a person who knowingly violated a prohibited act, with knowingly defined to include knowledge a reasonable person in the same circumstances would have possessed through due care. Section 21, at 15 U.S.C. section 2070, authorized criminal penalties for a knowing and willful violation. The enforcement record included a 4 million dollar civil penalty against Graco in 2005, a 3.4 million dollar penalty against Office Depot in 2015, and a 15.45 million dollar penalty against the Gree companies in 2016, each grounded in late or missing hazard reports.

### Q: How is the Consumer Product Safety Commission structured and who leads it?

The commission consisted of five commissioners appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, with the President designating one commissioner as chairman. Commissioners served seven year terms, with the founding terms staggered at three, four, five, six, and seven years from October 27, 1972, and removal was limited to neglect of duty or malfeasance in office. No more than three commissioners could belong to a single political party. Richard O. Simpson, formerly Assistant Secretary for Science and Technology at the Department of Commerce, served as the first chairman after the commission began operations on May 14, 1973, and Barbara H. Franklin served as the first vice chairman, elected by the commissioners.

### Q: What is SaferProducts.gov and how does it relate to the Consumer Product Safety Act?

SaferProducts.gov was the publicly searchable database of reports of harm that section 212 of the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2055a, directed the Commission to establish. Launched in March 2011, the database published reports submitted by consumers, health care professionals, public safety entities, and other permitted submitters, alongside comments from the manufacturer named in a report. A report spoke in the voice of the submitter, so analyses of the statute treated the database as sitting outside the scope of section 6(b), while additional Commission information entered only consistently with sections 6(a) and 6(b). Manufacturers received transmission of a report within 5 business days and publication followed no later than the 10th business day after transmission, subject to claims of material inaccuracy.

### Q: Does the Consumer Product Safety Act preempt state product safety laws?

Section 26, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2075, drew a qualified preemption line. Where a federal consumer product safety standard was in effect for a risk of injury associated with a product, a state or political subdivision could not maintain a requirement on performance, composition, contents, design, finish, construction, packaging, or labeling aimed at the same risk unless the state requirement was identical to the federal requirement. The commission could exempt a stricter state requirement that imposed a higher degree of protection without unduly burdening interstate commerce, and states retained authority over risks no federal standard addressed. Section 25 separately preserved common law and state law liability in damages actions, so compliance with a federal rule functioned as a floor rather than as a shield against private claims.

### Q: What is an imminent hazard under the Consumer Product Safety Act?

Section 12, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2061, defined an imminently hazardous consumer product as a consumer product that presented an imminent and unreasonable risk of death, serious illness, or severe personal injury. The definition set a higher and narrower threshold than the substantial product hazard standard of section 15, and the remedy ran through a federal district court rather than through an administrative hearing. The Commission could sue the product itself for seizure, the firms handling the product, or both, and a court that declared a product imminently hazardous could order notification of purchasers, public notice, recall, repair, replacement, or refund, with seizure and condemnation available against the product. Use of section 12 remained rare, because proof in court was harder and the section 15 process offered overlapping relief.

### Q: How does the Consumer Product Safety Act treat children's products differently?

After the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008, children's products, defined as products designed or intended primarily for children aged twelve years or younger, carried duties that the general product universe never bore. Section 101 treated a children's product containing lead above statutory limits as a banned hazardous substance, with total lead content limits falling to 100 parts per million where technological feasibility permitted and a separate 90 parts per million limit for lead paint. Section 102 required testing by an independent laboratory accepted by the Commission and issuance of a Children's Product Certificate, section 103 required tracking labels identifying production information, section 104 directed mandatory standards for durable infant and toddler products, and section 106 made the ASTM F963 toy safety standard mandatory by operation of law.

### Q: What role do voluntary standards organizations play under the Consumer Product Safety Act?

Voluntary standards organizations supplied much of the engineering content of the regulatory system after the 1981 amendments directed the Commission to rely on adequate voluntary standards with likely substantial compliance. ASTM International, through Committee F15 on Consumer Products, developed consensus specifications for juvenile products, toys, and furniture hazards, while Underwriters Laboratories combined standards development with testing and certification whose marks retailers and insurers demanded, and the American National Standards Institute accredited developers and audited adherence to due process, openness, balance, and consensus. Commission engineers and health scientists sat on consensus committees, supplied injury data from the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System, proposed test methods, and pressed committees to tighten weak provisions, exercising influence without the record burden of section 9 rulemaking.

### Q: What is the difference between a safety standard and a ban under the Consumer Product Safety Act?

A consumer product safety standard under section 7, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2056, set prospective, class wide requirements expressed in terms of performance or of clear and adequate warnings and instructions, on the premise that a product could be made acceptably safe and sold in compliant form. A ban under section 8, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2057, rested on the opposite conclusion: the Commission declared a product a banned hazardous product by rule where the product presented an unreasonable risk of injury and no feasible consumer product safety standard would adequately protect the public. Both instruments required the section 9 procedure and the findings of 15 U.S.C. section 2058(f)(3), including reasonable necessity, public interest, a reasonable relationship between benefits and costs, and the least burdensome adequate requirement.

### Q: Is the Consumer Product Safety Act still in force and where is it codified?

The act remained in force as amended and was codified at title 15, United States Code, sections 2051 and following, with individual provisions running from the findings and purposes at section 2051 through the definitions, standards, reporting, disclosure, penalty, and preemption sections addressed across the chapter. Major amendment arrived through the Consumer Product Safety Commission Improvements Act of 1976, the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 1990, and the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008, together with a corrective law in 2011 at Public Law 112-28 that exempted ordinary books and relieved small batch manufacturers. Each amendment cycle revised clocks, penalties, and procedures while preserving the core structure of an independent commission administering five statutes on a post market model.
