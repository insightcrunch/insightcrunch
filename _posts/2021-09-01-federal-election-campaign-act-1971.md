The Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971 is the statute around which federal campaign finance law is organized, and the question at the center of the statute is how money raised and spent to elect candidates for federal office is to be disclosed, limited, and policed. The enacted text supplies a formal identity of unusual precision. The short title is the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, Public Law 92-225, printed at 86 Stat. 3. President Richard Nixon signed the enacted bill, S. 382 of the 92nd Congress, on February 7, 1972. The title therefore carries a year earlier than the signature, and precision on that point matters, because the act named for 1971 became law in 1972 and took effect on April 7, 1972.

![The Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, the statute that rebuilt federal campaign finance law around disclosure, limits, public financing, and an enforcement commission](/assets/images/blog/blog-56.webp)

The act signed in 1972 was principally a disclosure statute with ceilings on media spending. Political committees registered and reported, contributions above stated thresholds entered a public record, and spending on communications media stood under a formula ceiling. The act imposed no general contribution limits on individuals, created no enforcement commission, and created no public financing. The architecture associated with the name arrived with the Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1974, Public Law 93-443, printed at 88 Stat. 1263 and signed on October 15, 1974, by President Gerald Ford. The amendments added contribution limits, expenditure limits, a commission, and a completed system of presidential public financing, and the limits provisions took effect on January 1, 1975. Attribution that credits the 1971 enactment with the whole structure misstates the sequence that the statute book records.

The lineage divides into distinct clusters, each with a separate date and a separate legal character. The predecessor regime under the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1925 supplies the background: disclosure without an administrator, spending limits reaching only political committees active in two or more states, and enforcement left to Congress. The 1971 act supplies the disclosure foundation and the media ceilings. The 1974 rewrite supplies the limits in four categories and places those limits, at enactment, in title 18 of the United States Code as section 608 and following. The Commission cluster concerns administration: six voting members, a ceiling of three members from one political party, and four affirmative votes at the decisive enforcement stages. The public financing cluster runs through a separate channel, because the tax checkoff and the Presidential Election Campaign Fund were created by the Revenue Act of 1971, Public Law 92-178, and the 1974 amendments built the payment system on that fund.

Two later clusters test the structure from opposite directions. In Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), the Supreme Court sustained contribution limits, disclosure, and public financing, and struck candidate expenditure ceilings, the ceiling on independent expenditures, and the limits on candidate personal funds. The soft money cluster begins with the amendments named for 1979, signed on January 8, 1980, by President Jimmy Carter, which placed grassroots and party building spending by state and local party committees outside the definitions of contribution and expenditure. The enforcement record closes the sequence, measured in deadlocked votes, civil penalties, and a loss of quorum between August 2019 and December 2020. One question runs through the Commission cluster without a settled answer. One reading treats the even partisan division, joined to the four vote requirement, as a design that produces deadlock on contested questions. A competing reading treats the same division as a protection against enforcement controlled by one party against the other. The record supports description of the structure and of the voting outcomes, and the choice between the two readings is contested ground that description alone does not settle.

## A Statute Known by a Name It Only Partly Earned

The Federal Election Campaign Act occupies a singular position in federal law because the name functions as a citation for an entire system. Lawyers, candidates, treasurers, and judges invoke the name to describe contribution limits, an enforcement commission, public financing for presidential campaigns, and a dense body of disclosure rules, and the invocation carries an assumption that a single enactment produced the whole structure. That assumption is the point of departure for close reading. The statute enacted under that name at the opening of the nineteen seventies was principally a disclosure statute with ceilings on media spending. The contribution limits, the commission, and the public financing program entered the law through a substantial rewrite three years after the year carried in the short title. A constitutional challenge then removed part of that rewritten structure within two years. A later amendment, presented as a set of adjustments to reporting and party activity, redirected the flow of money in ways that reshaped practice far beyond the modest description attached to the change. The name therefore describes a lineage rather than a single legislative act, and the lineage has to be separated into its layers before any provision can be understood on accurate terms.

The separation matters because attribution determines interpretation. A disclosure rule enacted in the earlier statute rests on a different legislative judgment from a contribution limit enacted in the rewrite, even when both provisions sit side by side in the same title of the United States Code and travel under the same short title in ordinary citation. A court decision striking one element of the rewrite says nothing direct about the validity of an element enacted earlier and left untouched. An enforcement design created in the middle of the decade cannot be explained by intentions expressed at the start of the decade, because a different Congress built the enforcement institution for reasons of its own. Treating the whole structure as the product of one enactment collapses those distinctions and produces a recurring error in public discussion of campaign finance law, in which the operative architecture is credited to a statute that contained none of its three most consequential components. The enacted text imposed no general contribution limits on individuals, created no enforcement commission, and created no public financing. Those absences are not gaps in the historical record. Those absences are the historical record, and any account that begins from the name rather than from the sequence of enactments inherits the error at the first step.

The formal identity of the statute can be stated with precision, and two of its elements mislead a casual reader. The short title is the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971. The enactment is Public Law 92-225, printed at 86 Stat. 3, and signed on February 7, 1972, by President Richard Nixon. The enacted bill was S. 382 of the 92nd Congress. As enacted, the statute was codified at title 2, United States Code, sections 431 and following. In 2014 the provisions were editorially reclassified to title 52, United States Code, sections 30101 and following, a change in codification placement rather than a change in enacted substance. The short title carries the year 1971 although the signature fell in 1972, and the discrepancy is more than a curiosity of drafting. The title year records the Congress year in which the legislation moved, while the signature date records the moment the bill became law, and operative provisions run from enactment and effectiveness rather than from the year printed in a title. A reader who dates the statute to 1971 misplaces the enactment by a year and compresses the short interval between the original statute and the rewrite that displaced so much of its content.

The cluster of legislation gathered around the name extends across the decade, and the pillar that begins with the earlier statute maps four subjects in sequence. The first subject is amendment. The Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1974, Public Law 93-443, printed at 88 Stat. 1263 and signed on October 15, 1974, by President Gerald Ford, substantially rewrote the earlier statute, with the limits provisions taking effect on January 1, 1975. The Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1976, Public Law 94-283, signed on May 11, 1976, by President Ford, rebuilt portions of the structure after the constitutional challenge. The Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1979, Public Law 96-187, printed at 93 Stat. 1339 and signed on January 8, 1980, by President Jimmy Carter, carried the name of the Congress year of passage although the signature fell in the following January. The second subject is the court. A structure that limits contributions and expenditures, compels disclosure, and distributes public money invites constitutional scrutiny of a particular intensity, and the Supreme Court's treatment of the rewritten statute fixed the boundaries inside which later Congresses legislated. The third subject is enforcement design. A disclosure statute without an administrator had already demonstrated, across nearly half a century under the predecessor regime, that reporting duties without an institution charged with receiving, publishing, and acting on reports decay into formality. The fourth subject is the money channel. Each change in the rules of contribution, expenditure, and party finance altered the route by which money reached campaigns, and the amendment at the end of the decade altered that route again, with consequences measured in the cycles that followed rather than in the debate that accompanied passage.

Understanding the earlier statute on accurate terms requires sustained attention to the regime that preceded the enactment, because the earlier statute was drafted against a body of law that had governed federal campaign finance for nearly half a century and had failed in ways that were specific, documented, and structural. The predecessor was the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1925, enacted on February 28, 1925. The 1925 statute consolidated the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1910, known as the Publicity Act, and its 1911 amendments. The consolidation gathered the earlier disclosure legislation into a single framework and carried that framework forward with revisions, and the resulting statute remained the basic federal campaign finance law from 1925 until the enactment of the statute signed in February 1972. Longevity, in that record, did not correspond to effectiveness. The 1925 statute survived because replacement required Congress to legislate against the financial practices of congressional campaigns themselves, and the institution charged with enforcement of the statute was Congress. No competing federal framework existed, and the statute's defects accumulated in plain view of the institution least inclined to correct defects that structured the financing of congressional elections.

The prompt for the 1925 revision was scandal. The Teapot Dome scandal supplied the political pressure under which Congress consolidated and revised the earlier disclosure laws. Disclosure legislation in the federal system has tended to advance when a scandal raises the political cost of inaction above the political cost of regulating campaign practice. The 1910 and 1911 legislation had established the principle that campaign receipts and expenditures in federal elections were a proper subject of public reporting. The 1925 consolidation preserved that principle, restated the machinery through which reporting was supposed to occur, and attached spending limits to the framework. The resulting statute therefore embodied two distinct legislative ideas. The first idea was publicity, the proposition that disclosure of the sources and uses of campaign money disciplines practice by exposing practice to voters, opponents, and newspapers. The second idea was limitation, the proposition that expenditure beyond a stated ceiling could be prohibited outright. The two ideas rested on different premises and carried different enforcement requirements. Publicity requires a recipient for reports, a duty to publish, and a realistic prospect that false or missing reports produce consequences. Limitation requires a definition of covered spending and an enforcer willing to proceed against candidates and committees. The 1925 statute stated both ideas and supplied the institutional support for neither, and the distance between the stated ideas and the supplied support explains the statute's career more completely than any account centered on the intentions of the enacting Congress.

The machinery of the 1925 statute can be described in a short compass, and the brevity of the description is itself informative. Political committees within the statute's coverage filed disclosure reports on a quarterly schedule. Contributions above 100 dollars were reported as items, which left smaller contributions outside the itemized record. Spending limits capped expenditures at 25,000 dollars for Senate campaigns and 5,000 dollars for House campaigns. The Senate figure rested on a formula of three cents per voter in the preceding election, a method that tied the ceiling to the size of the electorate rather than to the cost of communicating with that electorate. Coverage reached only political committees active in two or more states. Each element of that machinery carried a limitation inside its own terms. A quarterly schedule meant that money raised and spent in the closing weeks of a campaign entered the public record on a timetable set by the calendar of filings rather than by the calendar of voter decision. A ceiling stated as a formula produced a number with an appearance of calibration, while communication costs bore no stable relation to the count of voters in a preceding election. Coverage defined by activity in two or more states placed the national reporting framework around a category of committee that represented only part of the organizational structure through which campaign money moved.

The spending limits illustrate the structural weakness with particular clarity. A ceiling on campaign spending restrains practice only when the class of spenders subject to the ceiling matches the class of spenders active in campaigns, and only when spending by persons outside the candidate's formal organization can be counted against the ceiling or restrained directly. Under the summarized account of the statute's operation, the spending limits applied only to multi-state committees. A committee organized and active within a single state stood outside the limitation framework, however large its expenditures and however close its coordination with a campaign in practical terms. The limitation therefore governed the organizational form most visible at the national level and left adjacent forms untouched. A limitation that can be avoided by a choice of organizational form regulates the form rather than the spending. The figures fixed a legislative judgment about relative campaign scale in dollars at 1925 values, and the statute carried those values forward through decades in which the cost of reaching a statewide electorate changed beyond recognition. A ceiling that begins as a restraint can end as an irrelevance when the stated amount falls far below the ordinary cost of a competitive campaign, and a ceiling widely understood as irrelevant invites disregard for the reporting duties enacted alongside the ceiling.

The failures of the predecessor regime were catalogued in the published history of the Federal Election Commission, and the catalogue carries weight because the Commission administered the successor regime and described the predecessor from institutional knowledge of what administration requires. The Commission's summary identifies the central defect directly. No institution was charged with administering the law, so the provisions were largely ignored. A reporting system without an administrator has no office responsible for supplying forms, receiving filings in a single place, examining filings for completeness, publishing the disclosed information in usable form, or referring violations for action. Disclosure that is filed in scattered places and published in no systematic form informs few voters, and violations that no office detects produce no deterrent signal for committees deciding how much effort compliance deserves. The Commission's summary also records that enforcement was left to Congress, which rarely acted. Congressional enforcement placed the decision to proceed against a campaign in the hands of the institution whose members conducted campaigns under the statute, and proceedings after an election against a seated member or a defeated candidate served institutional interests weakly at best. The rarity of congressional action was therefore a predictable consequence of the assignment rather than an accident of particular Congresses.

A further defect struck at attribution, the element on which both disclosure and limitation depend. A candidate who claimed no knowledge of spending on the candidate's behalf was not liable for that spending. The rule severed the connection between benefit and responsibility. Spending undertaken to advance a candidacy could exceed the stated ceiling, escape the candidate's reported expenditures, and remain outside the candidate's liability, provided the candidate maintained an absence of knowledge about the spending. Knowledge of campaign finance activity became a source of legal exposure, while distance from that activity became a source of legal protection, and a candidate or campaign organization attentive to liability had reason to structure arrangements so that knowledge remained limited. Attribution rules determine whether a finance regime describes the campaign as voters experience the campaign or describes only the portion of the campaign conducted through the candidate's formal committee. A regime that attributes only formally authorized spending to the candidate records a fraction of the combined effect of all spending made to influence the result and presents the fraction as the account. The no-knowledge rule in the predecessor statute guaranteed that the recorded account and the experienced campaign diverged, and the divergence widened in any contest where substantial support arrived through channels outside the candidate's direct organization.

The coverage of the predecessor statute was narrowed further by constitutional adjudication, and the narrowing fell on the election that mattered most across a large part of the country. In Newberry v. United States, 256 U.S. 232 (1921), the Supreme Court held that the power of Congress over elections did not extend to party primaries or nominations. The holding removed primaries from federal reach at a time when, across the one-party South, the Democratic primary was the decisive election and the general election ratified the primary result as a formality. A federal disclosure and limitation regime addressed to general elections therefore addressed the less consequential stage of the electoral process in the region where one party's nomination settled the outcome. Candidates contested the nomination outside the federal framework and entered the general election with the decisive support already assembled. A later decision confirmed that the reporting framework retained force where federal authority did reach. In Burroughs v. United States, 290 U.S. 534 (1934), the Supreme Court upheld the reporting requirements for presidential elections. The pairing of the two decisions defines the operating space of the predecessor regime. Reporting duties for presidential elections survived constitutional challenge, while the primary elections that determined nominations across the one-party South remained outside federal coverage. The statute that resulted was constitutional in the applications the Court sustained and incomplete in the coverage the Court withheld, and both qualities persisted for the remaining life of the regime.

Prohibition formed a second layer of the predecessor regime, and prohibition shared the enforcement vacuum that disabled disclosure and limitation. The Tillman Act of 1907 had banned corporate contributions to federal candidates, two decades before the 1925 consolidation restated the disclosure framework. The ban on union contributions followed in the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947, four decades after the corporate ban and more than two decades after the consolidation. The two prohibitions therefore entered federal law at widely separated moments, through different legislative vehicles, in response to different political circumstances, and both prohibitions rested on the same institutional foundation as the disclosure statute enacted between the two dates. No administrator policed the boundary between a prohibited contribution and a permitted expenditure of organizational funds, and no office examined the flow of organizational money into federal campaigns as a routine function. The layered structure also complicated the legal landscape that any successor statute inherited. Disclosure duties, spending ceilings, a corporate contribution ban, and a union contribution ban coexisted without a unified definition of the committees and transactions covered, without a single recipient for reports, and without an enforcement body able to treat the layers as one system. Fragmentation of that kind penalizes straightforward compliance, because the obligations attached to a given transaction depend on characterization choices that no administrator exists to settle in advance.

By the late nineteen sixties, the argument that displaced the predecessor regime rested less on any single scandal than on cost. Aggregate campaign spending in 1968 exceeded 300 million dollars, on Brookings Institution figures as reported in the scholarly literature, and broadcast spending formed the fastest-growing component of that total. A spending total above 300 million dollars demonstrated that the financial scale of federal campaigns had moved far beyond the world in which ceilings of 25,000 dollars and 5,000 dollars described plausible campaign budgets. Broadcast spending as the fastest-growing component identified the mechanism of the change, as communication with a mass electorate through purchased broadcast time converted campaign competition into a contest over the purchase of access to voters. A disclosure regime without an administrator could not produce a reliable public account of spending at that scale. A limitation regime reaching only multi-state committees could not restrain spending at that scale. A prohibition regime without supervision could not police organizational money moving at that scale. The cost figures therefore functioned as a summary verdict on the predecessor structure rather than as a freestanding complaint about expensive campaigns.

The threshold of the enactment signed on February 7, 1972, is the point at which the predecessor account ends and the account of the named statute begins. The elements assembled at that threshold were a disclosure framework dating in principle to 1910 and in consolidated form to 1925, spending ceilings fixed at values that campaign costs had overtaken, a coverage boundary that excluded the decisive primary elections across the one-party South, attribution rules that protected a candidate who maintained an absence of knowledge, prohibitions on corporate and union contributions resting on no administering institution, and enforcement assigned to a Congress that rarely acted. Against those elements stood a campaign cost record for 1968 above 300 million dollars and a broadcast sector absorbing a growing share of campaign expenditure. The statute that crossed that threshold was principally a disclosure statute with ceilings on media spending, directed at the cost mechanism that the 1968 figures had identified. The limits, the commission, and the public financing that later attached to the name were absent from the enactment, and the absence defines the starting point from which the amendments of the decade proceeded. The name on the statute promised a comprehensive settlement of campaign finance. The text delivered a narrower instrument, and the distance between the name and the text is the distance the remainder of the lineage had to travel.

## The Act Congress Actually Passed First: Disclosure and Media Ceilings

The statute that reached the President in the opening weeks of 1972 was S. 382 of the 92nd Congress, enacted as Public Law 92-225 and printed at 86 Stat. 3. Its short title, the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, carried the year of its congressional passage, while its legal life began later. President Richard Nixon signed the bill on February 7, 1972, and its provisions took general effect on April 7, 1972. The obligations described in the statute bound no campaign until the spring of 1972. As enacted, the statute was codified at title 2, United States Code, sections 431 and following.

The bill was broader than the measure the President had refused two years earlier, and that refusal shaped the case for it. In 1970, President Nixon vetoed a bill that would have limited spending on broadcast communications alone, without reaching the other channels through which campaign money moved. When he signed S. 382 on February 7, 1972, his signing statement returned to that objection and restated it in his own terms, describing a broadcast-only ceiling as "one hole in a sieve." A ceiling placed on a single medium invited substitution into every medium left uncovered, so total spending could continue to climb while the regulated category merely shrank. The statute he signed rested on the opposite premise, pairing disclosure across the whole committee structure with ceilings addressed to media spending as a category.

The cost figures behind the legislation explained the attention given to media. Aggregate campaign spending in 1968 had exceeded 300 million dollars, on Brookings Institution figures as reported in the scholarly literature, and broadcast spending was the fastest growing component of that total. A Congress weighing a disclosure bill in that setting faced two distinct problems at once. Money entered through committees whose receipts and payments the older law had captured only partially, and left in large and rising amounts for advertising whose price no candidate could control alone. The 1971 act addressed the first problem with registration and reporting duties and the second with ceilings, and the balance between those two instruments defined its character. Disclosure was the load-bearing structure. The ceilings were attachments to it, confined to defined categories of spending and defined sums of personal money, alongside a deliberate decision to leave contribution size generally unregulated.

### What did the Federal Election Campaign Act itself require when it took effect?

When it took effect on April 7, 1972, the act principally required registration and regular public disclosure by political committees, itemized reporting of larger contributions, records of smaller ones, limits on media spending and on candidate and family money, and filing divided among three supervisory officers in Washington.

The disclosure architecture began with the political committee, because the statute organized its duties around committees rather than around individual donors. A political committee that expected its activity to reach 1,000 dollars was required to register, and registration converted an informal campaign organization into a reporting entity with a continuing legal identity under the statute. The threshold was low enough to capture the committees through which serious campaign money ordinarily moved, and crossing it was not a permission or a license. Registration was the doorway into a recurring cycle of reports that persisted for as long as the committee's activity continued. The design placed the duty on the organization best positioned to keep accounts, since a committee held the bank records, received the contributions, and authorized the expenditures that the reports were meant to reveal.

Below the registration threshold sat two further tiers of obligation, and together the three tiers gave the disclosure system its graduated shape. Committees were required to keep records of contributions beginning at 10 dollars, a figure low enough to make the underlying books of a campaign a legal artifact rather than a private convenience. Itemized public reporting attached at a higher point, above 100 dollars, so that contributions exceeding that sum were reported with the identifying detail that allowed a reader of the report to connect a named donor to a named amount. The distance between the two tiers was itself a policy choice. Record keeping protected the accounts against later dispute, while itemization protected the public character of the disclosure, and the higher second threshold kept published reports from being consumed by entries too small to inform any reader about the sources of a campaign's support.

The reporting calendar gave the filed information its rhythm. Committees filed quarterly reports during the year, and the quarterly cadence was supplemented by reports filed before and after an election, timed to the contest itself rather than to the calendar alone. A pre-election report placed a committee's financial position before the public at the point when the information could inform a vote before ballots were cast, and a post-election report closed the account after the counting, capturing late receipts and final settlement of debts. The quarterly reports between elections prevented the system from going dark during the long stretches in which committees raised money for contests far in the future. Disclosure was therefore not a single filing at the end of a campaign, but a repeating duty that followed the money through the entire cycle.

One prohibition sat inside the disclosure structure and gave its records a measure of protection against evasion. The statute banned contributions made in the name of another person. A contribution routed through an intermediary under a false name would have defeated both the record tier and the itemization tier, because the books would have shown a donor who had supplied nothing and concealed a donor who had supplied everything. The ban addressed the falsification directly rather than the amount, and it applied regardless of whether the sum involved would otherwise have been itemized. Disclosure worked only if the names disclosed were genuine, and Congress prohibited the one practice that made the record affirmatively false instead of merely incomplete.

The filed reports did not travel to a single destination, and the division of custody was one of the defining administrative choices of the statute. Reports were divided among three supervisory officers: the Clerk of the House of Representatives, the Secretary of the Senate, and the Comptroller General. Filings went to the officer connected with the contests supported, so House related filings accumulated in one office, Senate related filings in another, and the remaining national filings, including presidential ones, with the Comptroller General. The arrangement used officers who already existed and who already stood in a defined relation to the institutions whose elections were being reported. It required no new agency and no officer whose sole occupation was campaign finance administration. What it produced was a disclosure record held in three places under three custodies, each supervised by an officer whose campaign finance duties were an addition to a larger office.

The media ceilings formed the statute's second instrument, and section 104 carried the central formula. A candidate's spending on communications media was capped at the greater of two amounts, ten cents per voting-age resident of the area the candidate sought to represent, or 50,000 dollars. The per capita figure tied the ceiling to the size of the electorate, so a statewide candidate in a populous state received a larger allowance than a candidate for a single House district, while the 50,000 dollar floor protected candidates in small states and small districts from ceilings so low that ordinary advertising would have been impossible. Choosing the greater amount softened the provision where a rigid formula would have pressed hardest. Voting-age population was an imperfect proxy for the cost of reaching voters, since media markets crossed district lines, but the formula could be computed from published figures without any official's discretion.

Within the media ceiling sat a sub-limit addressed to the component of spending that had grown fastest during the 1960s. No more than 60 percent of the amount permitted under the media ceiling could be spent on broadcast communications. The sub-limit expressed a judgment that broadcasting presented a distinct problem, because broadcast time was purchased in markets whose prices rose with demand during a campaign, and because a candidate who declined to match an opponent's purchases risked disappearing from the medium voters encountered most. Capping the broadcast share pressed candidates to distribute spending across newspapers and other channels, rather than concentrating the entire lawful media budget in television and radio. Broadcasting retained a special place, but as a sub-limit inside a general media ceiling rather than as the ceiling standing alone that the President had vetoed in 1970.

A companion provision governed the price of broadcast time rather than the quantity a candidate could buy. During the 45 day and 60 day windows preceding an election, a broadcaster could charge a candidate no more than the lowest unit charge, the most favorable rate the station offered for the same class and amount of time in the same period. The provision treated price discrimination as part of the problem, since a station free to charge a premium could raise the real cost of a campaign without any committee deciding to spend more. The benchmark was the station's own commercial practice, and it required no official valuation. The windows confined the protection to the closing stretch of a campaign, when broadcast purchases were heaviest and when a premium charged to a candidate would fall on spending that could least easily be deferred or redirected.

The third set of ceilings reached money that came from the candidate and the candidate's family rather than from donors. A candidate for President could contribute 50,000 dollars of personal and family money to the candidate's own campaign, a candidate for the Senate 35,000 dollars, and a candidate for the House of Representatives 25,000 dollars. The descending scale followed the customary cost of the office sought, and including family money closed the obvious substitution of rerouting personal wealth through a spouse or a relative. Contemporaries read these ceilings as minor provisions, because most candidates did not finance campaigns from personal and family resources at anything near those sums. The ceilings bound the exceptional self-financed campaign rather than the typical one, and left untouched the far larger flow arriving from supporters, committees, and organized solicitation.

The corporate and labor provisions of the statute worked through a different mechanism, the separate segregated fund. Longstanding law prohibited contributions from corporate and labor organization treasuries to federal candidates, prohibitions dating to the Tillman Act of 1907 for corporations and the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 for unions. The 1971 act addressed the organizational channel that those prohibitions had left in an uncertain position. A corporation or a labor organization could establish and maintain a separate segregated fund, a distinct account fed by voluntary contributions from the individuals connected with the organization, and that fund could register and report as a political committee and support federal candidates. Treasury money remained confined to the establishment and administration of the fund, while the money the fund spent on candidates had to come from the separate contributions it collected.

That framework legitimated the political action committee as a permanent feature of federal elections. Before the statute supplied an express structure, an organization collecting political money from its members, employees, or shareholders operated against prohibitions written broadly enough to cast doubt on the enterprise. The separate segregated fund drew a line inside the organization's finances, separating the treasury, which could pay the costs of running the fund, from the fund itself, which held only the money its contributors had given for political use. Such a committee possessed a lawful origin for every dollar it raised and spent, with its own registration, reports, and thresholds. The device was available to corporations and labor organizations on parallel terms, and its significance lay in the institutional form it made ordinary, a standing committee attached to an organization, soliciting a defined constituency on a continuing basis.

The enforcement provisions matched the modesty of the rest of the design. Penalties for violations were modest, and the statute created no corps of investigators, no adjudicatory body, and no officer empowered to audit campaigns as a routine function. A disclosure system depends on the credibility of the consequences that follow a false or missing report, and the 1971 act supplied those consequences in limited measure. The burden of compliance fell on committees through the filing calendar, the record tiers, and the ban on contributions in another's name, while the sanctions behind those duties remained light. The gap between the elaborate reporting machinery and the light penalties reflected a Congress willing to compel publicity and cap defined categories of spending, but unwilling to build an apparatus to police campaigns with independent authority.

The choice of the three supervisory officers, and the refusal to replace them with an independent agency, was contested at the time and defended on the record. The Congressional Research Service account of the legislative history records Chairman Wayne Hays defending the arrangement against proposals for an independent agency. The defense rested on the adequacy of the existing officers for the tasks assigned, the receipt, custody, and availability of reports, and on resistance to lodging supervision of congressional elections in a new body outside the institutions whose members were being reported on. Divided custody carried costs the defense accepted. No single officer saw the whole of a national campaign's filings, and no officer held campaign finance administration as a primary mission. The arrangement preserved continuity with the older regime under the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1925, in which enforcement had been left largely to Congress itself, and deferred the question whether disclosure without an administering institution could command compliance.

An inventory of the omissions completes the description, because the act is most often misdescribed by attribution of provisions it never contained. The act imposed no general limits on contributions by individuals, so a donor could give to a federal candidate in amounts the statute did not cap, subject to the reporting duties that attached above the itemization threshold. It created no enforcement commission, and no body existed under it with authority to issue binding interpretations, conduct routine audits, or bring civil enforcement actions in its own name. It created no public financing of its own, and no candidate received federal funds under its provisions for a primary or a general election. The media ceilings, the broadcast sub-limit, and the family money ceilings were expenditure provisions of defined scope, and treating the statute as the origin of contribution limits, a commission, or public funding reads later law backward into a text that stopped short of all three.

Public financing arrived through a companion measure enacted in the same period, and its separate legislative identity explains the confusion. The Revenue Act of 1971, Public Law 92-178, a tax statute, created the one dollar checkoff and the Presidential Election Campaign Fund. Senator Pastore carried the mechanism into the revenue bill through a floor amendment. A taxpayer directed one dollar of tax liability to the fund by marking the checkoff on the tax return, a designation that added nothing to the tax owed and subtracted nothing from any refund, and the designated dollars accumulated in the Presidential Election Campaign Fund in the Treasury, to be paid out under the program the revenue act established. Because the device lived in tax law rather than in S. 382, the campaign act could be signed in February 1972 containing no public financing, while a mechanism enacted weeks earlier waited on the accumulation of designated funds.

The checkoff had an ancestor, and the ancestor's fate explained the care taken with the later design. A fund act had been enacted in 1966, creating an earlier version of a presidential campaign fund, and that act was terminated in 1967, before any election cycle had been financed under it. The 1966 statute thus supplied a precedent and a warning. A mechanism dependent on congressional willingness to sustain it could be extinguished between enactment and operation, and the checkoff answered that vulnerability by tying the fund's income to individual designations on tax returns rather than to a single appropriation a later Congress could withdraw. Senator Pastore's floor amendment revived the idea in that more durable form, and the fund it created was a creature of the tax code from its first day, administered through the return filing process.

The timetable of the companion measure ran well behind the timetable of the disclosure statute. The checkoff first appeared on tax returns for 1972, the year the Federal Election Campaign Act took effect, and taxpayers filing in that cycle made the first designations. The fund first paid out for the 1976 election cycle, four years after the designations began, because the program required a period of accumulation before payments could be made at the contemplated scale. Disclosure under S. 382 governed the 1972 elections, the checkoff began with 1972 returns, and public money first reached a presidential campaign in 1976, by which time the disclosure statute itself had been substantially rewritten by amendments enacted in 1974. Each element of the system therefore had its own date, its own statutory home, and its own path into operation.

Measured against the debate that produced it, the statute Congress enacted in S. 382 was a disclosure law with defined ceilings attached, supervised by three existing officers, enforced by modest penalties, and silent on the size of private contributions. Its registration threshold of 1,000 dollars, record tier at 10 dollars, and itemization tier above 100 dollars built a public record where the 1925 act had built one that the Federal Election Commission's own published history described as largely ignored for want of an administering institution. Its media formula in section 104, the greater of ten cents per voting-age resident or 50,000 dollars, and its 60 percent broadcast sub-limit addressed the spending category that the 1968 figures, exceeding 300 million dollars in aggregate, had made politically impossible to leave alone. The statute's boundaries were as deliberate as its provisions.

## The Rewrite: Limits on Giving, Limits on Spending

The Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971 entered the statute books as a disclosure law with media ceilings, and the presidential campaign of 1972 exposed the distance between that design and the practices it governed. The Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities documented secret funds and corporate contributions in the 1972 campaign, and the committee record changed the character of the problem that Congress faced. Secret funds defeated disclosure at its source, because money held outside reported accounts produced no report for any reader to inspect, no entry against which a later account could be checked, and no public trace at the moment when the money was raised and spent. Corporate contributions presented a different failure. A prohibition on corporate giving had stood in federal law since the Tillman Act of 1907, yet the documented contributions showed a ban operating inside the same enforcement vacuum that had weakened the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1925, under which no institution had been charged with administering the law and enforcement had been left to a Congress that rarely acted. The lesson drawn from the 1972 record was therefore not simply that reporting rules needed repair. The lesson was that a statute built on reports alone had no answer to money that never entered the reporting system, and no answer to a prohibition that no office existed to enforce.

That conclusion explains the change in legislative method between 1972 and 1974. Disclosure asks who gave, how much, and through which committee, and it trusts publicity to discipline the transaction after the money has moved. A limit operates earlier and more bluntly, because it fixes the size of the transaction itself before any report is filed. The amendments of 1974 adopted the second instrument alongside the first. Giving was capped, spending was capped, and the caps were drawn to reach the candidate, the committee, the individual contributor, and the outside spender alike, so that a channel closed in one place could not simply reopen in another under a different name. An enforcer belonged to the same logic. Limits without an administering institution would have repeated the central defect of the 1925 regime, whose provisions had been largely ignored in the absence of an office charged with applying them. Disclosure without limits or an enforcer no longer sufficed politically because the 1972 campaign had supplied documented instances of each failure mode in turn: concealment that disclosure could not penetrate, prohibited money that a prohibition alone had not stopped, and aggregate sums that dwarfed the modest ceilings of the older law. The rewrite accordingly treated amount, publicity, and administration as three parts of a single design rather than as alternatives among which Congress might choose one.

The vehicle for the rewrite was S. 3044 in the 93rd Congress, enacted as the Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1974, Public Law 93-443, 88 Stat. 1263. President Gerald Ford signed the amendments on October 15, 1974. The signature was not an unqualified endorsement of every element of the design. In his signing statement, President Ford recorded stated reservations about the public financing provisions, and those reservations formed part of the act's documentary record from its first day. The distinction between signature and endorsement mattered to the statute's later history, because the financing mechanism that drew presidential reservation in 1974 operated alongside limits that rested on a different footing, and the two components faced separate tests after enactment. The amendments substantially rewrote the 1971 act rather than amending it at the margins, replacing a disclosure statute with a comprehensive scheme in which contribution limits, expenditure limits, strengthened reporting, public financing, and a new administering commission occupied defined places in relation to one another.

Congress placed the limits in title 18 of the United States Code, as sections 608 and following, a location apart from the disclosure provisions that the 1971 act had carried in title 2 as sections 431 and following. Placement carried meaning in a scheme of this kind. The limits were not framed as instructions attached to a reporting form; they were prohibitions and ceilings stated in the part of the code where Congress set out the operative restrictions, and the reporting system then served in part to make compliance with those restrictions visible. The limits provisions took effect on January 1, 1975. That date gave the new ceilings a clean starting point at the opening of a calendar year and separated the period governed by the 1971 act alone from the period governed by the amended statute, so that contributions and expenditures fell under one regime or the other according to a fixed line rather than according to the date of an election alone. From January 1, 1975, the amounts that followed governed federal campaigns as enacted law, in the figures Congress had chosen in 1974.

### What were the original limits, in dollars, that the amendments imposed?

The amendments capped individual contributions at 1,000 dollars per candidate per election, multicandidate committee contributions at 5,000 dollars, and individual annual giving at 25,000 dollars in aggregate. Candidate spending, independent spending, and spending from candidate and family personal funds each carried separate ceilings, varying by office and election.

The first category limited contributions, the transfers of money and value to a candidate and to committees acting for a candidate. For an individual, the ceiling stood at 1,000 dollars per candidate per election. Each element of that formula performed work. The per candidate element tied the ceiling to a single candidacy, so the lawful amount was measured against the recipient rather than against the contributor's total activity. The per election element separated one contest from the next, and the amendments counted each primary election and each general election separately. A contributor could therefore give 1,000 dollars for a candidate's primary and a further 1,000 dollars for the same candidate's general election, because the statute treated the two elections as distinct occasions for the ceiling, while a contributor could not exceed 1,000 dollars within either election by dividing the excess among committees supporting the same candidate. The ceiling attached to the contribution in relation to the candidate, and routing a transfer through an intermediary committee did not enlarge the amount that could be given in support of that candidate for that election.

For a multicandidate committee, the contribution ceiling stood at 5,000 dollars per candidate per election, five times the individual figure. The higher amount recognized a different kind of contributor. A multicandidate committee pooled the resources of many persons behind a single committee structure, and Congress set its ceiling at a level reflecting that pooled character while keeping the same per candidate and per election measurement that governed individuals. The structure prevented the committee form from becoming an unrestricted channel, since a committee's giving to any one candidate for any one election remained bounded by a stated figure, and the multiplication of committees could not be used by a single source to reproduce an unlimited contribution under separate names without regard to the limits that applied to the source itself. Read together, the 1,000 dollar and 5,000 dollar figures established a graduated scale of lawful giving: the individual acting alone stood at one level, the organized committee pooling many contributors stood at a higher level, and neither level depended on the wealth of the giver or the size of the campaign receiving the money.

An annual aggregate ceiling completed the contribution design. An individual's total contributions were limited to 25,000 dollars in a calendar year, across the candidates and committees receiving them. The aggregate answered a problem that per candidate ceilings, standing alone, left open. A contributor bound only by a 1,000 dollar limit for each candidate could have spread contributions across a large number of candidates and committees and reached a total far beyond the scale that the per candidate figure suggested, acquiring through breadth the same weight in the system that a single oversized contribution would have carried. The 25,000 dollar aggregate closed that route by fixing the contributor's overall annual exposure under the limits, so that each individual decision to give counted against a finite total for the year. The three contribution figures therefore operated as a set: the per election ceiling governed the intensity of support for one candidacy, the committee ceiling governed the organized form of that support, and the aggregate governed the total volume of an individual's participation in a year.

The second category limited candidate expenditures, the spending side of a campaign conducted by the candidate and the candidate's campaign organization. Contribution limits alone controlled only the inflow of resources, and a campaign able to receive money in lawful amounts might nevertheless spend without any ceiling from accumulated funds, so the amendments placed ceilings on outlays as well. For the House of Representatives, the expenditure limit stood at 70,000 dollars per election. The per election measurement applied in the same manner as on the contribution side, so a House candidate's primary campaign and general election campaign each carried its own ceiling rather than sharing a single combined amount. The flat figure suited a district based office for which Congress chose a uniform national number, stated without reference to the population of the district, and the simplicity of that choice made the ceiling easy to state, easy to apply, and easy to test against a campaign's reported spending.

For the Senate, Congress rejected a single flat figure in favor of formulas that scaled the ceiling to the size of the state. The primary election limit was 100,000 dollars or eight cents per voting-age resident of the state, and the general election limit was 150,000 dollars or twelve cents per voting-age resident. The structure joined a fixed dollar floor to a population based calculation, so that a small state received the protection of the stated floor while a large state received a ceiling proportioned to the electorate a statewide campaign had to reach. The general election figures stood higher than the primary figures at both points of comparison, the floor rising from 100,000 dollars to 150,000 dollars and the per resident rate rising from eight cents to twelve cents, reflecting the larger contest that a general election presented after nomination. The Senate formulas show the drafting method of the amendments at its clearest: a ceiling was not a single number to be memorized but a rule that produced a number for each state, and the rule's inputs, the dollar floor and the voting-age population, were matters of public record rather than of administrative estimation.

For President, the expenditure ceilings stood at 10,000,000 dollars for the primary elections and 20,000,000 dollars for the general election. The scale reflected the office. A presidential campaign addressed a national electorate across every state, and its ceiling was stated in millions where Senate ceilings rested on state populations and the House ceiling stood at 70,000 dollars. The relationship between the two presidential figures also matched the pattern found elsewhere in the amendments, with the general election ceiling set at twice the primary figure in the presidential case and at a higher level in the Senate case, because the general election placed the nominee before the full electorate in a single decisive contest. Taken as a whole, the expenditure category rested on a proposition distinct from the contribution category. Contribution limits measured the relationship between a giver and a candidate; expenditure limits measured the campaign itself, on the theory embedded in the text that the total volume of spending formed part of what Congress sought to govern, whatever the source of the funds spent.

The third category limited independent expenditures, a form of spending that stood outside the candidate's campaign organization. The ceiling stood at 1,000 dollars on expenditures relative to a clearly identified candidate. Each word of that governing phrase drew a boundary. An expenditure was relative to a candidate when it concerned that candidate's election, advocating in connection with the candidacy rather than addressing public questions in the abstract, and a candidate was clearly identified when the spending identified the candidate with sufficient clarity that the expenditure could be tied to that candidacy. Spending on general political discussion that named no candidate fell outside the phrase, while spending directed at a named candidacy fell inside it, and the 1,000 dollar figure then governed how much a person could spend in that relative and identified manner during the period the ceiling covered.

Independence supplied the other boundary of the category. An independent expenditure was spending made without the candidate's control, cooperation, or direction, by a person acting apart from the campaign rather than as its agent or instrument. The distinction separated two kinds of support that produced similar public effects. Spending coordinated with a campaign functioned in substance as a contribution of services or purchases to that campaign and belonged under the contribution and campaign expenditure rules, while spending undertaken independently reached the electorate without passing through the candidate's accounts at all. Congress placed a ceiling on the independent form rather than leaving it ungoverned, setting the amount at 1,000 dollars, the same figure as the individual contribution ceiling. The symmetry expressed the amendments' premise that support for a candidacy was to be measured and bounded whether it arrived as a transfer to the campaign or as spending conducted alongside the campaign, and that a supporter could not outrun the contribution ceiling by spending directly on the candidate's behalf while claiming the protection of independence.

The fourth category limited the personal funds of the candidate and the candidate's immediate family. For a presidential candidate, the ceiling stood at 50,000 dollars; for a Senate candidate, at 35,000 dollars; and for a House candidate, at 25,000 dollars. The amounts covered the candidate and the immediate family together, so the family's resources were grouped with the candidate's own rather than treated as a separate reservoir available to each relative in turn. The category addressed a channel that contribution limits could not reach through their ordinary operation. A contribution ceiling governs what other persons may give, and a wealthy candidate drawing on personal and family funds was not receiving contributions from outside supporters at all, so without a personal funds ceiling the entire structure of limits could have been bypassed by self financing on an unlimited scale. By capping the amount that a candidate and the immediate family could put into the campaign from personal resources, the amendments treated personal wealth as one more source of campaign funds subject to a stated figure, graded by office in the same descending pattern that appeared in the expenditure ceilings, with the presidency at the highest figure, the Senate next, and the House at the lowest.

Around these four categories, the amendments gathered a set of supporting changes that adjusted the surrounding law to the new limits. Disclosure was strengthened, and the machinery of reporting was reorganized around a single principal campaign committee for each candidate. The designation of one principal committee gave the reporting system a center. Money raised and spent for a candidacy was to be accounted for through a defined committee structure with an identified locus of responsibility, rather than dispersed among overlapping committees whose separate reports left the full picture of a campaign's finances to be assembled by the reader. In a regime of limits, that centralization served enforcement as well as publicity, because a ceiling can be tested only against a complete account, and a complete account depends on contributions and expenditures flowing through records that belong to a known committee. The strengthened disclosure provisions and the limits thus reinforced one another: the reports disclosed the transactions, and the transactions disclosed were the ones the ceilings governed.

Two further supporting changes concerned the form that money could take and the payments that surrounded a candidacy. The amendments placed a ceiling of 100 dollars on cash contributions, so that a contribution above that amount had to travel in a form other than cash. The reason lay in the character of cash itself, which leaves no independent record as it passes from hand to hand and therefore defeats both reporting and the testing of limits. A 100 dollar cash ceiling preserved a place for small cash giving while requiring larger support to move through channels that generated documentation. The amendments also imposed limits on honoraria, the payments made to officeholders and candidates for speeches and similar appearances. Honoraria stood outside campaign accounts by definition, yet payments of that kind connected public office to private payment through a channel that the contribution rules did not directly govern, and placing limits on them brought that adjacent channel within the statute's general approach of stated ceilings on money moving around federal office.

The remaining supporting changes removed two features of the earlier law that no longer fitted the new scheme. The amendments repealed the media ceilings that the 1971 act had imposed, the category specific limits on spending for communications media that had been the older statute's principal restraint on outlays. Repeal did not leave media spending ungoverned; the general expenditure ceilings that the amendments enacted governed campaign spending as a whole, including the media purchases that the repealed ceilings had singled out, and a separate media specific cap would have duplicated the broader instrument while dividing spending into categories the new design treated together. The amendments also ended the old ban on contributions by government contractors. That categorical prohibition belonged to the earlier patchwork of bans laid over a disclosure regime, and its removal formed part of the substitution of a general system of limits, applicable by amount, for a system that had combined unenforced prohibitions with reporting rules lacking an administrator. Each supporting change therefore pointed in the same direction as the four categories of limit: toward defined amounts, documented transactions, and a structure that an administering institution could apply uniformly.

The design nevertheless rested on a premise that the amounts themselves could not guarantee. Ceilings stated in a statute do not collect reports, do not answer questions from committees about how the rules apply, do not audit accounts, and do not bring enforcement proceedings when a figure has been exceeded. The amendments assumed that a single administrator could run the whole scheme: receiving the strengthened disclosure, applying the contribution and expenditure limits across the presidency, the Senate, and the House, and standing behind the ceilings with enforcement authority sufficient to make the figures operative rather than declaratory. Every number in the four categories depended on that assumption, because a limit measured per candidate and per election can be enforced only by an institution able to gather the records of all candidates and all elections into one view, and an aggregate ceiling can be tested only by an office that sees a contributor's giving as a whole. The design of that administrator stands at the center of the statute's structure, and the balance of the scheme, its financing provisions, its reporting duties, and the constitutional tests that followed, turns on how that single administering institution was built and how its construction shaped the law that the limits announced on January 1, 1975.

## An Enforcement Commission Built Not to Favor Either Party

The statute Congress substantially rewrote in 1974 had been built, in its enacted form, around a single principal instrument: disclosure. Candidates and political committees reported what they raised and what they spent, on the theory that visible money would carry its own restraint. What the enacted statute did not build was an administrator equal to the disclosure it demanded. Custody of the reports rested with supervisory officers, and custody divided among separate officers is a different thing from administration. A report filed in one office and a related report filed in another could each be complete while the transaction connecting them remained unexamined by anyone whose duty covered both. The Federal Election Commission's own published history of the earlier law recorded the result of that arrangement plainly: where no institution is charged with administering a law, the provisions tend to be ignored, and enforcement left to Congress itself rarely occurs. Before the new substantive limits of 1974 could mean anything, the legislation had to answer a prior question: who would hold the reports, examine them, pursue violations through a civil process, and answer campaigns trying in good faith to comply. A limit without an administrator is a statement of aspiration addressed to no one in particular.

The split custody the amendments displaced deserves precise description, because the structure chosen in its place makes sense only as a reply to it. Under the inherited arrangement, filing obligations ran to three supervisory officers: the Clerk of the House for House elections, the Secretary of the Senate for Senate elections, and the Comptroller General, head of the General Accounting Office, for presidential elections. Each officer received the reports within a defined sphere, and could receive, file, and make available. None held authority to administer the statute as a whole, audit a campaign's books against its reports, investigate a suspected violation on the institution's own initiative, or carry a matter into court. The officers were custodians of records rather than regulators of conduct. A custodian's failure is a misfiled document. A regulator's failure is an unexamined transaction and an unbrought case. The inherited system could register the first and had no machinery even to notice the second. Money moving between committees connected to the same effort could cross from one custodial sphere to another without any office holding both ends of the movement, and referral depended on an official noticing a problem outside that official's own charge and on another authority choosing to take it up.

The Watergate record gave that structural weakness a public demonstration. The investigations that followed the Watergate affair, including those of the Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities, documented secret campaign funds, illegal corporate contributions, and money raised outside the disclosure system by the President's re-election committee, and that record became the immediate political foundation for the amendments of 1974. Its significance lay less in any single transaction than in the relationship it exposed between disclosure on paper and accountability in fact. A campaign finance system had existed, and reports had been filed under it, yet reconstructing what had actually happened fell to investigators assembled after the fact, working outside the ordinary machinery of the statute, because that machinery had neither a single custodian for the whole record nor an officer charged with following the record where it led. The lesson drawn in 1974 was that disclosure is not self-executing, and the amendments treated administration as a substantive choice. Reports discipline a campaign only when an institution reads them comparatively, tests them against the underlying books, answers questions about them with authority, and can proceed against a committee that files falsely, late, or not at all. Where any of those functions is missing, the obligation to report decays toward a formality, observed most carefully by campaigns that least needed watching.

Section 310 of the amendments created the Federal Election Commission, whose composition as enacted bore the marks of the problem it was meant to solve. The Commission consisted of six voting members, not chosen by a single appointing authority. Two were appointed by the President pro tempore of the Senate, two by the Speaker of the House, and two by the President, with the congressional appointments made on the recommendations of the party leaders of the respective chambers. Layered over the division among appointing authorities was a division within each pair: no more than one member of each appointed pair could be of the same political party, so each pair, by construction, crossed the party line. Three appointing authorities, each producing a bipartisan pair, yielded a commission of six in which neither major party could hold more than three seats, and in which every appointing authority had placed one member identified with each side of the partisan division. Alongside the six voting members sat the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House, or their deputies, serving ex officio and without votes. Their presence connected the new body to the custodial world it replaced, since officers of the two chambers had held filing functions under the older arrangement, but the connection was deliberately made nonvoting: the power to decide passed to the six appointed members. Every element of the composition served the same anxiety: a commission appointed by one authority, or drawn from one party, would carry the suspicion of instrumentality into every decision it issued, whatever the merits.

The powers transferred to the Commission matched the gaps the older arrangement had left open. Custody of disclosure came first: reports scattered among supervisory officers were gathered into a single repository, so a campaign's reporting picture could be read together. Civil enforcement accompanied custody, giving the Commission authority to pursue violations through the civil process and converting the statute from filing duties backed mainly by exposure into a body of law with an institutional plaintiff. Audits provided a means of testing reports against records rather than accepting filings at face value, a function of particular weight in public financing, where public money depended on the accuracy of what campaigns reported. Advisory opinions let campaigns and committees put a concrete question about the statute's application and receive an authoritative answer before acting. Rulemaking allowed the Commission to translate general commands into regulations, settling recurring questions once, publicly, and in advance. Administration of public financing completed the transfer, placing certification and oversight of the presidential funding system in the same hands that held disclosure and enforcement. The six functions formed a single logic: the institution that read the reports could audit them, audit supported enforcement, and the certifier of public funds drew on all the rest. The amendments gathered these powers deliberately, then divided the gathered power against itself.

The members served six-year staggered terms, placing the membership beyond the rhythm of any single election cycle and beyond any single round of appointments, so the body deciding a matter from one campaign would ordinarily include members appointed in different political seasons, and shifts in control of the presidency or of either chamber change such a commission gradually rather than at a stroke. Appointment in bipartisan pairs prevented a one-party commission at creation; staggered terms prevented one later. The corresponding cost was accepted: a body insulated from rapid change is slow to change when the political branches believe its course is wrong, and the stagger that blocks capture also dilutes accountability.

The decisive feature of the design was the voting rule attached to the Commission's most consequential acts. The statute required four affirmative votes at the decisive stages of enforcement: to find reason to believe a violation had occurred or was about to occur, opening a formal investigation; to find probable cause after investigation; to enter into conciliation, the negotiated resolution through which matters that proceed are ordinarily settled; and to authorize a civil suit when conciliation failed. The requirement attached precisely where the coercive power of the state was placed against a campaign: at the opening of a case, at its confirmation, at its settlement, and at the decision to carry it into court. A coalition of four, on a body constructed to contain no more than three members of either party, is a coalition that cannot be assembled from one party alone. At every decisive stage, at least one member appointed from across the partisan division had to join the determination before the institution could move.

The mechanical consequence follows from the arithmetic without any inference about motive or behavior. When the six voting members divide three to three at any decisive stage, the required four affirmative votes do not exist, and the matter ends, without a finding that the conduct under examination complied with the statute and without a finding that it violated the statute. It ends without a decision on its merits in either direction. A three to three division is therefore categorically different from a dismissal after investigation or a judgment after litigation, outcomes in which an answer exists and can be defended, criticized, appealed, or followed as guidance. In a deadlock, no institutional answer exists. The respondent is not vindicated, the complainant is not refused on any stated ground, and campaigns watching the closed matter learn nothing about where the statute's line runs, because the line was never drawn. The only certain information a deadlock transmits concerns the Commission itself: the question divided its members evenly. Given the method of appointment, an even division will most often be one in which each side holds together, on questions engaging the regulatory philosophy that separates the parties.

### Why does the Federal Election Commission so often divide evenly?

The division follows the arithmetic of appointment. Six voting members sit in three bipartisan pairs, no pair drawn from one party alone, and four affirmative votes are required to act. A question dividing three members from three therefore produces no decision, regardless of its merits or urgency in the matter.

The deeper reason lies in the character of the questions the Commission exists to answer. Campaign finance law regulates the means by which competitors seek power, and the two parties have long held genuinely different views about how closely money in politics should be supervised, how readily spending restrictions can be justified, and how much policing should rest on disclosure rather than prohibition. Those are disagreements about the proper scope of the regulatory enterprise itself, and members appointed on the recommendations of party leaders, serving on a body divided evenly between the parties, bring them into the room as part of the design. On questions of filing mechanics or the application of settled rules to ordinary facts, the differences have little to engage, and ordinary business can proceed by consensus or by coalitions forming along no fixed line. On questions presenting the underlying conflict directly, such as how aggressively an ambiguous provision should be read against a campaign, the evenly divided body divides evenly, as designed arithmetic predicts. Explanations treating such divisions as accidents of personality or failures of goodwill miss the structural point: personalities rotate through staggered terms, while the arithmetic remains and answers contested questions with silence.

On that foundation rests the claim that gives the design its sharpest characterization, the analytical claim at the center of the Commission's history: the Federal Election Commission was designed to deadlock. The claim concerns the relationship between structure and outcome. Of all the choices embedded in the amendments of 1974, the choice of an evenly divided commission, armed with a four-vote requirement at every decisive enforcement stage, has shaped enforcement more than any substantive limit the statute contains. Contribution limits and definitions of regulated conduct operate downstream of a prior institutional fact: before any of them can be applied to a contested matter, four members of an evenly split body must agree to apply them. Where that gate opens, the substantive law governs. Where it closes, it is beside the point, because the institution charged with applying it has produced no application. The even split is the feature through which all other features must pass. Deadlock, moreover, does not fall randomly across the docket. It falls on the questions that divide the parties along regulatory philosophy, the questions most in need of an institutional answer, because campaigns cannot predict the law's application to them from the law's text. A question commanding cross-party agreement resolves and yields guidance. A question dividing the parties produces no institutional answer, and that absence is itself a regulatory outcome, leaving conduct unpoliced and the standard unsettled each time the question returns. Designed to deadlock, in this sense, names a relationship between choice and consequence, not a psychological claim about individual drafters. Whatever mixture of motives produced the even split, it was chosen with its consequences available to be seen: a body of six, divided by construction into three and three, required to act by fours, will fail to act on precisely the questions that matter most to the contending philosophies, and enforcement since the Commission's creation bears that choice's imprint more deeply than any dollar figure or definition elsewhere in the statute.

The counter-reading deserves statement at full strength, in its own terms, because it begins from a premise the deadlock claim passes over quickly: election law is not ordinary law, and its enforcement is not ordinary enforcement. The officer who polices elections polices the process by which power itself is allocated. A regulator of mines or markets under one faction's control can do serious harm within a political order retaining the means of correcting it at the next election. An elections regulator under one faction's control can act upon the corrective mechanism itself, investigating the campaigns of the party out of power, sparing those of the party in power, and converting the neutral forms of enforcement into a standing advantage for incumbency. The even structure, on this reading, was chosen deliberately so that such an outcome could not occur. The officer policing elections could never become the instrument of the party holding power against the party out of it, because the party holding power could never hold the Commission. Its three seats would always face three seats held through appointments originating with the other side, and any action requiring four votes would require the concurrence of at least one member whose appointment traced to the opposing party. The safeguard operates mechanically, not by exhortation. It depends on counting, and it holds on the days when virtue, norms, and vigilance all fail.

The fear behind that design was not speculative in 1974, as the record before the drafters showed. The Watergate investigations that supplied the case for a commission also supplied a detailed account of how governmental machinery could be turned toward protecting one campaign and harassing its opponents. The same Congress had spent two years learning what a determined executive could do with the investigative and prosecutorial machinery of government aimed at political adversaries. To hand the new enforcement power to a body the party in power could control, in the immediate aftermath of that education, would have replicated the danger in a new institution. Enforcement of election law by a body one faction controls is itself a democratic danger, on this reading, because its victims cannot readily appeal to a public that encounters the abuse only through the distorted competition it produces. The bipartisan pairs and the ceiling of three seats for either party are therefore not concessions extracted from a reluctant majority, nor accidents of bargaining. They are the point. A commission either party could command would be worse than a commission that sometimes cannot act, because the first corrupts the electoral process it administers while the second leaves parts of that process unadministered. Between partisan enforcement and incomplete enforcement, the drafters of 1974 chose incomplete enforcement, a choice the counter-reading holds was considered, informed by fresh evidence, and defensible on that record.

The four-vote requirement, on this reading, carries a meaning the deadlock framing understates. The requirement forces cross-party agreement before the state moves against a campaign. An investigation opened, a probable cause finding entered, a conciliation imposed, or a suit filed is governmental coercion directed at participants in an election, with consequences that fall immediately and cannot be undone by a later vindication, since an election investigated under a cloud is an election already altered. Requiring such action to command support from both sides of an evenly divided body places a burden of persuasion across the political divide where the risk of partisan use is highest. Defenders of the design describe the requirement as a safeguard comparable to other supermajority designs in the constitutional and statutory order, in which sensitive powers are deliberately made harder to exercise than ordinary ones. The qualified majorities required for the most solemn decisions of the political branches express the same judgment: some decisions should not be taken by a bare majority, and failing to take them is the acceptable cost of requiring broad agreement first. Deadlock, on this reading, is the price of a protection rather than an oversight. A lock installed because the door guards the electoral process itself, refusing entry whenever only one faction presents itself, performs its installed function, and the occasions on which it refuses a meritorious entry are the premium paid for refusing a corrupt one. The counter-reading holds that the structure belongs to that family of choices, not to the family of drafting errors.

The two readings are analytically distinct, and conflating them produces most of the confusion in argument about the Commission. The claim named designed to deadlock is a claim about consequence: the even split governs the institution's output, that output on contested questions is characteristically no output at all, and the pattern has shaped enforcement more than the substantive provisions the institution exists to apply. The counter-reading is a claim about justification: the same pattern is what a safeguard against partisan enforcement would produce, the danger guarded against was demonstrated rather than imagined, and a design cannot be convicted of failure merely for exhibiting its built restraint. A structure can impose the costs the first claim describes and be worth them for the reasons the second gives. Neither reading refutes the other, because they answer different questions, one asking what the design does and the other asking what it prevents. Judgment requires holding both at once: Congress built an administrator strong in gathered powers and weak in partisan availability, able to act where the parties agreed and unable to act where they did not.

The first commissioners were seated in April 1975, taking up custody of disclosure, administration of the public financing system under which the Commission administered the publicly financed presidential election of 1976, and an enforcement charter whose decisive stages all required four votes. From that seating forward, the design operated as written: matters resolved where cross-party coalitions formed, matters closed without a merits decision where they did not, and the argument renewed over whether the silence at the system's center is a safeguard holding or an administrator declining to administer. Congress created a commission built not to favor either party, accepted the enforcement losses that construction guarantees, and left to later judgment whether the protection was worth the price exacted.

## Public Money for Presidential Campaigns: Design, Operation, and Abandonment

The public financing of presidential campaigns entered the Federal Election Campaign Act by stages, and the sequence matters because each stage answered a different problem. The idea itself long predated the statute. According to the Congressional Research Service, the idea of public financing of federal elections was first proposed in 1907, during the presidency of Theodore Roosevelt, who had urged public funding of federal campaigns in his messages to Congress. A proposal with that lineage was neither novel nor marginal when Congress returned to it in the second half of the twentieth century. It was, instead, a recurring answer to a recurring concern about the place of private money in the selection of a president, an answer that Congress considered, set aside, and considered again across several decades before a durable version reached the statute book. Public financing did not arrive with the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971 as originally enacted in February 1972. The funding mechanism arrived through a revenue statute in 1971, and the full system of payments arrived through the amendments of 1974. Treating the three steps as one step misstates how the program was built and, in time, how it came apart.

The first enacted step was brief. A fund act passed in 1966 was terminated in 1967, before any operating system of presidential public financing rested on it. The episode left the idea legislatively alive without leaving a working program in place. The operative beginning came with the Revenue Act of 1971, which created the checkoff and the fund through which presidential public financing later flowed. The placement was significant. Congress located the money not in the campaign statute itself but in the revenue system, so that the supply of public money depended on individual income tax returns rather than on a freestanding appropriation debated as campaign legislation. That choice shaped everything that followed. A program financed by designation on a tax form had a revenue stream that rose or fell with taxpayer behavior, and the behavior of taxpayers, measured across four decades, moved in one direction for most of the program's life. The checkoff therefore has to be understood as both a funding device and a measurement device. It supplied the fund, and the annual rate of designation recorded, in a single percentage, the willingness of filers to direct public money toward presidential campaigns.

The mechanics of the checkoff were deliberately small at the level of the individual return. A taxpayer designated 1 dollar from tax already owed, and a joint return carried a designation of 2 dollars. The amount was a designation of revenue, drawn from tax already owed, and its significance lay in aggregation rather than in the size of any single choice. Millions of individual designations, each too small to alter a filer's own position in any material way, combined into the fund from which matching payments and grants were later paid. The checkoff first appeared on 1972 returns. The amount held at the original level for two decades and then changed once, in direct response to the participation record. As participation fell, the designation was tripled to 3 dollars for 1993 returns. The increase stated the arithmetic problem plainly. A declining share of a growing number of returns could be offset, in part, by raising the amount each participating return designated, and Congress chose that offset rather than a redesign of the mechanism. The checkoff stayed voluntary, stayed attached to the individual return, and stayed small. The statute asked little of each filer, and after 1993 it asked three times as much of each participating filer, without altering the underlying structure that made total revenue a function of how many filers chose to take part.

The amendments of 1974 completed the system. Where the Revenue Act of 1971 had created the money, the Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1974 created the payments, and placed the arrangement in subtitle H of the Internal Revenue Code. Three forms of payment made up the completed design. Primary matching payments supported candidates seeking a party nomination. General election grants supported nominees in the campaign between the conventions and the election. Convention grants supported the national nominating conventions themselves. Each payment answered a distinct stage of a presidential campaign, and each carried the same underlying bargain. A candidate or a party that accepted public money accepted the conditions attached to that money, most consequentially a limit on spending. Public money under the 1974 design was therefore never a supplement without strings. It was an exchange, in which the Treasury supplied funds and the recipient surrendered the freedom to spend without a ceiling. Whether that exchange attracted candidates depended, in every cycle, on a comparison between the value of the public payment and the value of the freedom surrendered, and that comparison changed as the price of a competitive presidential campaign changed around a payment structure built from fixed sums.

The primary matching mechanism rewarded breadth of small contributions, and its details defined what breadth meant. To qualify, a candidate had to cross a threshold of 5,000 dollars in each of twenty states. The threshold counted only the first 250 dollars of each contribution, so a candidate could not satisfy a state threshold with a small number of large contributions alone. Twenty separate state thresholds had to be crossed, each built from contributions measured in units no larger than 250 dollars. The design pushed a qualifying campaign outward geographically and downward in contribution size at the same time. A candidate with intense support in a few places and no organization elsewhere faced a different task from a candidate with moderate support spread across the country, and the statute preferred the second pattern. The threshold performed a screening function before any public dollar moved. It separated candidates able to demonstrate a national base of small contributions from candidates unable to do so, and it performed that separation through the structure of the contributions themselves rather than through any official judgment about the seriousness of a candidacy.

Once a candidate qualified, the matching formula applied the same 250 dollar unit to the payment itself. The government matched, dollar for dollar, the first 250 dollars of each individual contribution. A contribution of 250 dollars drew a full match. A contribution larger than 250 dollars drew a match measured only on its first 250 dollars, with the remainder unmatched. The formula doubled the value of a small contribution to the receiving campaign while adding nothing beyond the fixed unit for a large one, and the incentive ran in a single direction. Campaigns operating inside the system gained the most public money by raising private money in small increments from many contributors, which was precisely the fundraising pattern the threshold had already required for qualification. Payments began on January 1 of the election year, a date that fixed the rhythm of the system. Qualification work belonged to the year before the election, and the public money arrived with the calendar year in which the primaries were held. Total matching payments to any candidate were capped at half the primary spending limit. The cap preserved a role for private fundraising throughout the primary campaign, since even a candidate who qualified early and raised efficiently reached a point at which no further public money followed further eligible contributions.

Eligibility, once gained, could also be lost, and the loss rule tied public money to demonstrated electoral performance. A candidate who failed to reach 10 percent of the vote in two consecutive primaries lost eligibility for matching payments. The rule gave the system a self-limiting quality during the primary season itself. A candidacy that the primary electorate declined to support at even a tenth of the vote, in two primaries held in sequence, stopped drawing public funds, whatever the state of its earlier qualification. Read together, the threshold, the match, the cap, and the loss rule formed a coherent order. Entry required breadth. Payment rewarded small contributions. Total support stopped at a fixed fraction of the spending limit. Continued support required a minimum measure of success before actual voters. Each element was stated as a number, and each number was fixed in the statute, so the coherence of the design and the rigidity of the design were the same quality viewed from opposite directions. A later age with different campaign prices inherited the numbers exactly as written.

The general election bargain was simpler and more demanding. A major party nominee who accepted no private contributions for the general election received a full grant equal to the 20 million dollar spending limit. The exchange was total at that stage of the campaign. Public money did not match private money in the general election under the completed design; public money replaced private money, and the nominee who took the grant conducted the general election campaign within the funded amount and the spending limit it embodied. Minor parties received proportional treatment, scaled in relation to the major party grant rather than set equal to it, so the statute graded public support according to a party's place in the preceding electoral order instead of treating every nominee identically. New parties stood on a different footing again. A new party whose nominee reached 5 percent of the vote became eligible for payments after the election, measured against a result already recorded rather than a standing already established. The three tiers expressed a single principle applied at different levels of demonstrated support. Established major parties received the full grant in advance. Minor parties received a proportion. New parties received payment afterward, and only after crossing a stated share of the vote.

The first operating cycle showed the system working as designed, at the scale the designers had funded. Per the records of the Federal Election Commission, each major party nominee in 1976 received about 21.8 million dollars. The figure stood close to the 20 million dollar grant level established in the 1974 design, with the difference reflecting the operation of the enacted formula rather than a departure from it. In that first cycle the grant was not a token contribution toward a campaign financed mainly from other sources. An amount at that level, delivered as the funding of a general election campaign whose spending the recipient had agreed to limit, constituted the campaign's financial foundation. The recipient's acceptance of the bargain, and the opponent's parallel acceptance, held general election spending within a frame that both campaigns shared because both had taken the same public money on the same terms.

The participation record traced the slow weakening of the revenue side. In 1976, designations appeared on 27.5 percent of returns. The share rose to a peak of 28.7 percent in 1980. Thereafter the direction reversed, and the reversal never corrected itself within the record covered here. By the returns for 2004, according to the Congressional Research Service, participation had fallen to 9.2 percent. House Report 113-292 placed participation at about 5 percent by 2012. The percentages describe a fund fed by a shrinking minority of filers. A program that began with more than a quarter of returns designating money ended the recorded period with roughly one return in twenty doing so. The 1993 increase in the designation amount, from 1 dollar to 3 dollars, operated against that trend without reversing it, because tripling the amount per participating return could not compensate indefinitely for a participation rate that fell by more than four fifths between the 1980 peak and the 2012 figure.

### Does presidential public financing operate?

The public financing system remained in the statute through the 2020 election cycle, and the checkoff and the fund continued to exist as legal mechanisms, but major party nominees had ceased accepting the general election grant in the 2012, 2016, and 2020 cycles, leaving the program operative in law and marginal in practice.

The abandonment of the system by leading candidates proceeded by steps, each taken by a candidate who calculated that the statutory bargain no longer served a winning campaign. In the judgment recorded by the Brookings Institution's Anthony Corrado, the 2000 cycle supplied the first decisive step at the primary stage. George W. Bush declined primary matching funds in 2000 while winning the nomination. The fact carried weight beyond one candidacy. A candidate had refused the primary system's money, refused the spending limit attached to that money, raised private funds without the ceiling, and won. The demonstration altered the calculation for later candidates more than any statutory change of the period altered it, because the statute after 2000 offered the same payments on the same terms as before, and the change lay entirely in what a successful candidacy had shown about the price of accepting them. The primary matching system depended on candidates valuing the match more than the limit. The 2000 nomination showed a candidate valuing the limit's absence more, and winning on that valuation.

The general election grant held longer and then fell at a single stroke. In 2008, Barack Obama became the first major party nominee to decline the general election grant. Per contemporaneous reporting, the opponent who accepted the grant received 84.1 million dollars. The contrast within that one cycle stated the new economics in full. One nominee conducted the general election under the funded limit. The other conducted the general election on private money raised without that limit, by choice, as the first major party nominee to make that choice. After 2008, the pattern fixed itself. No major party nominee accepted the general election grant in 2012. No major party nominee accepted the general election grant in 2016. No major party nominee accepted the general election grant in 2020. A payment that every major party nominee had accepted across the program's early decades passed, within three cycles of its first refusal by a major party nominee, into a payment that no major party nominee took. The statute continued to offer the grant. The offer stood on the same terms. The candidates for whom the grant had been designed declined it as a class, cycle after cycle, and each refusal made the next refusal less remarkable than the one before.

The convention grants, the third element of the 1974 design, ended by legislation rather than by refusal. A statute enacted in 2014 terminated convention grants and diverted the money to child health research. The termination differed in kind from the decline of matching payments and general election grants. Candidates had abandoned those payments by declining them, leaving the provisions in the statute while emptying them of participants. Congress ended the convention grants outright and redirected the funds to a purpose outside campaign finance altogether. The sequence of endings therefore ran in two modes. Where participation was voluntary, the system ended through accumulated individual decisions that no single decision maker controlled and no single decision reversed. Where Congress acted directly, the system ended through a statute that reassigned the money. By the close of the 2020 cycle, the completed design of 1974 had been vacated at every stage it funded. Primary matching payments had been declined by a winning candidate as early as 2000. The general election grant had gone unaccepted by major party nominees across three consecutive cycles. Convention grants had been terminated and their funds diverted in 2014.

The reason the system hollowed rather than fell lies in the form of its limits. The payments, the caps, and the grant were fixed sums written into the statute, while the market in which presidential campaigns bought advertising, organization, travel, and communication repriced those goods upward across the decades the fixed sums stood unchanged. A subsidy set as a fixed sum purchases whatever that sum buys in a given year, and no more. As prices rose, the same statutory amount bought a shrinking share of a competitive campaign. The bargain of 1974 had exchanged a spending limit for funding sufficient to run a credible general election at 1974 prices as adjusted under the enacted formula. The exchange rate embedded in that bargain deteriorated mechanically, without any legislator voting to worsen it and without any administrator setting it wrong. Each cycle, a nominee weighing the grant weighed a known amount of public money against an unknown but plainly larger amount of private money available outside the limit, and the growth of the second figure relative to the first was a function of campaign prices rather than of any defect in administration. Candidates responded to prices. The checkoff's declining participation compounded the effect on the revenue side, but the decisive pressure operated on the spending side, where the funded limit asked a nominee to contest a modern presidential election on a sum fixed by an earlier era's arithmetic.

Hollowing, as a mode of ending, left the statutory text standing while the practice departed. Congress never repealed the general election grant during the period covered here, and no statute forced a major party nominee to refuse it. The program's central provisions survived as law and failed as incentives, which is a different condition from abolition and produces a different historical record. A repealed program generates a debate and a vote. A hollowed program generates a series of individual refusals, each rational on the facts of its own cycle, accumulating into a result no participant had to defend as a general proposition. The public financing system built between 1971 and 1974 thus ended its period of effective operation without a single terminating decision addressed to the whole of it, apart from the convention grants removed in 2014. Its history divides cleanly. From 1976, public money functioned as the financial foundation of major party presidential campaigns whose nominees accepted its conditions. From the refusals that began in the 2000 primary season and reached the general election in 2008, public money functioned as an option that leading candidates examined and set aside. The statute offered the same exchange in both periods. The prices surrounding the exchange changed, and the exchange failed on price.

## The Court Divides the Statute: Contributions Stand, Expenditures Fall

The constitutional test of the 1974 amendments arrived almost as soon as the limits themselves took effect. In January 1975, Senator James L. Buckley of New York filed suit challenging the statute, joined by plaintiffs drawn from across the political spectrum, so that the case against the new regime was not the complaint of one faction or one kind of participant in federal elections. The plaintiffs attacked the architecture as a whole: the ceilings on what could be given, the ceilings on what could be spent, the disclosure duties, the public financing program, and the composition of the Federal Election Commission that the amendments had created to administer the system. The challenge proceeded through the expedited route the statute provided, with questions certified for appellate resolution, and the court of appeals upheld nearly all of the statute. That judgment set the terms on which the case reached the Supreme Court. The lower court had found the congressional design largely constitutional, and the question for the justices was whether a law built to govern the money in federal campaigns could survive the First Amendment at all, and if it could, which of its parts would survive and which would fall.

The answer came in Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), in a per curiam opinion issued on January 30, 1976, a little more than a year after the suit was filed and in the opening weeks of the first presidential election cycle to be conducted under the new system. Eight justices participated in the decision; Justice Stevens took no part. The opinion was unsigned, issued in the name of the Court as an institution, and its length and structure reflected the size of the task before it. The Court was not reviewing a single provision in isolation. It was reviewing an interlocking code in which limits, reporting duties, subsidies, and an enforcement agency had been designed to operate together, and it proceeded provision by provision, sustaining some parts of the design, invalidating others, and construing other provisions narrowly so that they could stand. The result was neither the wholesale validation the court of appeals had delivered nor the wholesale invalidation the plaintiffs had sought. The statute emerged from the decision divided against itself, with its rules on the giving of money largely intact and its rules on the spending of money largely destroyed, and that division governed the operation of federal campaign finance law for the decades that followed.

The reasoning that produced the division began from a premise the Court stated without qualification. Restrictions on contributions and expenditures both burden expression, because communication in a mass society requires money. Political speech at the scale of a federal election is not delivered from a soapbox to a gathered crowd. It is printed, broadcast, mailed, and organized, and every one of those activities costs money, so a legal ceiling on the money available for political communication is a restraint on the communication itself rather than a regulation of some conduct that merely happens to accompany speech. The Court refused to treat campaign finance rules as economic regulation that touched expression only incidentally. At the same time, the premise did not decide the case, because the Court held that the burden imposed by a limit on contributions differs in kind from the burden imposed by a limit on expenditures, and the constitutional fate of each category followed from that difference.

A contribution, in the reasoning of the per curiam opinion, is a general expression of support for a candidate. The contributor gives money to another person or committee, and the act of giving signals approval and allegiance, but the contribution does not itself state the contributor's own political message in the contributor's own words. The size of a contribution serves as a rough index of the intensity of that support, a larger gift indicating stronger support in a general way, yet the symbolic content of the act does not grow in proportion to the amount, and a limit on the amount therefore restricts one avenue of expression without silencing the contributor. The person whose contribution is capped remains free to speak independently, to spend without limit on the person's own advocacy, to volunteer, and to persuade others. Against that comparatively modest burden, the Court weighed the interest Congress had asserted, the prevention of corruption and the appearance of corruption in federal elections. Large contributions, the Court accepted, carry the danger of an improper understanding between donor and officeholder, and even the public perception of such understandings corrodes confidence in representative government. A ceiling on contributions addressed that danger directly, at the point where money passes into a candidate's hands, and the Court held that the interest was sufficient to justify the restraint the ceilings imposed.

Expenditure limits stood on the other side of the line because they operated differently. A ceiling on spending does not regulate a transfer of money to another person. It restricts the quantity of expression itself, determining how much speech a candidate or a citizen may purchase and therefore, in practical terms, how much communicating may be done. The Court treated such ceilings as direct restraints on political expression, and it found the justifications offered for them inadequate. In particular, the rationale of equalizing the relative ability of individuals and groups to influence the outcome of elections was rejected as an impermissible ground for restricting speech. The idea that government may limit the speech of some elements of society in order to enhance the relative voice of others, the Court held, is wholly foreign to the First Amendment. Once the equalization rationale was removed and the anticorruption interest was held to attach to contributions rather than to spending as such, the expenditure ceilings had no sufficient justification left, and they fell.

The distinction the opinion drew was therefore not between money that mattered to speech and money that did not. The Court conceded at the outset that both kinds of limits burdened expression, and it invalidated the expenditure ceilings precisely because the burden they imposed fell on expression itself. The distinction was between two ways in which money enters political communication. A contribution passes through the hands of another person, who decides how it will be used, and the contributor's own message is carried, if it is carried at all, by the candidate or committee that received the gift. An expenditure is the speaker's own act of communication, made with the speaker's own resources and in the speaker's own voice. A ceiling on the first was treated as a limit on a symbolic act of association and support, tolerable where it guarded against corruption. A ceiling on the second was treated as a limit on the amount of speech in the political arena, and the First Amendment, in the Court's reading, did not permit the government to decide that there had been enough of it. Every holding that followed in the case was an application of that single analytical move, repeated across the categories of the statute until each provision had been sorted to one side of the line or the other.

### What did Buckley v. Valeo decide, provision by provision?

The Court upheld the contribution limits, including the aggregate ceiling, and sustained disclosure and public financing, while striking the candidate expenditure ceilings, the independent expenditure ceiling, and the limits on a candidate spending personal funds, and it confined congressionally appointed commissioners to investigative and informational duties.

Taken category by category, the holdings tracked the four kinds of limits the 1974 amendments had imposed, and the pattern of survival and invalidation followed the reasoning exactly. The contribution limits were upheld. The ceilings on what an individual could give to a candidate, and the aggregate ceiling of 25,000 dollars on an individual's total contributions, were sustained as permissible restraints justified by the interest in preventing corruption and its appearance. Money given to candidates and committees could therefore remain subject to fixed legal maxima, and the basic premise of the 1974 design, that the inflow of money to campaigns was a fit subject for quantitative regulation, survived judicial review. The second category fared oppositely. The ceilings on candidate expenditures, the limits on what a campaign itself could spend in seeking office, were struck down. A candidate's spending of funds lawfully raised was expression by the candidate, and capping it capped the candidate's own speech. The third category met the same fate. The ceiling of 1,000 dollars on independent expenditures, on spending by individuals and groups acting independently of a candidate to support or oppose that candidate, was invalidated, because independent spending presented neither the corruption danger of a contribution handed to the candidate nor any justification the Court would accept for limiting the quantity of a citizen's expression. The fourth category, the limits on the amount of personal funds a candidate could spend on the candidate's own campaign, was likewise struck. A candidate's use of personal wealth to speak was expression like any other expenditure, and the interest in equalizing resources between a wealthy candidate and an opponent was precisely the equalization rationale the Court had held impermissible. Of the four sets of ceilings Congress had enacted in 1974, only the first remained.

Disclosure, by contrast, was upheld in general. The reporting and recordkeeping requirements of the statute, under which committees and contributors disclosed the sources and amounts of campaign money, were sustained against constitutional attack, and the principle that the financing of federal campaigns would be conducted on a public record survived the decision intact. One element of the disclosure regime was narrowed by construction. The reporting duty imposed on independent spenders, persons spending money of their own to influence an election outside a candidate's committee, was read down so that it reached spending for communications that expressly advocated the election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate. That narrowing construction, limiting the duty to express advocacy, kept the provision within constitutional bounds as the Court understood them, and it gave the phrase express advocacy a central place in the law that followed, because spending that stopped short of expressly advocating an electoral result stood outside the narrowed duty.

The survival of disclosure deserves emphasis within the inventory of holdings, because it meant that the oldest element of the federal regime was also its most durable. Reporting duties had been the core of federal campaign finance law since the predecessor statute, resting on the premise that the sources of campaign money were a legitimate subject of public knowledge whatever limits the law placed on the money itself. The 1974 amendments had greatly expanded that disclosure system, and the Court's decision left the expansion standing. A regime in which contributions were capped, spending was uncapped, and both were reported was not the regime Congress had designed, since Congress had paired its ceilings with disclosure as complementary halves of a single policy. It was, however, the regime the Constitution was held to permit, and administrators, candidates, and committees organized their conduct around it from the 1976 cycle onward.

Public financing was upheld as a voluntary bargain. The system of public funds for presidential campaigns enacted in the 1974 amendments did not compel any candidate to participate. A candidate could accept public money on the conditions Congress attached to it, including expenditure conditions that would have been unconstitutional if imposed directly on a candidate who declined the subsidy, or could refuse the money and finance a campaign privately without those conditions. Because participation was a choice, the conditions that accompanied the funds were treated as the terms of a bargain the candidate was free to reject, and the program was sustained. The distinction mattered beyond the presidential program itself, since it preserved a route by which Congress could attach spending conditions to public money that the First Amendment forbade it to impose on spending generally.

Justice White, dissenting in part, would have sustained far more of the statute, and his position deserves statement at its own strength rather than as a foil to the majority. In his view, Congress had legitimately treated unlimited spending in federal elections as a danger in itself, a force capable of distorting the electoral process and of drowning the voices of citizens of ordinary means beneath the spending of the wealthy, and the legislative judgment that the danger warranted ceilings on expenditures as well as on contributions was one the Court should have respected. Where the per curiam opinion saw expenditure ceilings as direct restraints on the quantity of expression that no sufficient interest could justify, Justice White saw a considered congressional response to a demonstrated problem of money in politics, enacted by the branch of government closest to the electoral process and best placed to judge its pathologies. His partial dissent accepted the constitutional framework of the case but rejected the majority's refusal to credit the congressional judgment on spending, and it identified the division the Court had drawn, between money given and money spent, as the point on which the statute's effectiveness would turn. The disagreement was not over whether elections could be corrupted by money, a premise common to the majority and the dissent alike. It was over the reach of the remedy Congress could choose. The majority confined the anticorruption interest to the moment of contribution and treated everything beyond that point as protected expression beyond legislative reach. Justice White treated the electoral process as a whole as the subject of legitimate congressional concern, and unlimited expenditures, in the position he stated, threatened that process in ways a legislature could act against even though the expenditures were also speech. The two positions framed the debate that every later amendment and every later case inherited, because each rested on a different account of what the First Amendment protects in an election: the quantity of expression each speaker can produce, or the integrity of the process in which all speakers compete.

The appointments holding struck at the enforcement machinery rather than at any limit. The Federal Election Commission created by the 1974 amendments included commissioners appointed by Congress, and the Court held that officers exercising executive functions could not be installed by that route. Commissioners appointed by Congress were confined to investigative and informational functions, the kinds of duties an agent of the legislature may perform. The executive functions of the Commission, the enforcement and administrative powers that involved executing the law against regulated persons, were reserved to officers appointed in conformity with the Appointments Clause of the Constitution, which channels the appointment of federal officers through the President and the Senate. The defect was structural and could not be cured by construction. A commission composed as the 1974 amendments had composed it could gather information and report, but it could not lawfully wield the enforcement authority the statute had assigned to it, and the agency at the center of the new system was disabled from performing its central task until Congress rebuilt it.

The Court nonetheless stayed its judgment on the Commission for thirty days, a pause designed to give Congress an opportunity to reconstitute the agency or to validate its actions by lawful means before the enforcement structure lapsed. The stay did not remove the practical disruption. As President Ford's signing statement on the 1976 amendments later recorded, the interruption reached the public financing program in operation, matching payments to presidential candidates were held up while the Commission's authority stood in doubt, and candidates twice sought relief to keep the payments moving during the interval between the decision and the statutory repair. A constitutional ruling about the manner of appointing commissioners thus produced an immediate administrative consequence in the middle of a presidential primary season, delaying funds that candidates had qualified for and around which campaign plans had been built, and demonstrating that the appointments defect was not an abstraction about organizational charts but a defect that stopped money and enforcement alike.

The repair came in the Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1976, Public Law 94-283, enacted as S. 3065 and signed by President Ford on May 11, 1976. Congress rebuilt the Commission on the lines the Court had drawn. All six commissioners were made presidential appointees, appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, so that every member exercising the Commission's executive authority held office through the appointment route the Constitution prescribes. The partisan balance rule was restated in general form as a cap providing that no more than three commissioners could belong to a single political party, preserving the structural guarantee of bipartisanship within the form the appointments holding required. Congress also conformed the limits provisions to the judgment. The expenditure ceilings the Court had struck were repealed as to candidates who did not take public money, removing from the statute the dead provisions the decision had invalidated rather than leaving unconstitutional ceilings on the books, while the voluntary public financing structure the Court had sustained, with its attendant conditions for participating candidates, was left in place.

The 1976 amendments did more than repair the damage the decision had done. Congress used the occasion to adjust the contribution rules, enacting a new limit of 20,000 dollars on individual giving to national party committees and a limit of 5,000 dollars on individual giving to any other political committee, extending the quantitative regulation of contributions into channels the original ceilings had not addressed in that form. The rules governing corporate and union fund solicitation were clarified, settling questions about the funds from which such organizations could seek political money and the persons from whom they could seek it. One anomaly survived the repair untouched. The statute's text retained the ex officio presence associated with the original Commission design, a leftover of the structure the Court had dismantled, and Congress enacted no amendment removing it. The occupants of the ex officio seats were finally removed not by legislation but by litigation, when the court of appeals acted in 1993 in Federal Election Commission v. NRA Political Victory Fund. The episode closed a repair that had otherwise been completed in 1976: the Commission that administered federal campaign finance law after Buckley v. Valeo was a body of presidential appointees administering a statute whose contribution limits, disclosure duties, and public financing program had been sustained, and whose expenditure ceilings had been removed first by judicial invalidation and then by congressional repeal.

## The Party-Building Door: How Unlimited Money Re-entered Through the Parties

The settlement that emerged from the repair work of 1976 had a divided character. One half of the older statute had fallen in litigation, and the half that survived regulated money in a particular place, namely money connected to candidates. Contribution limits governed what a donor could give to a candidate and what a candidate could receive, and the disclosure system tracked contributions and expenditures as the statute defined those terms. The surviving regime therefore had a clear center of gravity. It followed money as money moved toward a candidate, and it measured the obligations of donors, candidates, and committees by reference to that movement. Party money occupied a different position. Political parties raised and spent funds at the national level, where federal elections supplied the obvious frame, and also at the state and local level, where the same organization registered voters, maintained offices, printed material, and turned supporters out on election day in contests that mixed federal, state, and local candidates on a single ballot. Money held and spent at that lower level did not sit comfortably inside the candidate-centered definitions. It sat in a definitional borderland, and the borderland mattered because the statute reached only what its definitions reached.

The borderland was not a minor drafting untidiness. It went to the structure of American elections. A state party committee that registered voters in a county performed work that could help a candidate for the House of Representatives, a candidate for governor, and a candidate for the county courthouse in the same season, through the same act, paid for out of the same treasury. A local party committee that distributed campaign materials or organized transportation to the polls acted in a setting in which federal and nonfederal contests were inseparable in practice even when the law treated them as distinct categories. Any definition that classified the whole of such activity as federal expenditure would pull ordinary state party operations into the federal system of limits and prohibitions. Any definition that classified the whole of it as nonfederal would leave activity plainly connected to federal elections outside that system. The statute, after the 1976 repair, had no settled answer that commanded the field, and into that unsettled space the Commission and then Congress moved in the closing years of the decade. The sequence of those two moves, administrative recognition followed by statutory exclusion, opened the channel through which money without federal limits later flowed in very large amounts.

### Where did soft money come from?

Soft money came from a definitional opening rather than a spending grant. Late 1970s Commission advisory opinions recognized party building funds outside the federal account, and the 1979 amendments then excluded grassroots and party building spending by state and local party committees from the definitions of contribution and expenditure.

The administrative move came first. During the late 1970s, the Commission issued advisory opinions that recognized party building funds held outside the federal account. The recognition was significant less for any single request than for the classification it confirmed. A party committee could hold funds for building purposes in an account that the federal system did not treat as its federal account, and money placed there stood apart from the money that the committee raised and spent under federal limits for federal election activity. The opinions therefore drew a line inside the party treasury itself. On one side of the line stood the federal account, fed by contributions that satisfied federal amount limits and source restrictions and spent under federal rules. On the other side stood funds for party building, held separately and treated as belonging to a different regulatory world. Once that separation gained administrative recognition, the practical question changed. The question was no longer whether a party could organize its finances around two kinds of accounts. The question became how much activity could be assigned to the nonfederal side of the line, and that question Congress answered, in effect, in the amendments that closed the decade.

The Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1979 became law as Public Law 96-187, at 93 Stat. 1339, through the enacted bill H.R. 5010, and President Jimmy Carter signed the measure on January 8, 1980. The amendments carry the year 1979 in their name because that is the Congress year in which they passed, and the signature in January 1980 belongs to the same legislative sequence rather than to a separate enactment. Carter's signing statement objected to an unrelated solicitation provision in the measure, a detail that underlines the character of the bill as an assortment of adjustments rather than a single themed revision. The stated business of the amendments was paperwork relief. Congress presented the measure as a simplification of the compliance burden that the disclosure system placed on committees, candidates, and volunteers, and much of the text matched that presentation. Read at the level of its announced purpose, the 1979 package adjusted thresholds, audits, and exemptions. Read at the level of its operative definitions, the same package performed a second and larger act, and the distance between the announced purpose and the operative change is the central fact in the origin of soft money.

The paperwork provisions deserve statement at precision because they formed the public face of the amendments. The itemization threshold doubled, from 100 dollars to 200 dollars, so that smaller contributions no longer required the itemized treatment that the lower figure had demanded. Reporting thresholds rose as well, lifting the points at which reporting duties attached. The Commission lost the power to conduct random audits, and audits remained available only against committees selected for cause, a change that replaced routine exposure to examination with examination tied to a stated reason. Exemptions for volunteer activity widened, reducing the extent to which unpaid personal effort counted within the regulatory definitions. Each change, taken alone, reduced friction in the administration of the disclosure regime, and each could be defended in the vocabulary of burden reduction. A committee kept fewer itemized entries. A treasurer faced audit on cause rather than by lot. A volunteer gave time with less risk that the gift of time would be treated as a regulated contribution. The package cohered as relief, and that coherence explains why the amendments attracted little of the controversy that had surrounded the revisions of 1974 and 1976.

The operative change sat inside the definitions. The amendments took grassroots and party building spending by state and local party committees outside the definitions of contribution and expenditure. The categories named in the exclusion described the ordinary connective work of a party organization. Campaign materials fell within it. Voter registration fell within it. Get-out-the-vote work fell within it. Building funds, the money accumulated to construct and maintain the party as an institution, fell within it. None of these categories concerned a check written directly to a candidate, and that was the point. The exclusion addressed the borderland identified in the posture after 1976, and it resolved the classification question in favor of the nonfederal side. Spending that built the party, registered its supporters, and mobilized its voters at the state and local level was, for federal purposes, neither a contribution nor an expenditure. The words that triggered limits, prohibitions, and reporting duties in the federal system did not apply to it.

The mechanism deserves emphasis because a definitional exclusion performs its work differently from a spending authorization. Congress did not enact a provision stating that party committees could raise and spend unlimited sums for party building. No such grant appears in the amendments. Instead, Congress adjusted the reach of two defined terms, and the rest of the regulatory structure followed automatically. Contribution limits attach to contributions. Source prohibitions attach to contributions and expenditures. Reporting obligations, in large part, track contributions made and expenditures incurred. When an activity leaves the definitions, the obligations keyed to those definitions leave with it, without any further legislative sentence. The exclusion therefore created a lawful space rather than a licensed program. Money could be raised into that space in amounts that federal law did not cap, from the simple reason that federal law did not classify the raising and spending as the kind of transaction it capped. The absence of a federal limit was not an oversight layered on top of the exclusion. The absence was the exclusion, operating exactly as definitional drafting operates.

The allocation machinery that followed converted the exclusion from a classification into a working financial system. Party committees kept two kinds of accounts, a federal account subject to federal limits and a nonfederal account outside them, and committees paid for mixed activities by formula. A mixed activity was an activity that served both federal and nonfederal purposes at once, which described a large share of what state and local parties did in a federal election year. A registration drive enrolled voters for every contest on the ballot. A turnout operation delivered supporters to polling places where federal candidates appeared alongside state and local candidates on the same ticket. A headquarters, a staff, and printed material served the whole organization rather than a single race. Allocation allowed the committee to divide the cost of such an activity between its two accounts according to a prescribed formula, paying the federal share from regulated money and the nonfederal share from money that federal law did not limit. Through that division, unregulated money lawfully supported activity in federal election years, not by crossing the line between the accounts, but by honoring the line and paying the assigned share from the nonfederal side.

The machinery placed classification at the center of daily party administration. A treasurer receiving money or approving a payment first had to decide which side of the definitional line the transaction occupied, because that decision determined which account bore the cost and which body of rules governed the transaction. A purely federal activity belonged to the federal account. A purely nonfederal activity belonged outside it. The large middle territory of mixed activity required the formula, and the formula converted a legal distinction into a set of proportions that staff could apply to rent, salaries, printing, postage, and field operations without relitigating the character of the party's work on every occasion. That routinization mattered as much as the exclusion itself. A classification used once, in an unusual transaction, creates an exception. A classification embedded in the ordinary bookkeeping of every state and local committee creates a system, with its own habits, expectations, and fundraising practices built on top of it. By the time the amounts involved grew large, allocation was no longer an occasional accounting technique. Allocation was the ordinary way in which party organizations financed the shared infrastructure of campaigns that federal law divided into two regulatory categories.

Allocation also changed the economics of party fundraising. A donor whose giving to federal candidates and federal party accounts had reached the federal ceilings faced, in the nonfederal account, a request that carried no federal ceiling at all. A party fundraiser could describe a concrete institutional purpose for such a gift, a registration program, a turnout operation, a building fund, and the description was accurate as far as the categories of the exclusion went. The money entered an account that the party controlled, paid for activity that the party directed, and supported an organization whose entire purpose in a federal election year included the election of federal candidates, even though no dollar in the account counted as a contribution to any of them. The separation between the accounts was real as an accounting matter, and committees maintained it through the allocation formulas. The separation was less complete as a matter of organizational reality, since one party, one staff, and one election campaign stood behind both accounts. Soft money lived in that gap between accounting separation and organizational unity, and the gap was a product of the definitions rather than a violation of them. Nothing in the arrangement required concealment, evasion, or a strained reading of the statute. The arrangement followed from reading the statute exactly as the 1979 amendments had written the definitions.

The growth record shows how completely the parties took up the opening. The Brennan Center recorded 45 million dollars in soft money in 1988, a figure that placed the channel, less than a decade after the amendments, at a scale material to national party finance. By the 1992 cycle, the amount had risen to about 86 million dollars. The Congressional Research Service, reporting figures drawn from Commission data, recorded 262 million dollars in 1996 and 495 million dollars in 2000. The progression across those four benchmarks describes more than inflation in campaign costs. Between 1988 and 2000, the recorded total grew by an order of magnitude, and the largest increases in absolute terms fell in the final part of the sequence, as the total rose from about 86 million dollars in the 1992 cycle to 262 million dollars in 1996 and then toward half a billion dollars in 2000. Each benchmark rests on the source named with it, and the sources differ, the Brennan Center for the earlier figures and the Congressional Research Service, working from Commission data, for the later ones. Read together with that caution stated, the benchmarks trace a channel that moved from a supplementary source of party funds to one of the principal financial instruments of national politics within roughly a decade of serious use.

The channel belonged to both parties, and the figures for 2000 state the balance at precision. Of the 495 million dollars recorded for that cycle, Democratic committees accounted for 245.2 million dollars and Republican committees accounted for 249.9 million dollars, a division close to parity. Parity matters to the description of the institution. Soft money was not a device that one party discovered and the other resisted, and the near equality of the 2000 totals shows two national organizations drawing on the same definitional opening at comparable scale. Competition itself supplied part of the dynamic. A party that declined to raise nonfederal money while its rival raised such money in amounts approaching a quarter of a billion dollars would contest federal elections with a smaller registration apparatus, a smaller turnout operation, and a smaller institutional budget than its opponent, even though the money at issue could not lawfully be given to any federal candidate. The incentive operated symmetrically, and the totals reflect that symmetry. Soft money became, in that sense, a structural feature of party competition under the definitions as amended in 1979, maintained by the choices of both parties rather than by the advantage of either one.

The use of the money changed as the amounts grew. In its earlier form, the channel financed the activities named in the exclusion, the registration drives, turnout work, materials, and institutional maintenance that the phrase party building describes without strain. By the late 1990s, the money had migrated toward candidate-focused issue advertising. Party committees spent nonfederal funds on advertising that centered on a candidate and on the positions associated with that candidate, presented in the form of discussion of an issue rather than as an appeal connected to a campaign account. The migration followed the logic of the channel. Generic party building helped a party in diffuse ways across an entire ballot, while advertising concentrated attention on the contests that decided control of federal institutions, and the larger the sums in the nonfederal accounts grew, the stronger the pull toward the use with the most direct effect on those contests. The destination of the spending therefore moved closer and closer to the center that the federal definitions regulated, candidate-centered communication in a federal election, while the classification of the spending remained outside those definitions. That combination, proximity in substance and distance in classification, defined the mature soft money system and framed the legislative question that Congress eventually took up.

The form of the eventual response followed from the form of the opening. Because the channel rested on statutory definitions rather than on an administrative indulgence, no change of heart at the Commission could close it on the same terms that had created it. The advisory opinions of the late 1970s had recognized a separation between accounts, but the exclusion that gave the separation its scale after 1979 sat in the enacted text, and treasurers, fundraisers, and donors arranged their conduct around that text for more than two decades. Reversing the arrangement therefore required Congress to legislate against a settled practice, with reliance interests, established accounts, and two national party organizations built around the existing definitions. That requirement helps explain the interval between the migration of the money into candidate-focused advertising in the late 1990s and the statutory response of 2002. The practice to be ended was not an unintended gap exploited against the grain of the text. The practice ran with the grain of the text, and only a new text could redirect it.

The closure, when it came, arrived through a new statute rather than through reinterpretation of the old definitions. The 2002 statute, Public Law 107-155, signed on March 27, 2002, barred national party committees from raising or spending nonfederal money, with the bar taking effect on November 6, 2002, and the same statute restricted the use of such funds by state party committees in federal elections. The national committees, which had stood at the center of the growth record, lost access to the channel entirely, and state committees retained a narrower room that federal law bounded where federal elections were concerned. The details of that settlement, its litigation, and its consequences for party finance belong to the statute that enacted it. For the history of the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971 and its amendments, one fact completes the account. The channel that the 2002 statute closed is the channel that the 1979 amendments opened, when grassroots and party building spending by state and local committees left the definitions of contribution and expenditure and money without federal limits entered through the parties.

## The Two Arguments the Statute Has Never Reconciled, and the Record It Produced

Two incompatible accounts of what money does in an election have accompanied the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971 through every amendment, every court test, and every enforcement dispute since the modern limits took effect on January 1, 1975. Each account was stated in its strongest form by identifiable holders, each account captured part of the statute that survived, and neither account displaced the other. The text that emerged from the contest bears the marks of both, and the enforcement record that followed bears the marks of the contest itself.

### The anti-corruption case, as its holders state it

The anti-corruption case, in the form stated by the Buckley majority, begins with a transaction rather than with an abstraction. A contribution, on this account, is not merely a signal of support offered at a distance. A large contribution is an event inside a relationship between a giver with interests before the government and an officeholder with power over those interests. Holders of the argument state that size changes the character of the event. A small contribution expresses affiliation and adds one voice to a large number. A very large contribution creates gratitude, obligation, and expectation, and it does so whether or not any participant uses the language of bargain. The danger identified by the holders is therefore not confined to an explicit exchange of money for an official act. The danger extends to the quieter sequence in which access follows contribution, attention follows access, and decisions made under sustained attention drift toward the contributor without any single decision being purchasable or provably purchased.

Access occupies the center of the argument for a practical reason. Representation, as the holders describe the process, is rationed by time. An officeholder can meet only a limited number of people, return only a limited number of calls, and study only a limited number of briefs. When large contributors receive a disproportionate share of that scarce attention, the holders argue, representation itself is distorted even if every vote cast on the floor remains formally free. Constituents without money compete for the same hours and the same staff work against organized interests that have paid, in effect, for a place closer to the front of the line. The distortion does not require dishonesty in the officeholder. It requires only that human attention respond to financial support in predictable ways, and that a system permitting unlimited contributions predictably produce unequal access to the representative process.

The second strand of the case concerns appearance, and its holders treat appearance as an independent injury rather than as evidence of some other injury. The Buckley majority accepted that Congress could act against the appearance of corruption as well as against corruption itself, and the congressional findings tradition running from the 1974 debates forward repeatedly returned to the same point. On this account, democratic consent depends on a public belief that elections choose officeholders and that officeholders answer to voters. When citizens conclude that outcomes are arranged by large financial supporters, participation loses its rationale. A voter who believes that representation has been sold has little reason to register, to volunteer, or to accept an unfavorable result as legitimate. The holders state that the injury operates even in an election in which no bargain occurred, because the injury runs to confidence in the process, and confidence once lost alters behavior across the whole electorate. Appearance is therefore not a lesser substitute for proof in this argument. Appearance is the mechanism by which large contributions corrode the consent on which representative government rests.

From those two premises, the holders derive a preference for contribution limits and disclosure over broader prohibitions, and the Buckley majority framed the permitted tools in exactly that limited way. A contribution limit, on this account, addresses the point of maximum danger. The contribution is the moment at which money passes directly to a candidate and at which obligation can form, so a ceiling at that moment reduces the size of any obligation a single giver can create. The limit leaves the supporter free to speak independently, to volunteer, to persuade neighbors, and to associate with others for political action. Disclosure addresses the appearance strand by different means. A public record of who gave and who spent allows voters, journalists, and opposing campaigns to detect patterns, to test explanations, and to discount the statements of interested speakers accordingly. Commissioners favoring enforcement, including Commissioner Ann Ravel in her February 2017 report, carried the same reasoning into administration, holding that a statute built on limits and disclosure fails in its purpose when the agency charged with applying the statute cannot act on the record the statute requires candidates and committees to file.

The holders also answer the objection that proof of individual bargains is rare. On the stated reasoning, rarity of proof is a predictable feature of the conduct in question rather than evidence of its absence. A bargain over official action, if struck, would be concealed by both parties to it, and a rule that required proof of a completed bargain before Congress could act would leave Congress powerless against all but the clumsiest transactions. The 1974 debates, as the holders recount that tradition, treated the lesson of the preceding disclosure regime as institutional rather than personal. A law with reports and no administrator had been ignored, and a law that reached only some committees had been routed around. The remedy drawn from that experience was a comprehensive structure in which limits defined the permissible contribution, disclosure exposed the transaction, and a commission enforced both parts against all participants on the same terms.

Administrability forms part of the same stated case. A contribution ceiling, as its holders describe the device, is a rule known in advance, expressed as a figure, and applied without inquiry into the motive of any individual giver or the later conduct of any individual officeholder. Disclosure then supplies the evidentiary foundation that a motive based system would lack. Filed reports identify the source and size of contributions, committee records identify the recipients of expenditures, and the commission's audit and enforcement authority gives those records a reader with power to act on discrepancies. The holders argue that the combination matters more than either element standing alone. A limit without disclosure invites concealment through intermediaries, and disclosure without a limit documents obligations of a size the legislature has already judged dangerous while providing no means of reducing them.

A final element completes the strongest form of the case. The holders argue that contribution limits protect officeholders as well as voters. A candidate freed from the expectation of very large contributions is freed, on this account, from a fundraising contest in which each side must match the other or concede an advantage, and from the suspicion that attaches to every later decision touching a large supporter. The limit is described as a collective protection adopted in advance, sparing individual candidates the choice between dependence and defeat. Whether that description fits any particular race is a question the holders leave to the record of each cycle. The claim itself is structural. A system that permits unlimited direct contributions, the holders state, will produce either corruption, or the appearance of corruption, or both, and a legislature that has watched that production occur is entitled to interrupt it at the contribution, by the least speech-restricting means that reaches the danger.

### The free expression case, as its holders state it

The free expression case, in the form stated by the Buckley majority in its expenditure reasoning, begins at the opposite point. Political communication in a mass society, on this account, costs money at every step. Printing, broadcasting, organizing, traveling, and assembling an audience all require expenditure, and a limit on expenditure therefore operates as a limit on the communication itself rather than as a regulation of some separate financial conduct standing behind the communication. Holders of the argument state the point in terms of quantity. A ceiling on spending determines how many voters a message reaches, how often the message is repeated, and whether a campaign can answer an opponent before an election ends. A government that sets the ceiling has set, in practical effect, the volume of political debate permitted during the period when debate matters most, and it has done so through officials who are themselves participants in the electoral contest being regulated.

Incumbency gives the argument its institutional edge. An officeholder begins a campaign with advantages that no expenditure by the officeholder purchases in that cycle. Name recognition accumulated through years in office, a record that generates news coverage without campaign spending, established relationships with journalists and community organizations, and the staff and franked communication of the office itself all precede the first dollar of campaign expenditure. A challenger, by contrast, must purchase nearly every element of recognition. The challenger must introduce a name, establish credibility, and correct or answer the officeholder's account, and each of those tasks requires communication that costs money. Holders of the free expression case state that a spending limit therefore falls with unequal weight. The limit leaves the officeholder's accumulated advantages untouched while capping the one instrument by which a challenger can offset them. A rule presented as equal treatment of candidates operates, on this account, as protection for the candidates already known.

The third strand concerns the corruption rationale itself, and holders state it as a warning about expansion. Corruption, in its narrow sense, describes a bargain, and a bargain can be identified, prohibited, and punished. Influence and access, by contrast, have no natural boundary. Every effective speaker seeks influence, every organized constituency seeks access, and every successful campaign creates obligations of gratitude toward supporters of many kinds. If the prevention of influence counts as a sufficient reason for limiting political money, the holders argue, then the rationale reaches any financial support large enough to matter, and it reaches the speech that the support makes possible. The supervision that follows is not confined to finance. Officials administering such a rationale must decide which expenditures create too much influence, which speakers have become too effective, and which communications require restraint in the interest of the process. The holders state that a rationale without a stopping point licenses supervision of political debate itself, administered by the government whose members the debate is meant to judge.

Justice White's disagreement with the Buckley majority locates the boundary of the free expression holding with precision, because Justice White accepted much of the framework and rejected a specific part of its application. Justice White agreed that contributions could be limited, and his disagreement was directed at the majority's treatment of expenditures. On his stated position, limits on campaign spending were also a permissible means of serving the interests the statute invoked, and the majority erred in striking the expenditure limits that Congress had enacted. The free expression case in its judicial form therefore does not rest on a claim that every justice accepted its conclusions. The expenditure holding was contested from within the same decision that announced it, by a justice who shared the anti-corruption premises and disputed only the conclusion that spending stood on different constitutional ground from contributing. Later holders of the free expression position, described institutionally, included commissioners skeptical of broad enforcement theories and members of Congress who opposed tighter limits, and those holders repeated the majority's expenditure reasoning rather than Justice White's position in the disputes that reached the commission and the legislature in later decades.

The rebuttal to the appearance argument follows from the same premises. Holders of the free expression case state that public suspicion of money in politics, without proof of corrupt bargains, is too indeterminate a foundation for restricting communication. Suspicion can attach to any large expenditure, to endorsements by wealthy speakers, to newspaper support, and to the fundraising success of a popular candidate. A government empowered to restrict speech whenever speech creates an appearance of undue influence holds, on this account, a discretion that expands with public cynicism itself, so that declining trust produces broader power to restrict the debate through which trust might be rebuilt. The holders propose narrower tools instead. Bribery law punishes the bargain directly. Disclosure, which many holders of this position accepted as a lesser burden, exposes the source of funds without setting a ceiling on communication. Recusal and ethics rules govern the officeholder's conduct in office. Each of those tools, the holders state, addresses identified misconduct without rationing the speech of participants who have committed no misconduct at all.

Holders of the position add that expenditure does not command assent. Spending, on the stated account, purchases only the opportunity to persuade. A heavily financed campaign can lose to an opponent with a fraction of its budget when voters reject the message, distrust the messenger, or prefer the opponent's record, and electoral history in the holders' telling contains repeated instances of large expenditures failing to produce victory. A limit justified as a means of preventing the purchase of elections therefore rests, in this argument, on a premise that voters themselves refute at the polls with regularity. The communication reaches the audience, the audience weighs the communication against everything else known about the candidates, and the decision remains with the audience. Restricting the reach of the communication because the audience might be persuaded by it treats persuasion itself as the harm, and persuasion, the holders state, is the activity the electoral process exists to organize.

The strongest form of the case closes with a claim about who decides. Elections, on this account, assign the evaluation of speakers to voters. A voter who distrusts a candidate financed by large contributors can vote against that candidate, and a voter who values a challenger's message can support the challenger regardless of the message's cost. Limits transfer that evaluation to administrators and judges, who must classify expenditures, speakers, and motives under rules written before the contest begins. The holders state that errors in that classification fall predictably on outsiders, on new organizations without compliance staff, and on speakers whose arrangements fit poorly into categories designed for established campaigns. The statute, in the form the free expression holders would preserve, may expose money to view and may punish bargains proved against individuals. It may not, on their stated position, set the total quantity of political communication that a candidate, a committee, or an independent speaker is permitted to address to the electorate.

### The enforcement record in figures

The enforcement record supplies measurements for one part of the dispute, the capacity of the Federal Election Commission to act at all. Commissioner Ann Ravel's report of February 2017 measured the commission's voting record in closed enforcement matters across a decade. Deadlocked substantive votes in closed enforcement matters rose from 2.9 percent in 2006 to 30 percent in 2016, according to that report. Matters in which at least one vote deadlocked rose from 4.2 percent in 2006 to 37.5 percent in 2016, on the same source. Civil penalties moved in the opposite direction across the same period measured by the same report, falling from more than 5.5 million dollars in 2006 to 595,425 dollars in 2016. The Campaign Legal Center's 2021 analysis reported a comparable deadlock measure at about 40 percent for the years 2017 to 2020, extending the pattern described in the 2017 report across the following four years. A separate interruption compounded the voting record. The commission lacked a quorum from the end of August 2019 into December 2020, a period interrupted by a brief restoration of a quorum in the middle of 2020, and during the absence of a quorum enforcement matters could not proceed to resolution. Matters held past the five-year limitations period were extinguished by the passage of that period, so the cost of the quorum loss was measured in closed files as well as in delayed ones.

### What the statute never reached

An inventory of the statute's limits begins with the proposal that recurred and never became law. Public financing for congressional elections was proposed repeatedly and was never enacted, so the financing system the statute built for presidential elections had no congressional counterpart during the period ending with the 2020 election cycle. The second limit was judicial in origin. Spending undertaken independently of a candidate, freed by the 1976 holding in Buckley v. Valeo, stood outside the expenditure ceilings the statute had attempted to impose, and independent spending grew into a permanent channel alongside the regulated contributions the statute capped. The third limit was jurisdictional. Elections for state and local office lay outside the statute's reach, governed by separate state regimes of widely varying design, so a contributor or spender restricted in a federal race operated under different rules, or under no comparable rules, in races for governor, legislature, and municipal office. The fourth entry in the inventory is a sequence rather than a boundary. Each closure of a channel was followed by the opening or expansion of another. Contributions to candidates were capped, and money moved toward party activity outside the federal limits in the form described as soft money. Party channels were addressed in later legislation, and spending moved further toward organizations operating independently of candidates and parties. The sequence is stated here as the order in which the channels developed, without a finding on whether any single step caused the next. The statute's text, the court's expenditure holding, and the jurisdictional boundary together defined a regulated core, and political money in every period from 1975 to 2020 was also present outside that core, in amounts and through channels that the text of the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, as amended, did not purport to govern.
The enforcement figures and the inventory belong to the same description. A commission divided evenly between the two accounts of political money produced the voting pattern Commissioner Ravel measured for 2006 to 2016 and the Campaign Legal Center measured for 2017 to 2020, and a regulated core surrounded by unregulated channels produced a system in which the rules governing a contribution depended on the recipient, the independence of the spender, and the office at stake. Neither the figures nor the inventory resolves the underlying disagreement, because the disagreement concerns the meaning of the facts both sides record in common.

The statute settled less than the name promises and more than the narrow enactment of 1972 contained, and the distance between those two measures is the durable fact of the lineage. Disclosure settled first and lasted longest. A public record of contributions and expenditures, begun under the predecessor regime and rebuilt in 1972, survived the constitutional test of 1976 in general form and supplied the evidentiary base on which every other provision depended. Contribution limits settled second. The ceilings on giving, enacted in 1974 and sustained in Buckley v. Valeo, fixed the point at which money passed to a candidate as the point at which quantitative regulation was permissible, and the later course of adjudication begins from that distinction, as the chronology of [campaign finance supreme court cases](/2021/11/15/campaign-finance-supreme-court-cases/) sets out in sequence. Public financing settled as a bargain rather than as an entitlement: money in exchange for a spending limit, valid because acceptance was voluntary, and dependent for operation on candidates judging the exchange worth the price.

What never settled was the meaning of the distinction between giving and spending. The anti-corruption account, stated by the Buckley majority in the contribution reasoning and by Commissioner Ann Ravel in the enforcement context, treats a large contribution as the moment at which obligation forms and appearance corrodes consent. The free expression account, stated by the same majority in the expenditure reasoning and contested by Justice White from within the decision, treats a ceiling on spending as a ceiling on communication itself and rejects equalization as a ground for restraint. Each account governs part of the operative law. Contribution limits embody the first account, and the absence of expenditure ceilings embodies the second, and a single statute carries both principles without a principle for ranking the principles when a new channel presses against the boundary between the categories. Soft money pressed against that boundary for two decades, because party building money was neither a contribution to a candidate nor an expenditure by a candidate, and the closure of the channel required a new statute rather than an interpretation of the old definitions. The settlement enacted in 2002 is treated in the guide to the [Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002](/2021/09/15/bipartisan-campaign-reform-act-2002/), and the direct comparison of the two regimes appears in the analysis of [FECA and BCRA campaign law compared](/2022/01/01/feca-vs-bcra-campaign-law/).

The enforcement design holds the same tension in institutional form. An even division between the parties, joined to a requirement of four affirmative votes, guarantees that coercive action against a campaign carries support from across the partisan division, and the same arithmetic guarantees that contested questions can close without a merits answer. Commissioner Ravel measured the output of that arithmetic between 2006 and 2016 in deadlocked votes and falling civil penalties, and the Campaign Legal Center measured a comparable deadlock rate for 2017 to 2020, while the loss of a quorum from August 2019 into December 2020 suspended enforcement altogether for a period. Description of those figures does not decide whether the design failed or whether the design performed a protective function at a measurable price. The broader consequences of the operating record for candidates, parties, and voters are assessed in the analysis of [campaign finance law and its impact](/2021/12/15/campaign-finance-law-impact/). A neighboring disclosure regime of the same decade, built on financial reporting by public officials rather than by campaigns, is treated in the guide to the [Ethics in Government Act of 1978](/2021/10/15/ethics-in-government-act-1978-guide/).

The abandonment of public financing completes the pattern of a provision that survived adjudication and faded in operation. The checkoff participation rate fell from 28.7 percent on 1980 returns to 5.1 percent on 2012 returns, the designation rose from 1 dollar to 3 dollars without reversing the direction, the general election grant passed from universal acceptance between 1976 and 2004 to refusal by every major party nominee in 2012, 2016, and 2020, and convention funding ended by statute in 2014. None of those steps repealed the bargain enacted in 1974. Each step repriced the bargain, and candidates and filers responded to the price. The statute as a whole therefore records two kinds of legal change side by side: change by decision, in which a court or a Congress states a rule and the rule alters, and change by response, in which a rule stands untouched while the conduct governed by the rule moves elsewhere. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Federal Election Campaign Act create?

The act created a federal disclosure system for campaign money in the form enacted in 1972. Political committees expecting contributions or expenditures above 1,000 dollars in a calendar year registered and filed quarterly reports, supplemented by reports before and after an election. Reports itemized contributions above 100 dollars, committees kept records of contributions above 10 dollars, and contributions in the name of another person were banned. The act also created ceilings on media spending and on contributions by a candidate and immediate family to the candidate's own campaign, and a statutory framework for separate segregated funds maintained by corporations and labor organizations. Administration rested with three supervisory officers, and no enforcement commission formed part of the enacted design.

### Q: Why is the Federal Election Campaign Act commission designed to deadlock?

The designed to deadlock description is an analytical claim about structure, paired with a counter-reading that treats the structure as a protection. As rebuilt in 1976, the Commission held six voting members appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, and no more than three members could belong to one political party. Enforcement required four affirmative votes to find reason to believe, to find probable cause, to authorize conciliation, and to file a civil action. A division of three to three therefore closed a matter without a merits determination. One reading calls that outcome deadlock by design. The counter-reading calls the same arithmetic a safeguard, because action against a campaign required support from at least one member appointed from across the partisan division.

### Q: What contribution limits did the Federal Election Campaign Act set?

The contribution limits arrived with the amendments of 1974, principally in section 608(b) of title 18, United States Code. An individual could contribute no more than 1,000 dollars to a candidate and the candidate's authorized committees for any single election, and a primary and a general election counted as separate elections. A multicandidate committee could contribute no more than 5,000 dollars per candidate per election. Total contributions by an individual in a calendar year were capped at 25,000 dollars. The amendments of 1976 added a limit of 20,000 dollars per year on individual contributions to a national party committee and 5,000 dollars per year on contributions to any other political committee.

### Q: Did the Federal Election Campaign Act create public financing?

No, in the precise sense that the campaign act signed on February 7, 1972 contained no public financing system. The money mechanism came from the Revenue Act of 1971, Public Law 92-178, which created the 1 dollar tax checkoff and the Presidential Election Campaign Fund through a floor amendment offered by Senator John Pastore. The campaign amendments of 1974 then built the payment system on that fund, extending payments to primary matching funds, general election grants, and nominating convention grants. The fund first operated for the 1976 elections, when each major party nominee received about 21.8 million dollars for the general election, according to Commission records. Creation of the fund and creation of the payments were therefore separate acts in separate statutes.

### Q: What did the 1974 amendments add to the Federal Election Campaign Act?

The amendments of 1974, Public Law 93-443, signed on October 15, 1974, substantially rewrote the act signed in 1972. The amendments added four categories of limit covering contributions, candidate expenditures, independent expenditures, and candidate personal funds, with the limits provisions taking effect on January 1, 1975. Disclosure was strengthened and centralized around one principal campaign committee for each candidate, cash contributions above 100 dollars were prohibited, and limits on honoraria were imposed. The amendments created the Federal Election Commission in section 310, completed the presidential public financing system begun through the tax checkoff, repealed the media spending ceilings enacted in 1972, and ended the 1940-era ban on contributions by federal government contractors.

### Q: Where did soft money come from under the Federal Election Campaign Act?

Soft money came from a definitional opening in party finance. During the late 1970s, Commission advisory opinions recognized that state party committees could maintain building funds outside the federal account. The amendments of 1979, Public Law 96-187, signed on January 8, 1980, then codified and widened the opening by taking spending by state and local party committees on grassroots and party building activity outside the definitions of contribution and expenditure. Covered activity included campaign materials such as signs, bumper stickers and sample ballots, voter registration and get-out-the-vote drives, and party building funds for headquarters and administration, provided the activity was not for the purpose of influencing a particular federal election under the statutory tests then applied. Money for that activity therefore sat outside federal limits and source prohibitions, and the name soft money attached to that outside position.

### Q: What did the Federal Election Campaign Act replace?

The act repealed and replaced the Federal Corrupt Practices Act regime. The Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1925, enacted on February 28, 1925, had consolidated the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1910, known as the Publicity Act, and its 1911 amendments, and had remained the basic federal campaign finance law for nearly half a century. That regime required quarterly disclosure reports, itemized contributions over 100 dollars, and set spending limits of 25,000 dollars for Senate campaigns and 5,000 dollars for House campaigns, while coverage reached only political committees active in two or more states. The published history of the Commission summarized the failure in direct terms: no institution was charged with administering the law, the provisions were largely ignored, and enforcement was left to Congress, which rarely acted.

### Q: What is the public law number of the Federal Election Campaign Act?

The public law number is Public Law 92-225. The enacted bill was S. 382 of the 92nd Congress, and the statute was printed at 86 Stat. 3. President Richard Nixon signed the measure on February 7, 1972, although the short title carries the year 1971. As enacted, the statute was codified at title 2, United States Code, sections 431 and following. An editorial reclassification in 2014 moved the provisions to title 52, United States Code, sections 30101 and following, without changing enacted substance. The companion numbers for the major amendments follow the same pattern, with the amendments of 1974 enacted as Public Law 93-443, the amendments of 1976 as Public Law 94-283, and the amendments named for 1979 as Public Law 96-187.

### Q: Who enforces the Federal Election Campaign Act?

The Federal Election Commission enforces the act through the civil enforcement authority assigned by the amendments of 1974 and rebuilt by the amendments of 1976. The Commission holds six voting members appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, serving six-year staggered terms, with no more than three members affiliated with the same political party. The Commission took over custody of disclosure from the three supervisory officers and also holds audit, advisory opinion, rulemaking, and public financing administration functions. Four affirmative votes are required at the decisive enforcement stages, covering a reason to believe finding, a probable cause finding, conciliation, and the filing of a civil action, so an evenly divided Commission closes a matter without a merits determination.

### Q: What is the difference between hard money and soft money under the Federal Election Campaign Act?

Hard money is money raised under federal limits and source prohibitions, and soft money is money raised outside the federal definitions for party building and grassroots activity. A contribution to a candidate subject to the 1,000 dollar per election ceiling is hard money, counted, reported, and tested against the statute. Soft money entered through the opening recognized by Commission advisory opinions in the late 1970s and widened by the amendments of 1979, under which specified state and local party spending sat outside the definitions of contribution and expenditure. National party soft money reached 495 million dollars in the 2000 cycle, divided between Democratic committees at 245.2 million dollars and Republican committees at 249.9 million dollars, on Commission data reported by the Congressional Research Service.

### Q: How does presidential public financing work under the Federal Election Campaign Act?

Presidential public financing under the completed design operated as an exchange of public money for a spending limit. For the primaries, a candidate qualified by raising more than 5,000 dollars in contributions of 250 dollars or less in each of at least twenty states, and the Treasury matched dollar for dollar the first 250 dollars of each individual contribution, with payments beginning on January 1 of the election year and total matching payments capped at one half of the primary spending limit. For the general election, a major party nominee who accepted the grant received the full spending limit, set at a base figure of 20 million dollars adjusted for cost of living, and could not accept private contributions for the general election campaign itself. Convention grants formed the third payment before a statute enacted in 2014 terminated convention funding and redirected the money to pediatric research.

### Q: What is the tax checkoff and where did it come from?

The tax checkoff is a designation on an individual income tax return that directs part of a tax liability to the Presidential Election Campaign Fund. A taxpayer designated 1 dollar of tax already owed, or 2 dollars on a joint return, and the designation did not increase the amount of tax owed. The checkoff originated outside the campaign act, in the Revenue Act of 1971, Public Law 92-178, through a floor amendment offered by Senator John Pastore. An earlier fund act of 1966 had been terminated in 1967 before taking effect. The checkoff first appeared on 1972 returns, the fund first operated for the 1976 elections, and Congress tripled the designation to 3 dollars for 1993 returns as participation fell from a peak of 28.7 percent on 1980 returns.

### Q: How long do Federal Election Commission members serve and who appoints them?

Commission members serve six-year staggered terms. Under the design enacted in 1974, two voting members were appointed by the President pro tempore of the Senate, two by the Speaker of the House, and two by the President, with each authority acting on the recommendations of party leaders and barred from appointing more than one member of the same political party in a pair. The Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House sat as ex officio members without a vote. Buckley v. Valeo confined congressionally appointed members to investigative and informational functions, and the amendments of 1976 rebuilt the appointment route, under which all six voting members are appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate and no more than three may belong to one political party.

### Q: What happened to the expenditure limits in the Federal Election Campaign Act?

The expenditure limits enacted in 1974 were struck by the Supreme Court and then repealed for candidates outside the public financing system. In Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), the Court held that ceilings on candidate expenditures directly restricted the quantity of political expression and that equalizing the relative ability of speakers to affect elections was not a permissible justification for the restriction. The candidate expenditure ceilings therefore fell, together with the ceiling on independent expenditures and the limits on personal funds. The amendments of 1976 repealed the struck expenditure ceilings for candidates who did not accept public funds. Spending limits applied after that repeal only as a condition accepted voluntarily in exchange for public financing, because participation in that system was a choice rather than a compulsion.

### Q: Did the Federal Election Campaign Act limit how much of their own money candidates could spend?

The enacted statutes limited candidate and family money, and the Supreme Court struck the limit. Both the act signed in 1972 and the amendments of 1974 capped contributions by a candidate and the candidate's immediate family to the candidate's own campaign at 50,000 dollars for President and Vice President, 35,000 dollars for the Senate, and 25,000 dollars for the House. In Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), the Court held those personal funds limits unconstitutional, treating a candidate's use of personal and family resources as expenditure protected against the equalization rationale. After the decision, a candidate's spending of personal funds carried no ceiling under the statute, while contributions from other persons to the same campaign remained subject to the contribution limits the Court upheld.

### Q: What spending limits did the Federal Election Campaign Act set for House and Senate candidates?

The amendments of 1974 set candidate expenditure limits in section 608(c) of title 18, United States Code. A House candidate could spend 70,000 dollars in a primary election campaign and 70,000 dollars in a general election campaign, because each election carried a separate ceiling. A Senate candidate faced a formula rather than a flat figure. The primary limit was the greater of 100,000 dollars or eight cents multiplied by the voting-age population of the state, and the general election limit was the greater of 150,000 dollars or twelve cents multiplied by the voting-age population. Presidential candidates received ceilings of 10 million dollars for the primary campaign period and 20 million dollars for the general election. Buckley v. Valeo struck the candidate expenditure ceilings in 1976.

### Q: What events led Congress to rewrite the Federal Election Campaign Act?

The rewrite followed the abuses documented in the presidential campaign of 1972. The Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities, known as the Ervin Committee, and parallel investigations documented secret campaign funds, illegal corporate contributions, and money raised outside the disclosure system by the President's re-election committee. Those findings struck at the design signed in 1972 from two directions. Secret funds defeated disclosure, because money held outside the reported system generated no report for voters or officials to inspect. Illegal corporate contributions showed a prohibition operating inside an enforcement vacuum, because the corporate ban in the Tillman Act of 1907 had no administering institution behind the text. Congress responded in the amendments of 1974 with limits, centralized disclosure, public financing, and a commission.

### Q: Is the Federal Election Campaign Act in force?

Through September 1, 2021, the act, as amended and as narrowed by Buckley v. Valeo, supplied the statutory framework for federal campaign finance at title 52, United States Code, sections 30101 and following, and no repeal of the framework appears in the record through that date. The operative content at that date differed sharply from the text enacted in 1974. Contribution limits and disclosure provisions sustained in 1976 formed part of the framework, expenditure ceilings had been struck and repealed, public financing provisions stood in the statute while major party nominees declined the general election grant in the 2012, 2016, and 2020 cycles, and the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 had closed the national party soft money channel. Survival of the framework and survival of each original provision are therefore separate questions with separate answers.

### Q: Where is the Federal Election Campaign Act codified?

As enacted in 1972, the act was codified at title 2, United States Code, sections 431 and following. The amendments of 1974 placed the new limits in title 18, United States Code, as section 608 and following, and those limits were later moved to title 2 before the editorial reclassification of 2014 carried the campaign finance provisions to title 52, United States Code, sections 30101 and following. A citation to section 431 in a source from the 1970s and a citation to section 30101 in a later source can therefore identify the same statutory lineage at different points in the codification history. The public financing payments sat in a separate location, in subtitle H of the Internal Revenue Code, chapters 95 and 96.

### Q: What should a student know about the Federal Election Campaign Act for an exam?

For an exam, the central point is sequence rather than a single date. The act named for 1971 was signed on February 7, 1972, and contained disclosure and media ceilings without general contribution limits, a commission, or public financing. The tax checkoff came from the Revenue Act of 1971, and the amendments of 1974 added the four categories of limit, the Federal Election Commission, and the completed public financing system. Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), upheld contributions and struck expenditures, and the amendments of 1976 rebuilt the Commission as a body of presidential appointees. The amendments named for 1979 opened the soft money channel through party building definitions, and participation figures, from a checkoff peak of 28.7 percent on 1980 returns to 5.1 percent on 2012 returns, measure the decline of the financing system.
