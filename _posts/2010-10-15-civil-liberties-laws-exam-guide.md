Examinations on this subject rarely reward the candidate who knows the most names. They reward the candidate who can place a proposition in its correct legal home before reaching for authority, and civil liberties laws are tested in precisely that discriminating way. A question may present a person taken into custody, a period of detention, a board that has or has not reported, or a representation that has or has not been considered, and the marks turn on a prior and quieter act of identification. Which clause governs this situation, which statute if any operates under that clause, and which decided case if any supports the proposition being advanced. That order is the namable claim on which sound answers in this area rest, and it deserves to be stated plainly at the outset. Clause first, statute second, case third. The candidate who reverses the order, reasoning backwards from a remembered case to a provision never properly identified, may write fluently and still answer a question that was not asked.

The loss of marks follows a recognisable pattern. A candidate memorises case names and statute names as though they were self-sufficient units of learning, detached from the text that gives them meaning. The names are retained, often accurately, but the relationship between them is not understood, so a safeguard belonging to arrest is offered as an answer about detention, or a proposition drawn from one factual setting is transferred without examination to another setting where the governing clause has switched the safeguard off. The examiner does not need to set an obscure question to expose that weakness. An ordinary question, framed around an ordinary distinction, is enough, because the distinction between arrest and preventive detention runs through the whole area and governs which rights exist, which authority must act, within what period, and by what procedure. A candidate who has not fixed that distinction in advance must improvise it under examination pressure, and improvisation tends to reproduce the most familiar phrase rather than the correct clause.

There is a second habit that costs marks. Many candidates treat a case as a slogan, remembering a short form of words associated with its name and deploying that form wherever the surrounding vocabulary looks similar. A case, however, holds a proposition within limits, and those limits are part of what the case decided. To cite authority for a proposition it does not hold is worse than citing no authority at all, because it tells the examiner that the name was learned without the reasoning. The study method that avoids these errors is unglamorous. Read the clause. Identify the situation to which it applies. Only then attach the statute, and only after that attach the case. Revision arranged by clause, rather than by case name, encourages that productive direction, starting with the constitutional text and asking what each part of it does, to whom it applies, whom it excludes, and what condition it imposes on the State.

## Where civil liberties laws sit in the examinations

The material sits principally in the polity and constitution portion of the civil services general studies papers, in the constitutional law portion of the law optional, and in state judicial services examinations. That spread explains why the same body of law can appear in markedly different registers. In the general studies setting, the emphasis tends to fall on constitutional structure, on the relationship between a right and its exceptions, and on the place of an institution within the scheme of safeguards. In the law optional, the candidate is expected to handle the text with greater doctrinal closeness, tracing how a clause is worded, how an exception is framed, and how a proposition is confined by the language that creates it. State judicial services examinations test the material with a more doctrinal and case driven slant, so the ability to state what a case actually decided, and to resist stating more than it decided, carries particular weight there. A preparation strategy that serves one setting does not automatically serve the others. The candidate who learns only broad themes will be under-prepared for doctrinal precision, while the candidate who learns only case propositions without the clause architecture beneath them will struggle to organise an answer when the question is framed structurally rather than by reference to a named dispute.

Certain question types recur as types, and recognising the type is often the first step towards answering the question that has actually been set. One recurring type requires clause and provision identification, where the candidate must locate the governing text before discussing it, and where a wrong location at the start invalidates much of what follows however well written it may be. Another requires statute identification and status, where the candidate must name the relevant law and state correctly whether the provision relied upon is in force, in what form it operates, and whether an amendment said to have changed it has in fact taken effect. A third type turns on a case proposition, where the candidate must state what a decision holds and, with equal care, what it does not hold. A fourth turns on institution identification, where the question presents a body performing a function within the safeguard scheme and asks, in substance, whether the candidate understands what that body is, how it is composed, and what consequence follows from its report or from the absence of its report. A fifth turns on federalism allocation, where the issue is the distribution of authority within the constitutional structure and the candidate must place the power in question in its correct location rather than assume that a subject involving liberty or detention belongs automatically to a single level of government. None of these types rewards a recital of everything the candidate knows about the area. Each rewards selection, and selection depends on identification.

Where the marks leak can be described with some precision, because the same three errors recur across otherwise different answers. The first is confusing arrest safeguards with detention safeguards. Arrest and preventive detention are not two names for one event, and the safeguards attached to each are not interchangeable. An answer that carries a safeguard from the arrest setting into the detention setting, without checking whether the governing clause preserves it there, builds its conclusion on a right that may have been expressly taken away. The second error is citing a case for a proposition it does not hold. This error often arises from second hand learning, where a case has been reduced in notes to a line that the decision itself would not support, and the candidate reproduces the line because it is short, memorable and associated with a famous name. The examiner, reading for the proposition rather than the name, marks the answer down for the mismatch. The third error is stating amended law that never commenced. Amendment and commencement are separate events, and an amendment that has received assent but has not been brought into force does not alter the operative text. An answer that states the amended position as the law in force is not partly correct. On the point in issue, it is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that a prepared candidate is expected to avoid entirely.

These leaks share a common cause. Each arises when the candidate reaches the end of the analytical chain before completing the beginning. The safeguard is remembered before its clause is checked. The case name is remembered before its proposition is verified. The amendment is remembered before its commencement is confirmed. In the answer itself, the governing clause should therefore be named early, in terms, so that the examiner can see that the answer stands on the correct foundation before it develops. Statutory material should be introduced as material operating under that clause rather than as a substitute for it, and case material should be introduced for the proposition it supports, expressed narrowly enough to be defensible.

## The clause architecture of Article 22

Article 22 rewards clause by clause reading more directly than almost any other part of this subject, because its internal division is itself the central teaching point. Clause (1) provides that a person who is arrested has the right to be informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds of arrest, and the right to consult and to be defended by a legal practitioner of choice. Two distinct protections are joined in that single clause, and each should be noticed separately. The first is informational. The arrested person is to be told why the arrest has been made, and the obligation is expressed with a measure of urgency in the words as soon as may be. The second is professional. The arrested person may consult a legal practitioner of choice and may be defended by such a practitioner. The choice belongs to the arrested person, and the clause speaks of consultation and defence, which are the ordinary incidents of a person facing the process that follows an arrest. Clause (2) then adds a protection of a different kind. A person who is arrested must be produced before a magistrate within twenty four hours, excluding the time necessary for the journey. The protection here is institutional and temporal. Custody following arrest is not to continue on the authority of the arresting power alone beyond the stated period, and the clock is defined with care, the journey time being placed outside the twenty four hours rather than left to argument. Read together, clauses (1) and (2) describe the safeguard scheme for ordinary arrest. Information, access to a practitioner, and prompt production before a magistrate are its three elements, and each element answers a different risk in the period immediately following an arrest.

### Which clauses of Article 22 govern ordinary arrest, and which govern preventive detention?

Clauses (1) and (2) govern ordinary arrest, securing information about the grounds, access to a legal practitioner, and production before a magistrate within twenty four hours. Clauses (3) to (7) govern preventive detention, with clause (3) removing the arrest safeguards from a person detained under a preventive detention law.

The turn in the Article comes at clause (3), and everything in this area depends on reading that turn without softening it. Clause (3) provides that clauses (1) and (2) do not apply to an enemy alien, and do not apply to a person who is arrested or detained under a law providing for preventive detention. The effect is exclusionary. The very safeguards that clauses (1) and (2) have just conferred are taken away from the two classes named, so far as those clauses are concerned. A detenu under a preventive detention law therefore cannot claim, as a matter of Article 22, the clause (1) right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner, nor the clause (2) right to production before a magistrate within twenty four hours. That does not leave the detenu without safeguards under the Article. It means that the safeguards for detention are found elsewhere in the Article, in clauses (4) to (7), and that they are safeguards of a different character. Conflating the two sets is the foundational error in this subject, and clause (3) is the textual reason why the conflation is an error rather than a permissible approximation.

Clause (4) supplies the first and most examined of the detention safeguards. It bars detention beyond three months unless an Advisory Board, consisting of persons who are, have been, or are qualified to be judges of a High Court, reports before the expiration of the three month period that there is in its opinion sufficient cause for the detention. The safeguard is therefore conditional in structure. Detention up to the stated period stands on one footing, and detention beyond it requires the opinion of an Advisory Board of the prescribed composition, given within the prescribed time. The clause also imposes an outer limit of a different kind, because detention under clause (4) may in no case exceed the maximum period prescribed under clause (7). Clause (5) addresses communication and challenge. Where a person is detained under a preventive detention law, the authority making the order must, as soon as may be, communicate to that person the grounds on which the order has been made, and must afford the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order. Communication of grounds and opportunity of representation belong together, since a representation made without knowledge of the grounds would be an empty form. Clause (6) then qualifies clause (5). Nothing in clause (5) requires the authority to disclose facts which that authority considers to be against the public interest to disclose. The qualification is directed to facts, and it rests on the consideration of the authority making the order, a structure that candidates should state accurately rather than expand into a general power to withhold the grounds themselves. Clause (7) completes the scheme by empowering Parliament to prescribe, by law, the classes of cases and the circumstances in which a person may be detained for a period longer than three months without obtaining the opinion of an Advisory Board, the maximum period for which a person may be detained under a preventive detention law, and the procedure to be followed by an Advisory Board in an inquiry.

The clean split within the Article is the highest yield single fact in the area, and the proposition deserves emphasis because so much else follows from it. Clauses (1) and (2) address arrest. Clauses (3) to (7) address preventive detention, beginning with the exclusion that defines the boundary between the two regimes. A candidate who holds that split firmly can approach a question by first asking which side of the line the facts fall on, and that single decision determines which safeguards are even candidates for discussion. On the arrest side, the discussion concerns grounds, consultation and defence by a practitioner of choice, and production before a magistrate within twenty four hours excluding journey time. On the detention side, the discussion concerns the three month threshold and the Advisory Board under clause (4), the communication of grounds and the opportunity of representation under clause (5), the public interest qualification under clause (6), and the prescribing power of Parliament under clause (7). The two discussions share a vocabulary of liberty and custody, which is why they are so easily confused, but they do not share a safeguard scheme, and treating shared vocabulary as shared law is the precise mechanism by which marks are lost. Understanding [the preventive detention system as a whole](/2010/06/01/preventive-detention-law-india-guide/) begins with respecting that separation, because the system is built on the premise that detention without trial is governed by its own specified safeguards rather than by the safeguards that attend an ordinary arrest.

Examiners exploit the muddle in predictable ways, and anticipating the exploitation is part of preparation. A question may describe a detention and invite the candidate to discuss production before a magistrate, relying on the candidate to import clause (2) across the clause (3) boundary. A question may describe a detenu and invite discussion of a right to be defended by a legal practitioner, relying on the candidate to import clause (1) in the same way. A question may compress arrest and detention into a single narrative, so that the candidate must separate the stages and assign each stage to its governing clauses rather than apply one set of safeguards to the whole sequence. In each case the trap is placed in the open, at the boundary that clause (3) draws, and it catches candidates whose knowledge is organised by topic headings such as personal liberty rather than by the text of the Article.

The most widely repeated version of the muddle is the claim that Article 22 guarantees a lawyer to every person deprived of liberty. Stated in that breadth, the claim is incorrect, and its incorrectness is instructive because it shows how a true proposition about one clause becomes a false proposition about the whole Article. Clause (1) does confer a right to consult and to be defended by a legal practitioner of choice, but it confers that right on a person who is arrested, and clause (3) removes clauses (1) and (2) from a person arrested or detained under a preventive detention law. The consultation right in clause (1) is therefore switched off for a detenu by clause (3), as a matter of the text of Article 22 itself. Representation before an Advisory Board, where it arises within the detention scheme, is a different and narrower thing from the clause (1) right to consult and be defended by a practitioner of choice. It belongs to the Board procedure on the detention side of the Article, and it is not a continuation of the arrest safeguard under another name. Summaries tend to preserve the attractive right while dropping the exclusion that limits it. The text preserves both, and the examination tests both.

## The 44th Amendment trap: the amendment that is not in force

No single point in this area discriminates between prepared and unprepared candidates as cleanly as the status of Section 3 of the Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978. The provision is well known, widely reproduced in notes, and persistently misstated, because the story of its enactment is easier to remember than the story of its commencement. Section 3 would have substituted clause (4) of Article 22. Under the substitution, the period after which an Advisory Board must report would have been reduced from three months to two months, so that detention beyond the shorter period would have required the Board opinion that the unamended clause attaches to the longer one. The substitution would also have reconstituted Advisory Boards. Boards were to be constituted on the recommendation of the Chief Justice of the appropriate High Court, with a serving judge of a High Court as chairman and with members who were serving or retired judges of a High Court. The contrast with the unamended clause (4) should be stated exactly. The clause in force speaks of persons who are, have been, or are qualified to be judges of a High Court. The uncommenced substitution spoke of a differently composed Board, recommended through the Chief Justice of the appropriate High Court and chaired by a serving High Court judge. A candidate who merges the two compositions, or who attributes the substituted composition to the law in force, has fallen into the trap in one of its standard forms.

### Is the two-month advisory board period the law in force under Article 22?

No. The two month period in Section 3 of the Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978 has never been brought into force because the required notification was never issued. The unamended clause (4) therefore continues to operate, and an Advisory Board must report before detention exceeds three months.

The reason the substitution never became operative lies in the commencement structure of the Amendment Act itself. The Act received Presidential assent on 30 April 1979. Its other provisions were brought into force in stages in 1979. Section 3, however, was left to be commenced by notification, and that notification has never been issued. The consequence is stark and should be stated without qualification. Section 3 has never been notified, so the substituted clause (4) never replaced the original clause (4). The unamended clause (4), with its three month period and its Boards composed of persons who are, have been, or are qualified to be judges of a High Court, remains the law. Assent made the Amendment Act an Act. Assent did not, by itself, commence every provision within it, and where the Act left a provision to be brought into force by notification, the absence of the notification is decisive. Candidates who treat the passage and assent of an amendment as equivalent to the amendment being in force collapse two legal events into one, and on this topic that collapse produces a wrong statement of the operative constitutional text.

The point was tested directly in A K Roy v Union of India, decided on 28 December 1981 by a five judge Constitution Bench presided over by Chandrachud CJ. The Supreme Court upheld the National Security Act, and the Court declined to issue a mandamus compelling the Central Government to bring Section 3 of the 44th Amendment Act into force. That refusal matters for examination purposes because it forecloses a line of argument that candidates sometimes advance as though it were available, namely that the Court required the executive to commence the provision. The Court did no such thing. Gupta J, dissenting on this point, would have directed commencement within two months, and the dissent should be identified as a dissent when it is mentioned, because attributing the dissenting direction to the Court as a whole reverses the outcome on the very issue for which the case is cited in this context.

A subsidiary detail in the history of the National Security Act reinforces the lesson and is worth learning in its correct form. The National Security Ordinance of 1980 had, in its clause 9, anticipated Advisory Boards in the amended form. The National Security Act as enacted, however, provided in Section 9 for Advisory Boards under the unamended clause (4). The movement from Ordinance to Act is therefore itself an illustration of the governing principle. Anticipation of an amendment is not commencement of the amendment, and a legislature enacting a detention statute worked with the constitutional text that was actually in force rather than with the text that Section 3 would have substituted had it been notified. A candidate who notices that detail understands the topic at the level examinations reward, because the detail shows the unamended clause operating as the framework within which detention legislation was framed.

The examination payoff can be stated without exaggeration. Notes and answer keys that state two months as the operative period state the law wrongly. The error is common enough that examiners can rely on it to sort answers, because a candidate who writes two months reveals at once that the commencement question was never asked, while a candidate who writes three months and explains why the two month substitution does not operate reveals a habit of verification that examiners trust when they meet it elsewhere in the script. The discriminator between a prepared and an unprepared candidate is knowing that assent is not commencement. A candidate may have seen the two month period many times in print and may reproduce it with complete confidence, and confidence of that kind is the enemy the question is designed to meet. Print in a set of notes is not notification in law, and repetition is not commencement. For answer writing, the safe form is habitual. State the law in force first, naming the unamended clause (4), its three month period, and its Board composition. Then deal with the amendment, and only then add the case, in its place. On this question clause first is the difference between stating the Constitution as it operates and stating a Constitution that was enacted in part, assented to, and never brought into being.

## The detention statute chain: 1950 to the National Security Act

The detention statute chain is best learned as a sequence of enactments and endings rather than as a shelf of statutes that all stand together. That distinction sounds modest, yet it governs a large share of the marks available in this area, because an answer that places a statute in the wrong period, or treats a lapsed or repealed law as living law, misstates the legal position before any analysis has begun. The chain has a beginning in the Preventive Detention Act of 1950, a middle in the Maintenance of Internal Security Act of 1971, and a later form in the National Security Act of 1980. Between those enactments lie gaps, repeals and a lapse, and those intervals matter as much as the enactments themselves. A candidate who understands why each statute arrived, what it replaced or failed to replace, and how it ended, possesses a framework into which procedure, safeguards and case law can be placed with far less strain on memory.

The Preventive Detention Act, 1950, Act 4 of 1950, was independent India's first preventive detention statute. It was enacted in February 1950, within weeks of the Constitution commencing, and that timing is itself an examinable point. Preventive detention was not a later departure from a settled constitutional order that had operated for a generation without it. It arrived at the very start, alongside the Constitution, as part of the arrangements through which the new state proposed to govern. The Act was a temporary measure, extended repeatedly, and it expired at the end of 1969, lapsing on 31 December 1969. Each element of that description carries weight. Its early enactment connects it to the founding compromise examined in the next part of this segment. Its temporary character distinguishes its original design from a permanent detention code. Its repeated extension shows a temporary measure being kept alive by successive decisions rather than by a single founding choice. Its lapse on a stated date closes it. After 31 December 1969, the Act of 1950 was not a statute under which detention could be defended as subsisting law, however central it remains to the history of the subject and however often it appears in accounts of the founding period.

The ending of the 1950 Act also guards against a common simplification, namely the assumption that one general detention statute simply succeeded another in an unbroken line. The Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971, Act 26 of 1971, known as MISA, followed after a gap. The gap is part of the chain, not an embarrassment to be smoothed over in revision. It shows that the presence of a general preventive detention statute was a legislative choice made afresh, not an automatic condition of Indian government. When MISA was enacted in 1971, Parliament created a new instrument rather than reviving the Act of 1950, and an answer should preserve that difference in language. To write of the 1950 Act being renamed, continued or converted into MISA would be inaccurate on the facts supplied by the sequence itself. One statute had lapsed, time had passed, and a distinct statute was then enacted with its own number, its own life and, in due course, its own ending.

MISA occupies the middle of the chain because of both its enactment and its use. It was the detention instrument of the Emergency period from 1975 to 1977. That association explains why MISA carries a historical charge that an ordinary statute list cannot convey. In an examination answer, naming MISA without locating it in the Emergency misses the reason the statute is studied at such length, while treating its Emergency use as if it described the whole of its existence compresses its history in the opposite direction. The disciplined approach is chronological. MISA was enacted in 1971, it served as the detention instrument during the Emergency from 1975 to 1977, and it was repealed in 1978 by the Janata government that had been elected after the Emergency. The repeal is as significant as the enactment. It was a deliberate legislative ending carried out by a government whose election followed the Emergency, and it forms part of the restoration examined below. Once that repeal is grasped, the classic examination trap becomes easy to avoid. MISA is not in force. An answer that invokes MISA as a living source of detention power, rather than as a repealed statute of major historical and constitutional significance, commits a basic error of status.

The National Security Act, 1980, Act 65 of 1980, followed in the aftermath of that restoration period. Its position in the chain therefore raises a harder question than simple chronology, because a general detention statute returned soon after a repealed predecessor had been dismantled. For the purposes of status, however, the position is clear. The National Security Act is the later general statute in the sequence, enacted in 1980, and under it the maximum period of detention is twelve months. That maximum should be stated precisely and without embellishment. Twelve months is a ceiling supplied by the statute, not an entitlement to detain for twelve months in every case, and not a period that can be extended in an answer by loose reference to administrative practice. Precision on a figure of this kind is one of the small disciplines by which an examiner distinguishes a prepared candidate from a candidate relying on general impressions of security law. The figure also gives the chain a concrete endpoint for revision. The 1950 Act lapsed in 1969, MISA was repealed in 1978, and the National Security Act of 1980 prescribes a maximum detention period of twelve months.

Alongside the chain stand statutes that must be recognised for what they are and, equally, for what they are not. The Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act, 1974, COFEPOSA, Act 52 of 1974, covers detention for smuggling and foreign exchange violations and remains in force. Its detention power is real, and for that reason candidates are tempted to insert it into the security chain as though chronology alone determined membership. The brief for sound revision resists that temptation. COFEPOSA sits beside the security chain rather than inside it, because its grounds are economic. Smuggling and foreign exchange violations concern the protection of economic regulation and national resources through a distinct statutory purpose, and the Act should be classified by that purpose. Its date, 1974, places it between MISA and the National Security Act in time, but date is not the organising principle of the chain. Grounds and purpose are. An answer that says COFEPOSA remains in force is correct on status. An answer that then treats it as another link in the general security sequence without noting its economic grounds has classified badly, and classification is often the very skill being tested.

Two further statutes sit alongside rather than within the chain, for reasons that differ from COFEPOSA and from each other. The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958, Act 28 of 1958, is a deployment and powers statute for disturbed areas rather than a detention code. Its place in the wider study of civil liberties is secure, because powers exercised during deployment bear directly on liberty, but its statutory character should not be blurred. To call AFSPA a preventive detention statute is to mistake the field in which it operates. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967, Act 37 of 1967, presents the converse temptation. It belongs to the law of prosecution, as a prosecution statute with detention only through ordinary arrest and remand. Custody under such a process is not the same juridical thing as detention under a preventive detention order, even though the lived experience of loss of liberty may be compared in broader discussion. The examination distinction is formal and must be kept. Preventive detention in the chain rests on a detention statute directed to prevention. Arrest and remand under a prosecution statute belong to a criminal process directed to trial. A candidate who merges the two loses the conceptual boundary on which questions in this area are built.

The practical revision rule that emerges from the chain is therefore threefold in substance, though it should be carried in the mind as a single habit. First, ask whether the statute is in force, has lapsed, or has been repealed. Second, ask what grounds it addresses and whether those grounds place it inside the general security chain or beside it. Third, ask what kind of statute it is, whether a detention code, a deployment and powers statute, or a prosecution statute. The 1950 Act and MISA are the classic traps on the first question, because their fame tempts candidates into listing them as in force. AFSPA and the UAPA are the classic traps on the second and third questions, because their closeness to security and liberty tempts candidates into treating them as detention statutes. COFEPOSA tests a finer judgment, since it does contain detention but on economic grounds, and remains in force while sitting beside the chain. Mastered in this way, the chain ceases to be a list to be recited and becomes a method of placement that can be applied under examination pressure.

## Four periods that organise the whole area

Statutes acquire meaning from the periods in which they were enacted, used and judged, and the whole area of civil liberties detention can be organised through four periods. The device is not a substitute for knowledge of individual enactments. It is a means of holding that knowledge in an order that survives the examination hall, where a question may approach the subject through a safeguard, a ground of challenge, or a federal question rather than through chronology. A candidate who holds the four periods can place any statute or case without memorising dates in isolation, because each date becomes a position within a story of constitutional choice, excess, restoration and supervision. The periods also prevent the most persistent distortion in this field, which is the treatment of preventive detention as a timeless power that has always existed in the same form and attracted the same judicial response.

The first period is 1950 and the founding compromise. A Constitution that guaranteed personal liberty in Article 21 also wrote preventive detention into Article 22 itself. That juxtaposition should not be presented as an accidental contradiction discovered by later commentators. It was a choice made in the constitutional text, defended in the Constituent Assembly on grounds of the security situation of a new state and criticised there by members who saw detention without trial as alien to the freedom the Constitution promised. Both sides of that founding argument belong in a strong answer. The defence explains why preventive detention was constitutionalised rather than left outside the text. The criticism explains why its presence has remained contested in principle even where its validity is accepted in law. To present only the defence produces an answer that sounds complacent about a power to detain without trial. To present only the criticism produces an answer that cannot explain why Article 22 exists in the form it does. The compromise lies precisely in the holding together of a liberty guarantee and a detention provision within the same constitutional settlement, with the argument about that settlement recorded from the beginning.

The second period runs from 1971 to 1977, covering the enactment of MISA and then the Emergency, when the detention machinery was used at its widest and habeas corpus itself failed in the Supreme Court. If the first period supplies the constitutional origin, the second supplies the warning. MISA was enacted in 1971, before the Emergency, and then became the detention instrument of the Emergency period from 1975 to 1977. The sequence matters because it shows how a statute enacted in one setting can be used at a scale and in a climate quite different from the setting of its enactment. Examination answers that treat enactment and use as a single event miss that lesson. The failure of habeas corpus in the Supreme Court during this period marks its constitutional depth. Habeas corpus is the remedy most directly associated with testing unlawful detention, and its failure meant that the difficulty lay not merely in harsh administration of a statute but in the response of the highest court when liberty was pressed hardest. No account of safeguards in this area is complete unless it reckons with the fact that a safeguard on paper and a safeguard in a crisis are different things, and that the second period is studied precisely because that difference was exposed.

The third period, from 1978 to 1980, is the restoration that repealed MISA and passed the 44th Amendment, followed within a short span by the re-enactment of a general detention statute in the National Security Act. This is the most instructive period for candidates who prefer simple narratives, because it refuses to supply one. The restoration dismantled and rebuilt the machinery. MISA was repealed in 1978 by the Janata government elected after the Emergency, and the 44th Amendment belonged to the same broad movement of constitutional repair. Yet within a short span the National Security Act of 1980 enacted a general detention statute again. A candid answer holds both movements together. It does not describe the restoration as the end of preventive detention, because the National Security Act disproves that description. It also does not describe the repeal of MISA as an empty gesture, because a repeal is a real legislative act and the amendment formed part of the restoration settlement. The examinable insight is that Indian detention law after the Emergency was reshaped by reaction against abuse without the underlying power being abandoned. The machinery was condemned in one form, dismantled, and then authorised in another form by the ordinary legislative process.

The fourth period is the long period after 1980 of judicial supervision without legislative change, in which the statutes stayed and the courts policed the procedure. During this period the courts quashed detention orders for delay, for non-communication of grounds and for non-consideration of representations, while leaving the detention power itself intact. That combination defines the period. Judicial activity was real and consequential for individual detainees whose orders were quashed, yet it operated on the manner in which the power was exercised rather than on the existence of the power. A candidate who describes the courts in this period as abolitionist misreads the record, just as a candidate who describes them as passive ignores the quashing of orders on procedural grounds. The accurate formulation is supervision without legislative change and without judicial removal of the power itself. Procedure became the principal field of contest, and grounds of challenge concerning delay, communication and representation acquired their importance within that field.

Read together, the four periods give a candidate a portable map. A statute from the founding can be recognised by its connection to the compromise of Articles 21 and 22. A statute or event associated with the widest use of detention and the failure of habeas corpus belongs to the second period and should be handled with the gravity that association requires. A repeal, an amendment and a re-enactment crowded together signal the third period and invite analysis of a restoration that both dismantled and rebuilt. A challenge founded on delay, non-communication of grounds or non-consideration of a representation, resulting in an order being quashed while the power survives, belongs to the grammar of the fourth period. The map does not answer a question by itself. It tells the candidate where the question lives, which tensions are likely to be in play, and which errors of status or character to avoid before the detailed answer begins. That is a considerable advantage in a subject where statutes with similar names, overlapping grounds and very different legal fates are easily confused.

## The federal split: Entry 9 and Entry 3

Preventive detention in India is also a federal subject, and its federal structure is written into the distribution of legislative power in a way that examination questions reward. The starting point is not the identity of the legislature that passed a particular statute, but the ground on which detention is authorised. Entry 9 of the Union List gives Parliament power over preventive detention for reasons connected with the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, or the security of India, and over persons subjected to such detention. Entry 3 of the Concurrent List gives Parliament and the state legislatures power over preventive detention for reasons connected with the security of a State, the maintenance of public order, or the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community, and over persons subjected to such detention. Set side by side in prose, the two entries reveal a partition of grounds. Defence, foreign relations and the security of India fall within Entry 9. Security of a State, public order and essential supplies and services fall within Entry 3. The lists overlap in vocabulary of security, yet they are not interchangeable, and the candidate's task is to read the ground stated in the statute with care.

### Why does the Constitution itself authorise preventive detention?
The Constitution authorises preventive detention because the founders treated liberty and state security as commitments to be held together. Article 22 therefore placed detention within the constitutional text itself, with safeguards, rather than leaving it to emergency power alone, reflecting the security concerns pressed upon a newly independent state.

The examinable consequence is that the grounds are partitioned, not shared. The first federalism question to ask of any detention law is which entry its grounds track. If a statute rests on reasons connected with the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, or the security of India, its field is Entry 9 and legislative power belongs to Parliament. If a statute rests on reasons connected with the security of a State, the maintenance of public order, or the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community, its field is Entry 3, where Parliament and the state legislatures both have power. The partition explains why a question may turn on a phrase in the statute that a hurried reader treats as background language. The ground is not background. It is the jurisdictional key. Public order, in particular, should not be dissolved into a general notion of national security merely because both concern safety. Entry 3 names public order as a ground in the concurrent field, and that placement has consequences for which legislatures may act.

The National Security Act illustrates the reach of the partition because it draws on both fields. It is a central statute, but its significance for federal analysis lies in the breadth of the grounds it covers rather than in the bare fact of central enactment. A state goondas statute stands at the other point of comparison, resting on Entry 3 grounds such as public order. The comparison teaches the method. Two statutes may both concern preventive detention and may produce similar orders against individuals, yet their federal foundations differ because their stated grounds differ. An examiner who sets such a comparison is testing whether the candidate reads a statute from its grounds outward. The candidate who begins by asking whether the subject feels like a state subject or a central subject, in some loose political sense, has started from impression. The candidate who quotes or paraphrases the ground, matches it to Entry 9 or Entry 3, and only then discusses the legislature, has started from the constitutional text.

Repugnancy enters the analysis because Entry 3 is concurrent, and it should be explained at the level of principle rather than through elaborate excursions beyond the materials required here. Because Entry 3 is concurrent, a state law and a central law can coexist in the field. Coexistence does not mean that conflict is impossible, nor does it mean that both laws prevail where they cannot stand together. Conflicts are resolved by the repugnancy doctrine in Article 254, under which the central law prevails to the extent of the repugnancy unless the state law has been reserved for and received Presidential assent. Each part of that principle should be stated with restraint. The central law prevails only to the extent of the repugnancy, which confines the displacement to the area of conflict. The Presidential assent exception is not a general state override, but a specific constitutional route by which a reserved state law that has received assent is treated differently under the doctrine. In an examination answer, that level of principle is sufficient to show understanding of how concurrent power operates when two laws occupy the same Entry 3 field and pull in different directions.

The partition also explains why federalism questions in this area are so persistent and so fair. They do not depend on obscure learning. They depend on reading. The statute states its grounds, the entries state their grounds, and the candidate is asked to perform the match and draw the consequence. Where the match is to Entry 9 alone, state competence is not established by the importance of the problem within a state. Where the match is to Entry 3, concurrent competence opens the further possibility of both central and state legislation and, with it, the possible need to consider Article 254. Where a central statute such as the National Security Act draws on both fields, the analysis must acknowledge that breadth rather than forcing the statute into a single entry for neatness. Neatness is a poor guide in federal questions. The constitutional design is partitioned, layered and, in the concurrent field, capable of producing overlap that the repugnancy doctrine then manages.

For revision, the federal split should therefore be rehearsed as a sequence of questions expressed in prose and applied in the same order each time. What ground does the detention law invoke. Which entry contains that ground in the same or closely corresponding language. Does that entry lie in the Union List or the Concurrent List. If it lies in the Concurrent List, is there also a central law in the field with which a conflict could arise, and if so, how would Article 254 resolve that conflict at the level of principle. Those questions convert a memorised pair of entries into an analytical instrument. They also connect the federal part of the syllabus to the statute chain and the four periods, because a detention order never raises only one kind of issue. It may raise status, period, grounds, competence and procedure in the same problem. The candidate who can separate those issues, deal with competence by reference to Entry 9 and Entry 3, and resist treating every security claim as a Union claim, writes the kind of ordered answer that this subject consistently rewards.

## The human rights institutions: one Act, three different bodies

The Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 repays careful reading in an examination because it does several different things at once, and marks are lost when those things are collapsed into one another. A single statute creates a national commission, provides for state commissions, and provides for courts. Those three creations do not share the same character, the same remit, or the same kind of power. A commission inquires and recommends. A court tries and decides. A state commission works within a federal division of subjects. Once that architecture is held firmly in mind, the detailed provisions fall into place around it, and the familiar traps in this area lose much of their power to mislead.

The chronology of the Act is itself a tested point, and it should be stated with precision. The Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, cited as Act 10 of 1994, was brought into force retrospectively from 28 September 1993, the date of the ordinance it replaced, and the National Human Rights Commission was constituted on 12 October 1993. Two dates therefore sit close together and perform different work. The first explains the legal continuity between the ordinance and the Act that replaced it, and it accounts for the naming of an Act numbered in one year for another. The second marks the constitution of the Commission as a functioning body. A candidate who merges the two dates signals that the statutory history has been learned loosely.

The definition at the heart of the Act is found in Section 2(1)(d), and it deserves to be reproduced in substance in any substantial answer. That provision defines human rights as the rights relating to life, liberty, equality and dignity of the individual guaranteed by the Constitution or embodied in the International Covenants and enforceable by courts in India. Each element carries weight. The rights are organised around life, liberty, equality and dignity. Their source is stated in the alternative, so a right may qualify because it is guaranteed by the Constitution or because it is embodied in the International Covenants. The closing requirement, that the rights be enforceable by courts in India, tethers the definition to enforceability and prevents it from expanding into a general statement of aspiration.

### Is the National Human Rights Commission a constitutional body?
No. The National Human Rights Commission is a statutory body created by the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, not a body established by the Constitution. That single classification separates it from the Election Commission and the Union Public Service Commission in examination answers.

The classification matters because it determines the source from which the institution draws its existence and its authority. A constitutional body owes its existence to the Constitution itself, whereas a statutory body owes its existence to an Act of Parliament, and the National Human Rights Commission falls squarely in the second category. It exists because the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 created it, and its functions, its limitations and its reach are to be found in that Act rather than in a constitutional article. The examination discriminator is dependable. The National Human Rights Commission is a statutory body, not a constitutional one, unlike the Election Commission or the Union Public Service Commission. Functional similarity is not constitutional identity, and an answer that keeps the two ideas separate will handle both short and long questions with greater safety.

The same discipline should be applied across the family of national commissions, because the family is deliberately mixed and the mixture is the point of many questions. The National Commission for Women is statutory under its 1990 Act. The Commissions for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes are constitutional under Articles 338 and 338A respectively. The phrase national commission therefore tells a candidate nothing by itself about legal status, and any answer that reasons from the label rather than from the source instrument is exposed to error. The sound method is to name the source for each body in turn, giving the article where the source is the Constitution and the Act where the source is a statute.

Confusion in this field is fed by a genuine feature of the Act rather than by carelessness alone. The chairpersons of the National Commissions for Minorities, for Scheduled Castes, for Scheduled Tribes and for Women sit as deemed members of the National Commission. That arrangement creates a visible link between the National Human Rights Commission and the other national commissions, and it explains why candidates run the institutions together. The link, however, is one of membership and coordination. It does not alter the legal source of any body concerned, it does not transfer functions from one body to another, and it does not convert a statutory commission into a constitutional one or the reverse.

Section 12 sets out the functions of the National Human Rights Commission, and those functions are headed by inquiry, on its own initiative or on a petition, into complaints of violation of human rights or negligence in their prevention by a public servant. The structure of that formulation should be noticed. The Commission may act on its own initiative, so its work is not confined to cases that a complainant happens to bring before it, and it may also act on a petition presented to it. The subject matter of the inquiry reaches both violation of human rights and negligence in the prevention of such violation by a public servant, which means that a failure to prevent can fall within the inquiry just as a direct act can. An answer that captures both limbs, the initiative and the petition, and both objects, the violation and the negligent failure to prevent, gives a fuller account of Section 12 than one that reduces the function to a general power to investigate complaints.

The character of what the Commission produces at the end of an inquiry is the second great discriminator in this area, and it is frequently the point on which an otherwise competent answer falters. The outcomes of the Commission are recommendations, not binding orders. The Commission can recommend compensation and can recommend prosecution, and it can approach the Supreme Court or a High Court for directions, but recommending and approaching are not the same juridical acts as ordering and directing. A candidate who writes that the Commission awarded compensation or directed prosecution has attributed to the Commission a species of power it does not possess, and an examiner will treat that attribution as a substantive error rather than as loose phrasing. The better practice is to choose verbs with care throughout an answer on this subject. The Commission inquires, it recommends, and it approaches a court. Courts, by contrast, direct, order and decide. Keeping the verbs apart is a simple discipline, and it protects an answer from the most common single mistake made about this institution.

That recommendatory character should be analysed rather than merely recited. If the outcomes are recommendations, then the force of the Commission lies in inquiry, in findings placed on record, in the recommendation of compensation or prosecution, and in the capacity to move a constitutional court for directions where directions are required. Its authority is real but routed through other institutions for enforcement, and that routing is a feature of the statutory design. An answer should not slide from the observation that the Commission recommends into the conclusion that it does nothing, or from the observation that it can approach a court into the claim that it exercises the powers of that court.

Two limitations on the Commission require separate treatment, because each is specific and each is capable of deciding a problem question. The first is found in Section 19, which restricts the Commission where the armed forces are concerned. In that field the Commission may only seek a report from the Central Government and then make recommendations on that report. The ordinary pattern of inquiry is therefore displaced by a narrower procedure, and a candidate should not write of the Commission inquiring into a matter involving the armed forces as though the general function applied without qualification. The second limitation is found in Section 36(2), which bars the Commission from inquiring into a matter more than one year after the act complained of. That is a limitation expressed in time, and it operates as a bar on inquiry rather than as a matter of discretion. Read together, the two provisions teach a wider lesson. The Commission is drawn with defined functions, defined outcomes and defined exclusions, and an answer earns its marks by reproducing the exclusions with the same care given to the functions.

The state level of the structure must be described on its own terms and not as a smaller copy of the national level. The same Act provides for State Human Rights Commissions, constituted by state governments, whose remit runs to the State List and Concurrent List subjects. The federal character of that remit is the feature to underline. A State Commission is not a branch office of the National Commission, and its field is described by reference to the legislative lists, which ties the institution to the distribution of subjects between the Union and the states. An answer that states the constituting authority and the list based remit in one clear movement will satisfy most questions in this area, whereas an answer that assumes symmetry between the two levels will misdescribe both.

Human Rights Courts complete the trio, and they are the body most often misdescribed of the three. Under Section 30, Human Rights Courts are courts of session specified by the state government with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court for the speedy trial of offences arising out of the violation of human rights. Every element performs work. The court is a court of session, which locates it within the hierarchy of trial courts. It is specified by the state government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court. Its purpose is the speedy trial of offences arising out of the violation of human rights. A Human Rights Court is therefore a trial court, not a commission, and that is the distinction candidates collapse. The collapse leads a candidate to attribute recommendatory functions to a body that tries offences, or trial functions to a body that recommends. The remedy is to hold the vocabulary steady. Commissions inquire and recommend under this Act. Courts specified under Section 30 try offences.

A comparison across the three bodies can close a long answer effectively, provided it preserves the distinctions. The National Commission and the State Commissions share the commission form, the inquiry function and the recommendatory outcome, and they differ in constituting authority and in the field marked out by the legislative lists. The Human Rights Courts share neither the commission form nor the recommendatory outcome, because they are courts of session charged with trial.

Complaints under the Act should be handled in an answer as a matter of forum, function and time taken together. The candidate should be able to say that the Commission inquires on its own initiative or on a petition into complaints of violation or of negligence in prevention by a public servant, that the product of that inquiry is a recommendation which may extend to compensation or to prosecution, that the Commission may approach the Supreme Court or a High Court for directions, that a matter involving the armed forces follows the restricted route in Section 19, and that Section 36(2) bars inquiry into a matter more than one year after the act complained of. This guide is written for examination preparation and general legal information, and it is not advice on any individual complaint or detention, where limitation periods and forum rules are unforgiving and advice on the specific facts should be taken.

## The case spine: proposition before citation

The governing rule for case law in a civil liberties answer is that knowing what a case is for beats knowing its citation. A citation without a proposition is decoration, and decoration earns little from an examiner reading for legal understanding. A proposition, by contrast, can be deployed. It can support an argument, it can be distinguished from a rival proposition, and it can be connected to the facts of a problem question. The candidate should therefore learn each leading case as a short statement of what the court held, with the composition of the bench and the fate of the decision in later law attached to the statement. Bench strength matters because examiners test it directly and because it affects the weight a candidate should claim for a decision. Later treatment matters because a proposition that has been overruled, or a doctrine that did not survive, must never be presented as though it still governed the subject.

### What is A K Gopalan the authority for in an examination answer?
A K Gopalan is authority for the silo doctrine and for a narrow reading of procedure established by law. The majority treated Articles 19 and 21 as separate codes and upheld preventive detention in substance while striking down Section 14.

A K Gopalan v State of Madras (1950) is the starting point of the spine, and it should be learned in its several distinct holdings. The case was decided by a six judge bench presided over by Kania CJ. The majority upheld the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 in substance, but struck down Section 14 of that Act, which had barred disclosure. That combination matters, because a candidate who reports only the upholding will miss the one provision that fell, and a candidate who reports only the striking down will invert the general result. The more influential part of the decision lies in its reading of the fundamental rights chapter. The majority read Articles 19 and 21 as separate codes, an approach known as the silo doctrine, under which each article was treated as occupying its own compartment. Alongside that approach, the majority read the expression procedure established by law in Article 21 as procedure laid down by enacted law rather than as a fairness standard against which enacted law could itself be measured. Those two readings reinforced one another, and together they left a preventive detention law facing a far narrower field of constitutional challenge than it would face under a more integrated and more demanding reading.

The dissenting element in Gopalan should not be treated as a footnote, because it contains the seed of the approach that later prevailed. Fazl Ali J dissented in part, favouring reading the articles together. That preference does not alter what the majority held, and it should not be presented as though it did. Its value lies in showing that the silo doctrine was contested from within the bench that produced it, and that the alternative reading of the articles as a connected scheme was articulated at the outset. A candidate who notices the dissent can present the development of the law as a genuine argument conducted over time rather than as a sudden change of direction.

The silo doctrine did not survive, and the case that displaced it must be stated with equal precision. In Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978) the Supreme Court read Articles 14, 19 and 21 together and required the procedure depriving a person of liberty to be just, fair and reasonable. The contrast with Gopalan is the teaching point. Where the earlier majority separated the articles, Maneka Gandhi joined them, so that a law affecting liberty could be tested against the requirements of equality and of the freedoms as well as against Article 21 itself. Where the earlier majority treated procedure established by law as procedure laid down by enacted law, Maneka Gandhi demanded a quality in the procedure, namely that it be just, fair and reasonable. A candidate should be able to move between the two decisions in a single paragraph without blurring either.

Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976) occupies a different place in the spine, and it should be presented as a decision about the Emergency and about access to a court rather than as a general authority on detention. The case was decided by a five judge bench, which held by four to one that during an Emergency, with the enforcement of Article 21 suspended, no person had locus to move a court for habeas corpus against a detention. The proposition is stark, and it should be stated without softening. Khanna J dissented, holding that life and liberty could not be placed at the mercy of the executive even in an Emergency. The majority holding and the dissent should always be taught together, since an account of [the Jabalpur habeas corpus case](/2010/07/15/adm-jabalpur-habeas-corpus-case/) that omits the dissent withholds the material with which examiners most often invite evaluation. The candidate should also note the condition on which the majority holding turned, namely the suspension of the enforcement of Article 21, because removing that condition turns a historically situated holding into a false general rule.

The fate of ADM Jabalpur in later law is as much a part of the proposition as the holding itself. In Justice K S Puttaswamy (Retd) v Union of India (2017), a nine judge bench recognising privacy as a fundamental right expressly overruled ADM Jabalpur, and Chandrachud J described the majority judgments in it as seriously flawed. The bench strength matters, because a nine judge bench overruling a five judge decision settles the question of authority with unusual completeness. The context matters as well, since the overruling occurred in a decision whose central holding concerned privacy as a fundamental right. A candidate citing ADM Jabalpur for its historical position, for the dissent of Khanna J, or for the fact of its overruling is using the case correctly. A candidate citing it as living authority for the denial of habeas corpus is making an error that the express overruling in Puttaswamy was designed to foreclose.

A K Roy v Union of India (1982) belongs in the spine as a five judge bench decision that upheld the National Security Act, and it also produced the commencement holding on Section 3 of the 44th Amendment. That holding is referenced here in a single sentence, as its detail lives in another part of this guide and is not re-taught in this part. The restraint is itself an examination lesson, since a well constructed answer gives each point its full treatment in one place and cross refers elsewhere rather than repeating it. For the spine, what the candidate needs from A K Roy is the proposition that the National Security Act was upheld, placed after Maneka Gandhi so that the survival of a preventive detention statute under the more demanding approach to Article 21 can be registered.

The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act line of authority requires the same proposition first handling, because the subject attracts broad assertions that the cases do not support. In Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v Union of India (1997), a five judge Constitution Bench upheld the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 while reading in safeguards, including periodic review of a disturbed area declaration at intervals of six months. The proposition therefore has two limbs that must be kept together. The Act was upheld, and safeguards were read in. A candidate who cites the case only for the upholding presents a statute sustained without qualification, and a candidate who cites it only for the safeguards presents a statute more heavily conditioned than the bench held it to be. The balanced statement is the accurate one, and it should be carried into any discussion of [the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 explained](/2010/08/15/afspa-1958-explained/) or of the disturbed area mechanism.

The second authority in that line qualifies the first in an important respect. In Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association v Union of India (2016), the Supreme Court held that members of the armed forces do not enjoy blanket immunity for excessive or retaliatory force in an area under the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act and that deaths at the hands of the security forces in Manipur required investigation. The proposition is framed negatively and that framing should be preserved. The court denied blanket immunity for a defined category of force, described as excessive or retaliatory, and it required investigation of deaths in the circumstances before it. Read together, Naga People's Movement and Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association give the candidate a coherent pair. The statute stands, safeguards are read into its operation, immunity is not blanket where force is excessive or retaliatory, and deaths require investigation. That pair will carry an answer through most questions on the Act with greater credit than a general position unsupported by either holding.

Ram Manohar Lohia v State of Bihar (1966) supplies the spine with its most portable analytical device, and it is a case that candidates frequently cite without being able to state what it decided. The decision gave the concentric circles. Law and order is the widest circle, public order lies inside it, and the security of the State is the smallest circle, so that an act must reach the public order circle before it can ground preventive detention. The image is a test, not an ornament. It requires the candidate analysing a detention to locate the act complained of within the correct circle and to refuse to treat every breach of law and order as though it were a disturbance of public order. An act that affects only the widest circle has not crossed the threshold that preventive detention requires. This is also the natural place in an answer to draw upon [the comparison of preventive detention and arrest](/2010/10/01/preventive-detention-vs-arrest-india/), because the circles explain why preventive detention, which is directed at anticipated harm of a sufficient order, cannot be justified by conduct amounting to no more than an ordinary breach of law and order of the kind that the ordinary processes of arrest and prosecution exist to meet.

The spine as a whole should be memorised as a sequence of propositions with their benches and their later treatment attached. Gopalan separated the articles and read procedure as enacted law, upholding preventive detention in substance while striking down the bar on disclosure in Section 14, with Fazl Ali J favouring in part the integrated reading. Maneka Gandhi joined Articles 14, 19 and 21 and required a procedure that is just, fair and reasonable. ADM Jabalpur denied locus for habeas corpus during an Emergency on a suspended Article 21, over the dissent of Khanna J, and was expressly overruled by the nine judge bench in Puttaswamy, which recognised privacy as a fundamental right. A K Roy upheld the National Security Act. Naga People's Movement upheld the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act with safeguards read in, and Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association denied blanket immunity for excessive or retaliatory force and required investigation of deaths in Manipur. Lohia supplied the circles by which the gravity of an act is measured before detention can be grounded on it. A candidate who commands that sequence can locate any single case within the movement of the whole, and can answer a thematic question by selecting the propositions that bear on it, leading in each instance with what the case is for.

## Working an unfamiliar statute: the clause map in action

A clause map earns its place in preparation only if it works on a statute the candidate has not revised line by line, without a text in hand. Preparation organised statute by statute fails that test, because knowledge arrives attached to the name of an Act and collapses when the name is unfamiliar. The map reverses the order. It begins with the constitutional provisions that govern every preventive detention law in India, derives the questions those provisions force any such law to answer, and then reads the unfamiliar statute as a set of answers to questions already known. The statute chosen here makes the point deliberately. The Tamil Nadu Prevention of Dangerous Activities of Bootleggers, Drug-Offenders, Forest-Offenders, Goondas, Immoral Traffic Offenders, Sand-Offenders, Slum-Grabbers and Video Pirates Act, 1982, Tamil Nadu Act 14 of 1982, known in practice as the Goondas Act, is a statute many candidates will recognise by its short name and few will have studied in detail. That combination is precisely the condition an examination creates.

The first move is classification, and classification does substantive work rather than serving as a label. The Goondas Act is a state preventive detention law directed at named classes of persons, including bootleggers, drug offenders and goondas, and its stated ground is preventing activities prejudicial to the maintenance of public order. Each element carries a consequence. That the law is a state law raises legislative competence, answered by locating the ground within the Seventh Schedule. That the law is preventive rather than punitive determines which parts of Article 22 are engaged and which are excluded, and therefore which protections a detained person can claim. That the law proceeds by named classes means the detaining authority must bring the individual case within one of those classes, so the fit between the person detained and the class invoked becomes part of the legal contest. None of this requires knowledge of the Act beyond the description given, and each observation structures the answer before any detail of the Act is reached.

Competence follows from the ground, and the ground stated is public order. Because the ground is public order, the enabling power is Entry 3 of the Concurrent List, not Entry 9 of the Union List. That distinction is the analytical hinge of the exercise, because a candidate who reaches for Entry 9 whenever preventive detention is mentioned has substituted association for reasoning. Entry 9 of the Union List carries preventive detention for reasons connected with the defence of India, relations with foreign powers and the security of India. Entry 3 of the Concurrent List carries preventive detention for reasons connected with the security of a State, the maintenance of public order and the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. Public order falls within the second entry and not the first, so a Tamil Nadu law aimed at activities prejudicial to the maintenance of public order traces its competence to the Concurrent List.

The next move concerns the relationship between the state Act and the National Security Act, and an uninformed answer is likely to treat the two as interchangeable instruments available at the option of the detaining authority. Detention orders against the classes named in the Goondas Act are to be made under this Act and not under the National Security Act. The state statute is therefore not a supplement to be used when the central statute feels inconvenient, nor is the central statute a general reserve power covering the same ground for the same persons. Orders against those classes travel under the state law, with its own conditions and safeguards, and analysis of any individual order begins with the state text.

With competence settled, the map turns to Article 22, and the decisive provision is clause (3). Because the detention is preventive detention, clauses (1) and (2) of Article 22 fall away under clause (3). The consequence must be stated plainly, because its severity is the point. A person detained under the Goondas Act has no right under clauses (1) and (2) to the protections that attend an ordinary arrest, and in particular there is no right to production before a magistrate within twenty four hours and no trial at which a charge is proved. The absence of a trial follows from the nature of preventive detention itself, which rests on a satisfaction that a person is likely to act in a manner prejudicial to the stated ground, rather than on proof that an offence has been committed.

The exclusion in clause (3) does not leave the detained person without constitutional protection, and the map next identifies what survives. Because clause (4) applies, detention beyond three months needs an Advisory Board report, and the figure is three months, not two, because Section 3 of the Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978 has never been commenced. The weight of clause (4) lies in what it reveals about the design. The Constitution does not trust executive satisfaction alone beyond a short initial period. Past the three month point, continued detention depends on an external body reporting that there is sufficient cause for the detention, and that dependence converts the Board from an administrative formality into the structural check on which longer detention rests.

Because clause (5) applies, grounds must be communicated to the detained person and a representation must be allowed. This safeguard carries more litigating weight in practice than any other part of the scheme. Communication of grounds makes the representation real rather than formal, since a person who does not know the material on which the detaining authority acted cannot answer it, and a representation is the mechanism by which the detained person places an answer before the authority and, through the process that follows, before the Advisory Board. The representation, together with the Board, is where challenges to such orders are fought. Challenges turn on whether grounds were communicated, whether they were communicated in a manner that allowed an effective representation, whether the representation was considered, and whether the Board process was followed, rather than on the kind of contest over evidence that a criminal trial would produce.

The remedial route follows from the same logic, and ordinary criminal instincts mislead here. The challenge route is habeas corpus under Article 226 or Article 32, not bail, because there is no charge to be bailed against. Bail presupposes a charge, a court seized of that charge, and a question about custody pending trial. Preventive detention has none of those elements. What is challenged is the legality of the detention itself, the authority of the order, the ground on which it rests, and the observance of the safeguards in clauses (4) and (5), and habeas corpus is the writ fitted to that challenge because it requires the detaining authority to justify the detention before a constitutional court. An answer that asks for bail in a preventive detention problem has misclassified the detention at the first step, and the error shows how much of the subject depends on holding the preventive and punitive categories apart from the opening sentence onwards.

An honest working of the map must then show where the map runs out, and this matters as much as the parts where the map succeeds. The precise maximum period the state Act fixes, the officer authorised to pass the order, and the Act's own reporting timelines are text specific facts the map cannot supply. The clause map is built from the Constitution, and the Constitution sets the frame within which a preventive detention law must operate. It does not enact the internal detail of any particular statute. Those questions are answered by the sections of the Act itself, and no amount of constitutional reasoning can deduce them. A good answer says so rather than guessing them. That candour marks the boundary between derived analysis and invented recollection, and an examiner is well placed to distinguish a candidate who states the limit of the method from a candidate who fills the gap with a confident figure drawn from a different statute altogether.

Other states run parallel statutes of the same family, for example the Maharashtra Prevention of Dangerous Activities Act, 1981, the Gujarat Prevention of Anti-Social Activities Act, 1985, the Karnataka Prevention of Dangerous Activities Act, 1985 and the Kerala Anti-Social Activities (Prevention) Act, 2007, and the Jammu and Kashmir Public Safety Act, 1978 stands within the same broad family. The titles differ, the classes named differ, and the internal detail of each statute must be read from its own text, but the same map reads each of them. In every case the opening questions recur in the same order. What is the stated ground, and which List entry carries that ground. Is the detention preventive, so that clause (3) excludes clauses (1) and (2). Does clause (4) govern detention beyond three months through an Advisory Board. Does clause (5) require communication of grounds and permit a representation. Is the remedy habeas corpus rather than bail. A candidate who can put those questions to an unfamiliar statute has acquired something more durable than a summary of it, because the method survives the forgetting of detail in a way that memorised summaries do not.

## Recall and reasoning: an honest division

Preparation for this area is often misdirected because two different kinds of knowledge are treated as though they were one. Some material is pure recall. No method generates it, no reasoning reconstructs it if it is missing, and a candidate either carries it accurately into the examination hall or does not. Other material is reasoning. It can be produced on the day from principles already understood, applied to facts supplied in the question, by a candidate who has practised the moves rather than memorised their outcomes. An honest division between the two is therefore the allocation decision on which the rest of preparation depends.

The recall list begins with the List entries and the grounds each carries. Entry 9 of the Union List carries preventive detention for reasons connected with the defence of India, relations with foreign powers and the security of India. Entry 3 of the Concurrent List carries preventive detention for reasons connected with the security of a State, the maintenance of public order and the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. The pairing of entry and ground must be exact, since a candidate who knows that preventive detention appears in both Lists but cannot say which grounds sit in which entry cannot perform the competence step of the clause map. The same exactness is required for the clause numbers of Article 22 and what each does. Which clauses clause (3) displaces, which clause imposes the Advisory Board condition on longer detention, and which clause requires communication of grounds and permits a representation are facts of the constitutional text. An answer that attaches the Board or the representation to the wrong clause is marked as wrong however elegant its surrounding argument may be.

Bench strength belongs to the same category. The number of judges who decided a leading case is a fact no argument can supply, yet it carries real significance, because the authority of a proposition in later argument is bound up with the strength of the bench that laid it down. The figures to be held are six in Gopalan, five in ADM Jabalpur, nine in Puttaswamy, five in A K Roy and five in Naga People's Movement. The status of each statute in the chain belongs on the recall list for a parallel reason. The Preventive Detention Act of 1950 lapsed, MISA was repealed, and the National Security Act and COFEPOSA remain in force. Status sounds like a minor detail until a question turns on it, at which point it becomes the whole question, since an answer that analyses a lapsed or repealed statute as though it governed, or treats a statute in force as a historical curiosity, has failed before its argument begins.

Two further items complete the recall list. The first is the three month figure in clause (4) of Article 22 as the law stands. That figure is recall because it cannot be derived, and it is also a trap, because a candidate who has encountered an uncommenced reform may carry the wrong period into the hall with complete confidence. The safeguard against that error is colder memorisation of the position in force. The second is the position of the National Human Rights Commission, namely its recommendatory character, together with the one year bar in Section 36(2) of the Protection of Human Rights Act. Whether the Commission can command or only recommend, and whether a complaint raised after the statutory period can be entertained at all, are questions answered by the statute in fixed terms. Reasoning about why a human rights body ought to have binding powers has its place in an evaluation answer, but it does not alter what Section 36(2) says or what recommendatory means, and the two must never be allowed to blur into each other.

Against that list stands the reasoning side, and its items share a common feature. Each is a move that can be performed on material supplied in the question itself. Applying the clause map to an unseen statute is the clearest instance, since the statute in the question provides the ground and the classes, and the candidate provides the sequence of constitutional questions. Placing a statute in one of the four periods into which this area of law is divided for study purposes is likewise a reasoning act, because it asks the candidate to read the character of a law, its ground, its safeguards and its setting, and to assign it to the period whose pattern it fits. Deciding whether a described problem is law and order or public order using the concentric circles is a third such move, reasoning from the reach and gravity of the problem described rather than searching memory for a matching example. Evaluating a reform proposal against the founding compromise is the fourth, testing a proposal against the settlement the Constitution embodies in Article 22 and asking what it preserves, what it disturbs, and whether the disturbance is defended or merely assumed.

The study consequence follows directly. The recall list in this subject is short. It should be memorised cold, tested repeatedly until the entries, the clauses, the bench strengths, the statuses, the three month figure, the character of the Commission and the one year bar can be produced without hesitation and without error, and then maintained rather than endlessly re-studied. The reasoning side is where the larger share of preparation belongs. The clause map should be worked on statutes not previously analysed until the sequence becomes automatic, period placement practised on unfamiliar examples, the law and order and public order distinction applied to varied factual descriptions, and reform proposals tested against the founding compromise in writing under time pressure. The reason is the marking reality of the papers in this subject. The papers reward the reasoning side and merely filter on the recall side. Recall errors disqualify an otherwise strong answer, which is why the short list must be exact, but recall alone does not earn the marks that separate candidates, because every serious candidate in the hall carries the same list. Separation comes from the quality of the moves made with material the candidate has never seen, and preparation time should be spent where separation is decided.

## How questions are framed, and how to build the answer

Questions in this subject are less varied in their demands than their wording suggests, and a candidate who can identify the demand inside a question has already done a large part of the work of answering it. Identification demands ask which clause, which entry or which body is engaged by a stated situation. They are answered by exact location, and their danger is elaboration, since an identification answer that wanders into general discussion spends its length everywhere except on the point asked for. Proposition demands ask the candidate to state what a case decided and to defend or criticise that decision. They are answered by an accurate statement of the proposition first and an argued judgment second, in that order, because criticism of a proposition that has not been correctly stated convinces no examiner. Status demands ask whether a statute or an amendment is in force. They appear simple and are frequently decisive, since the whole treatment of a problem changes with the answer, and they punish the candidate whose knowledge of a reform proposal has merged in memory with knowledge of the law in force. Allocation demands ask whether a matter belongs to the Centre or to a state, or whether a function belongs to a commission or to a court. They are answered by working from the governing entry or statute to the institution, showing the route rather than announcing the destination. Evaluation demands ask whether preventive detention should exist in a constitutional democracy at all, and they are answered well only when both sides are put at full strength before a position is taken.

The evaluation demand requires particular care, because a candidate who states one side strongly and the other as a gesture has not evaluated anything. The retention case deserves to be put as its makers put it. As made by the Union in A K Roy v Union of India, the case is that preventive detention is a constitutional power deliberately written into Article 22 for situations where prosecution after the event cannot protect the community. The argument is not that detention is an unfortunate practice tolerated at the margins of the Constitution. It is that the power was placed inside the fundamental rights chapter itself, with safeguards in the same Article, because there are harms that the criminal process, which waits for an act and then proves it, arrives too late to prevent. Alongside that argument stands the defence made by governments of the National Security Act as a narrow, Board supervised instrument aimed at prevention rather than punishment. On that defence, the Act is confined by its grounds, supervised through the Advisory Board mechanism, and directed at stopping anticipated harm rather than inflicting deserved suffering, and those limits are presented as the answer to the charge that the power is arbitrary. An answer that engages the retention case must engage it in this form, as a claim about deliberate constitutional design supported by an institutional claim about narrowness and supervision, not as a caricature of executive convenience.

The abolition or strict limitation case has makers of equal standing, and it too must be stated in its strongest form. As made by the petitioners in A K Roy, the challenge attacked the preventive detention power as it stood, pressing for the safeguards and the surrounding rights to be read in a manner that confined the power far more tightly than the Union accepted. Khanna J, dissenting in ADM Jabalpur, gave the limitation case its most enduring moral form in the argument over the worth of a liberty right that vanishes when it is needed, an insistence that a right which offers no protection in the emergency for which protection matters most has been misunderstood from the start. Gupta J, dissenting in A K Roy, added an institutional argument directed at executive refusal to commence a reform Parliament had enacted, so that the objection was not only to the content of the law but to the executive holding in suspense a change the legislature had already made. The Janata government's repeal of MISA in 1978 completes the case from the political side, since repeal by a government formed in reaction to Emergency detention stands as a political verdict on what detention powers had made possible, delivered through the ordinary democratic process rather than through a court. Together, these arguments contend that a liberty right must hold under pressure, that enacted reform must not wait on executive pleasure, and that the political community itself has on occasion judged the power, as exercised, to have failed.

An answer to an evaluation demand earns its marks by holding those two cases together long enough for a judgment to be formed between them, and the same discipline governs every other type of demand. The building sequence is constant. The candidate first identifies the clause engaged, so that the answer is anchored in the constitutional text rather than in general impressions of fairness. The candidate then locates the statute or institution in issue, placing it by its ground, its entry and its status, so that the discussion that follows is about the right law and the right body. Next the candidate states the proposition with its case, giving the rule or holding and the authority for it together. The answer then acknowledges the strongest counter point, not a weak version selected for easy rebuttal, but the objection at full strength, whether that objection is a dissent, a competing allocation, or the opposing side of the evaluation divide. The answer closes with a defended position, a conclusion supported from the material already laid out, rather than a summary that restates both sides and decides neither. A proposition without acknowledgement of its counter point reads as advocacy rather than analysis, and a position without the preceding steps is assertion.

The marking logic behind the sequence can be stated in a single comparison. A proposition stated without its citation scores better than a citation stated without its proposition. A candidate who sets out, accurately, what a case decided, and argues from that statement, has given the examiner law to reward even if the name or the bench strength momentarily escapes memory, because the substance of legal knowledge is the proposition and its reasoning. A candidate who produces the name of a case, correctly presented, but cannot say what the case decided or why it matters to the question asked, has given the examiner a label and nothing to mark. That logic explains the shape of the whole guide. Recall matters because errors of entry, clause, status and figure corrupt propositions at their root, and for that reason the short recall list must be exact. Reasoning matters more because marks live in the propositions built, tested against counter points, and carried to a defended position. A candidate who enters the examination with the recall list cold, the clause map practised on unfamiliar statutes, both sides of the evaluation argument ready at full strength, and the building sequence settled as a habit, is prepared for the questions as they are framed, including questions whose precise form has never been seen before.

## The revision lattice: organising the whole area on one page

Revision fails in this area for a reason that has little to do with effort. A candidate may have read Article 22 several times, worked through the preventive detention statutes in chronological order, and summarised the leading cases in a separate notebook, and still lose marks on a straightforward question because the material was stored by source rather than by the form in which it is asked. Statutes are read one after another. Cases are read one after another. The examination does not proceed in that order. It proceeds by question type. A question asks for a provision, or for the identity and status of a statute, or for the proposition a case actually decided, or for the character of an institution, or for the distribution of legislative power between the Union and the States. Each type has its own anchor, its own characteristic confusion, and its own short resolving line, and the candidate who has revised by type recognises the demand of the question before reaching for content. The candidate who has revised by source has to translate under pressure, and translation under pressure is where structural errors enter.

The lattice that follows is therefore organised by question type rather than by statute. That choice is deliberate. A statute based arrangement would reproduce the order of reading and would flatter the sense of familiarity that reading produces. A type based arrangement reproduces the order of answering. It forces a different discipline. For each row the candidate holds three things at once. The first is an anchor, the fixed point to which the answer must be attached before any elaboration begins. The second is a confusion, the particular error that examiners in this area are able to detect at once because it recurs in script after script. The third is a resolving line, one sentence that settles the confusion and can be written, spoken, or tested without support from notes. If the resolving line cannot be produced cleanly, the row has not been revised, however many pages stand behind it. If the line can be produced cleanly and then expanded with clause, chronology, or proposition as the question requires, the row is secure.

The five rows cover the whole area because the area, for examination purposes, divides in that way. Constitutional provision questions turn on the internal architecture of Article 22, and nothing else will answer them if that architecture is misplaced. Statute identification questions turn on the chain of general preventive detention legislation and on the difference between a statute that formed part of that chain and a statute that remains in force. Case proposition questions turn on attribution, on assigning each proposition to the case that actually decided it and refusing propositions to cases that decided something narrower or something different. Institution questions turn on source and effect, on whether a body is constitutional or statutory and on whether its conclusions bind or recommend. Federalism questions turn on partition, on which grounds of preventive detention belong to the Union field and which belong to the Concurrent field. A candidate who sees those five demands as five separate skills, rather than as one undifferentiated subject called civil liberties, revises more honestly, because a weakness in one row can no longer be concealed by strength in another.

There is a further advantage in the form itself. A lattice fits on one page, and a subject that fits on one page can be surveyed whole. Survey matters in this area because so many errors are errors of boundary. The arrest safeguards are mistaken for detention safeguards when the boundary between clauses (1) and (2) and clauses (3) to (7) is not kept in view. A repealed or lapsed statute is listed as living law when the boundary between the history of the chain and its present member is not kept in view. A proposition is assigned to the wrong case when the boundary between the silo doctrine and the later fairness reading of Article 21 is not kept in view. A recommendatory commission is treated as a court, or a trial court is treated as a commission, when institutional boundaries are blurred. Legislative competence is assigned to Parliament alone when the partition of grounds is not kept in view. In each instance the candidate often knows the material on both sides of the boundary. The failure is to place it on the correct side. A one page structure whose entire purpose is the placing of boundaries addresses that failure directly.

The lattice should therefore be read as a diagnostic instrument before it is read as a summary. A summary tells the candidate what the subject contains. A diagnostic tells the candidate where an answer is likely to break. The confusion column performs that second function. It names the error in advance, in plain terms, so that the error loses the advantage of surprise. A candidate who has repeatedly confronted the statement that clauses (1) and (2) are being treated as detention safeguards will notice the same movement beginning in a draft answer and will stop it. A candidate who has only read a correct exposition may never notice, because a correct exposition does not show what an incorrect answer looks like from inside. Revision that includes the named confusion alongside the correct anchor trains recognition as well as recall, and recognition is the skill that the examination hall rewards first.

| Question type | Anchor to hold | The commonest confusion | The one-line answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitutional provision questions | Article 22, clause by clause | Treating the arrest safeguards in clauses (1) and (2) as if they applied to detention | Clauses (1) and (2) govern arrest; clauses (3) to (7) govern preventive detention, and clause (3) switches the first pair off. |
| Statute identification questions | The chain from the 1950 Act to the National Security Act | Listing the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 or the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971 as in force | The 1950 Act lapsed at the end of 1969 and MISA was repealed in 1978; the National Security Act, 1980 is the general detention statute in the chain. |
| Case proposition questions | One proposition per leading case | Citing A K Gopalan for the fairness of procedure under Article 21 | Gopalan upheld the 1950 Act on the silo doctrine; the fairness reading of Article 21 arrived with Maneka Gandhi in 1978. |
| Institution questions | The Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 | Assuming the National Human Rights Commission is a constitutional body or that its recommendations bind | The Commission is statutory and recommendatory; Human Rights Courts under Section 30 are trial courts, a different thing. |
| Federalism questions | Entry 9 of the Union List and Entry 3 of the Concurrent List | Assuming Parliament alone can legislate on preventive detention | The grounds are partitioned: defence, foreign relations and the security of India to the Union; the security of a State, public order and essential supplies to the Concurrent field. |

The lattice is used in a revision cycle by working row by row, and by refusing to move at the speed of reading. Reading a row takes seconds and proves almost nothing. The test for each row is oral and exact. The candidate looks at the question type, covers the remaining columns, and states the anchor, the confusion, and the one-line answer aloud, in full sentences, without approximation. Approximation is the warning sign. A candidate who says that Article 22 deals broadly with arrest and detention has approximated, because the whole point of the first row is that the Article deals with them in separate clauses with a switching provision between them. A candidate who says that the older detention statutes are no longer applicable has approximated in the second row, because the disciplined answer names the manner of ending, lapse for the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 at the end of 1969 and repeal for the Maintenance of Internal Security Act in 1978, and then names the National Security Act, 1980 as the general statute that remains. Precision of that kind cannot be faked aloud, which is why the spoken test is superior to a silent rereading of notes.

Only the rows that fail should be expanded. That rule protects revision time and directs it where the loss of marks is actually located. If the federalism row fails, the candidate returns to Entry 9 of the Union List and Entry 3 of the Concurrent List and rebuilds the partition of grounds until the one-line answer can be spoken without hesitation. If the federalism row holds, it is left alone, however interesting further reading on legislative distribution might be. If the case row fails, the candidate returns to the proposition discipline, one proposition per leading case, and separates the silo doctrine upheld in A K Gopalan from the fairness reading of procedure established by law that arrived with Maneka Gandhi in 1978. Expansion is therefore remedial rather than accumulative. The candidate is not collecting more material in each cycle. The candidate is repairing a named joint in a structure that is otherwise held firmly, and each successive cycle should require less expansion than the one before it. A cycle that requires the same expansion in the same place has identified a fault that ordinary revision is not repairing, and that fault then deserves a fuller return to the clause map or the case proposition in its original form.

The spoken test also changes the status of the one-line answer. In the lattice the line looks like a compression, a shortened version of a longer truth held elsewhere. In use the relationship reverses. The one-line answer becomes the primary possession, and the longer material becomes its support. That reversal matches the conditions of answering. Under time pressure a candidate does not begin from a mass of undifferentiated knowledge and hope that structure emerges during writing. A disciplined candidate begins from the resolving line, places it early, and then earns further marks by expanding it in the direction the question demands, by clause for a provision question, by chronology for a statute question, by proposition for a case question, by source and effect for an institution question, and by ground for a federalism question. The line gives the answer its spine. Expansion gives it weight. An answer with weight and no spine collapses into generality, and generality is what the confusion column describes in each row.

The lattice should be kept as the top sheet of a personal file, with the fuller working material held behind it in the order the rows require. The clause map of Article 22 belongs behind the first row, drawn so that clauses (1) and (2) stand on the arrest side of the divide, clause (3) stands as the provision that switches those safeguards off for the classes it covers, and clauses (3) to (7) together govern preventive detention. The statute chain belongs behind the second row, arranged so that ended statutes and living statutes cannot be mistaken for one another. Case propositions belong behind the third row, stated as propositions first and citations second, so that attribution can be tested in either direction. The institutional notes on the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 belong behind the fourth row, keeping the statutory and recommendatory character of the National Human Rights Commission distinct from the trial court character of Human Rights Courts under Section 30. The entry map belongs behind the fifth row, holding the partition of grounds where it can be checked against any question on competence. Kept in that order, the file ceases to be a store of notes and becomes an answering instrument whose first page tells the candidate how every later page is to be used.

Rehearsal should follow the same order. A candidate preparing for a written paper gains little from revising the lattice in silence and then meeting its questions for the first time in the hall, so the rows should be converted into prompts and worked under timed conditions; one practical way of doing that is to [run the lattice and your previous-year question sets as a slide deck in the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), advancing one row at a time and speaking the resolving line before the next slide is allowed to appear. The method sounds austere because it removes the comfort of recognition. A slide that asks for the anchor of an institution question does not supply the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 as a heading to be recognised. It requires the Act to be produced. That act of production, repeated until it is unhurried, is the difference between familiarity and command. Candidates who organise their wider constitutional revision in the same spirit may also find the pattern familiar from [the companion study map for constitutional amendments](/2010/05/15/constitutional-amendments-upsc-study-map/), where a large constitutional area is likewise reduced to anchors that can be tested rather than merely reread.

A final discipline governs the cycle as a whole. The lattice is not enlarged. When new material is mastered, it is filed behind the correct row; it does not become a sixth row, and it does not stretch an existing one-line answer into a paragraph. The moment the top sheet ceases to fit on one page, it ceases to perform its function, because its function is survey at a glance. A detail earns a place behind a row only if it expands an anchor, sharpens a confusion, or supports a resolving line. That severity is not a denial of depth. Depth has its place in the file and in the expanded answer, while the top sheet has a narrower office, to hold the structure steady when time is short and the question is unfamiliar.


## Verdict: a small map, held firmly

The claim that runs through the whole area can be named in three steps, and the order of the steps is part of the claim. Clause first, statute second, case third. A candidate begins with the constitutional architecture, because Article 22 decides what kind of deprivation of liberty is under discussion and which safeguards belong to it. The candidate proceeds next to the statute, because the statute must be identified by name, placed in its chain, and recognised as living law or as an ended predecessor before its provisions can carry any argument. The candidate turns last to the case, because a case proposition has force only when it is attached to the correct clause and the correct statutory setting.

The ground for confidence is the size of the area when it is seen structurally. Held as a structure, the area is small enough to be mastered. Two structures in fact carry most of it. The first is the clause architecture of Article 22, with its division between arrest and preventive detention and its switching provision in clause (3). The second is the statute chain, running from the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 through the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971 to the National Security Act, 1980, with the endings of the earlier statutes understood as clearly as their enactment. Over those two structures stands a short list of propositions drawn from the leading cases and from the institutional and federal provisions, propositions that can be stated one by one and tested one by one. A candidate is not required to master an endless field. The requirement is to hold two structures firmly and to supervise them with propositions that are accurately attributed.

That analysis explains why the errors that cost marks in this area are so often structural errors rather than knowledge gaps. A knowledge gap is an absence, and its remedy is acquisition. A structural error is a misplacement. The candidate has met clauses (1) and (2) and has met clauses (3) to (7), but applies the first pair where the second group governs. The candidate knows the names in the statute chain but cannot say which members have ended and which remains. The candidate knows the names Gopalan and Maneka Gandhi but assigns the fairness reading of Article 21 to the decision that upheld the silo doctrine.

Structural errors are for that reason the cheapest kind to fix. Cheap does not mean effortless. It means that the return on a focused effort is high and durable, because a single correction governs many future answers. Learning to place clause (3) correctly alters every Article 22 answer the candidate will ever write. Learning to refuse a proposition to a case that did not decide it alters every case answer. A candidate facing a knowledge gap must acquire material item by item. A candidate facing a structural error must repair a joint, and the repair then works silently across the paper. Revision time should be allocated with that economy in mind, searching first for misplacements and treating fluent recall of isolated facts as an insufficient standard until the facts stand in their correct relations.

Preparedness, judged by the way the papers actually test, has a practical shape. A prepared candidate can run the clause map on an unseen statute, reading its provisions against Article 22 and asking which side of the clause divide each provision occupies, rather than waiting for a familiar statute name to trigger a memorised summary. A prepared candidate can state the propositions without leaning on citations, giving the proposition in a complete sentence before naming the case that supports it, so that the citation confirms the statement instead of substituting for it. A prepared candidate can refuse the two-month trap, the inviting shortcut that tempts a hurried answer into compressing a governed period or condition into a neat figure detached from the clause that qualifies it, and can return instead to the text and its structure. The examination hall will supply none of the apparatus, only a question and a limited period, so the discipline has to survive as habit: read the question for its type, name its anchor, state its resolving line, and only then expand along the line the type prescribes.

For the examination hall, hold the rule in its shortest form and apply it to every question before writing a word of substance: clause first, statute second, case third, and no proposition without its correct home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How are civil liberties laws tested in the civil services examination?

They are tested less as narrative history than as structure. The recurring demands are identification demands, such as which clause of Article 22 governs a described situation, which statute in the detention chain is in force, and which body a described institution is; proposition demands, which ask what a leading case decided; and evaluation demands, which ask whether a detention power should exist in its present form. A candidate who has organised the area around the clause architecture of Article 22 and the chronological chain of statutes can meet each type, because every question in the area is generated by one of those two structures or by the short list of cases that supervise them.

### Q: What should you identify first in a civil liberties question, the clause, the statute or the case?

The clause. Almost every question in this area is generated by one clause of Article 22, and identifying that clause first fixes the ground rules before any statute or case is reached for. A question about production before a magistrate is a clause (2) question; a question about grounds and representation is a clause (5) question; a question about detention beyond three months is a clause (4) question. Once the clause is named, the relevant statute slot and the governing proposition follow in order. Candidates who begin with a remembered case name routinely answer a neighbouring question instead, because the case they remember was decided under a different clause from the one the examiner has set.

### Q: Which preventive detention facts are most often asked in exams?

The facts that recur are the structural ones. The split inside Article 22, with clauses (1) and (2) governing arrest and clauses (3) to (7) governing preventive detention, is asked in many forms. The three-month period in clause (4), after which an Advisory Board must report, and the status of each statute in the chain, are asked with traps built in: the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 has lapsed and the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971 has been repealed, while the National Security Act, 1980 remains in force. The grounds partition between Entry 9 of the Union List and Entry 3 of the Concurrent List, and the non-commencement of Section 3 of the 44th Amendment, complete the set that examiners return to.

### Q: How do you remember the Article 22 clauses for the exam?

Remember the article as two blocks and a hinge. The first block, clauses (1) and (2), protects a person who is arrested: grounds told at once, a lawyer of choice, and a magistrate within twenty-four hours. The hinge is clause (3), which switches that block off for enemy aliens and for preventive detention. The second block, clauses (4) to (7), is the detention code: the three-month Advisory Board checkpoint in clause (4), grounds and representation in clause (5), permitted non-disclosure in clause (6), and Parliament's power to prescribe longer detention and Board procedure in clause (7). Anchoring each clause to its function, rather than reciting the text, makes the sequence retrievable under pressure.

### Q: How can you place a detention statute in its period without learning the dates by rote?

Place it by the political logic of one of four periods. A statute of 1950 belongs to the founding compromise, when detention was written into the Constitution beside the liberty it limits. A statute of the early 1970s belongs to the period that produced the Maintenance of Internal Security Act and then the Emergency. A statute or amendment of the late 1970s belongs to the restoration, which repealed MISA and passed the 44th Amendment. A statute of 1980, the National Security Act, belongs to the long period that followed, in which the machinery was rebuilt and the courts supervised its procedure without the legislature changing it. The period supplies the context an answer needs; the year can then be attached to the period rather than memorised in isolation.

### Q: Which detention grounds come from the Union List and which from the Concurrent List?

Entry 9 of the Union List gives Parliament preventive detention for reasons connected with the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, or the security of India. Entry 3 of the Concurrent List gives Parliament and the state legislatures preventive detention for reasons connected with the security of a State, the maintenance of public order, or the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. The grounds are partitioned rather than shared, so the enabling entry follows from the ground written into the statute. A state goondas law rests on public order in Entry 3; a central law aimed at the security of India draws on Entry 9, and the National Security Act draws on both fields.

### Q: Is ADM Jabalpur still relevant for judiciary exams?

It is, and in two distinct ways that should not be merged. Doctrinally, the case is the negative pole of Indian liberty law: the 1976 majority held that with the enforcement of Article 21 suspended in an Emergency, no habeas corpus petition could be maintained against a detention, and that holding stood until the nine-judge bench in Puttaswamy overruled it in 2017. Institutionally, Khanna J's dissent remains the standard study in judicial courage and in the cost of its absence, since he alone held that life and liberty survive an Emergency. Judicial services examinations test both layers, the doctrine because it structures the law of Article 359, and the dissent because it is the area's central lesson about courts.

### Q: How do you answer a question on a state detention law you have never studied?

Run the clause map before reaching for anything specific. Ask which ground the statute names, because that fixes the enabling entry, usually Entry 3 of the Concurrent List for public order. Ask which safeguards survive, and the answer is the Article 22 detention code: grounds communicated under clause (5), a representation to the detaining authority, an Advisory Board checkpoint under clause (4), and habeas corpus under Article 226 or Article 32 as the challenge route, since there is no charge and therefore no bail. Then state honestly what the map cannot supply, such as the Act's own maximum period and authorised officers, rather than inventing them. That combination of structure and candour is what such questions reward.

### Q: Which civil liberties facts are pure recall that reasoning cannot replace?

A short, closed list. The clause numbers of Article 22 and the function of each. The grounds in Entry 9 of the Union List and Entry 3 of the Concurrent List, and which legislature each empowers. The bench strengths of the leading cases: six judges in A K Gopalan, five in ADM Jabalpur, nine in Puttaswamy, five in A K Roy and five in Naga People's Movement. The status of each statute in the chain, lapsed, repealed or in force. The three-month figure in clause (4) as the law stands. The statutory and recommendatory character of the National Human Rights Commission. Everything else in the area, applying the map, placing a statute, judging a problem by the concentric circles, can be reasoned out on the day from those fixed points.

### Q: How should you write a Mains answer on AFSPA?

Build it around the declaration, not around the powers. Section 4 of the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 attracts the attention, but the examinable centre is Section 3: who may declare an area disturbed, with the Governor, the Administrator and the Central Government all empowered, and no requirement of state concurrence for a central declaration. Then bring in Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v Union of India, in which a five-judge bench upheld the Act while reading in safeguards, including periodic review of the declaration at intervals of six months, and the 2016 decision in the Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association case, which denied blanket immunity for excessive force. Close with the federalism tension stated fairly, since that is where the argument in the area actually lives.

### Q: How should previous-year questions be used when preparing civil liberties?

Use them as a map of demands rather than as a prediction of questions. Sort the questions encountered by type, identification, proposition, status, allocation and evaluation, and note which structure each type draws on: the Article 22 clauses, the statute chain, the List entries, the institutions under the Protection of Human Rights Act, or the case propositions. A type that recurs is a structure worth mastering; a structure that generates no questions can be revised lightly. Working this way also exposes the traps the papers build in, such as offering the two-month advisory board period as a plausible option, and it converts a pile of old papers into a revision order. Predicting that a specific question will repeat is the least valuable use of the material.

### Q: Which human rights commissions do aspirants confuse?

The confusion runs in two directions. Within the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, candidates merge the National Human Rights Commission, the State Human Rights Commissions and the Human Rights Courts into one body, when the first two are commissions making recommendations and the third is a court of session trying offences. Across the wider landscape, candidates assume every national commission has the same legal character, when the National Human Rights Commission and the National Commission for Women are statutory, while the Commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Backward Classes are constitutional, under Articles 338, 338A and 338B. The deemed membership of the other commissions' chairpersons in the National Commission makes the boundary look softer than it is, and examiners test exactly that boundary.

### Q: What bench strengths go with the leading civil liberties cases?

Six judges decided A K Gopalan v State of Madras in 1950, with Kania CJ presiding. Five judges decided Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla in 1976, by four to one. Nine judges decided Justice K S Puttaswamy (Retd) v Union of India in 2017, the bench that recognised privacy and overruled the Jabalpur majority. Five judges decided A K Roy v Union of India, the National Security Act case, and five judges, sitting as a Constitution Bench, decided Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v Union of India in 1997. Bench strength is a favourite preliminary examination discriminator in this area because it cannot be reasoned out; it is either known or it is not, which is why it belongs on the recall list.

### Q: How much case law do you need for civil liberties questions?

Less than candidates fear, held more precisely than most hold it. Five decisions carry the area: A K Gopalan for the silo doctrine and procedure established by law, ADM Jabalpur for the Emergency failure and its overruling in Puttaswamy, A K Roy for the National Security Act and the commencement holding, Naga People's Movement for the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, and Ram Manohar Lohia for the concentric circles of law and order, public order and the security of the State. For each, the requirement is one accurate proposition and the limits of what was decided. Beyond that spine, additional case names add little, and a wrong proposition attached to a famous name loses more than an unnamed correct proposition would have done.

### Q: How do you write about the 44th Amendment in an answer without misstating the law?

Separate the two events that notes habitually compress into one. The Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978 was passed and received Presidential assent on 30 April 1979, and Section 3 of that Act would have reduced the Advisory Board period in Article 22 from three months to two and reconstituted the Boards under the Chief Justice's recommendation. But the Act's own commencement clause left each provision to be notified, and Section 3 has never been notified, so the Constitution as it operates retains three months and the original Board composition. Write both halves, enactment and non-commencement, and the answer is safe. Writing only the first half states the law as it is not, which is the single most penalised error in this area.

### Q: Is it better to read the leading judgments in full or to work from summaries?

For this area, a layered approach serves better than either extreme. The propositions of the five spine cases should be taken from reliable summaries and standard commentaries, because the examination rewards the proposition, its limits and its later history, and those are stated more cleanly there. Two texts repay direct reading in extract: Khanna J's dissent in ADM Jabalpur, because its reasoning is itself examinable and frequently characterised in questions, and the operative directions in Naga People's Movement, because the safeguards read into the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act are the holding, not commentary on it. Reading every judgment in full spends preparation time on material the papers do not test, while relying on summaries alone leaves a candidate unable to handle a question framed around a dissent.

### Q: What is the commonest mistake in preventive detention questions?

Treating preventive detention as a species of arrest. The error appears in several costumes: giving the detenu the clause (1) rights to a lawyer of choice, or production before a magistrate within twenty-four hours under clause (2), when clause (3) withdraws both; proposing bail, when there is no charge and the remedy is habeas corpus; and describing detention as punishment for an offence, when its constitutional character is prevention on the detaining authority's satisfaction. Each version flows from importing the arrest framework into a detention question. The correction is mechanical: the moment a question concerns preventive detention, clauses (1) and (2) leave the page and clauses (3) to (7) take their place, and every consequence follows from that substitution.

### Q: Is civil liberties worth preparing in depth for the civil services examination?

It is worth preparing to structural mastery, which is a different investment from exhaustive depth. The area is bounded: one constitutional article read clause by clause, one chain of statutes with known endings, one partition of List entries, one institutions Act, and five cases with one proposition each. That is a small corpus by polity standards, and it generates questions across the general studies papers, the law optional and the judicial services examinations, so the return on the investment is spread widely. What does not repay effort is accumulation beyond the structure, long lists of minor decisions and detention statistics that the papers cannot fairly test. Master the map, hold the short recall list cold, and the area converts from a source of traps into a reliable score.

### Q: How do the state judicial services examinations test this area differently from the civil services?

The centre of gravity shifts from policy to doctrine. Civil services questions in this area tend to ask for evaluation, federal allocation and institutional design, framed for a generalist answer. Judicial services papers press harder on the operative law: the precise clause of Article 22 engaged by a fact situation, the holding and bench of the leading cases, the availability of habeas corpus and the grounds on which detention orders are quashed, such as delay in considering a representation or failure to supply the materials relied on. ADM Jabalpur and the law of Article 359 carry more weight, and answers are marked against legal accuracy rather than breadth of context. A candidate preparing for both should revise the same map but weight the judicial services version toward propositions, procedures and remedies.

### Q: How do you answer a question that asks whether preventive detention should be retained or abolished?

Give each side its strongest form before taking a position. For retention, the case made by the Union in A K Roy v Union of India: the power is not an executive improvisation but a deliberate constitutional choice in Article 22, aimed at prevention where prosecution after the event cannot protect the community, and disciplined by the Advisory Board and by judicial review of the procedure. Against, the case of the petitioners in the same litigation, Khanna J's dissent in ADM Jabalpur on rights that vanish when needed, and the political verdict of 1978, when MISA was repealed and the 44th Amendment tried to tighten clause (4). Then defend a conclusion, commonly retention under stricter safeguards, and name the deciding consideration rather than splitting the difference.
