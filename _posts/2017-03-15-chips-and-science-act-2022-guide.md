When the CHIPS and Science Act reached the president's desk in the summer of 2022, it arrived under a name that promised coherence: one law, one purpose, one national project. The signature on August 9, 2022 turned H.R. 4346 into Public Law 117-167, and the headlines condensed the achievement into a single round number, roughly 280 billion dollars. That number, repeated across coverage for months afterward, did more to confuse readers than any other feature of the episode. The document the president signed was not one statute but two, bound together by legislative necessity rather than by logical design. The first division carried industrial policy with real money behind it: appropriated funds for semiconductor manufacturing incentives, research facilities, and workforce programs, plus a tax credit for building advanced fabrication plants on American soil. The second division carried a different kind of instrument altogether: authorizations for the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the Department of Energy's Office of Science, including a new foundation directorate for technology and innovation, all of which amounted to permission for Congress to spend money later rather than money itself. The single most important fact about American science policy, as this statute illustrates it, lives in that difference. Understand which half of the law moves cash and which half moves intentions, and nearly every headline about the measure becomes legible. Confuse the two, and the misunderstandings multiply: the spending that never happened gets reported as spending, the intentions get scored as achievements, and the actual industrial policy, the part with money and conditions attached, disappears into the fog of the larger number.

## Two statutes, one signature

### What are the two divisions of the CHIPS and Science Act?

Division A, the CHIPS Act of 2022, appropriates 52.7 billion dollars for semiconductor manufacturing, research, and workforce programs for fiscal years 2022 through 2027, alongside a 25 percent investment tax credit. Division B, the Research and Development, Competition, and Innovation Act, authorizes large sums for federal science agencies without appropriating them. Only Division A moves money.

The statutory identity of the measure is worth fixing precisely, because the public conversation around it has been loose with labels. The enrolled bill was H.R. 4346, a House bill whose official title as enacted was the deliberately uninformative "Making appropriations for Legislative Branch for the fiscal year ending September 30, 2022, and for other purposes." That bland title is the fingerprint of a legislative vehicle: a minor bill that both chambers agreed to hollow out and refill with unrelated text once a deal was struck. The Congressional Research Service identifies the popular title as the CHIPS and Science Act. The 117th Congress passed it on July 27 and July 28, 2022, the president signed it on August 9, 2022, and it entered the statute books as Public Law 117-167, recorded at 136 Stat. 1366. None of that is contested. What remains widely misunderstood is the document's internal structure, and that structure is the frame through which everything else in this article should be read.

Division A is the semiconductor half. Its short title is the CHIPS Act of 2022, and its content is the industrial policy: direct appropriations totaling 52.7 billion dollars across fiscal years 2022 through 2027, distributed through four funds for manufacturing incentives, research and development, workforce education, defense-related microelectronics, and international technology security cooperation. Alongside those appropriations sits the Advanced Manufacturing Investment Credit, a 25 percent investment tax credit codified at section 48D of the Internal Revenue Code for qualified investment in advanced manufacturing facilities. This half of the law is money with conditions attached. It appropriates real dollars, it creates a tax benefit that reduces federal revenue, and it binds recipients to conduct requirements that reach beyond American borders. It is, in the plainest terms, the government paying companies to build and expand chipmaking capacity in the United States and punishing them financially if they use the assistance to expand advanced capacity in the wrong countries.

Division B is the science half. Its short title is the Research and Development, Competition, and Innovation Act, and its content is authorization: multi-year spending permissions for the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the Department of Energy's science programs, plus the creation of a new foundation directorate oriented toward technology, innovation, and regional competitiveness. Authorization is the step that makes spending legal. It is not the step that makes spending happen. An authorization says Congress may appropriate up to a stated sum in a given fiscal year; it moves no money from the Treasury. The practical outcome of Division B depended entirely on subsequent appropriations acts, the annual funding bills that decide what agencies actually receive, and those acts did not reach the authorized levels. This is not a technicality or a footnote. It is the central fact of the science half, and the reason the article's companion table lists, for each major program, the amount authorized, whether appropriations followed, and through which mechanism. The namable claim of this profile is that two statutes, one signature, describes the entire situation: the semiconductor half is industrial policy with cash and conditions attached, the science half is a wish list requiring annual appropriations, and treating them as a single act produces every misunderstanding about what this law did.

The distinction matters because American science policy runs on the gap between the two instruments. Authorizations are the language of aspiration. They are how Congress declares that a program should exist at a certain scale, and they are politically cheap, because an authorization commits no funds and creates no winners or losers in the current budget. Appropriations are the language of consequence. They are fought over in committee, scored by budget analysts, and felt by agencies. A statute that pairs 52.7 billion dollars in real appropriations with authorizations of several times that amount will be read by most of the public as a spending package equal to the sum of both, and that reading is wrong in exactly the way that matters. The semiconductor money exists. The science authorizations are intentions. The difference between those two halves is the single most important thing to understand about the CHIPS and Science Act, and by extension about how the United States funds, or declines to fund, its scientific enterprise.

One more piece of the identity deserves attention before the history: the statute's relationship to its predecessor. The CHIPS for America programs were first established and authorized, but not funded, by Title XCIX of the William M. Thornberry National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2021, Public Law 116-283, signed on January 1, 2021. That earlier law created the legal architecture for semiconductor incentives and assigned it to the Department of Commerce, but it left the architecture empty of cash. Division A of the 2022 act did two things at once: it appropriated the 52.7 billion dollars that filled the architecture, and it revised the underlying 2021 provisions, adding the three additional funds for defense, workforce and education, and international technology security, and writing the guardrail and clawback provisions that the 2021 text lacked. This is why the Congressional Research Service describes Division A as appropriating funds "for the CHIPS for America provisions enacted in" the 2021 defense authorization. The 2022 act did not invent the program from nothing. It funded a program Congress had already authorized nineteen months earlier, and it hardened the program with conditions that reflected how the political argument had evolved in the meantime.

## Three Years to a Signature: How the CHIPS and Science Act Passed

The statute that emerged in August 2022 was the surviving fragment of a legislative effort that began more than two years earlier and died at least once along the way. Its origins lie in two converging anxieties of 2020: a global semiconductor shortage that idled auto plants and exposed the fragility of concentrated supply chains, and a bipartisan conviction that China was out-investing the United States in the research and industrial capacity that would define the next decades of technological competition. The first legislative expression of that conviction was the Endless Frontier Act, introduced in May 2020 in both chambers, S. 3832 in the Senate on May 21 and H.R. 6978 in the House the next day, which proposed a vast expansion of the National Science Foundation around a new technology directorate. The bill died with the 116th Congress, but its core idea, a technology-focused NSF directorate, survived into everything that followed.

The Endless Frontier proposal had paired the directorate with a $100 billion five-year authorization, a figure that set the scale for everything after it. Its death was ordinary: referred to committees, never marked up, overtaken by the pandemic and the election. But the idea had acquired sponsors in both parties who believed American research funding had grown too concentrated in biomedical science and too diffuse in its connection to industrial competitiveness, and those sponsors carried the directorate concept into the 117th Congress as the centerpiece of the Senate's bill.

In the 117th Congress the effort took Senate form as the United States Innovation and Competition Act, S. 1260, introduced by Majority Leader Chuck Schumer on April 20, 2021. After weeks of amendment fights, the Senate passed it on June 8, 2021, by 68 to 32, Record Vote 226, with nineteen Republicans joining the Democratic majority. The bill combined the semiconductor incentives, which had been authorized without funds in the fiscal year 2021 defense authorization act, with a sweeping science and competitiveness agenda: the NSF expansion, the technology directorate, regional hubs, research security provisions, and trade measures. It was, in substance, the broad competition and innovation bill, and its comfortable bipartisan margin suggested a durable consensus.

The Senate's margin survived genuine fights. An amendment from Senator Marco Rubio to bar grants to companies with financial ties to China was tabled 55 to 40, and an amendment from Senator John Cornyn to reshape the semiconductor incentive program itself failed 42 to 58. Both votes previewed the fault lines that would later matter: how tightly to couple the subsidies to restrictions on China-facing business, and how much discretion to leave the Commerce Department in designing the awards. The semiconductor provisions that passed were the ones both parties could describe as security policy, which is why they survived the later narrowing when the broader bill did not.

The House answered with its own version, the America COMPETES Act, H.R. 4521, which passed on February 4, 2022, by 222 to 210, nearly along party lines, with a single Republican voting yes and a single Democrat voting no. The House bill was longer and more ideologically freighted than the Senate's, carrying trade, labor, and immigration provisions that Senate Republicans opposed, alongside its own $52 billion in semiconductor funding and its own ambitious science authorizations. The two chambers had now passed two different bills on the same subject, which under normal procedure meant one thing: a conference committee to reconcile them.

The House bill's distinct contents explain why the conference failed. Beyond the semiconductor money and the science authorizations, COMPETES carried a $45 billion supply-chain resilience program, trade enforcement measures, labor provisions, and immigration reforms for STEM graduates and entrepreneurs that had no counterpart in the Senate bill and that Senate Republicans treated as unacceptable additions. The House had loaded its vehicle with the priorities of its committees; the Senate had no intention of accepting them. Conference committees reconcile differences only when both chambers want an agreement more than they want their own provisions, and by the spring of 2022 that condition no longer held. The Senate obliged in March 2022 by substituting the text of its bill into the House's vehicle, H.R. 4521, and conferees were named. The two bills were collectively nicknamed the Bipartisan Innovation Act, a name that did not survive contact with the conference. For an explanation of the procedure that was supposed to follow, this [how conference committees work](/2022/03/15/conference-committees-explained/) guide lays out the mechanics.

The conference never produced a report. Through the spring and early summer of 2022 the negotiators deadlocked over the House bill's trade and labor provisions, over the scale of the science authorizations, and over unrelated political fights that kept intruding on the talks. By July the effort was widely described as stalled, and its obituaries were being drafted.

The final near-death experience came from outside the bill entirely. In late July, news of a separate Democratic reconciliation agreement on taxes, climate, and health care, the package that became the Inflation Reduction Act, infuriated House Republicans, whose leader publicly tied the two bills together and whipped against the CHIPS package in retaliation. For roughly a day the House vote looked genuinely uncertain. It held, because the semiconductor provisions had constituencies in both parties that transcended the procedural grievance, but the episode showed how contingent the rescue was: a bill that had taken three years to assemble nearly died in its final week over legislation it had nothing to do with. What rescued the semiconductor provisions was a strategic retreat: Senate leadership, with White House support, abandoned the comprehensive bill and narrowed the package to the two elements that commanded genuine bipartisan majorities, the semiconductor incentives with their appropriations and the science authorizations stripped of the most contested policy riders.

The rescue required a legislative vehicle, and the choice of vehicle reveals how Congress actually moves bills when the normal process fails. Leadership reached for H.R. 4346, a bill that had passed the House a year earlier, on July 28, 2021, by 215 to 207, as the Legislative Branch Appropriations Act, and that the Senate had then passed with an amendment by unanimous consent on June 22, 2022. It was, in the jargon, a shell: a passed bill whose contents could be entirely replaced by amendment, allowing the Senate to act without going back through committee.

The amendment ping-pong that followed is visible in the Congressional Record's unwieldy action line, the Senate amendment to the House amendment to the Senate amendment, each layer representing one chamber's rewrite of the other's text. The procedure looks absurd and is entirely routine: it is how the Senate moves major legislation when the committee process is too slow and the conference process has failed. What matters is what the procedure permitted, which was a straight up-or-down vote on the narrowed package in each chamber, with no further amendments and no conference to get lost in.

The Senate's final action included a revealing procedural footnote. Before adopting the substitute, the Senate voted 64 to 33 to waive all applicable budgetary discipline with respect to the amendment, Record Vote 270, the same margin as final passage. The waiver was necessary because the bill's mandatory appropriations and tax provisions ran afoul of the budget resolution's pay-as-you-go rules, and waiving those rules required sixty votes. The identical tallies suggest the coalition held together across both votes: the senators who wanted the bill were willing to set aside budget procedure to get it. In the House, no such waiver was needed, but the leadership's decision to bring the bill up under a structured rule with one hour of debate served the same gatekeeping function, ensuring the narrowed package could not be amended back into the broader bill the conference had failed to produce. On July 27, 2022, the Senate adopted a complete substitute, Amendment 5135, and concurred in the House amendment with that amendment by 64 to 33, Record Vote 271. The next day the House concurred in the Senate's action by 243 to 187, with one member recorded present, Roll Call 404. President Biden signed the bill on August 9, 2022, and it became Public Law 117-167. Senator John Cornyn of Texas, a Republican who had championed the semiconductor funding from the beginning, denounced the endgame maneuvering in sharp terms on the Senate floor.

The enrolled bill actually contained three divisions, though only two matter for this article's purposes. Division C, the Supreme Court Security Funding Act, provided security funding for the justices, a response to threats that had intensified that summer; it rode the vehicle because the vehicle was moving. Its presence is a useful reminder of how omnibus legislating works: a must-pass bill accumulates unrelated provisions as it moves, and the CHIPS and Science Act's title describes only the cargo its sponsors wanted advertised.

The final margins show the bill passing more comfortably in the Senate than in the House. In the Senate, 64 of 97 votes cast went in favor, a 66 percent yes share, with seventeen Republicans joining most Democrats. In the House, 243 of 430 votes cast went in favor, a 56.5 percent yes share: 219 Democrats and 24 Republicans in favor, 187 Republicans opposed, one Democrat recorded present. Both were bipartisan majorities, and the House vote was bipartisan in a thinner, more grudging sense, with the Republican leadership actively whipping against the bill even as two dozen of its members defected. The Senate's margin reflected the chamber where the semiconductor provisions had always been most popular; the House's reflected a Democratic majority spending political capital to carry a bill its own leadership had substantially rewritten in the Senate's image.

The House vote's composition tells its own story about the bill's politics. Nearly all of the yes votes came from Democrats, who supplied 219 of the 243, while the 24 Republican yes votes represented members, many with semiconductor facilities or research universities in their districts, willing to defy their leadership's whip operation. The 187 no votes were nearly all Republican, and the single member recorded present was a Democrat unwilling to vote either way. The pattern, enthusiastic Democratic support plus a minority of Republicans voting their districts over their party, is the signature of industrial policy in a polarized chamber: popular enough to pass, contested enough that every future appropriation would be a fight.

The passage story matters for more than color, because it explains why the statute looks the way it does. The semiconductor division carries appropriated money because the semiconductor appropriations were the irreducible core, the provision that could not be dropped without losing the coalition. The science division carries authorizations rather than appropriations because the authorizations were what survived of the broader innovation agenda once the comprehensive bill collapsed; appropriating the science money would have required a different and larger fight, one the salvaged package could not sustain. Every structural feature of the law, the two divisions, the two kinds of money, the conditions on the cash, is a fossil of the three-year negotiation that produced it. Read the statute as a designed system and it looks incoherent; read it as the surviving fragment of a collapsed negotiation and it makes perfect sense.

## The eighteen months between authorization and money

One episode in the passage story deserves its own telling, because it previews the article's central theme in miniature. The semiconductor incentive programs were authorized in January 2021, nineteen months before they were funded. Title XCIX of the fiscal year 2021 defense authorization created the CHIPS for America architecture: manufacturing incentives, research programs, workforce efforts, all assigned to the Department of Commerce, all fully specified in law, and all empty of cash. For a year and a half, the United States had a semiconductor industrial policy on paper and no money to execute it. The episode is a clean demonstration of the authorization-appropriation distinction operating inside the semiconductor story itself, not just between the two divisions of the 2022 act.

The gap did not go unnoticed by the industry the program was meant to serve. Fabrication plants take years from groundbreaking to volume production, and investment decisions about where to build are made years before that. As the months passed without appropriations, chipmakers warned that the delay was threatening expansion plans, a warning reported contemporaneously as the legislation moved through its various forms. The public face of the lobbying campaign was Patrick Gelsinger, the chief executive of Intel, who argued the industry's case in Washington and in the press. In January 2022, Intel announced a 20 billion dollar investment in a new semiconductor manufacturing complex in Ohio, explicitly contingent on government support, and President Biden invited Gelsinger to attend the State of the Union address that month as a guest of honor, a piece of political theater designed to dramatize the stakes. The message was legible: private capital was prepared to move, but it wanted the public commitment first.

This dynamic, private investment conditioned on public money, is worth pausing over, because it sits at the center of the efficiency debate the statute never resolved. Supporters read the contingency as proof that the subsidies were decisive: without the public commitment, the private investment would go elsewhere, and the country would lose the capacity. Skeptics read the same contingency as evidence of bargaining power: a profitable industry extracting public money for investments it might have made anyway, or making elsewhere, with the threat of departure as leverage. The statute's design tries to split the difference through the competitive award process and the commercial-viability criteria, which are supposed to direct money toward projects where it changes outcomes rather than toward projects that need no help. Whether review processes can reliably make that distinction, when the applicants know more about their own investment plans than the reviewers ever will, is the hard question underneath the program's administration. The eighteen-month gap made the question urgent without making it answerable, and the appropriations, when they finally came, arrived with the argument still unsettled.
## Division A: the semiconductor half, in depth

### How much money did the CHIPS Act actually appropriate for semiconductors?

Congress appropriated 52.7 billion dollars for semiconductor programs for fiscal years 2022 through 2027. The total breaks into 39 billion dollars for manufacturing incentives, 13.2 billion dollars for research, development, and workforce programs, and 500 million dollars for international technology security cooperation. Every dollar of it is an appropriation, meaning the money was actually provided, not merely authorized.

The distinction between those three lines is worth more than the total, because each line buys a different kind of thing and each is administered by a different part of the government. The 39 billion dollars funds the CHIPS Incentives Program, the competitive grant and loan program through which the Department of Commerce assists the construction, expansion, and modernization of fabrication facilities in the United States. Within that 39 billion, 2 billion is reserved for mature-technology production, the older-generation chips used in automobiles, defense systems, and industrial equipment, a carve-out that reflects the pandemic-era shortages that hit carmakers hardest. The reservation matters because it directs a portion of the money away from the cutting edge and toward the unglamorous middle of the technology stack, the legacy nodes whose absence idled assembly lines.

The 13.2 billion dollars for research, development, and workforce programs subdivides further. Up to 11 billion dollars supports Commerce Department research and development efforts, including the National Semiconductor Technology Center, a new public-private research hub; the National Advanced Packaging Manufacturing Program, focused on the assembly and testing technologies that turn silicon wafers into finished components; and additional research and workforce initiatives, including new Manufacturing USA institutes oriented toward chipmaking. Two billion dollars establishes the CHIPS for America Defense Fund, which supports microelectronics research, development, testing, and workforce training for defense applications, administered with the Department of Defense. Two hundred million dollars creates the CHIPS for America Workforce and Education Fund, directed to the National Science Foundation for microelectronics workforce training and education. The remaining 500 million dollars of the 52.7 billion establishes the CHIPS for America International Technology Security and Innovation Fund, administered through the Department of State in coordination with development finance agencies, to support international information and communications technology security and semiconductor supply chain activities with allied and friendly countries. Thirty-nine plus 13.2 plus 0.5 equals 52.7, and the arithmetic is worth checking because the figure is so often garbled in retelling.

How the 39 billion dollars actually reaches companies is as important as the amount. The money is not distributed by formula, by entitlement, or by political geography. It is awarded through a competitive process run by the CHIPS Program Office inside the Department of Commerce, which issues notices of funding opportunity for different segments of the supply chain, evaluates applications against published criteria, and negotiates individual award agreements. The first such notice, covering commercial fabrication facilities, was released in February 2023. Applications are assessed on economic and national security objectives, commercial viability, the financial strength of the applicant, technical feasibility, workforce development plans, and broader community impacts, criteria described publicly by the program office's leadership. The process unfolds over years rather than months: separate funding rounds cover leading-edge fabrication, current-generation and mature-node production, materials and equipment suppliers, and research facilities, each with its own application window and review timeline.

The program's phased structure is itself a design choice worth understanding. Rather than a single application window, Commerce released separate funding opportunities for different segments of the supply chain, with distinct submission timelines for leading-edge fabrication, current-generation and mature-node production, and upstream suppliers of materials and equipment. The Congressional Research Service has noted that the sequencing of these rounds shapes who can access the money: early rounds favor the largest fabrication projects, while suppliers and smaller firms wait for later windows, and the staggered timing means the full allocation of the 39 billion dollars unfolds over years rather than in a single disbursement. The structure reflects a deliberate prioritization, but it also means that any accounting of the program's results has to specify which round, which segment, and which year. Without those specifics, it is not really an accounting at all.

The award agreements carry conditions that go well beyond the guardrails discussed later in this article. Recipients must demonstrate significant worker and community investments, including opportunities for small businesses and disadvantaged communities. Construction supported by the funds must pay Davis-Bacon prevailing wages. And for awards exceeding 150 million dollars, recipients must share a portion of any returns above a mutually agreed threshold with the federal government, an upside-sharing provision the Congressional Research Service has described as a way for taxpayers to participate in the financial success the subsidies help create. These terms make the incentives look less like a blank check and more like a negotiated partnership, which was precisely the point: the political argument for handing public money to profitable manufacturers required that the government extract commitments in return.

The workforce provisions deserve separate attention because they address the least visible bottleneck in the whole enterprise. Fabricating semiconductors requires technicians, engineers, and construction workers with specialized skills, and the domestic pipeline for those workers had atrophied along with the domestic fabrication base. The statute's workforce architecture spreads across several instruments: the 200 million dollar Workforce and Education Fund, the workforce components of the Commerce research programs, and the workforce development criteria embedded in the manufacturing incentive awards themselves, which require applicants to present credible plans for recruiting and training the people who will staff the facilities. Labor market analysts estimated the industry would need to add more than 236,000 workers to meet the goals associated with the act, a figure that gives a sense of the scale of the human-capital challenge relative to the construction challenge. Money can buy buildings faster than it can produce trained technicians.

The research facilities funded through Division A are the part of the semiconductor half most likely to be overlooked, because they do not produce chips for sale. The National Semiconductor Technology Center is conceived as a shared research infrastructure where companies, universities, and national laboratories can collaborate on the process technologies that no single firm would fund alone. The National Advanced Packaging Manufacturing Program targets the back end of production, the assembly, testing, and packaging steps where much of the value in modern chipmaking now resides and where American capability had also thinned. These are appropriated programs, real money for real institutions, and they represent the part of the industrial policy that invests in shared knowledge rather than in individual companies. Whether shared research infrastructure can substitute for the dense private ecosystems that grew up around fabrication in East Asia is one of the open questions of the program, and it is a question the statute does not answer.

A word is owed here about what the manufacturing incentives do not do, because the gap between the mechanism and the coverage is the source of a recurring misunderstanding. The incentives are awarded to specific projects through a competitive process, construction of a fabrication plant takes years from groundbreaking to volume production, and attributing any particular facility to the statute requires tracing the award: which project received how much, under which funding round, with which conditions, and on what timeline. Most public discussion does not do this tracing. Coverage tends to treat the passage of the act as the cause of every subsequent fabrication announcement, and to treat announcements as completions, collapsing a multi-year chain of application, award, construction, equipment installation, and qualification into a single event called "the CHIPS Act building a factory." The statute builds nothing by itself. It creates a program that awards money to projects that, if they proceed, build facilities over several years. Any claim about what the act built has to survive that chain, award by award.

## The full stack: research, defense, workforce, and allied coordination

The manufacturing incentives dominate public discussion of Division A, but the division's architecture is a full stack, and the non-manufacturing layers reveal what Congress thought the manufacturing money alone could not do. Start with research. The National Semiconductor Technology Center is the most ambitious of the research instruments: a shared hub where firms, universities, and national laboratories pursue the process technologies that are too expensive and too risky for any single company to develop alone. The premise is that American weakness in fabrication is not only a matter of missing factories but of missing shared knowledge, the dense web of process know-how that accumulates where fabrication is actually done. The National Advanced Packaging Manufacturing Program addresses the back end of production, the assembly, testing, and packaging steps that convert silicon wafers into finished components. Packaging has become one of the most technically demanding stages of chipmaking, and American capability there had thinned along with everything else. The Manufacturing USA institutes add a network model borrowed from earlier industrial policy: regionally anchored institutes where industry and academia collaborate on manufacturing innovation. All of these are appropriated programs, funded from the 13.2 billion dollar research, development, and workforce line, and they represent the part of the division that invests in capabilities no single award recipient owns.

The defense layer has its own logic. The CHIPS for America Defense Fund, with 2 billion dollars in appropriations, supports microelectronics research, development, testing, and workforce training oriented toward defense applications. The military's dependence on commercial fabrication is one of the quieter premises of the whole statute: the weapons systems the Department of Defense buys contain chips produced in the same commercial facilities, and often in the same foreign facilities, as consumer electronics. A defense establishment that cannot assure the provenance and integrity of its microelectronics has a supply chain problem that no amount of procurement reform can fix. The Defense Fund is the division's answer, a dedicated channel for ensuring that defense needs shape part of the research agenda rather than being an afterthought to commercial priorities.

The workforce layer, funded at 200 million dollars through the National Science Foundation, looks small against the manufacturing billions, and the disproportion is itself instructive. Fabrication plants are capital-intensive; the money follows the concrete and the cleanrooms. But the constraint that binds hardest in practice is often human. The statute spreads workforce development across the dedicated fund, the workforce components of the Commerce research programs, and the workforce criteria in the manufacturing awards, a redundancy that suggests Congress understood the pipeline problem without quite knowing how to price it. Training a semiconductor technician takes years; building a plant takes years; the two timelines have to arrive together or the plant opens without a staff. The division's workforce provisions are an attempt to synchronize them.

The international layer is the least discussed and perhaps the most revealing about the statute's theory of the problem. The CHIPS for America International Technology Security and Innovation Fund, with 500 million dollars in appropriations, is administered through the Department of State in coordination with development finance institutions, supporting information and communications technology security and semiconductor supply chain activities with allied and friendly countries. The premise is that American fabrication capacity alone cannot secure the supply chain, because the chain spans dozens of countries and the most advanced production will remain, for the foreseeable future, concentrated in allied East Asian economies. The fund is therefore an instrument of allied coordination rather than of domestic construction: it pays for the diplomacy and the technical cooperation that align allied policies on export controls, investment screening, and supply chain resilience. Read together, the four funds describe a theory in which manufacturing incentives build the domestic base, research programs rebuild the knowledge base, workforce programs staff both, defense funding secures the military's share, and international funding aligns the allies. Whether a single division of a single statute can execute all five missions at once is a fair question. That Congress attempted it in one division is a measure of how broadly the semiconductor problem was understood by 2022.
## The investment tax credit: section 48D

### What is the 48D tax credit, and who can claim it?

Section 48D of the Internal Revenue Code creates the Advanced Manufacturing Investment Credit: a credit equal to 25 percent of qualified investment in an advanced manufacturing facility, available for property placed in service after December 31, 2022. Eligible claimants are manufacturers of semiconductors or semiconductor manufacturing equipment in the United States; foreign entities of concern are excluded.

The credit is the second pillar of the semiconductor half, and it works through an entirely different channel than the appropriations. Where the 39 billion dollars in manufacturing incentives requires an application, a competitive review, and a negotiated agreement, the tax credit is claimed on a tax return by any eligible taxpayer that makes qualifying investments. The statute defines an advanced manufacturing facility as one whose primary purpose is the manufacturing of semiconductors or semiconductor manufacturing equipment, and it defines qualified investment as the basis of qualified property placed in service during the taxable year as part of such a facility. Qualified property, in turn, is tangible property subject to depreciation or amortization that is integral to the operation of the facility, either constructed by the taxpayer or acquired with original use commencing with the taxpayer. Buildings count, but only the portions used for manufacturing; office space and administrative areas are excluded.

The credit's window, as enacted, is defined by two dates. Property must be placed in service after December 31, 2022 to qualify at all, and no credit is allowed for property whose construction begins after December 31, 2026, the cutoff written into section 48D(e). For property whose construction began before January 1, 2023, only the portion of the investment attributable to construction after August 9, 2022, the date of enactment, counts toward the credit. The design is deliberate: the credit rewards new investment decisions made after the law's passage, not projects already underway, and it phases out for projects that start too late. A taxpayer that claims the credit must reduce the depreciable basis of the property by the credit amount, preventing a double benefit on the same dollars. And in a provision that mirrors the guardrails on the grant side, a taxpayer that claims the credit and then engages in certain transactions involving semiconductor manufacturing in China or another foreign country of concern during the ten-year period after the property is placed in service must repay the credit. The tax code and the grant program enforce the same geopolitical bargain through different instruments.

One feature of the credit deserves emphasis because it determines who can actually use it. Section 48D(d) allows an elective payment, commonly called direct pay: eligible taxpayers may treat the credit as a payment of federal income tax, with any excess refunded. The election cannot be made earlier than 270 days after enactment. Direct pay matters because a tax credit is worthless to a company with no tax liability in the relevant year, and because many of the firms making the largest fabrication investments have complex tax positions. Without the direct-pay election, the credit would flow disproportionately to the most profitable incumbents; with it, the credit functions more like a grant, delivering cash value regardless of the claimant's tax bill. The Treasury Department and the IRS finalized regulations implementing the credit in late 2024, filling in the operational details that the statute left to rulemaking.

The credit is not an appropriation, and this point belongs in any careful accounting. A tax credit reduces federal revenue rather than spending federal dollars, and its cost depends on how many taxpayers claim it and how much they invest, which cannot be known in advance. Budget analysts estimate the revenue effect rather than observing it. This is why the credit sits uneasily in headline figures about the act's size: it is real government support, it has a real fiscal cost, but it is not money Congress handed out. It is money Congress declined to collect. The distinction changes how one evaluates the program's efficiency, because the credit flows automatically to qualifying investment without the competitive review, the negotiated conditions, or the upside-sharing that the grant program imposes. Whether that automaticity is a virtue (speed, simplicity, broad availability) or a defect (windfalls for investment that would have happened anyway, no leverage over corporate behavior) is one of the live arguments about the statute's design.

Like the grant program, the credit required an afterlife in regulation before it could function. The Treasury Department and the Internal Revenue Service issued proposed regulations in March 2023, took public comment on the definitions of qualified property and advanced manufacturing facilities, and published final regulations in October 2024, effective that December, with the direct-payment election rules finalized separately in March 2024. The regulatory work is where the statute's broad phrases became operational tests: what counts as integral to a facility's operation, how the recapture rules interact with corporate restructurings, how partnerships and pass-through entities claim the credit. The two-year gap between enactment and final rules is normal for a credit of this complexity, and it meant that the earliest investment decisions made under the credit were made under proposed rather than final guidance, with taxpayers permitted to rely on the proposals if followed consistently. The episode is a reminder that a tax credit, like a grant program, is not self-executing. Congress writes the incentive; the executive branch writes the instructions; the distance between the two is where the real policy lives.

For completeness, readers consulting present-day sources will find that Congress later revisited the credit's rate. A 2025 statute raised the rate to 35 percent for qualified property placed in service after 2025, according to the Internal Revenue Service. The description above reports the credit as enacted in 2022, which is the provision the CHIPS and Science Act itself created.
## Why supporters wanted it, and why opponents objected

Industrial policy is contested across the political spectrum, and the CHIPS and Science Act drew its opposition from both ends of it. The arguments deserve to be presented with equal care, because each side was arguing about a different risk, and the statute's design reflects the collision between them.

The strategic autonomy rationale begins with a number. During the debate, the Semiconductor Industry Association reported that the American share of global chip fabrication had fallen from about 37 percent in 1990 to about 12 percent by the early 2020s. Supporters treated that decline as the surface symptom of a deeper dependence: the most advanced fabrication capacity concentrated in a handful of facilities in East Asia, American industries from automobiles to medical devices dependent on supply chains they did not control, and the Department of Defense dependent on commercial fabrication for the microelectronics in weapons systems. The pandemic shortages made the abstract concrete. When chip scarcity idled auto plants and lengthened lead times across manufacturing, the case for domestic capacity stopped being a trade association talking point and became a kitchen-table fact. Senator Maria Cantwell of Washington, who chaired the Commerce Committee with jurisdiction over the legislation, put the argument in blunt terms during the debate: if the United States did not start building fabrication capacity at home, it would not catch up. Senator Schumer framed the bill as a bid to out-innovate, out-produce, and out-compete rival economies in the industries of the future. The national security gloss was not decorative. The guardrails, the restrictions on expansion in countries of concern, and the technology controls only make sense inside a theory that semiconductor fabrication is strategic infrastructure, comparable in importance to energy or shipbuilding, and that a country that cannot produce its own advanced chips is vulnerable in ways that go beyond economics.

The subsidy efficiency objection begins with a different number: the cost. Senator Pat Toomey of Pennsylvania, a free-market Republican, argued that the right way to encourage investment and strengthen competitiveness was through policies that benefit all industries rather than targeted subsidies for one. In a statement opposing the bill, he warned that the United States should not emulate what he called Beijing's semi-socialist economic model in the name of competing with it. The economic logic behind that position is the standard critique of industrial policy: governments are poor judges of where capital should go, subsidies distort investment decisions, and money awarded to projects that would have proceeded anyway is a windfall rather than an incentive. On this view, the fabrication plants the act subsidizes might have been built without public money, or built in different configurations, or built by different firms, and the taxpayer has no reliable way to know which dollars were decisive.

From the other end of the spectrum came a parallel objection with different demands. Senator Bernie Sanders, an independent who caucuses with the Democrats, voted against the final package and argued that the subsidies amounted to corporate welfare for a highly profitable industry, noting on the Senate floor that semiconductor firms had spent hundreds of billions of dollars on stock buybacks over the preceding decade rather than on domestic fabrication. Sanders filed an amendment with Senator Elizabeth Warren that would have conditioned the assistance on a no-buyback pledge, a ban on outsourcing, neutrality in union organizing, and the issuance of warrants or equity stakes giving the government a financial interest in recipient firms. The amendment was not adopted. What the final statute did include was narrower: a prohibition on using the awarded funds themselves for stock buybacks or dividend payments, the Davis-Bacon wage requirement, the worker and community investment expectations, and the upside-sharing provision for large awards. The distance between what Sanders demanded and what the statute delivered measures the political reality of the coalition: the bill needed the votes of members who would not have supported equity stakes or union-neutrality mandates, and the conditions it imposed were the ones the majority could bear.

Both objections share a premise worth naming: the statute spends public money on private companies, and the justification for doing so has to survive scrutiny. The autonomy side answers that the market, left alone, had produced a strategically dangerous concentration of production abroad, and that the pandemic proved the cost of that concentration. The efficiency side answers that the cure may not work, that subsidized capacity may prove uneconomic, and that the political process that allocates the money will favor incumbents and the well-connected. The statute's own design acknowledges both anxieties. The competitive award process, the commercial-viability criteria, and the upside-sharing answer the efficiency worry by subjecting awards to review and giving taxpayers a share of extraordinary returns. The guardrails, the domestic-construction requirement, and the technology controls answer the autonomy worry by ensuring the money serves the strategic purpose it claims. Whether the balance is right is a judgment the reader has to make; the article's job is to make the terms of the judgment clear.

The defenders' answer to the efficiency objection deserves to be stated in its strongest form, because it is more sophisticated than a simple appeal to patriotism. It runs as follows. Semiconductor fabrication exhibits market failures that neutral policy cannot fix: enormous fixed costs, decade-long investment horizons, learning-by-doing effects that concentrate know-how where production already exists, and foreign governments that subsidize their own producers without restraint. In such a market, the argument goes, the absence of American industrial policy is not neutrality but unilateral disarmament; every other major producing economy supports its chipmakers, and a United States that refuses to do so is choosing to lose the industry rather than choosing a free market. The competitive award process, on this view, is the efficiency safeguard: money flows to commercially viable projects that meet national security objectives, not to political favorites, and the guardrails ensure the subsidized capacity stays aligned with the strategic purpose. The critics' rejoinder is equally coherent: governments cannot reliably identify the investments that would not have happened without help, the review process will be captured by the largest incumbents with the best grant writers, and the history of industrial policy is littered with expensive bets on technologies the market then abandoned. Neither side can prove its case from the statute's text. The text sets up the experiment; the results belong to the years after enactment, measured award by award, fab by fab, against the counterfactual that no one can observe.
## Authorization is permission; appropriation is money

The conceptual heart of the CHIPS and Science Act, and the reason this profile keeps returning to the vocabulary of budget process, is the difference between an authorization and an appropriation. The distinction is simple to state and remarkably hard to keep in mind, which is why so much of the public discussion of the statute went wrong.

An authorization is Congress saying a program may exist and may spend up to a stated amount. It creates legal authority. It establishes the program's purposes, its administering agency, its eligibility rules, and a ceiling on what it is permitted to cost. An appropriation is Congress saying the program shall receive a stated sum from the Treasury. It moves money. The two steps are separate by constitutional design: the authorizing committees write the laws that create programs, and the appropriations committees write the laws that fund them. A program can be authorized for years without ever receiving a dollar, and a program can receive dollars only if an authorization, or some equivalent authority, permits it. Division B of the CHIPS and Science Act, the Research and Development, Competition, and Innovation Act, is almost entirely the first step. It authorizes large sums for the science foundation, the standards institute, and the energy department's science programs across multiple fiscal years, and it creates the new foundation directorate for technology and innovation. It appropriates nothing.

This is why the companion table to this article lists, for each major science program, three columns: the amount authorized, whether appropriations followed, and the mechanism. The table is the article's findable artifact because it converts the conceptual distinction into visible fact. The pattern it shows, program after program, is that the authorized sums were not matched by appropriated sums. The authorizations were permissions granted in a moment of legislative enthusiasm; the appropriations were decided later, in the annual funding bills, under different political conditions and tighter budget constraints, and they came in lower. None of this was secret. The Congressional Research Service and the Congressional Budget Office both publish the distinction as a matter of routine, and every experienced observer of science policy knows that authorizations routinely exceed appropriations. But the public conversation around the CHIPS and Science Act did not behave as though it knew. Headlines reported the combined figures as though the authorizations were spending. Advocates cited the authorized sums as achievements. And the most common recurring error in coverage, assuming that the science authorizations were funded, or assuming that the act appropriated money for the new foundation directorate, flowed directly from treating the two halves as one act.

There is a reason the confusion is so persistent, and it is not only innumeracy. Authorization figures are politically useful. A member of Congress can point to a large authorization as evidence of commitment to science without voting to spend the money. An agency can plan around an authorization as though it were a promise. An advocacy organization can count authorized dollars as a win. The system produces a steady supply of large numbers that look like spending and function as rhetoric. The CHIPS and Science Act is the clearest recent illustration of the mechanism because the two halves sit side by side in a single enrolled bill, inviting exactly the conflation the system encourages. The semiconductor half appropriated 52.7 billion dollars and added a tax credit; those are fiscal facts. The science half authorized sums several times larger; those are permissions. The roughly 280 billion dollar headline figure that dominated coverage blends the two categories, and possibly the estimated revenue cost of the tax credit as well, into a number that describes neither what was spent nor what was authorized but a little of each. The combined figure should never be quoted as spending, and the reason is not pedantry. Quoting it as spending credits the statute with fiscal consequences it did not have, and it lets the actual fiscal consequences, the real appropriations and the real revenue cost, hide inside a number too large to examine.

The political economy of the authorization deserves one more observation, because it explains why Congress keeps producing the gap. Authorizations are scored differently than appropriations in the budget process: an authorization without an appropriation creates no immediate budgetary cost, which makes large authorizations cheap to vote for and attractive to include in a bill whose coalition needs something for everyone. The science authorizations in Division B were, in this sense, the coalition maintenance of the 2022 salvage operation: they gave the innovation agenda's supporters a reason to vote for a bill whose core was semiconductor money, without requiring the appropriators to find the funds. Everyone involved understood the arrangement. The authorizers got their numbers; the appropriators kept their discretion; the advocates got their talking points; and the agencies got permissions they would then have to convert into appropriations through the annual cycle, where most of the numbers did not survive. Cynical is the wrong word for this arrangement. It is simply how a legislature that separates permission from funding behaves when it wants to promise more than it is prepared to pay for.

The series thesis thread, the gap between what a statute authorizes and what is delivered, finds its clearest and most recent expression here. Most statutes hide the gap in separate bills passed in separate years. This one puts authorization and appropriation in adjacent divisions of a single public law, signed on a single day, and dares the reader to notice that one half moves money and the other moves intentions. Noticing is the whole assignment.
## The conditions debate: what the statute required and what it rejected

The guardrails did not arrive fully formed. They are the survivors of a fight over what conditions should attach to public money given to private, profitable companies, and the fight's casualties are as instructive as its survivors. The debate ran on two tracks at once: what recipients must not do with the money, and what they must do in return for it.

The sharpest version of the conditions argument came from Senator Bernie Sanders, who treated the manufacturing incentives as corporate welfare and said so on the Senate floor. His critique had a specific evidentiary base: over the preceding decade, semiconductor companies had spent roughly 250 billion dollars, about 70 percent of their profits, on stock buybacks rather than on research or domestic fabrication, a figure he cited from the Congressional Record during the debate. The industry asking for public money, in his telling, had demonstrated where its priorities lay when left to its own devices. Sanders, together with Senator Elizabeth Warren, filed an amendment that would have conditioned the assistance on a package of requirements: no stock buybacks, no outsourcing of jobs, no repeal of existing collective bargaining agreements, neutrality in union organizing efforts, and, most aggressively, the issuance of warrants or equity stakes giving the federal government a financial interest in recipient companies. The equity demand was the philosophical core of the proposal: if taxpayers socialize the risk of building fabrication capacity, taxpayers should share in the returns. The amendment was not adopted, and Sanders voted against the final bill.

What survived of that agenda is a study in legislative compromise. The statute bars recipients from using the awarded funds for buybacks or dividends, but it does not bar buybacks as corporate policy; a company may continue repurchasing its own shares with its own money. It requires Davis-Bacon prevailing wages on subsidized construction and expects worker and community investments, but it does not require union neutrality or protect collective bargaining agreements. It imposes upside-sharing for awards above 150 million dollars, giving the government a share of extraordinary returns, but it does not take equity warrants or board seats. Each surviving condition is a weaker version of a stronger demand, and each weaker version was the price of keeping the coalition together. Members who supported the subsidies as industrial policy would not have voted for equity stakes; members who demanded equity stakes would not have voted for unconditional subsidies. The conditions that passed were the ones both camps could tolerate.

The national security conditions followed a different political logic. Unlike the buyback and labor demands, which divided the coalition, the guardrails against expansion in countries of concern united it. Restricting recipients from building advanced capacity in China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea was the rare provision that satisfied both the autonomy rationale and the efficiency objection: it ensured the money served its stated strategic purpose, and it answered the charge that taxpayers were funding companies that would take the money and invest it abroad anyway. The technology controls and the foreign-entity-of-concern ineligibility operated on the same logic. Where the economic conditions were contested, the security conditions were consensual, which is why they are written in the statute's most mandatory language, with clawback rather than discretion as the remedy.

There is a final irony in the conditions debate worth naming. The fiercest opponents of the subsidies, Sanders on the left and free-market conservatives on the right, agreed that handing public money to profitable chipmakers was suspect, but they disagreed completely about the remedy. Sanders wanted stronger conditions: more strings, more government leverage, a financial stake. The market-oriented critics wanted fewer subsidies: no strings because no money, or money available to all industries through neutral tax and regulatory policy rather than targeted awards. The statute chose a third path, subsidies with moderate conditions, which meant it fully satisfied neither camp. The no votes in both chambers, 33 in the Senate and 187 in the House, with one present, came from both directions at once. That is the normal fate of industrial policy in a polarized legislature: the middle holds, and both wings dissent.

## The guardrails: conditions on the cash

### What are the CHIPS Act guardrails?

The guardrails are the national security conditions in section 103 of Division A. Incentive recipients may not use the funds abroad, expand advanced chipmaking capacity in countries of concern for ten years, collaborate on specified technology with foreign entities of concern, or use the funds for buybacks or dividends. Violations trigger clawback of the award.

The guardrails are the most legally novel part of the statute, and they are described here in the statute's own terms, because paraphrase has a way of softening what the provisions actually do. Every recipient of funding under the CHIPS Incentives Program must enter into a required agreement with the Secretary of Commerce. The agreement memorializes the restrictions, defines their key terms for that recipient, and provides the mechanism for enforcement. The restrictions bind not only the recipient but the recipient's affiliated group, defined by reference to the Internal Revenue Code's affiliated-group rules in a way that captures foreign corporations under common ownership. A company cannot take the money in its American subsidiary and expand advanced fabrication through its overseas affiliates free of the conditions. The conditions travel with the corporate family.

The first and most discussed restriction is the expansion prohibition, enforced through what practitioners call the expansion clawback. During the ten-year period beginning on the date of the award, the covered entity may not engage in any significant transaction involving the material expansion of semiconductor manufacturing capacity in the People's Republic of China or any other foreign country of concern. The statute leaves the precise definitions of "significant transaction" and "material expansion" to be specified in each required agreement, and the Department of Commerce supplied the operative definitions in its final guardrail rule published in September 2023. A significant transaction is any investment valued at 100,000 dollars or more, or a series of transactions aggregating to that amount during the agreement's term. Material expansion means increasing a facility's production capacity by more than five percent through the addition of cleanroom or other physical space, a threshold deliberately set low enough to capture modest expansions while allowing normal equipment upgrades and efficiency improvements at existing facilities. Semiconductor manufacturing, for these purposes, spans wafer production, front-end fabrication, and back-end assembly, testing, and packaging. If a recipient violates the prohibition, the statute provides for the full recovery of the funds provided under the program: the entire award comes back.

The prohibition is not absolute, and the exceptions are as instructive as the rule. The statute and the final rule carve out existing facilities producing legacy semiconductors, and they permit expansion of capacity for legacy semiconductors at new facilities that predominantly serve the market of the foreign country of concern, defined as at least 85 percent of output incorporated into final products used or consumed in that market. Legacy semiconductors are defined by reference to technology generations: for logic chips, 28 nanometers or older, with corresponding thresholds for memory technologies. The logic of the exception is that the national security concern is about advanced capability, not about the mature chips that go into consumer appliances and automobiles. A recipient may therefore maintain and even grow its legacy production in the listed countries, subject to the definitions, while being barred from building or expanding leading-edge and advanced facilities there. Recipients that plan a significant transaction must notify Commerce in advance so the department can assess the national security risk, and the statute contemplates mitigation agreements for transactions that raise concerns short of a flat prohibition. The notification requirement turns the guardrail from a passive condition into an active monitoring regime.

The second restriction is the technology prohibition, enforced through the technology clawback. A covered entity that knowingly engages in any joint research or technology licensing effort with a foreign entity of concern, relating to a technology or product that raises national security concerns, is subject to full recovery of the award. The knowledge requirement matters: the provision targets deliberate collaboration, not inadvertent contact. The covered technologies are those Commerce identifies as raising national security concerns and communicates to the recipient before the collaboration occurs, which in practice means semiconductors critical to national security and items controlled for national security or regional stability reasons under the export control regulations. Joint research means research and development undertaken by two or more persons; technology licensing means making patents, trade secrets, or know-how available to another party. The provision reaches the recipient's affiliates as well, and it sits alongside a threshold eligibility rule: foreign entities of concern may not receive incentive funds at all, and applications are reviewed for national security risk from foreign control, access to information, or other mechanisms.

The defined terms deserve precision, because they are the vocabulary in which the statute thinks. A "foreign country of concern" is defined, at 15 U.S.C. 4651(7) as added by section 103(a)(4) of Division A, to mean the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, the People's Republic of China, the Russian Federation, and the Islamic Republic of Iran, plus any other country that the Secretary of Commerce, in consultation with the Secretary of Defense, the Secretary of State, and the Director of National Intelligence, determines to be engaged in conduct detrimental to the national security or foreign policy of the United States. Commerce's implementation includes Hong Kong within the China designation. A "foreign entity of concern" is a broader and more functional category: entities on sanctions lists, designated terrorist organizations, entities convicted under espionage, export control, or sanctions laws, and entities owned by, controlled by, or subject to the jurisdiction or direction of the governments of the named countries. The country definition draws a map; the entity definition draws a net.

Two further restrictions complete the picture. The statute prohibits recipients from using incentive funds to construct, modify, or improve a semiconductor facility outside the United States, a straightforward requirement that the domestic subsidy build domestic capacity. And it prohibits the use of the funds for the purchase of listed equity securities of the recipient or its parent company, or for the payment of dividends or other capital distributions on the recipient's common stock. That last restriction was the remnant of the Sanders amendment's buyback demands that survived the legislative process: not a ban on buybacks as corporate policy, but a ban on using taxpayer money to fund them.

The statute left the operational definitions to the executive branch, and the rulemaking that followed is part of the guardrails' story. Commerce published a proposed rule in March 2023, took comment from industry and other stakeholders, and issued the final rule in September 2023, supplying the definitions of significant transaction, material expansion, and semiconductor manufacturing capacity described above, along with the legacy-semiconductor exceptions and the notification procedures. The final rule's low thresholds, 100,000 dollars for a significant transaction and five percent for material expansion, were deliberate: Commerce wanted the net to catch even modest expansions while leaving room for routine maintenance and efficiency upgrades. The department also required recipients to notify it of planned significant transactions so that national security risks could be assessed before the fact, and it reserved the ability to negotiate mitigation agreements where a transaction raised concerns short of a flat violation. Commerce must also report to Congress on potential violations and mitigations, creating a legislative oversight channel alongside the contractual one. One institutional detail sharpens the picture: the Department of Commerce is also the agency that reviews export license applications for dual-use goods, software, and technology, including items used in semiconductor design and manufacturing. The same department that awards the money monitors the export licenses, which means compliance with the guardrails is visible to the government through more than one window. Companies negotiating required agreements have had to assume, correctly, that the left hand knows what the right hand is doing.

### How does the CHIPS Act clawback work?

If a recipient violates the expansion or technology restrictions, the Department of Commerce recovers the award. The expansion clawback applies to a prohibited significant transaction during the ten years after the award; the technology clawback applies to knowingly pursuing covered research or licensing with a foreign entity of concern. Both recover the full funding amount.

Step back, and the guardrails represent something genuinely unusual in American industrial policy. Subsidies in the United States have traditionally come with domestic conditions: build here, hire here, pay prevailing wages here. The CHIPS guardrails add an extraterritorial condition: take our money, and your corporate conduct abroad, in specific countries, in a specific industry, over a specific decade, becomes our business, with the full award as the penalty for noncompliance. The condition is memorialized in a contract, defined by regulation, monitored through mandatory notification, and enforced by clawback. Whether that architecture deters the behavior it targets, or simply prices it into corporate planning, or drives affected firms to forgo the subsidies entirely, are questions the statute leaves to experience. What the statute does not leave open is the principle: the semiconductor half of the CHIPS and Science Act is industrial policy with cash and conditions attached, and the conditions reach as far as the money does.

## Division B of the CHIPS and Science Act: The Science Half

The first half of this statute spends money. The second half expresses intentions about money, and the distance between those two operations is the hinge on which the entire law turns. Division B carries its own short title, the Research and Development, Competition, and Innovation Act, and it runs to hundreds of pages of permissions, ceilings, new programs, and policy directives for the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the Department of Energy, and a long list of smaller science and education initiatives. Almost nothing in it writes a check. Where Division A appropriated tens of billions in direct spending that the Commerce Department could begin obligating, Division B authorized sums that only later appropriations bills could convert into spendable funds. Readers who carry that distinction from this point forward will understand the statute better than most of the commentary published about it.

The bundling of the two divisions was itself a legislative strategy, and understanding it explains why the money and the intentions share a public law number. The semiconductor incentives needed sixty Senate votes and a presidential signature; standing alone, they were a corporate subsidy package that many legislators found difficult to vote for without a broader public purpose attached. The science authorizations supplied that purpose: they let supporters describe the bill as an investment in research, education, and regional equity rather than a handout to profitable firms, and they gave the House and Senate science committees, whose members had spent years drafting the competition provisions, a stake in the outcome. Each half made the other passable. The cost of the bargain was conceptual confusion that persists in coverage to this day, because a bill sold as a $280 billion investment in science and chips was, in fiscal reality, a $52.7 billion investment in chips with a $200 billion science wish list attached to it.

### What Is the Difference Between an Authorization and an Appropriation?
An authorization gives an agency permission to receive money and sets a ceiling Congress intends to fund. An appropriation is the money itself, provided in a separate spending bill. The CHIPS and Science Act authorized roughly $174 billion for science agencies but appropriated almost none of it, so the science half was a statement of intent, not a check.

The two-step structure is a deliberate feature of congressional procedure, not an accident or a drafting error. Authorizing committees, in this case principally the House Science Committee and the Senate Commerce Committee, write the substantive law: they create programs, define missions, set eligibility rules, and declare how much an agency may receive in each fiscal year. Appropriations committees then decide, in the twelve annual spending bills, how much each agency actually gets. The authorization is addressed to the future; the appropriation is addressed to the Treasury. A program can be authorized for decades without ever receiving its authorized level, and a program can receive appropriations year after year on the strength of an expired authorization that nobody has bothered to renew. The two tracks answer to different majorities, different calendars, and different political pressures.

The statute's own language marks the boundary. Section 10303, the core NSF provision, reads in the familiar formula: "There are authorized to be appropriated to the Foundation $11,897,480,000 for fiscal year 2023." The phrase "authorized to be appropriated" is doing precise legal work. It creates no budget authority. It obligates no funds. It instructs the appropriators that they may provide up to that amount, and it signals to the agency, to grantees, and to universities planning faculty hires what Congress considers the appropriate scale. Compare the Division A formula, where Congress wrote "there is appropriated" language, designated the sums as emergency spending, and made them available until expended. In Division A, the statute was the spending bill. In Division B, the statute was a letter to a future spending bill, and future spending bills are free to answer with smaller numbers or with silence.

The Congressional Budget Office treats the two operations accordingly. When it estimates a bill's effect on the deficit, direct appropriations and tax provisions score as changes in outlays and revenues, because they alter federal cash flows the moment the president signs. Authorizations of appropriations score at zero. They are, in budget terminology, discretionary authorizations: they create no budget authority and require a subsequent appropriations act before a dollar can move. This is why the CHIPS and Science Act's official cost estimate reflected the semiconductor money and the tax credit but not the science authorizations, and why press accounts that added the authorizations into the law's cost were doing arithmetic the scorekeepers explicitly declined to do.

Congress writes authorizations in large numbers partly because the numbers are free. An authorizing committee pays no budgetary price for ambition: its ceilings do not appear in the deficit estimate, do not require offsets, and do not compete against other programs in the same bill. The committee's incentive is to state the largest plausible need, both to signal seriousness to the appropriators and to give its allied agencies and constituencies a flag to rally around. Appropriators, who do pay the price, then discount the flag. The result is a structural inflation of authorization levels relative to what the appropriations process will bear, and anyone reading a science authorization should mentally apply that discount before quoting the figure.

The statute sometimes authorizes in a different register worth recognizing. Alongside the specific dollar ceilings, Division B contains provisions authorizing such sums as may be necessary, the traditional formula for programs Congress wants to keep alive without committing to a number. The dollar ceilings get the headlines, but the open-ended authorizations do quieter work: they keep programs legally eligible for appropriations after their specific authorizations lapse, which matters because appropriators routinely fund programs whose authorizations expired years earlier.

The two committees also operate on different theories of what a number means. To an authorizing committee, the authorization level is a statement of need: this is what the agency requires to do its job well, as judged by the members who oversee its programs most closely. To an appropriations subcommittee, the same number is a claim competing against every other claim in a fixed total, to be honored only insofar as the politics of the moment allow. Neither theory is wrong; they are answers to different questions. But the public conversation about the CHIPS and Science Act almost always adopted the authorizers' theory, treating the $81 billion NSF figure as a description of what the Foundation would receive, when the appropriators' theory was the one that would govern. Reading the statute correctly means holding both theories in mind and knowing which one controls the money.

This is why the science division's headline figures behave so differently from the semiconductor division's. When a summary says the act "provides" $81 billion for the National Science Foundation, the verb is doing quiet violence to the mechanism. The act provides permission for $81 billion across five fiscal years. Whether any given year's permission becomes money depends on the Commerce, Justice, Science appropriations subcommittees, on the overall caps governing discretionary spending, and on the competition among every other program those subcommittees fund. Once appropriated, the money flows through grant competitions, cooperative agreements, laboratory budgets, and fellowship programs whose mechanics are described in this [guide to federal research funding law](/2017/05/01/federal-research-funding-law/), but none of that machinery starts moving until an appropriations act turns the key.

The National Science Foundation authorization is the largest single piece of Division B, and it repays close reading. The statute authorized roughly $81 billion for the Foundation across fiscal years 2023 through 2027, a trajectory designed to roughly double an agency whose annual budget sat just under $9 billion in fiscal year 2022. The yearly schedule climbs steeply: about $11.9 billion for fiscal year 2023, about $15.6 billion for fiscal year 2024, and continuing upward through the window. Within those totals the statute assigned specific ceilings to the Foundation's accounts, about $9.05 billion for research and related activities in fiscal year 2023 rising to $12.05 billion in fiscal year 2024, about $1.95 billion for STEM education in fiscal year 2023 rising to $2.5 billion the next year, with smaller lines for major research equipment, agency operations, and the inspector general. The out-years climbed further: research and related activities to $12.85 billion in fiscal year 2025, $13.8 billion in fiscal year 2026, and $14.7 billion in fiscal year 2027, with STEM education rising to $2.7, $2.85, and $3.0 billion across the same years. The shape of the schedule matters: it back-loads the growth, which means the largest promised increases arrive in the years farthest from enactment, exactly the years most exposed to shifting political conditions and spending caps.

Inside the totals, the statute wrote policy as well as numbers. The STEM education account carried specific authorizations for the Robert Noyce Teacher Scholarship program, for graduate research fellowships, and for a STEM Teacher Corps pilot, alongside the Foundation's broader fellowship and traineeship portfolio. The research account fenced money for the mid-scale research infrastructure program and directed the Foundation to steer a share of funding toward states and regions that historically receive less federal research money, the long-standing effort to spread capacity rather than concentrate it in a handful of coastal research hubs. The act also expanded the Foundation's demographic data collection and directed attention to workplace inclusion, caregiver support for grantees, and harassment prevention. These are the provisions that would have mattered most to working scientists if funded: more grants, larger grants, and new instruments in more places. Their fate followed the same rule as the topline. An authorized fellowship expansion that never receives appropriations does not fund a fellow.

### Did the CHIPS and Science Act Fund the New NSF Technology Directorate?
No. The act created and authorized the Directorate for Technology, Innovation and Partnerships, with a five year schedule rising from $1.5 billion in fiscal year 2023 to $4.1 billion in fiscal year 2027, but it appropriated no operating money for the directorate. The only CHIPS money NSF received was a one time $335 million supplemental.

The Technology, Innovation and Partnerships directorate, universally shortened to TIP, was the most symbolically freighted creation in Division B. The idea descended from the Endless Frontier Act's proposal for a technology directorate that would push the Foundation beyond its historic core of curiosity-driven basic research toward use-inspired work in fields like artificial intelligence, quantum information science, advanced manufacturing, advanced communications, energy, and materials science. The enacted statute established TIP as a new NSF directorate with its own authorized funding line inside the research and related activities account: $1.5 billion for fiscal year 2023, $3.35 billion for fiscal year 2024, $3.55 billion for fiscal year 2025, $3.8 billion for fiscal year 2026, and $4.1 billion for fiscal year 2027. Those five figures sum to about $16.3 billion, which is worth stating plainly because contemporaneous press summaries commonly rounded the directorate's five-year figure to $20 billion. The statute's own schedule is the authoritative number, and the gap between the two figures is a small-scale rehearsal of the article's larger theme: even the descriptions of the authorizations drifted upward in retelling.

TIP's authorized portfolio included some of the act's most talked-about programs, including the Regional Innovation Engines, which were meant to seed technology clusters outside the coastal research hubs. But authorized is the operative word in that sentence. The directorate existed in law with a multi-billion-dollar permission slip and no operating appropriation behind it. The single exception proves the rule: Division A included a one-time $335 million supplemental appropriation to the Foundation, split in the agency's budget documents between research activities and STEM education, which TIP used to begin standing up early programs. Three hundred thirty-five million dollars is real money, and it let the directorate make initial awards, but it is a rounding error against a $16.3 billion five-year authorization, and it was a single infusion rather than the start of the authorized ramp. Every subsequent year's TIP funding had to be fought for inside the Foundation's overall annual appropriation, which, as the next section shows, never approached the authorized trajectory.

The directorate's intellectual premise deserves a paragraph, because it explains why TIP attracted both excitement and suspicion. The Foundation's historic mission, drawn from the postwar blueprint for American science policy, is curiosity-driven basic research: fund the best ideas wherever they arise, and trust that knowledge eventually finds uses. TIP inverts the sequence. It starts from national needs, secure supply chains, competitive industries, deployable technologies, and works backward to the research that serves them, through programs like the Regional Innovation Engines that pair universities with firms, investors, and state governments in specific places. Supporters call this use-inspired research and argue the Foundation had drifted too far toward knowledge for its own sake. Skeptics worry that a technology directorate with its own large budget line becomes a magnet for political direction of science. The statute did not resolve that argument; it created an institution inside which the argument would continue, and then declined to fund the institution at the scale the argument assumed.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology received the same treatment at a smaller scale. Division B authorized about $9.7 billion for NIST across fiscal years 2023 through 2027, commonly rounded to $10 billion in summaries, against an annual baseline near $1.2 billion. The statute's schedule, set out in a Congressional Research Service analysis of the act, assigned the money across the agency's three accounts: scientific and technical research and services climbing from about $979 million in fiscal year 2023 to about $1.28 billion in fiscal year 2027, industrial technology services rising from about $372 million to $800 million, and construction of research facilities held at $200 million a year. Inside those accounts sat the programs Congress wanted to grow: the Manufacturing Extension Partnership, which serves small and medium manufacturers, authorized at $2.3 billion over the window; the Manufacturing USA institutes; and expanded work on standards, measurement science, and supply chain information. As with NSF, these were ceilings awaiting appropriations, and the appropriations that followed came in below them.

The NIST authorization repays the same account-level attention. The scientific and technical research and services account funds the laboratory programs, the measurement science and standards work that underpins everything from semiconductor metrology to cybersecurity frameworks. The industrial technology services account funds the outward-facing programs: the Hollings Manufacturing Extension Partnership, a federal-state network that advises small and mid-sized manufacturers, and the Manufacturing USA institutes, public-private consortia organized around specific advanced manufacturing technologies. The construction account covers the physical plant, the aging laboratories whose renovation needs the statute acknowledged with a steady $200 million a year. Each of these had constituencies that read the authorized numbers as promises. Each learned, on the appropriations cycle, the difference between a ceiling and a check.

The Department of Energy's Office of Science, the largest single funder of physical science research in the federal system, drew an authorization of about $50.3 billion over the five-year window, roughly a 35 percent increase over its baseline. The statute layered additional science and innovation authorizations on top, about $17.6 billion for purposes including low-emissions steel production research and other technology areas. It authorized but did not appropriate $12 billion for the Advanced Research Projects Agency for Energy, a distinction that deserves emphasis because ARPA-E is routinely described as a CHIPS and Science Act winner when the statute gave it permission rather than funds. The act also created a new nonprofit Foundation for Energy Security and Innovation to channel philanthropic money toward energy research, directed new research programs in basic energy sciences, and imposed an extensive set of research security provisions governing foreign talent recruitment programs, Confucius Institutes, and research ethics across the science agencies.

The Office of Science authorization distributed its $50.3 billion across the office's six program offices, and a Federation of American Scientists analysis laid the authorized and appropriated figures side by side: advanced scientific computing research, basic energy sciences, biological and environmental research, fusion energy sciences, high-energy physics, and nuclear physics, each with its own authorized ramp. Fusion drew the steepest authorized increase, reflecting the statute's bet on a bold decadal vision for fusion pilot plants, while basic energy sciences, the largest office, carried the biggest dollar growth. The ARPA-E line is the sharpest illustration of the authorization-appropriation distinction in the energy title: the statute authorized $12 billion for the agency over the window and appropriated none of it, leaving the high-risk, high-reward project agency to live on its annual appropriations as before. The research security title, meanwhile, did not need money to bite: its restrictions on foreign talent recruitment programs, its constraints on Confucius Institutes, and its research ethics requirements took effect as law regardless of appropriations, a reminder that Division B's regulatory provisions, unlike its funding provisions, were self-executing.

Beyond the three flagship agencies, Division B authorized $10 billion for the Commerce Department to designate twenty geographically distributed regional technology and innovation hubs, a program aimed at spreading research capacity to communities hollowed out by deindustrialization. It carried extensive NASA provisions, workforce and STEM education directives, and demographic data collection requirements. The sheer breadth of the division is part of its political story: it assembled the science policy wish lists of multiple committees into a single vehicle, which made the authorization totals enormous and the subsequent appropriations shortfall almost structurally inevitable.

The regional technology hubs program illustrates the authorization pattern in miniature. Division B authorized $10 billion for the Commerce Department to designate twenty geographically distributed hubs linking research universities with private industry in communities hollowed out by deindustrialization, an explicit attempt to spread innovation capacity beyond the clusters where it concentrates. The authorization was among the act's most popular provisions with governors and mayors. It appropriated nothing. Whatever hub activity followed had to be funded through later appropriations or repurposed economic development money, which meant the program's celebrated geography, twenty hubs across the country, existed in the statute as a map without a travel budget.

Division B's internal organization mirrors the committee structure that produced it. Its titles track the three flagship agencies, an NSF title, a NIST title, and an Energy title, each drafted substantially by the committees that authorize those agencies, with additional titles for regional innovation, STEM education, and research security. That organization explains both the division's coherence and its sprawl: each committee contributed its standing wish list, and the resulting whole reads less like a single strategy than like three agency reauthorization bills and several policy initiatives bound under one cover. The binding was the point. Separate bills would have faced separate fights; one bill let every committee's priorities ride the semiconductor provisions' momentum.

That shortfall has a lineage. The pattern of a sweeping science authorization followed by appropriations that never arrive repeats across [survey of science policy legislation](/2017/06/01/us-science-policy-legislation/), and the closest precedent is instructive. The America COMPETES Acts of 2007 and 2010 set ambitious doubling trajectories for the same three agencies, NSF, NIST, and the DOE Office of Science, and those trajectories evaporated after the 2011 debt-limit showdown produced a decade of statutory budget caps. The CHIPS and Science Act's authors knew that history. They wrote the authorizations anyway, on the theory that a stated target shapes appropriations debates even when it is not met. Whether that theory holds is a question the funding record answers in the negative, but the mechanism is worth understanding on its own terms: an authorization is a committee's best argument about what an agency deserves, and appropriators are free to find the argument unpersuasive.

## The Funding Gap: What Congress Authorized Versus What It Appropriated

Stating the gap plainly is this article's central service, so here it is without cushioning. For fiscal year 2023, the first year of the authorization window, Congress appropriated about $9.87 billion for the National Science Foundation against an authorized $11.9 billion, a miss of just over $2 billion, or 17 percent. That appropriation included the one-time $335 million CHIPS supplemental; without it, the miss would have been wider. The Department of Energy's Office of Science fared somewhat better in percentage terms: the fiscal year 2023 omnibus provided $8.1 billion against an authorized $8.9 billion, about 9 percent short. These figures come from a Federation of American Scientists analysis published in May 2023, while the ink on the authorization was barely dry, and they already showed the authorized ramp bending away from the appropriated reality.

Fiscal year 2024 made the divergence unmistakable. The year's appropriations were written under the Fiscal Responsibility Act's caps on discretionary spending, the product of the spring 2023 debt-limit agreement, which held non-defense spending roughly flat. Inside that constraint, the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2024, Public Law 118-42, signed on March 9, 2024, provided $9.06 billion for the National Science Foundation. Set that against the authorized $15.6 billion for fiscal year 2024 and the scale of the miss comes into focus: the agency received 42 percent less than the statute's target, and the $9.06 billion was itself an 8 percent cut from the prior year's level, reversing much of the increase the fiscal year 2023 omnibus had provided. An American Institute of Physics analysis put the three agencies together at roughly $8 billion below their fiscal year 2024 CHIPS targets, with most of the shortfall concentrated at NSF.

NIST's fiscal year 2024 appropriation landed near $1.46 billion against an authorized $1.65 billion, a cut of roughly 12 percent from the prior year that rolled back about half of the increase Congress had provided the year before. The DOE Office of Science escaped outright cuts, receiving $8.24 billion, a 1.7 percent increase that built on the prior year's 8 percent rise, but that still sat about $1.3 billion below the $9.54 billion authorized for fiscal year 2024, and the American Institute of Physics noted that the amount would not keep pace with inflation, forcing hard choices about program priorities. Across all three agencies, the pattern was identical: appropriators funded the agencies, sometimes generously by the standards of a capped year, but nowhere near the authorized glide path.

Two features of this record deserve emphasis because they answer the most common misunderstandings. First, the shortfall was not a clerical failure or a delayed disbursement. Appropriations subcommittees wrote these numbers deliberately, in full knowledge of the authorized levels, under binding caps and competing demands. The authorization did not create an entitlement to the money; it created a target that a later Congress weighed against every other claim on discretionary spending and found too expensive. Second, the semiconductor half of the statute was insulated from all of this by design. Division A's money had been appropriated directly, as emergency spending available until expended, which meant no appropriations subcommittee could shrink it in a later bill. The two halves of the act thus experienced the post-enactment years in opposite ways: one half's money was locked in at signing, and the other half's money had to be re-won every year in the appropriations process, where it lost.

The fiscal year 2023 omnibus, enacted in December 2022, showed both the promise and the limit of the authorization strategy. Appropriators gave NSF a genuine increase, lifting the agency from about $8.8 billion to about $9.9 billion, and they framed the $335 million CHIPS supplemental as a down payment on the act's ambitions. A down payment implies installments to follow. The installments never came. When the debt-limit agreement of spring 2023 capped discretionary spending, the authorization targets became the first casualty of the new arithmetic: non-defense spending held roughly flat for fiscal year 2024, and an agency authorized for $15.6 billion received $9.06 billion instead. Inside that total the pain was unevenly spread. The research and related activities account, the core grant-making engine, absorbed the deepest dollar cut, while the STEM education account fell by an even larger percentage from its authorized level, which meant the fellowship expansions and teacher programs the statute had promised were among the first ambitions deferred.

It is worth asking why appropriators felt free to disregard targets set by the same Congress only months earlier. Part of the answer is institutional: appropriations subcommittees guard their prerogative to set funding levels, and they treat authorizations as advisory by long custom. Part is arithmetic: under binding caps, every dollar above the NSF target is a dollar taken from another program in the same bill, and the Commerce-Justice-Science bill funds the Census, federal law enforcement, and NASA alongside the Foundation. Part is political memory: appropriators had watched the COMPETES doubling targets evaporate a decade earlier, and they priced the new targets accordingly. None of this is a scandal in procedural terms. It is the system operating as designed, with the design flaw, if one sees it that way, located in the decision to announce $200 billion of intentions as though intentions were expenditures.

The asymmetry also distorted agency planning in ways the topline figures hide. An agency holding directly appropriated money available until expended can sign multi-year commitments, hire against a known budget, and tell grantees what to expect. An agency living on authorizations and annual appropriations must plan against a ceiling it may never reach, which means the rational strategy is to plan against the appropriation and treat the authorization as decoration. Over time that trains everyone involved, program officers, university administrators, and prospective graduate students, to discount congressional authorizations entirely, which degrades the authorization process into theater. The CHIPS and Science Act did not create this dynamic, but its roughly $200 billion of unfunded science authorizations made it the largest single demonstration of the dynamic in modern science policy.

There is a sympathetic reading of the authorization strategy, and it should be given its due. Authorization levels function as opening bids and as signals to agencies, universities, and private partners about the direction Congress wants. Defenders of the strategy make a subtler point worth recording. Even unmet, they argue, an authorization level functions as a focal point: it tells appropriators, agencies, and the White House budget office where the relevant committees stand, and appropriations negotiated in its shadow tend to land higher than they would have without it. The fiscal year 2023 omnibus increases for NSF and NIST are the best evidence for that claim, and supporters credit the authorization targets with creating the political permission for those increases. The reply is that a focal point that misses by 42 percent in its second year is not focusing much. The honest verdict is mixed: the authorizations probably helped at the margin in fiscal year 2023 and were largely irrelevant by fiscal year 2024, which is another way of saying their influence decayed exactly as fast as the budget caps required. Appropriators sometimes treat them as genuine guidance, and the fiscal year 2023 omnibus did give NSF and NIST meaningful increases, even if they fell short of the targets. But the sympathetic reading has a limit, and the fiscal year 2024 record marks it. When the same Congress that authorized a 42 percent increase for an agency turns around and cuts that agency's budget by 8 percent, the authorization is not functioning as a glide path. It is functioning as rhetoric, and the honest description of the science division is that it authorized a future Congress chose not to buy.

For the research community the gap had concrete consequences that abstract budget figures understate. Universities had planned faculty hiring, graduate admissions, and facility investments against the authorized ramp; the fiscal year 2024 cut forced the Foundation to reduce grant success rates and shrink award sizes instead, which meant fewer early-career researchers funded and longer gaps between grants for established ones. Laboratory directors at the Energy Department's national labs faced the same arithmetic on a smaller scale: an Office of Science funded about 14 percent below its authorized level could not sustain both its operating facilities and its research programs at the planned levels, and something had to give. These are the second-order effects of treating authorizations as spending. The statute's numbers shaped real decisions by real institutions, and when the appropriations did not follow, the institutions absorbed the difference.

## The authorization pattern this statute repeats

### Why does the CHIPS and Science Act's funding gap look familiar?

Because Congress ran the same play fifteen years earlier and it ended the same way. The America COMPETES Act of 2007 put the same science agencies on an authorized doubling path, appropriations never reached the authorized levels, and the 2010 reauthorization repeated the pattern. The 2022 gap is the predictable output of a system that separates permission from funding.

The precedent begins with a National Academies report. In 2005, at the request of several members of Congress, the Academies released Rising Above the Gathering Storm, which warned that the United States was losing its edge in science and technology and recommended doubling federal basic research funding over seven years, with emphasis on the physical sciences and engineering. The report gave the doubling idea an authoritative pedigree and a specific number, and it converted a diffuse anxiety about competitiveness into a legislative program with a measurable target.

President George W. Bush picked up the target in 2006 with the American Competitiveness Initiative, which proposed doubling funding over ten years for a defined set of accounts: all of the National Science Foundation, the Department of Energy Office of Science, and the National Institute of Standards and Technology laboratory and construction accounts. The initiative chose those accounts deliberately. Because Congress appropriates to agencies and programs rather than to fields of research, the physical science accounts served as measurable stand-ins for the broader goal of strengthening American research capacity outside the biomedical sciences, which already received the largest share of federal research money through the health agencies.

Congress wrote the doubling path into law the following year. The America Creating Opportunities to Meaningfully Promote Excellence in Technology, Education, and Science Act, known universally as the America COMPETES Act, became Public Law 110-69 when President Bush signed it on August 9, 2007, exactly fifteen years to the day before the CHIPS and Science Act would be signed. The statute authorized appropriations for the targeted accounts for fiscal years 2008 through 2010. The authorized levels implied a compound annual growth rate of 10.3 percent over the fiscal year 2006 baseline, a pace that would have doubled the accounts in seven years if sustained. For fiscal year 2010 the statute authorized 8.132 billion dollars for the National Science Foundation, 5.814 billion for the Department of Energy Office of Science, and about 634.5 million for the standards institute accounts. These were authorizations, permissions with ceilings, carrying the same legal character as the Division B authorizations of 2022.

Appropriations did not reach the authorized levels. The Congressional Research Service, reviewing the doubling effort, reported that actual appropriations for fiscal year 2010 corresponded to a 6.4 percent annual growth rate over the 2006 baseline, an eleven-year doubling pace rather than the authorized seven-year pace. The shortfall was not the product of a single hostile Congress or a single bad budget year. It was the appropriations process doing what the appropriations process does: weighing the authorized claims against every other claim on discretionary spending and discounting them. The authorization had been the easy vote, taken in a moment of bipartisan enthusiasm for competitiveness. The appropriations were the hard votes, taken year after year under tighter constraints, and the numbers that survived were smaller.

Congress tried the authorization again. The America COMPETES Reauthorization Act of 2010, Public Law 111-358, extended the authorizations for fiscal years 2011 through 2013. The politics had already shifted. The reauthorization passed the Senate by unanimous consent but cleared the House by 228 to 130, a far narrower margin than the broad bipartisan support the 2007 act had enjoyed, in large measure because of disputes over the scale of the authorized spending. The pattern was repeating one level up: even the permission was getting harder to grant, while the funding continued to lag the permissions already granted.

The Congressional Research Service later summarized the outcome in a single sentence that belongs in any honest account of American science policy: Congress's appropriations fell short of the authorizations in these acts, and no presidential budget request has referenced the America COMPETES Act doubling goal since fiscal year 2017. The doubling effort did not fail for lack of authorization. It failed, to the extent it failed, at the appropriations stage, which is the only stage where money moves. The authorized trajectory survives in the statute books as a statement of what Congress once considered the appropriate scale. The appropriated trajectory survives in agency budgets as what the country was actually willing to pay.

The parallel to 2022 is structural, not coincidental. In both cases an authorizing coalition agreed on large multi-year numbers for the same three science agencies. In both cases the numbers were politically cheap because authorizations score at zero in the budget process and require no offsets. In both cases the appropriators, facing fixed totals and competing claims, funded below the authorized levels. The difference is scale: the 2007 gap was measured in single-digit billions across three agencies, while the 2022 gap, with the National Science Foundation alone authorized at about 15.6 billion dollars for fiscal year 2024 against 9.06 billion appropriated, is measured in tens of billions. The mechanism did not change. Only the numbers got larger, which made the gap larger too.

There is a further parallel worth naming. The 2007 act, like the 2022 act, paired its science authorizations with a technology program meant to connect research to industrial competitiveness. The 2007 statute replaced the Advanced Technology Program with the Technology Innovation Program, aimed at small and medium sized firms and universities, and appropriations for that program ran from fiscal year 2008 to fiscal year 2011 before ending. Programs created by authorization live or die by appropriation, and the technology program of 2007 died that way within four years. The new foundation directorate for technology and innovation created in 2022 faces the same structural test: an authorization gives it legal existence and a ceiling, but only appropriations give it staff, grants, and regional programs.

None of this means the authorizations were meaningless. An authorization sets the terms of the argument for years. It tells agencies what scale to plan for, gives appropriators a ceiling they may approach, and provides advocates a benchmark against which to measure each year's funding bill. The doubling effort of 2007 did move appropriations upward, just not as far or as fast as the authorized path. The honest reading, the one the Congressional Research Service invites, is that authorizations are the opening bid in a multi-year negotiation with the appropriations process, and the opening bid is routinely discounted. Readers who understand that will not be surprised that the science division of the CHIPS and Science Act, authorized with such fanfare in August 2022, was being funded well below its authorized levels within two fiscal years. They will recognize the oldest pattern in American science policy, running on schedule.

## Dissecting the $280 Billion Headline

### Is the CHIPS Act Really a $280 Billion Law?
Only if authorizations counted as spending, which they do not. The figure combines about $52.7 billion in appropriated semiconductor funds, roughly $24 billion in expected tax credit claims, and about $200 billion in science authorizations Congress never fully funded. Treating the total as money spent is the most common error in coverage of the law.

The $280 billion figure is not invented; it is an assemblage, and each of its three components belongs to a different budgetary category. The first component, about $52.7 billion, is real appropriated money for semiconductors: the $50 billion CHIPS for America Fund, the $2 billion defense fund, and the smaller international and wireless funds, all directly appropriated in Division A as emergency spending available until expended. The second component, roughly $24 billion, is the Congressional Budget Office's estimate of claims under the 25 percent advanced manufacturing investment credit, new section 48D of the tax code, measured as revenue forgone through fiscal year 2031 rather than as outlays. The third component, about $200 billion in press summaries or about $174 billion in tighter accountings, is the Division B authorizations: permissions for future appropriations that the budget scorekeepers correctly recorded as costing nothing until some later bill provides the funds.

Why the assemblage misleads is a matter of budget mechanics. When the Congressional Budget Office estimated the bill's deficit effect, it counted the direct appropriations and the tax credit, because those change federal cash flows on enactment. It counted the authorizations at zero, because an authorization, standing alone, changes no cash flow. A reader who treats the $280 billion as the law's cost is therefore crediting the statute with roughly $200 billion of spending that the government's own scorekeepers said had not occurred and might never occur. The honest rendering is the one Wikipedia's summary reaches for: the act "authorizes roughly $280 billion," with only $52.7 billion appropriated. The verb matters more than the number.

The mechanics of federal budget scoring explain why the official cost never included the authorizations. Direct appropriations like Division A's are mandatory spending: the statute itself provides the budget authority, and the Congressional Budget Office records the outlays as they will occur. The investment credit is a tax expenditure: it reduces revenues, and the budget office estimated the revenue loss at more than $24 billion through fiscal year 2031 based on projected corporate claims. Authorizations of appropriations are neither. They appear in the budget resolution's discretionary totals as assumptions, and they enter the deficit calculation only when an appropriations bill acts on them. This is not a technicality; it is the difference between Congress spending money and Congress describing money it might spend later. Press coverage that summed all three categories into a single cost was adding two kinds of real fiscal effects to a category the scorekeepers deliberately set at zero.

The error pattern is worth cataloging because readers will keep encountering it. Some accounts described the act as committing $280 billion to be spent over five years, a phrasing that converts authorizations into outlays by grammatical fiat. Others reported the science division's targets as funding the agencies received, eliding the appropriations step entirely. A subtler variant treats the tax credit's $24 billion estimated revenue loss as $24 billion of spending, which misstates both the direction of the cash flow and who receives it. None of these framings reflects dishonest intent; all of them reflect the difficulty of explaining a bill whose headline number was designed, in part, to be impressive. But the cumulative effect is a public record in which the most quoted figure about the law is also the least informative about what the law did.

Readers will also encounter competing figures for the semiconductor half alone, and those deserve a brief reconciliation. Some accounts cite $52.7 billion, the appropriated total for semiconductor manufacturing, research, and workforce programs. Others cite $54 billion, which counts the wireless fund's $1.5 billion more explicitly. Still others cite $76 billion, which folds in the $24 billion estimated value of the investment tax credit alongside the $52.7 billion in appropriations. All three are defensible; they simply draw the boundary of the semiconductor provisions in different places. The $76 billion figure is the most complete accounting of the division's fiscal footprint, combining outlays and revenue loss, while the $52.7 billion figure is the strict appropriations total. What none of these figures includes is any of the science authorizations, which is exactly the boundary the $280 billion headline erases.

The signing ceremony on August 9, 2022, showcased the rhetoric the numbers could not support. The president called the law a once-in-a-generation investment in America itself, and the White House materials emphasized the largest figures the statute contained. There is nothing unusual in that; presidents sell bills with their biggest numbers. But the CHIPS and Science Act is an unusually sharp case of the gap between the sales pitch and the statute, because the pitch's centerpiece was never the law's price. A listener who took the ceremony at face value would have believed Congress had spent $280 billion on chips and science. Congress had appropriated $52.7 billion for chips, created a tax credit expected to cost about $24 billion in forgone revenue, and authorized roughly $200 billion for science that later Congresses declined to provide. The ceremony was not false, but it was denominated in the wrong unit: intentions, not outlays.

The tax credit component deserves a closer look, because its design contrasts instructively with the other great tax-credit statute of that summer. Section 48D allows a credit equal to 25 percent of qualified investment in an advanced manufacturing facility, defined as a facility whose primary purpose is manufacturing semiconductors or semiconductor manufacturing equipment, for property placed in service after 2022 where construction began by the end of 2026. The credit is effectively refundable through a direct-pay election, so firms without tax liability can still capture its value, and it carries a ten-year recapture: a taxpayer that claims the credit and then invests in advanced semiconductor capacity in China or another foreign country of concern during the decade after the property is placed in service can lose it. It is a single, flat-rate, industry-specific incentive with one behavioral condition attached.

Seven days after the CHIPS signing, on August 16, 2022, the president signed the Inflation Reduction Act, whose energy tax credits work on a markedly different philosophy, as described in this profile of [the energy statute enacted days later with a contrasting tax credit design](/2016/10/15/inflation-reduction-act-2022-energy/). Where the CHIPS credit offers one rate for one industry, the energy statute spreads dozens of credits across technologies and conditions their full value on prevailing-wage payment, apprenticeship utilization, domestic content, and project siting, with bonus adders that can multiply a credit's value several times over. The CHIPS design bets that a simple, predictable subsidy moves corporate location decisions; the energy design bets that layered conditions steer not just where investment happens but how the work is performed and with whose inputs. Both are industrial policy through the tax code, but they embody opposite theories of what a tax credit should demand in return, and the contrast is a useful reminder that the CHIPS Act's conditions, the guardrails and the recapture, operate at the level of corporate conduct abroad rather than workforce conduct at home.

A reader who wants to avoid the headline trap in future bills can apply a short discipline, stated here as prose rather than steps. First, separate the bill's divisions and ask of each whether it appropriates, authorizes, or does neither. Second, read the verbs: there is appropriated spends money, authorized to be appropriated describes money, and such sums as may be necessary declines to choose. Third, check the budget scorekeeper's table, which records authorizations at zero and thereby reveals what the bill actually costs. Fourth, discount any number that appears only in a press release or a fact sheet rather than in the enrolled text. Applied to the CHIPS and Science Act, this discipline yields the table below, which is the most honest single-page summary of the statute this article can offer.

## Two Statutes, One Signature

The namable claim stands as the article's closing frame: two statutes, one signature. The semiconductor half is industrial policy with cash and conditions attached, direct appropriations that no later Congress needed to renew, a refundable tax credit scored at roughly $24 billion, and guardrails that make the subsidy conditional on a decade of corporate conduct abroad. The science half is a wish list requiring annual appropriations, $81 billion of permission for the National Science Foundation, $50.3 billion for the Energy Department's science office, nearly $10 billion for the standards institute, and a new technology directorate authorized at about $16.3 billion over five years, nearly all of it dependent on appropriations bills that, in the fiscal years following enactment, did not reach the authorized levels. Treating the two halves as a single act, a single $280 billion achievement, produces every misunderstanding about what this law did: it overstates the spending, it understates the conditions, and it mistakes the authorization of a directorate for the funding of one.

The series thesis thread runs through the whole record at its clearest and most recent. The gap between what a statute authorizes and what is delivered is not an abstraction in this case; it is measurable in billions, dated to specific appropriations acts, and concentrated in the agencies the statute claimed to champion. Fiscal year 2023 missed the NSF target by 17 percent. Fiscal year 2024 missed it by 42 percent while cutting the agency's budget outright. The three flagship science agencies ended fiscal year 2024 roughly $8 billion below the targets the act had set for that year alone. None of this means the authorizations were meaningless; a stated target shapes debates, justifies requests, and gives agency planners a planning horizon. But a target is not a transfer, and the CHIPS and Science Act is the modern textbook case for reading the verb before the number whenever a statute's price tag is quoted.

Readers who want a longer view of how Washington has purchased technological capacity can set this statute's grant-and-credit model beside the contract-centered model of the space age in this [comparison of federal technology models](/2017/05/15/space-act-vs-chips-act-model/); the two approaches share a strategic vocabulary and almost nothing else in their mechanics. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the CHIPS and Science Act fund?

The act funded three different things under one signature. Division A, the CHIPS Act of 2022, appropriated 52.7 billion dollars in fiscal years 2022 through 2027, split into 39 billion for manufacturing incentives, 13.2 billion for research and development plus workforce development, 500 million for an international technology fund, and 1.5 billion for a wireless fund. The same division created the 25 percent Advanced Manufacturing Investment Credit under Section 48D, scored at about 24 billion dollars, which applies to qualified investment in advanced manufacturing facilities. Division B, the Research and Development, Competition, and Innovation Act, authorized roughly 200 billion dollars for the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the Department of Energy Office of Science over fiscal years 2023 through 2027, but those were authorizations, meaning Congress granted permission to fund them later through separate appropriations bills.

### Q: Why is the CHIPS and Science Act really two different laws?

Public Law 117-167 contains two divisions with different legal effect and different subject matter. Division A is the CHIPS Act of 2022, which appropriated 52.7 billion dollars for domestic semiconductor manufacturing incentives, research programs, and workforce development, plus the Section 48D tax credit. Division B is the Research and Development, Competition, and Innovation Act, which authorized funding for the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the Department of Energy Office of Science across fiscal years 2023 through 2027 but did not appropriate a single dollar. One division spent real money on semiconductor factories; the other gave congressional permission for future spending on basic research and science agencies. The two halves were combined into a single enrolled vehicle, H.R. 4346, because the broader competition bill had collapsed and lawmakers needed a vehicle that could reach the president's desk quickly. Calling them one law is technically correct but hides the gap between appropriation and authorization.

### Q: How much of the CHIPS and Science Act was actually appropriated?

Only Division A carried real money. Congress appropriated 52.7 billion dollars in Division A for fiscal years 2022 through 2027: 39 billion for manufacturing incentives, 13.2 billion for research and development and workforce development including the National Semiconductor Technology Center, 2 billion for a Defense Fund, 200 million for workforce and education, 500 million for an international fund, and 1.5 billion for a wireless fund. Separately, the Section 48D investment tax credit was scored at roughly 24 billion dollars in foregone revenue, which is a tax expenditure rather than an appropriation. Division B authorized about 200 billion dollars for the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the Department of Energy Office of Science over fiscal years 2023 through 2027, but authorized means permitted, not spent. In fiscal year 2023 the National Science Foundation received 9.874 billion in appropriations against 11.897 billion authorized, and in fiscal year 2024 it received 9.06 billion against 15.647 billion authorized, so the authorization levels were never reached.

### Q: What are the guardrails in the CHIPS and Science Act?

The guardrails are conditions in Division A attached to the semiconductor incentive awards. Recipients must sign agreements that bar them, for ten years, from engaging in significant transactions that materially expand semiconductor manufacturing capacity in foreign countries of concern. The statute names China, including Hong Kong, North Korea, Russia, and Iran, and allows other countries to be added. A violation triggers a clawback of the full award, meaning the entire grant must be returned. There is an exception for legacy semiconductors, such as 28 nanometer logic chips, so ordinary expansion of mature-node production is not prohibited. The agreements also restrict recipients from using the funds for stock buybacks or dividends beyond defined limits. These provisions apply to awards under Division A only; they do not appear in Division B. The structure reflects Congress's aim of reshoring advanced chip production rather than subsidizing manufacturing that could later migrate to the listed countries.

### Q: What tax credit did the CHIPS and Science Act create?

Division A created the Advanced Manufacturing Investment Credit under Section 48D of the Internal Revenue Code. The credit equals 25 percent of a taxpayer's qualified investment in an advanced manufacturing facility for semiconductors or semiconductor manufacturing equipment, for property placed in service after December 31, 2022. No credit is available if construction of the facility begins after December 31, 2026, which creates a fixed window for eligible projects. The credit includes a direct-pay election, so a taxpayer can receive the value as a cash payment from the Treasury rather than only as an offset against tax liability, a feature the drafting team noted differs from typical credits and from the later Inflation Reduction Act designs. The Joint Committee score of roughly 24 billion dollars represents foregone revenue, not an appropriation. The credit applies independently of the Division A grant programs, though guardrail agreements tied to grants can affect a recipient's broader investment plans.

### Q: How long did the CHIPS and Science Act take to pass?

The legislation grew out of a competition-policy effort that began with the Endless Frontier Act in May 2020. The Senate passed the U.S. Innovation and Competition Act, or USICA, by 68 to 32 on June 8, 2021. The House passed its counterpart, the America COMPETES Act, by 222 to 210 on February 4, 2022. A conference committee meant to reconcile the two bills collapsed, and leaders then drafted a narrowed rescue package focused on semiconductors and science authorizations. The Senate passed that package, enrolled as H.R. 4346, by 64 to 33 on July 27, 2022. The House followed by 243 to 187, with one member voting present, on July 28, 2022. President Biden signed Public Law 117-167 on August 9, 2022. Measured from the Endless Frontier Act, the effort spanned more than two years; measured from the conference collapse, the final sprint took only weeks.

### Q: Was the CHIPS and Science Act bipartisan?

Yes, in both chambers, though the margins tell two stories. In the Senate, USICA passed 68 to 32 in June 2021, and the final narrowed bill passed 64 to 33 on July 27, 2022, with both parties contributing substantial yes votes. In the House, the margins were thinner and more partisan: America COMPETES passed 222 to 210 in February 2022, and the final bill passed 243 to 187 with one present on July 28, 2022. The Republican support concentrated around the semiconductor manufacturing provisions and the China competition framing, while many members of both parties objected to the size and scope of the science authorizations. President Biden, a Democrat, signed the bill, but the semiconductor incentives had roots in proposals from both parties. The law is fairly described as bipartisan but more comfortable in the Senate, where both the original and the final votes cleared with broad coalitions.

### Q: What new NSF directorate did the CHIPS and Science Act create?

Division B authorized the Technology, Innovation and Partnerships directorate, known as TIP, as a new directorate within the National Science Foundation. TIP was designed to support use-inspired and translational research, regional innovation ecosystems, and partnerships with industry, in contrast to the foundation's traditional basic-science directorates. The law authorized funding for it as part of the overall National Science Foundation authorization of about 81 billion dollars for fiscal years 2023 through 2027. Authorization, however, is not an appropriation: Congress gave the foundation permission to operate TIP and permission to spend at those levels, but the money itself had to come through later appropriations bills. In practice the foundation's actual appropriations fell far below the authorized levels, with 9.874 billion appropriated in fiscal year 2023 against 11.897 billion authorized and 9.06 billion in fiscal year 2024 against 15.647 billion authorized. TIP exists as authorized structure operating within those constrained appropriations.

### Q: What is the difference between an authorization and an appropriation?

An authorization is congressional permission; an appropriation is the money itself. The distinction explains the entire two-division structure of Public Law 117-167. Division A contained appropriations: 52.7 billion dollars that Congress actually provided for semiconductor incentives, research, and workforce programs in fiscal years 2022 through 2027. Division B contained only authorizations: about 200 billion dollars that Congress said the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the Department of Energy Office of Science could receive over fiscal years 2023 through 2027, without providing any of it. An authorization sets a ceiling and a purpose, but agencies cannot spend a dollar until a separate appropriations bill supplies the funds. The gap between the two is the central recurring error in coverage of the act: treating the authorized 200 billion as though it were spent. Fiscal year 2023 and 2024 appropriations for the National Science Foundation came in billions below their authorized levels, which proves the point.

### Q: Is the roughly 280 billion dollar headline figure real spending?

No. The roughly 280 billion dollar figure combines three different categories and quoting it as spending is the central recurring error in coverage of the act. About 52.7 billion dollars was actually appropriated in Division A for semiconductor programs in fiscal years 2022 through 2027. About 24 billion dollars was the scored value of the Section 48D investment tax credit, which is foregone revenue, not spending. The remaining roughly 200 billion dollars was authorized, not appropriated, in Division B for the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the Department of Energy Office of Science over fiscal years 2023 through 2027. Authorized money is permission, and the later appropriations fell far short: the National Science Foundation received 9.874 billion in fiscal year 2023 against 11.897 billion authorized and 9.06 billion in fiscal year 2024 against 15.647 billion authorized. Real enacted resources were the 52.7 billion appropriation plus the 24 billion credit score; everything else was a promise, not a payment.

### Q: What does the clawback in the guardrails do?

A violation of the guardrail agreements triggers a clawback of the full award. Under Division A, recipients of semiconductor incentive funding must agree not to engage in significant transactions that materially expand semiconductor manufacturing capacity in foreign countries of concern for ten years. If a recipient breaks that agreement, the government recovers the entire award, not a prorated share and not merely the portion connected to the offending transaction. The all-or-nothing design is deliberate: a partial penalty might be treated as a cost of doing business, while the loss of the full grant makes prohibited expansion economically irrational. The clawback operates through the required agreements rather than through the tax code, so it applies to grant recipients under Division A. Legacy semiconductors, such as 28 nanometer logic chips, are excepted from the expansion bar, which narrows what counts as a violation. The provision converts the national security condition from a statement of intent into an enforceable financial consequence.

### Q: Which countries do the guardrails cover, and how long do they last?

The guardrails bar significant transactions that materially expand semiconductor manufacturing capacity in foreign countries of concern for ten years from the award. The statute names China, including Hong Kong, North Korea, Russia, and Iran, and gives the government authority to designate additional countries. The ten-year clock is tied to each recipient's agreement, so the restriction follows the award rather than applying as a general industry rule. The bar is not absolute: legacy semiconductors, such as 28 nanometer logic chips, are excepted, so expansion of mature-node production in the listed countries does not trigger a violation. Only transactions that materially expand capacity for advanced semiconductors count. The restriction applies to recipients of Division A incentive funding, and breaching it triggers the clawback of the full award. The list reflects the national security framing of the CHIPS Act of 2022: the incentives were meant to pull advanced manufacturing to the United States, not to subsidize capacity that could later benefit the listed countries.

### Q: How is the semiconductor funding actually awarded?

The 52.7 billion dollars appropriated in Division A is not distributed as automatic grants. The Commerce Department administers a competitive award process: applicants submit proposals for manufacturing incentives, research programs, and workforce development, and the department selects recipients and negotiates terms over a period of years. The statute structured the money across fiscal years 2022 through 2027, with 39 billion for manufacturing incentives, 13.2 billion for research and development and workforce development, 500 million for an international fund, and 1.5 billion for a wireless fund, so awards roll out in stages rather than all at once. Semiconductor fabrication plants take years to construct, which means the appropriated money commits long before factories produce chips. Recipients must sign the guardrail agreements, including the ten-year bar on expanding advanced manufacturing capacity in foreign countries of concern and the restrictions on buybacks and dividends. The Section 48D tax credit operates separately through the tax code, not through the Commerce Department process.

### Q: Why were the science agencies never funded at their authorized levels?

Division B authorized roughly 200 billion dollars for the National Science Foundation, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and the Department of Energy Office of Science over fiscal years 2023 through 2027, but an authorization only permits spending; it does not provide it. The money had to come through later appropriations bills, which face their own caps, negotiations, and competing priorities. The National Science Foundation received 9.874 billion in appropriations in fiscal year 2023 against 11.897 billion authorized, and 9.06 billion in fiscal year 2024 against 15.647 billion authorized under Public Law 118-42, signed March 9, 2024. Across the three agencies the shortfall against fiscal year 2024 authorization targets was about 8 billion dollars. Authorizations routinely exceed appropriations in federal science policy, and nothing in Division B compelled Congress to meet the targets. The result is that the headline science investment exists mostly on paper, while the semiconductor appropriations in Division A were real money from the start.

### Q: What happened to the broader competition bills, USICA and COMPETES?

The CHIPS and Science Act was a narrowed rescue of a collapsed larger effort. The Senate passed the U.S. Innovation and Competition Act, or USICA, by 68 to 32 on June 8, 2021. The House passed its own version, the America COMPETES Act, by 222 to 210 on February 4, 2022. A conference committee was supposed to reconcile the two into a single sweeping competition bill, but the conference broke down amid disagreements over scope and provisions unrelated to semiconductors. Rather than let the entire effort die, congressional leaders stripped the package down to its two most viable pieces: the semiconductor incentives that became Division A and the science agency authorizations that became Division B. That narrowed bill, enrolled as H.R. 4346, passed the Senate 64 to 33 on July 27, 2022, and the House 243 to 187 with one present on July 28, 2022. The broader trade, immigration, and research provisions of USICA and COMPETES never became law.

### Q: What was H.R. 4346 before it carried the CHIPS and Science Act?

H.R. 4346 was a legislative vehicle, a bill chosen for its procedural position rather than its original content. As enrolled, it carried Public Law 117-167, but the CHIPS and Science Act text was substituted into the bill late in the process after the USICA and COMPETES conference collapsed. Using an existing vehicle let leaders bypass earlier procedural steps and move the narrowed semiconductor and science package directly to floor votes. The Senate passed the substituted bill 64 to 33 on July 27, 2022, and the House passed it 243 to 187 with one present on July 28, 2022, before President Biden signed it on August 9, 2022. The practice is common in Congress: when time is short and a broad negotiation fails, a rescue package rides inside a vehicle that is already procedurally available. The number H.R. 4346 therefore tells a reader nothing about the law's substance; the substance is in Division A and Division B.

### Q: What did Division B authorize for NIST and the DOE Office of Science?

Division B authorized about 9.7 billion dollars for the National Institute of Standards and Technology over fiscal years 2023 through 2027, and about 50.3 billion dollars for the Department of Energy Office of Science over the same period. Both were authorizations, meaning Congress permitted spending at those levels but appropriated none of the money in the act itself. The National Science Foundation received the largest Division B authorization, about 81 billion dollars over the same five years, including the new Technology, Innovation and Partnerships directorate. Together the three authorizations account for the roughly 200 billion dollars often quoted in the law's headline figure. Like the foundation, NIST and the Office of Science depended on later appropriations bills to turn permission into funding, and the three agencies together ran about 8 billion dollars below their fiscal year 2024 authorization targets. The pattern is the same across all three: ambitious authorized ceilings, constrained actual appropriations.

### Q: Did the act appropriate money for the new NSF TIP directorate?

No. Division B authorized the Technology, Innovation and Partnerships directorate within the National Science Foundation, but it appropriated zero dollars for TIP or for any other Division B program. The roughly 81 billion dollars authorized for the foundation over fiscal years 2023 through 2027 was a ceiling and a statement of intent, and TIP's share of that ceiling still had to be funded through later appropriations bills. Those bills did not come close: the foundation received 9.874 billion in appropriations in fiscal year 2023 against 11.897 billion authorized, and 9.06 billion in fiscal year 2024 against 15.647 billion authorized. A directorate that is authorized but under-appropriated must operate within whatever the foundation's actual budget allows, competing with the agency's existing basic-science directorates for the same constrained dollars. The distinction matters because headlines often describe TIP as though Congress had endowed it; in fact Congress only gave it permission to exist.

### Q: How does the 48D credit's direct pay feature differ from typical credits?

Most business tax credits only offset tax liability, which limits their value to companies with enough taxable income to absorb them. The Section 48D Advanced Manufacturing Investment Credit, worth 25 percent of qualified investment in advanced manufacturing facilities, includes a direct-pay election: an eligible taxpayer can choose to receive the credit's value as a cash payment from the Treasury instead of using it against taxes owed. That design matters for semiconductor projects because fabrication plants require enormous upfront capital spending before they generate revenue, a profile that can leave a builder with little current tax liability to offset. The direct-pay window is bounded: the credit applies to property placed in service after December 31, 2022, and no credit is available if construction begins after December 31, 2026. The drafting team noted that this direct-pay structure differs from typical credit designs, including the later Inflation Reduction Act credits, and it operates independently of the Division A grant programs.

### Q: What happened to the 10 billion dollar regional tech hubs authorization?

Division B authorized 10 billion dollars over fiscal years 2023 through 2027 for regional technology and innovation hubs, a program meant to spread research-driven economic development beyond the established coastal centers. Like every other Division B provision, it was an authorization, not an appropriation: Congress declared that 10 billion could be spent but provided none of it in the act. The hubs therefore depended on later appropriations bills, which arrived in far smaller amounts than the authorized ceiling. The program exists on the same footing as the other Division B authorizations: a statutory framework with ambitious targets and constrained funding. The tech hubs illustrate the broader pattern of the Research and Development, Competition, and Innovation Act, in which the headline figures were permissions rather than payments. Readers who encounter the 10 billion figure should treat it as an authorization ceiling, alongside the National Science Foundation's 81 billion, NIST's 9.7 billion, and the DOE Office of Science's 50.3 billion.
