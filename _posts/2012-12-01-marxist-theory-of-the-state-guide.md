Ask a room of educated readers what the Marxist theory of the state is, and most of them will give the same answer in nearly the same words. The state, they will say, is a committee for managing the affairs of the bourgeoisie, an instrument in the hands of the ruling class, a machine whose apparent neutrality conceals whose interests it serves. The answer has the ring of a quotation because it is one, lifted from a pamphlet of 1848, and it has survived because it is short, quotable and easy to teach. What the room will almost never be told is that this sentence represents the crudest position inside the tradition it claims to summarise, that the most influential Marxist writers on the state spent their careers arguing against it, and that the tradition they worked in contains not one theory of the state but at least four, distinct in what they claim, distinct in what they predict, and in places flatly incompatible with one another. This article is a guide to those four theories, to the texts that carry them, and to the strange structural fact that explains why there are four of them instead of one.

![The Marxist theory of the state, the committee formula, relative autonomy and the rival theories compared - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-14.webp)

That structural fact deserves a name at the outset, because everything else in the article hangs on it. The missing volume is the book on the state that Marx planned and never wrote. In the late 1850s Marx sketched a work in six books, covering capital, landed property, wage labour, the state, foreign trade and the world market. Of that architecture, only the material gathered under the first heading grew into the published work we have, and even that remained unfinished at his death. The book on the state was never begun as a book. What exists instead is a scattering of treatments across forty years: a youthful critique of Hegel, a polemical pamphlet, a piece of political journalism of genius, an occasional address on a Paris insurrection, a set of marginal notes on a party programme, and, from Engels after Marx had died, an attempt to explain where the state came from in the first place. Every Marxist theory of the state since then has been a reconstruction assembled from these fragments, and the assembly choices are interpretive acts. Two honest scholars, reading the same fragments and weighting them differently, can build theories that contradict each other, and the history of the subject is largely the history of that happening.

The discipline this article practises follows from the finding. Each of the four theories is stated separately, in its own terms, with its own textual home and its own principal weakness, and they are never merged into a synthetic Marxist view of the state, because no such synthesis exists in the sources and pretending otherwise is exactly the error the committee formula commits in miniature. The four positions are these. Instrumentalism holds that the state is an instrument wielded by the ruling class, and that its behaviour is explained by who controls it. Structuralism holds that the state serves capital through the constraints of the structure it administers, regardless of who staffs it. The Bonapartist model, drawn from Marx's own analysis of France at mid century, holds that under specifiable conditions the state apparatus acquires a substantial autonomy of its own and rules over a society whose classes have fought themselves to a standstill. And the strategic relational approach holds that the state is neither a tool nor a subject but a social relation, a terrain whose institutional shape systematically favours some strategies and actors over others. Around these four stands a fifth element that is not a theory of the existing state at all: the Commune model, the one institutional form Marx ever endorsed, which belongs in the account because it shows what he thought should replace the apparatus the other four theories describe.

A reader who works through the article should leave able to do three things. The first is attribution: to say which text carries which claim, and to hear at once when a speaker attributes to Marx a doctrine that belongs to a reconstruction. The second is separation: to state instrumentalism and structuralism as rival explanations rather than as two names for one idea, since the famous debate between their champions was bitter precisely because they cannot both be right in their strong forms. The third is application: to take the missing volume finding seriously as a working rule, naming which reconstruction is in use before using it, and knowing what evidence would count against that reconstruction. Readers who want the surrounding architecture can find the state's structural location set out in the account of [base and superstructure explained](/2010/02/01/base-and-superstructure-explained/), and the class relation that every one of these theories presupposes in the [marxist theory of class guide](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/); this article assumes both and concentrates on the political institution itself.

## The missing volume: a plan, a gap, and an unfinished chapter

The six book plan survives in Marx's correspondence of 1858 and in the preface to his 1859 contribution to the critique of political economy, and its order matters. Capital came first, then landed property, then wage labour, and only then the state, followed by foreign trade and the world market. The sequence was not a table of contents for its own sake. It expressed a method: begin with the most abstract relations of the economy, and introduce each more concrete level only when the abstractions beneath it had been secured. The state, on this plan, was to be treated after the economic categories had been developed, as the concentrated political form in which the relations already analysed express themselves and react back upon them. Whatever Marx's book on the state would have said, it would have said it with the whole critique of political economy underneath it, and it would have been able to draw distinctions that the earlier scattered writings, produced before that critique existed, could not yet support.

Why the book was never written is a question with a mundane answer and a more interesting one, and the FAQ section of this article takes up the mundane side, which involves the sheer scale of the first book consuming the remaining decades of Marx's life, his health, his journalism and the political demands that repeatedly pulled him away from the desk. The more interesting answer is that the plan's own logic made the state volume progressively harder to write. A theory of the state adequate to the method would have had to show, level by level, how the political form relates to property relations, to class formation, to the inherited machinery of bureaucracy and army, and to the very different histories of the states Marx knew, the Prussian, the French, the English, the American. The scattered writings treat each of these somewhere. None treats them together, and the evidence of Marx's working life suggests he knew the difference between possessing the materials and possessing the book.

The gap has a companion that sharpens it. The third volume of Capital breaks off, in the form Engels published, just as its final chapter turns to classes, and that chapter stops after little more than a page, at the very moment it begins to ask what constitutes a class. The parallel is instructive. In both places the architecture of the work promises a systematic treatment of a political category, classes in the one case, the state in the other, and in both places the treatment trails into fragments. Later tradition responded to the two gaps in the same way, by building. Theories of class were constructed from the analyses scattered through the historical writings, and theories of the state were constructed from the Brumaire, the Commune writings, the Manifesto and the early critique of Hegel. The constructions are not worthless. Several are brilliant. But a construction carries the builder's choices inside it, and the first service a guide can perform is to keep reminding the reader that building happened at all.

It is worth pausing on what the missing volume does not mean. It does not mean Marx had no views about the state; few writers of the century wrote about political power more often or more vividly. It does not mean the scattered treatments are random; they cluster around recognisable problems, the origin of political power separated from society, the autonomy an apparatus can win, the form a workers government might take. And it does not mean that any reconstruction is as good as any other, since the fragments constrain what can honestly be built from them, and some popular constructions violate the fragments outright. What the gap means is procedural. There is no canonical text to appeal to when two Marxist theories of the state disagree, no chapter and verse that settles whether the state is best understood as an instrument, a structure, an autonomous apparatus or a terrain. The disagreements have to be settled the way other scholarly disagreements are settled, by evidence and argument, and the tradition's century of argument about the state is not a scandal to be explained away. It is what a tradition looks like when its founder left the central political volume unwritten.

### Why does the unwritten volume matter so much?

Because its absence governs everything built in its place. Marx projected a six book critique of political economy with the state as the fourth volume, and he never began it, so his remarks survive scattered across journalism, pamphlets and notes. Every later theory is therefore an assembly, and the assembly choices are themselves interpretations.

## What actually exists: the scattered corpus

The fragments themselves deserve a survey before the theories built from them, because the four reconstructions differ largely in which fragments they treat as load bearing. Five bodies of writing do most of the work, and a sixth, by Engels, supplies the account of origins. Each has a date, a genre and an occasion, and the genre matters more than textbook summaries admit. A pamphlet written for a league of exiles in a revolutionary year speaks in slogans because slogans are its job. A piece of journalism written to explain a coup to newspaper readers argues from the concrete case. An address written to defend a defeated insurrection draws lessons its author knows will be contested. Reconstructions that lift a sentence from one genre and install it as doctrine in another are the source of half the confusion in this field.

The earliest stratum is the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, written in 1843 and unpublished in Marx's lifetime, together with the essay on the Jewish question from the same period. Here the state appears as an abstraction standing over against civil society, claiming to represent the universal interest while private life proceeds beneath it governed by property and egoism. The essay draws its famous distinction between political emancipation, the granting of equal citizenship within a state that leaves the real inequalities of civil society untouched, and human emancipation, which would require those real relations themselves to change. Whatever its limits, this early work fixes two permanent features of the tradition: suspicion of the state's claim to stand above social division, and attention to the gap between formal political equality and material inequality. Instrumentalists and structuralists alike can quote it, which is another way of saying it predates their difference.

The Manifesto of 1848 supplies the second stratum and the tradition's most quoted sentence, treated in its own section below. The third is the journalism of the French crisis, above all The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, published in 1852, where the apparatus of the French state, its bureaucracy, army and police, swells into a power that seems to stand independent of the society beneath it because the classes contending for it have exhausted one another. The fourth is the address on the Paris Commune, The Civil War in France, written in 1871 while the events were still warm, where Marx identifies in the Commune's improvised institutions the political form his tradition had lacked. The fifth is the Critique of the Gotha Programme of 1875, marginal notes on the programme of a German workers party, which discuss what the state can and cannot be in a transition between capitalism and communism and scold the party for treating the existing state as a benefactor to be petitioned. Engels's book of 1884 on the origin of the family, private property and the state then attempts what Marx never attempted, a systematic account of how the state arose with class division, resting heavily on the anthropology available at the time.

Hold that survey in mind and the later history becomes legible. When twentieth century theorists rebuilt the subject, each rebuilt from a different shelf of this small library. The instrumentalists generalised the Manifesto. The theorists of autonomy generalised the Brumaire. The Commune supplied the normative model against which existing states, including states calling themselves socialist, were measured and usually found wanting. The structuralists, working through the early critique of Hegel and the mature economic writings, argued that the whole library pointed to a conclusion none of its volumes stated: that the state's service to capital is built into its form and situation, not into the intentions of its personnel. And the strategic relational school, arriving last, concluded that the library's very diversity was the finding, that the state is not the kind of thing that could be an instrument or a subject at all. The sections that follow take the strata in order before turning to the four theories and the debate that defined them.

## The young Marx: political emancipation and the state as abstraction

The 1843 critique of Hegel is a manuscript argument with a system, and its target is precise. In Hegel's philosophy of right, the state is the sphere in which the divisions of private life are overcome: the bureaucrat, the monarch and the estates embody a universal interest that the egoistic pursuits of civil society cannot generate from themselves. Marx inverted the picture. The universal interest, he argued, is not embodied in the state; it is claimed by the state, and the claim is the ideology of a specific body of officials whose own material interest lies in the perpetuation of the separation between state and society. The bureaucracy treats the state as its private property in effect, guarding its mysteries, advancing its careers, and mistaking its own corporate survival for the public good. Readers who find this passage of the young Marx startlingly fresh are usually responding to the fact that it is an analysis of an apparatus and its interests, not of a class wielding an instrument, and later theorists of state autonomy regularly return to it for that reason.

The companion essay on the Jewish question extends the argument from the apparatus to the citizen. Its question, posed by Bruno Bauer, was whether Jews could be emancipated while remaining Jews, and Marx's reply widened the question until it covered the modern state as such. A state can emancipate itself from a religion, he argued, can declare all citizens equal regardless of confession, property or birth, while leaving every one of the real distinctions intact in civil society, where they govern actual lives. Political emancipation is therefore a genuine advance and a strictly limited one: it perfects the split between the citizen, equal in the heaven of the state, and the bourgeois, unequal on the earth of the market. The distinction matters for everything that follows in this article, because it gives the tradition a way of valuing democratic forms without mistaking them for the whole of emancipation, and it frames the question that the article on [marxism and democracy critique](/2013/03/15/marxism-and-democracy-critique/) takes up directly: what, exactly, did Marx think political democracy achieves, and what did he think it cannot achieve by itself.

Two cautions belong with the young Marx. The first is that these writings precede the critique of political economy by more than a decade, so their account of why the state behaves as it does rests on a philosophy of human nature and alienation that the mature work replaced with an analysis of production relations. Reconstructions that treat the 1843 manuscripts as the finished theory are making a choice, sometimes defended on the ground that the early problematic was never really abandoned, sometimes made in simple ignorance of the chronology. The second caution is that the young Marx's state is largely the Prussian state as filtered through Hegel's idealisation of it, a bureaucratic monarchy quite unlike the parliamentary republics and mass democracies that later theory had to explain. The categories he forged against that target travelled surprisingly well. But they travelled, and a careful reader keeps the journey in view, since a theory built to expose a mystified bureaucracy may need adjustment before it can explain a state that taxes, insures, educates and conscripts on a scale no Prussian minister imagined.

## The committee formula and what it was written to do

### Is the state just a committee of the ruling class?

That formulation comes from the Communist Manifesto of 1848 and represents the most instrumentalist position in the tradition. Later Marxist theorists argued the state serves capital through structural constraint regardless of who staffs it, and can act against particular capitalists to preserve the system as a whole.

The sentence itself, in its usual English form, runs that the executive of the modern state is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie, and the first ten words are quoted at the head of this article's discussion because they are the part everyone knows. Notice what the sentence actually says, once it is read slowly rather than recited. Its subject is the executive, not the state as a whole; the judiciary, the legislature, the local authorities and the permanent administration are outside its grammatical reach. Its verb is managing, an administrative word, and its object is the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie, a phrase that quietly concedes the bourgeoisie has affairs in common that no individual capitalist can manage alone, which is why a committee is needed at all. Even the tradition's most instrumentalist sentence, read closely, contains the seed of the problem that undid instrumentalism: if capital needs a committee because its members cannot agree, then the committee has work of its own to do, and the question of how much discretion that work involves is open from the start.

Context tightens the point. The Manifesto was a pamphlet commissioned by a small league of revolutionaries and published in the February of a year in which thrones were falling across Europe. Its rhetoric is the rhetoric of exposure: it strips the veil from institutions, showing the cash nexus beneath the sentimental phrase, the ruling class beneath the neutral institution. A pamphlet that hedged its central claim about the state with qualifications about structural constraint and relative autonomy would have been a worse pamphlet and a forgotten one. Marx himself, in later years, treated the text as a historical document; the prefaces he and Engels added to later editions note passages that would be worded differently and a programme that had in parts grown antiquated, while insisting the general argument stood. The tradition's error was not writing the sentence. It was the later habit of citing the sentence as though it were the conclusion of the unwritten volume, a theory of the state in eleven words, when its author had planned to spend a book on the subject and had already, four years after the pamphlet, written an analysis of France that the sentence cannot accommodate.

That analysis is the standing embarrassment of the pure committee reading. In the Brumaire, discussed in detail below, the French state is not managing the common affairs of the bourgeoisie at the bourgeoisie's direction. The bourgeoisie, in Marx's account, surrenders political power in order to keep its social power, abandoning parliament to a plebiscitary adventurer and an enormous parasitic apparatus, because its own rule had become incompatible with the order its property required. A committee does not dismiss its employers, confiscate their political voice and rule in its own interest for two decades. Whatever the Brumaire's theory is, it is not the Manifesto's, and any honest statement of Marxist state theory has to hold both texts in view and explain their difference rather than quote one and forget the other. This is the first concrete payoff of the missing volume finding: the fragments disagree in emphasis because they were written for different occasions against different targets, and the reconstruction that begins by admitting this is the only kind worth having.

## Engels and the origin of the state

### What did Engels attempt that Marx never did?

A systematic account of origins. In his book of 1884 Engels argued the state arose when society divided into classes, as a power apparently standing above society to hold the conflict within the bounds of order, marked by public power, taxation and territorial organisation rather than kinship.

Engels's book on the origin of the family, private property and the state was the closest thing the founding generation produced to a systematic treatise on the state, and it is important to register that it is a work of 1884, written after Marx's death, resting on the ethnology of its moment, particularly the work of Lewis Henry Morgan on kinship and early society. Its central claim is genetic rather than functional. The state is not part of the natural furniture of human association. It emerges at a determinate stage, when production has generated a surplus, society has split into classes with opposed economic interests, and the old kinship based organisation can no longer contain the antagonism. A new power is then constituted, armed bodies of men, prisons, taxes to pay for them, officials organised by territory rather than by blood, which presents itself as standing above society and moderating its conflicts while in practice securing the dominance of the economically strongest class.

The genetic claim does work that the functional claims of the other theories cannot do for themselves. If the state arose with class division, then the state is not a neutral arbiter that classes happen to capture from time to time, because there was never a stateless class society for it to preside over innocently; the institution and the division are coeval. The claim also disciplines the tradition's expectations about the state's disappearance, a topic this article deliberately leaves to its own treatment, since what has a historical beginning can in principle have a historical end. But the book's weaknesses are equally part of the record. Its anthropology has been overtaken: the unilinear sequence of stages, the portrait of primitive communism and the matriarchal conjectures belong to nineteenth century evolutionism, and later scholarship has complicated every one of them. Its functional slide is also visible in its own pages, moving from the state's origin in class division to conclusions about every state's conduct, as though genesis settled function. Modern readers who use Engels well use him for the question he taught the tradition to ask, why a separate public power exists at all, and decline to inherit his ethnography whole.

There is a subtler legacy. Engels described the state as a power apparently standing above society, and the word apparently carries the whole ambiguity that later theory fought over. Read one way, the appearance is a disguise: the state seems above the fray while serving the dominant class beneath it, which is the instrumentalist reading. Read another way, the appearance records something real: the state genuinely is separate from society, staffed by its own personnel with interests of its own, and the separateness itself, not the personnel's sympathies, is what has to be explained, which opens the road to the autonomy and structural accounts. That a single adverb can support two research programmes is not a defect of Engels's prose. It is the missing volume problem again, at the scale of a word. The systematic treatment that would have settled the relative weight of disguise and separateness is the treatment nobody wrote, and the tradition has been weighing the adverb ever since.

## The Brumaire: an apparatus that escaped its makers

The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte is the text to which every serious account of Marx on the state eventually returns, and its canonical reading guide sits in this series at the [eighteenth brumaire readers guide](/2013/07/15/eighteenth-brumaire-readers-guide/); here the concern is narrower, with what the book contributes to state theory specifically. Its occasion was the coup of December 1851, in which Louis Bonaparte, president of the French republic and nephew of the first Napoleon, dissolved the assembly and made himself emperor in waiting. The puzzle Marx set himself was how a figure he regarded as a mediocre adventurer could defeat every organised class in France and rule through an apparatus of half a million officials and soldiers. The answer he constructed remains the most concrete analysis of a state machine in the whole tradition.

The argument runs through the classes. The French bourgeoisie wanted order for its property but found that its own parliamentary rule generated the disorder it feared, factions fighting in the assembly, a press it could not control, parties that raised expectations it could not meet. It therefore acquiesced in the destruction of its own political power to preserve its social power, its property, its factories, its rents. The peasantry, the largest class, a mass of smallholders sharing conditions but no organisation, could not represent itself and so was represented, its vote delivering France to a name and a memory. The proletariat, defeated in 1848, could not yet contend. Into the vacuum stepped the executive, with its enormous bureaucratic and military organisation, its layers of officials appointed from above, its interest in its own expansion, until, in Marx's image, the state seemed to have made itself independent of society altogether, a parasite body enclosing French life. The machinery had been built by earlier regimes, perfected by each, and inherited by all; every revolution had improved the apparatus instead of smashing it, and Bonaparte simply took possession of the finished instrument.

Three theoretical deposits fall out of this analysis, and later theory banked all three. The first is the concept of the exceptional state: a regime in which the executive rises above a balance of class forces, ruling in the long term interest of property while dispensing with property's political representatives. The second is the idea that the apparatus has interests, budgets, careers, habits of command, that are not reducible to the wishes of any class, so that explaining a state's conduct requires studying the machine and not only its supposed masters. The third is the historical observation that state machineries are inherited, not designed: each new regime receives the ministries, the prefectures, the codes and the police of the old, and the weight of that inheritance constrains what any government, of whatever colour, can do on taking office. No account of the state that cannot accommodate these three deposits can claim the Brumaire as an ancestor, and instrumentalism in its strong form cannot accommodate the first two at all, which is why the theorists of autonomy treat 1852, and not 1848, as the tradition's real founding date for state theory.

### When does an executive rise above the classes it serves?

When the contending classes have fought themselves to a standstill and the possessing class prefers order to its own political rule. Marx built the pattern from Louis Bonaparte's France, where a plebiscitary leader and an enormous bureaucracy governed over exhausted classes, preserving property while dispensing with the proprietors' parliament.

The concept travelled, and its travels show both its power and its risks. Applied to Bismarck's Germany, to the fascist regimes of the twentieth century in some analyses, and to military governments resting on exhausted civilian parties, it identifies a real recurrent configuration: the normal channels through which a dominant class rules politically are blocked or discredited, the subordinate classes cannot rule, and the apparatus, army, bureaucracy, presidency, governs in the interval, securing the conditions of accumulation while humiliating every class in turn. Its risk is elasticity. A label that fits every strong executive explains none of them, and the careful use of Bonapartism requires the user to specify the balance that produced it, the classes whose stalemate is being exploited, and the evidence that the apparatus is pursuing purposes of its own rather than the programme of a class fraction. Used with that discipline, it is the tradition's best tool for a class of regimes that neither the committee formula nor ordinary pluralism describes well. Used as an insult for governments the writer dislikes, it is merely a learned way of not analysing.

It is also the seed from which the concept of relative autonomy grew, and the genealogy matters for the misreading it corrects. Relative autonomy is not a late concession wrung from Marxists by the embarrassing behaviour of real states, not a patch applied in the 1960s when the committee formula failed. Its root text is from 1852, four years after the Manifesto, by the Manifesto's own author, describing the most advanced capitalist state in Europe. The two ideas coexist in the corpus from the beginning because they answer different questions: the committee formula describes who benefits from the normal working of a bourgeois state, while the Brumaire describes what happens to the apparatus when the normal working breaks down. Reconstructions err when they elevate either answer into the whole theory. The grid later in this article therefore gives the Bonapartist model its own row, as a theory in its own right, rather than treating it as a footnote to the other two, and the section on relative autonomy below traces how Poulantzas and others generalised the Brumaire's exceptional case into a claim about capitalist states as such.

## The Commune: the one institutional model Marx endorsed

In the spring of 1871, following France's defeat in the war with Prussia, Paris rose and governed itself for a little over two months under the Commune before being drowned in blood by the Versailles government. Marx, who had warned against the rising as untimely, defended it once it existed in an address to the International, The Civil War in France, and the address contains the tradition's only extended positive account of a political form. Its measures, as Marx catalogued them, were concrete: the standing army replaced by the armed people; officials elected, revocable at short notice and paid a workman's wage; the police stripped of political functions; the church disestablished; judges made elective and responsible; the Commune itself a working body, executive and legislative together, rather than a parliament of talkers. And the lesson Marx drew was stated without varnish: the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready made state machinery and wield it for its own purposes. The apparatus described in the Brumaire, centralised, hierarchical, parasitic, had to be broken, and the Commune was the form, at last discovered in his phrase, under which its replacement could begin.

The Commune model earns its place in this article not as a theory of the capitalist state but as the tradition's measuring rod for all states. It converts the critical theories into a question: how far is any given state from a form in which officials are revocable, paid like those they serve, and deprived of a separate armed force? By that rod, Marx measured the governments of his own side as severely as any other, and his followers have done the same, which is why the Commune is the text most often quoted inside the tradition against states that ruled in the tradition's name. The full narrative and its contested lessons are treated in this series in the account of the [paris commune marx analysis](/2013/02/15/paris-commune-marx-analysis/), and the present article's narrower point is about the shape of the corpus. The one place where Marx described institutions in detail is a description of institutions improvised by insurgent Parisians in nine weeks and destroyed before they could be tested by peace. The missing volume problem is at its most acute here. The tradition possesses a rich critical literature on the state it opposes and a nine week sketch of the state it wants, and the disproportion between those two possessions explains a great deal of its subsequent political history.

Honesty about the Commune also requires its difficulties. Marx's address was a defence written in the middle of a polemic, and it reads the Commune's intentions from its decrees, many of which were never implemented; the body he praised as a working form also spent its short life improvising under siege, and historians have disputed how far the picture of a unified anti state form survives the record of its factions and failures. The measures themselves raise questions the address does not answer: how revocability and workman's wages scale to a complex economy, what replaces the administrative expertise the old apparatus embodied, who coordinates the communes. Later writers filled these silences in incompatible ways, some treating the Commune as a literal blueprint, others as a directional principle, the smashing of the separate apparatus and the subordination of officials, compatible with many institutional designs. The difference between those two readings is, once again, an assembly choice made necessary by a text that was never going to settle it, and it is better to see the choice being made than to inherit it unnoticed.

## The Gotha notes: the state in transition

The Critique of the Gotha Programme, written in 1875 as marginal notes on the draft programme of a unifying German workers party and published by Engels only in 1891 against the party's wishes, is the last of the major fragments and in some ways the coldest. Its political target was the programme's genuflections toward the existing state: demands addressed to the Prussian German state as though it were a neutral benefactor, talk of the free state, faith in state aid for cooperatives under a regime that had four years earlier been shooting at the movement's Parisian cousins. Marx's scorn for this posture is theoretically loaded. A state, the notes insist, is the organ of the class that rules, and to petition it as though it stood above classes is to misunderstand what it is; freedom, he remarks, consists in converting the state from an organ superimposed upon society into one completely subordinate to it, a sentence that reaches back to the Commune and forward to every later argument about whether the existing apparatus can be used or must be transformed.

The notes also contain the tradition's most consequential few sentences about the state in a transition period, where Marx writes that between capitalist and communist society lies a period of revolutionary transformation, and that the state in that period can be nothing but a dictatorship of the proletariat. This article does not unpack that phrase, because the phrase has its own tangled history and its own treatment in this series, at [dictatorship of the proletariat](/2013/01/01/dictatorship-of-the-proletariat/), where its uses from the Commune to the twentieth century are separated. What matters here is the structural point the Gotha notes add to the corpus. They show the mature Marx still holding, in 1875, that the state is a class organ whose form tracks the society beneath it, still refusing the idea of the state as a free standing benefactor, and still leaving entirely unspecified the institutional content of the transition he names. The man who scolded a party programme for vagueness about distribution left his own political architecture at the level of principle. Readers who find that frustrating are feeling the missing volume from inside: the fragments give the tradition its critical power and its normative direction, and they withhold, permanently, the systematic treatise that would have told it how the pieces compose.

A word belongs here to the second great absence that shadows this one, the withering away of the state, the expectation, stated most fully by Engels, that as class division disappears the state loses its function and fades rather than being abolished at a stroke. That doctrine raises distinct questions about coercion, administration and the famous problem of who guards the stores in a society without a state, and it is treated on its own terms at [withering away of the state](/2013/01/15/withering-away-of-the-state/). In this article it enters only as a boundary marker: every theory in the grid below is a theory of the state under class society, and none of them should be inflated into an account of what replaces it.

## The four state theories grid

The reconstructions can now be laid side by side. The grid that follows is this article's findable artifact, and it is constructed to enforce the separations the article has been practising. Read across a row and you have one theory, whole: what it says the state is, what it says explains the state's behaviour, what it therefore predicts when a government of the left takes office, what evidence would falsify it, and the weakness its critics press hardest. Read down a column and you have the exact points at which the theories disagree. The fifth row is the Commune model, included under protest from tidiness, since it is not a theory of the existing state at all; it is placed here because readers persistently ask where it fits, and the honest answer is that it fits as the tradition's counter image, the form against which the states described by the other four rows are found wanting.

The grid is a map, not a verdict, and the sections that follow walk each row at the length it deserves. Two warnings about its use. The falsifiability column is the one textbook summaries always omit, and it is the most useful, because a theory whose defenders cannot say what would count against it has left science for rhetoric, and the strongest objection to this whole field, given its full hearing below, is precisely that Marxist state theory as popularly used fails that column completely. And the predictions column is deliberately framed around left governments, not because the theories speak only to that case, but because that case is the experiment history keeps running: a party committed against capital's interests wins office inside a capitalist state, and each theory says, in advance and differently, what happens next.

## Instrumentalism: Miliband and the sociology of the state elite

Instrumentalism received its modern, book length form in Ralph Miliband's The State in Capitalist Society, published in 1969, and the book's method is as important as its thesis. Miliband did not argue from the Manifesto. He argued from the directories: who, in the advanced capitalist countries, actually occupies the command posts of the state, the senior civil service, the judiciary, the officer corps, the regulatory boards, the central banks; from what families and schools they come; what boards they sit on after office; which interests their social world makes visible to them and which it renders unthinkable. His finding was that the state elite is drawn overwhelmingly from the propertied and professional classes, shares their formation and their assumptions, and is interlocked with corporate capital by career, kinship and club. On that evidence he concluded that the state in capitalist society serves the interests of capital not by conspiracy but by composition: the instrument is wielded by hands formed to wield it in one direction, and its much praised neutrality is the neutrality of a referee recruited exclusively from one team's supporters.

The virtues of the book are the virtues of its method. It is falsifiable in principle, since its claims are about identifiable people and can be checked, qualified or refuted by better sociology. It explains real phenomena that pure structural talk dissolves: the revolving door between ministries and boardrooms, the social closure of judicial recruitment, the ease with which some interests obtain a hearing and others obtain a leaflet. And it restored agency to a tradition that was drifting toward a functionalism in which nobody did anything and the structure did everything. Its limits appeared as soon as critics asked what the sociology actually explains. Personnel overlap shows who governs; it does not by itself show why governments of quite different composition, staffed by trade unionists, teachers and former miners, so often administer policies continuous with those of their predecessors. Nor does it explain the state's regular action against the expressed wishes of business: factory acts in the nineteenth century, antitrust in the twentieth, environmental and labour regulation resisted by the very class the state supposedly serves. Miliband had answers to some of this, distinguishing the interests of capital as a whole from the clamour of particular firms, but each answer moved him a step toward the structural ground he had meant to refuse, and the tension is the subject of the debate section below.

One clarification protects Miliband from his caricature. He was not a crude theorist of the committee. He insisted that the state has a genuine independence of action in detail, that governments enjoy real discretion within limits, that the ruling class in advanced capitalism does not rule directly but through representatives who must win elections and legitimate their power in universal terms, which constrains what they can do openly for their class. His quarrel with pluralism was not that other interests never win; it was that the game is played on a field tilted by the composition and commitments of the state elite and by the economic power that stands behind it. Even so, the architecture of his explanation runs through persons, and that is the load bearing choice. Where the explanation runs through persons, replacing the persons should change the outcome. The stubborn continuity of outcomes across very different persons is the fact his theory handles worst, and it is the fact his great antagonist built a career upon.

### What separates the two great explanations of the capitalist state?

One explanation runs through people: the class origins, education, networks and pressure surrounding those who staff the state explain why it serves capital. The other runs through position: dependence on private investment and the institutional separation of economics from politics constrain any personnel to serve accumulation, whatever their origins or intentions.

## Structuralism: Poulantzas and the state as a relation in embryo

Nicos Poulantzas, a Greek scholar working in Paris, published Political Power and Social Classes in 1968, a year before Miliband's book, and the two works passed each other like manifestos from different centuries. Where Miliband counted people, Poulantzas analysed structures. His starting point was that the capitalist state is defined by a specific institutional separation: under capitalism, unlike earlier modes of production, the extraction of surplus is economically organised through the wage contract, and direct political coercion is hived off into a separate, specialised public sphere. The state presents itself as the representative of the people as a whole, of citizens abstracted from their class positions, precisely because its form separates the political from the economic. That separation, for Poulantzas, is not a veil over class rule. It is the mechanism of class rule in its capitalist form, because it constitutes individuals as citizens while constituting classes elsewhere, and it organises the dominant class, itself divided into fractions with competing interests, into a political unity it could never achieve on its own, while disorganising the dominated classes by addressing them as individual citizens, taxpayers, parents, consumers.

The state's service to capital, on this account, does not depend on who staffs it and indeed may require distance from capitalists. Poulantzas gave that distance the name relative autonomy and treated it not as an exception but as a structural necessity: a state glued to the immediate demands of individual firms would serve capital badly, since capital's long term interest in stable accumulation, educated labour, infrastructure and social peace routinely conflicts with each firm's short term interest in lower taxes and weaker regulation. The state must be autonomous enough from every capitalist to act for capital. Its personnel might be aristocrats, technocrats or the sons of workers; the structure of its situation, dependent for revenue on an accumulation process it does not control, staffed by institutions that embody the separation of the political, disposes it to reproduce the relations it administers. In Poulantzas the explanatory weight shifts decisively from hands to terrain, and the committee formula is explicitly criticised as a misunderstanding of how the capitalist state works, a criticism aimed at the tradition's own slogans as much as at its enemies.

The costs of the position are symmetrical to Miliband's. Structural explanation courts a functionalism in which whatever the state does is read back as what capital needed: if it represses unions, it serves accumulation; if it builds welfare states, it serves accumulation by reproducing labour power and buying peace; if it loses a war or bankrupts itself, the account strains. Poulantzas also wrote a prose of legendary difficulty, in which structures have effects and regions have instances in a vocabulary that many readers experienced as a machine for avoiding plain statements, and his categories, fractions, power bloc, hegemonic fraction, multiplied faster than the evidence that could discriminate among them. His later work moved considerably: the final book, State, Power, Socialism, published in the year of his death in 1979, redefined the state as a material condensation of a relationship of forces, a phrase that conceded more to struggle, contingency and the strategic school that followed than the high structuralism of 1968 would have allowed. The Poulantzas of the debate with Miliband, however, is the Poulantzas of the structuralist pole, and it is that figure the next section stages.

## The Miliband and Poulantzas debate

The exchange between the two men, conducted mainly in the pages of New Left Review from 1969 into the middle of the following decade, with Ernesto Laclau intervening and Poulantzas returning for a rejoinder, is the canonical debate of Marxist state theory, and it deserves to be given as a debate, with blows landing in both directions, rather than as a footnote in either man's biography. It began with Poulantzas reviewing Miliband's book. His charge was methodological before it was political. Miliband, he argued, had adopted the problematic of his opponents: by asking who governs and answering with a sociology of elites, he treated the state as a tool that subjects pick up, and located explanation in the motives and origins of individuals, the very framework of the pluralist and elite theories he claimed to refute. The real question, for Poulantzas, was why the state in capitalist society serves capital regardless of the social origins of its personnel, and that question can only be answered at the level of structure. A state staffed entirely by the children of workers, inserted into the same institutional separation and the same fiscal dependence, would be driven to the same service; Miliband's evidence about recruitment, even if wholly accurate, explained nothing essential.

Miliband's reply, published the following year, is one of the great polemical performances of the period and it does not concede an inch of method. The structuralism on offer, he argued, was a structural super determinism in which the agents of history evaporate: classes become supports of structures, politicians become personifications, and every inconvenient fact about who actually decides, and what they believe, and whom they lunch with, is waved away as surface. He pressed the empirical point with relish. If personnel do not matter, why do states with similar structures behave so differently under different governments; why do business communities fight so hard over appointments, and fund parties, and maintain their networks into the state, if the wiring of the structure makes all of it decoration? And he pressed the logical point that has never gone away: a theory that redescribes every outcome as structurally necessary, after the outcome is known, has insulated itself against the evidence, and the insulation is not sophistication but evasion. Laclau's intervention complicated both positions by questioning whether classes have political identities waiting to be expressed at all, and Poulantzas's rejoinder refined his own account of class fractions and the power bloc, but the exchange's enduring value is the clean opposition it staged between two explanations that the wider tradition kept trying, dishonestly, to hold at once.

Read the debate as a student of method and its lesson generalises beyond this subject. The two men agreed about the conclusion, that the capitalist state systematically favours capital, and disagreed about everything that makes a conclusion into a theory: the mechanism, the evidence, the predictions, the conditions of refutation. Their agreement is what outsiders call the Marxist theory of the state. Their disagreement is what the field actually is. A generation of textbooks resolved the tension by listing both names under a composite heading, instrumentalist and structuralist factors, as though a mechanism and its rival could be added like ingredients. The grid in this article refuses that resolution, and so did both men, which is why their quarrel remains the best single education the subject offers. Anyone who has understood why Miliband and Poulantzas could not both be right has understood more about the theory of the state than anyone who has memorised the committee formula and the word hegemony together. Later writers, Jessop most systematically, tried to transcend the opposition by changing the object, treating the state neither as instrument nor as structure but as a relation and a terrain; whether that transcendence succeeds, or merely renames the tension, is the question the strategic relational section takes up.

## Relative autonomy: the concept and its evidence

### How much independence can a capitalist state actually possess?

A bounded but genuine amount. The state can act against the direct wishes of the dominant class, including against particular capitalists, while still securing the conditions for accumulation as a whole. Poulantzas argued such distance is structural, rooted in the separation of the political from the economic, and necessary because a divided ruling class needs an organiser outside itself.

The word relative does as much work as the word autonomy, and most misuse of the concept consists in dropping it. The claim is not that the state is autonomous, a free standing power pursuing ends of its own in indifference to the society it governs; that is the Bonapartist limiting case, and even it, in Marx's account, preserved the property order while humiliating the proprietors. The claim is that within limits set by the state's dependence on a privately owned accumulation process, the state possesses real discretion: it can choose among policies, discipline individual capitals, impose costs on the class as a whole for the class's own long term good, and pursue projects, national integration, military glory, bureaucratic expansion, that belong to the apparatus and its political managers rather than to any boardroom. The theoretical task is to specify the limits and the discretion together. Accounts that celebrate the discretion and forget the limits drift into a pluralism with Marxist vocabulary. Accounts that recite the limits and forget the discretion drift back into the committee. The concept exists to hold the two in one frame, which is why it became the tradition's most used and most abused term.

### When a state taxes or prosecutes capital, which theory is embarrassed?

None of the serious ones, and that is the point the slogan misses. States tax capital, regulate it, dissolve its combinations and jail its executives, and the structural account predicts such discipline as necessary for the system. The discriminating case is action against the accumulation process itself, which the theory predicts will summon investment collapse, flight or constitutional crisis.

The historical record supplies the distinction with content. The factory legislation of the nineteenth century was carried against the furious opposition of the mill owners whose interests it injured, by a state in which landed and commercial interests, reforming officials and working class pressure combined; Marx himself treated the Ten Hours agitation as a victory of the political economy of labour over the political economy of property, while noting that the limitation of the working day served the reproduction of labour power and thus of the system that exhausted it. The antitrust campaigns of the American state dissolved combinations that particular capitalists had built their fortunes on, in the name of a competitive order that benefited capital in general at the expense of its most successful members. Bismarck constructed social insurance over the objections of German employers, partly to undercut the socialists, partly to bind workers to the Reich. In each case the state acted against capitalist interests in the plural and the particular, and in each case the structural reading claims the same acts as its own evidence, since the long term conditions of accumulation, a healthy workforce, a competitive market, a loyal population, were being secured against the short sightedness of the class's individual members.

The pattern cuts the other way as well, and a balanced account must say so. The same record contains episodes where states attempted to move against the accumulation process itself, or were believed to threaten it, and the corrective mechanisms the theory names arrived on schedule: investment withheld until governments changed course, currencies attacked, capital exported, constitutional proprieties discovered to have been breached. The structural account's scientific status depends on keeping these two classes of episode distinct in advance, not sorting them afterwards. If action against capitalists that succeeds is always reclassified as serving the system, and action that fails is always reclassified as proof of the limits, the theory has arranged to be right whatever happens, which is the pluralist objection examined below in its strongest form. The honest structuralist therefore owes the reader predictions stated before the sorting: which kinds of measure will meet the corrective mechanisms, which will not, and why. The best of the structural dependence literature, taken up shortly, attempted exactly that discipline, and it is on that literature, rather than on the rhetorical versions, that the theory should be judged.

## The strategic relational approach: Jessop and the state as terrain

Bob Jessop, whose work from The Capitalist State in 1982 through State Theory in 1990 and beyond rebuilt the field after the Miliband and Poulantzas exchange had exhausted its first energies, began from the suspicion that both sides of that debate had misconceived their object. Instrumentalism treated the state as a thing, a tool that a subject could pick up. High structuralism treated it as a subject in its own right, a structure with effects that no one willed. Jessop proposed instead that the state be approached as a social relation. Its core, in his austere formulation, is an institutional ensemble, a set of centres and branches with specific powers, and its exercise of power is never the property of the state itself but of the political forces that act in and through its institutions. The famous consequence is his concept of strategic selectivity: the institutions of any actual state are not neutral between strategies. Their rules, their time horizons, their points of access and veto, their inherited routines privilege some actors, some demands and some time scales over others, systematically and before any particular decision is taken. A state organised around a powerful treasury and a central bank insulated from parliament selects for strategies that speak the language of fiscal credibility. A state with strong corporatist channels selects for organised interests that can deliver their members. The terrain does not determine the battle, but no serious account of the battle can ignore the terrain.

The approach earns its row in the grid because it changes what an explanation looks like. Instead of asking the single question, does the state serve capital, it asks a series of discriminating questions: which fractions, organised how, through which institutions, with what strategies, over what time horizon, and with what feedback into the state's own form. It also reconnects state theory to the Brumaire's deepest insight. Marx's France was a terrain whose inherited institutions, prefectures, codes, an army of officials, made some outcomes easy and others nearly impossible, and the coup was a strategy that the terrain selected for. Jessop's Marxism is in this sense the most Brumairean of the modern schools, and its author has been explicit that the approach is a development of the later Poulantzas, the state as condensation of a balance of forces, with the metaphor made rigorous: a balance of forces does not exist in the air, it is condensed in institutions that then shape the forces that contend within them.

The costs are the mirror of the virtues. An approach that answers the big question by dissolving it into discriminating smaller ones risks losing the phenomenon that made the subject worth studying: the gross, recurrent asymmetry between capital and everyone else in their dealings with states, which the older theories named directly and the strategic vocabulary can describe so flexibly that the naming fades. Critics also press the falsifiability column of the grid. If every outcome can be redescribed as the product of selectivities and strategies, what configuration of evidence would show the approach to be wrong, rather than merely underapplied? Jessop's defence is that the approach is a method of analysis rather than a theory with a single testable core, a guide to constructing explanations of particular states and conjunctures, to be judged by the explanations it yields. That defence is respectable and it concedes the critic's main point, that the strategic relational approach does not compete with instrumentalism and structuralism at the same game. It referees a different one. The reader should therefore treat it in the grid as what it is: the most sophisticated instrument the tradition has produced for analysing a concrete state, purchased at the price of the sweeping claim that made the tradition famous.

## The rivals at full strength: pluralism and the state as its own actor

Neutrality in this article is not a courtesy extended to opponents after the verdict is safe. The rival accounts of the state are the majority position in political science, they rest on serious evidence, and several of the Marxist positions in the grid exist only because the rivals landed blows that had to be answered. Pluralism first. In the form associated with Robert Dahl's study of New Haven and the wider postwar literature, pluralism holds that power in modern democracies is dispersed among many groups, business among them but not sovereign over them; that different groups prevail on different issues; that elections, parties, unions, churches, professional bodies and movements constitute real counterweights; and that the state is best pictured as an arena and arbiter among contending interests rather than as the executive of any one of them. The evidence is not trivial. Democracies with strong business communities have enacted progressive taxation, welfare states, consumer and environmental protection and labour rights, often over organised business opposition, and have sustained those settlements for generations. Any theory of the state that cannot look at that record without flinching has failed before it starts.

The institutionalist and statist rivals press a different point. Weber's definition of the state by its claim to a monopoly of legitimate physical force within a territory directs attention to what states distinctively are: coercive and administrative organisations with capacities, and incapacities, of their own. The literature that urged scholars, in Theda Skocpol's phrase, to bring the state back in documented officials pursuing goals, state building, war making, extraction, social control, that cannot be read off the preferences of any social class, and explained policy by the inherited capacities of apparatuses as much as by the pressures on them. Marxists will notice, correctly, that the Brumaire got there first; the apparatus with interests of its own is Marx's discovery before it is anyone else's. But the statist literature adds what the Marxist tradition often lacked, a comparative and administrative seriousness about how apparatuses differ, why some can tax and plan and others cannot collect a census, and it does so without needing the apparatus's behaviour to serve a class function in the end. Taken together, the rivals establish a standing challenge: show the mechanism by which class power bends the arena, or concede that the arena metaphor, corrected for the unequal resources of the players, is the better default account. The strongest Marxist work of the last half century can be read as a series of attempts to meet exactly that challenge, and the weakest as a refusal to admit it had been posed.

## The strongest objection: a theory that explains everything explains nothing

The pluralist objection at full strength is not that Marxists are biased, nor that some states sometimes defy business. It is a charge about the logic of the theory itself. States in developed democracies regularly enact policies that capital opposes: taxation that capital funds and resents, labour regulation that raises its costs, antitrust that dismantles its combinations, welfare provision that strengthens workers' refusal of bad jobs. A theory that greets each such policy as further proof of its thesis, the state serving capital's long term interest against its short term wishes, has made itself impossible to lose. If the state serves capital openly, the theory is confirmed. If the state acts against capital and the system survives, the theory is confirmed again, since the action must have been what the system needed. A theory consistent with every possible outcome predicts none of them, and the objection concludes that Marxist state theory, at least in its popular structural form, is not a theory at all but an interpretive habit, a way of redescribing results after they are known.

The objection must be answered at the level at which it is posed, and the honest answer begins with a concession. Against instrumentalism, the objection mostly fails, because instrumentalism makes checkable claims about personnel and influence that can be and have been tested, qualified and in places refuted; the objection's real target is undisciplined structuralism, and against that target it succeeds. A structuralism that sorts episodes after the fact deserves the charge. The defensible structural claim is narrower and can be stated as a prediction about asymmetry rather than a verdict about outcomes. It predicts that the range of policies a capitalist state can durably pursue is systematically skewed: measures compatible with continued private investment proceed through ordinary politics, while measures that threaten the investment process itself, the security of property titles, the expectation of profit, the currency, encounter a distinctive set of sanctions that do not depend on anyone's conspiracy, capital flight, investment strike, bond market punishment, fiscal crisis, and that these sanctions operate whoever is in office and whatever the personnel believe. It predicts, in other words, asymmetry in what is politically feasible, not uniformity in what governments do, and it can be wrong: a state that durably socialised investment, or durably ignored those sanctions without breakdown, would falsify it. Whether the historical record shows the asymmetry, or shows democratic publics simply choosing within a genuinely open range, is an empirical argument, and it is the argument the structural dependence literature tried to conduct properly.

That literature deserves its paragraph because it is where the tradition's claims met their most rigorous testing. Charles Lindblom, no Marxist, argued in Politics and Markets that business occupies a privileged position in democratic politics because officials depend on its performance for their own success: investment decisions are private, their consequences are public, and governments therefore court business confidence as a condition of governing at all, a mechanism he called the automatic punishing recoil of the market. Fred Block modelled the constraint through the concept of business confidence and the division between a ruling class that need not rule directly and state managers whose own institutional interest in revenue and stability aligns them with accumulation. Adam Przeworski and Michael Wallerstein formalised the structural dependence of the state on capital in models where the constraint could be examined rather than asserted, and where its strength varied with institutions, exactly as a scientific claim should. The verdict of that research programme is mixed in the way real verdicts are. The constraint is real, measurable and variable; it is not a wall but a gradient, steeper in some institutional settings and decades than in others, and democratic politics retains genuine room within it. Marxist state theory survives the pluralist objection in the form of that gradient. It does not survive as the committee, and its popular defenders do it no service by pretending the narrower, tested claim and the sweeping slogan are the same theory.

## Theory and record: the objection from the tradition's own states

The second objection comes not from the theory's rivals but from the twentieth century, and neutrality requires it at full strength. Whatever the correct theory of the capitalist state, the tradition that produced it also produced states that ruled in its name, and those states did not wither, did not subordinate their officials to society on the Commune pattern, and did not obviously serve the class in whose name they governed. They built apparatuses larger and less accountable than the ones Marx analysed, staffed them with a stratum whose privileges were real though differently constituted, and treated the Commune's measures, revocability, workman's wages, the armed people, as antiques. A critic is entitled to ask whether a tradition whose political theory was so comprehensively inverted by its own political practice can be trusted about the state at all, and to suspect that the missing volume conceals not an accident of biography but a genuine blindness: a theory rich in the critique of other people's states and empty where its own would have to be designed.

The separation this article has practised from its first page is the beginning of the reply, and it is a separation with teeth in both directions. A theory of the capitalist state is a claim about a specific object, states embedded in societies where production is privately owned and accumulation is privately decided, and it stands or falls on evidence about that object. The Soviet and similar states were not that object; they arose where capitalist development was weak or destroyed, under conditions of scarcity, encirclement and war that the classical texts did not analyse, and their apparatuses are evidence about a different problem, the dynamics of a post capitalist bureaucracy, on which the classical corpus is genuinely thin, the Commune sketch and the Gotha principles apart. But the separation cannot be used as a laundering device. The tradition's record is evidence about the tradition: about what its concepts failed to anticipate, above all the possibility that an apparatus built to abolish class rule could consolidate interests of its own more durable than the classes it displaced, a possibility the Brumaire's analysis of bureaucratic independence contained in embryo and the tradition largely declined to apply to itself. The fair conclusion is disciplinary. Marxist theories of the capitalist state should be judged as theories of the capitalist state, neither credited with the achievements nor charged with the crimes of regimes outside their object, and the tradition should be charged, separately and heavily, with its failure to build the missing volume's other half: a theory of the post capitalist state, its dangers and its safeguards, for which the Commune's nine weeks were never going to be enough.

## The missing institutional account

The third objection is quieter than the other two and in the long run may matter more. Suppose every claim in the grid were true of the capitalist state. The tradition would still lack something its rivals possess: an institutional account of what should stand in the state's place, or of how a state committed to social transformation should be organised, checked, staffed and restrained. Liberal political theory, whatever its blindnesses, has spent centuries on constitutions, separations of power, rights against the state, the independence of courts. The Marxist corpus, by the accident of the missing volume and the habit of critique, is long on the pathology of states and short on their design. Its one institutional sketch is the Commune, a wartime improvisation; its transitional formula is a phrase; its treatment of law, of administration, of the permanent civil service a socialist government would inherit on its first morning, is scattered to the point of absence. Critics from within the tradition, and not only its enemies, have argued that this absence was not innocent in its consequences: movements trained to see the state as an instrument to be seized or a machine to be smashed arrived in power with no theory of how to run one justly, and improvised, and the improvisations hardened.

The reply available to the tradition is real but partial. Part of the absence is principled: Marx refused to write recipes for the cook shops of the future, holding that institutional forms would be discovered in struggle as the Commune had been, and that a blueprint drawn in advance by a theorist would be a utopian imposition on people entitled to invent their own emancipation. Part of it is the division of labour the corpus actually exhibits: the theory of the capitalist state is a critical science, and its job is to explain the object that exists, not to draft its successor. But the refusal has a price that honesty should record. A principle that forms must be discovered in struggle is a poor comfort when the discovery goes wrong, and the tradition's later writers, from the Austro Marxists worrying over administration to modern theorists of participatory institutions, have effectively conceded the objection by doing the design work their founder declined. The article on [marxist state theory today](/2013/04/01/marxist-state-theory-today/) follows those later attempts and their application to concrete politics; here it is enough to mark the shape of the gap, because the gap is the missing volume seen from the other side. Marx planned to analyse the state that existed. He never planned, and no one in the founding generation wrote, the companion volume on the state that ought to.

## What the theories predict about a left government

The grid's prediction column can now be unfolded into the case that gives this subject its permanent urgency. A party is elected on a programme hostile to important capitalist interests: wealth taxes, public ownership of utilities, strong labour rights, capital controls. It takes office with a majority, sincere ministers and a civil service it did not appoint. Each theory in the grid narrates the following years in advance, and the narrations differ in ways that evidence can, in principle, discriminate. The instrumentalist watches the personnel: the ministers' formation, the officials they inherit, the lobbyists at the door, the careers awaiting the compliant after office, and predicts a gradual socialisation of the government into the world it administers, its programme diluted by a thousand reasonable conversations. The structuralist watches the indicators: investment intentions, the currency, the bond spread, the fiscal position, and predicts the discipline arriving impersonally, a crisis of confidence that forces the programme's abandonment or the government's, with the personnel's sincerity making no difference to the timetable. The theorist of autonomy watches the apparatus: the central bank, the courts, the security services, the permanent administration, and predicts friction from institutions with their own doctrines and time horizons, which may sabotage, delay or selectively amplify the programme according to their corporate lights. The strategic relational analyst watches the terrain: which of the state's selectivities the new government can use, which vetoes are positioned where, what strategies the opposition's forces can pursue through the inherited institutions, and predicts an outcome that depends on the government's skill at reshaping the terrain while fighting on it.

History supplies episodes that each narration claims, and the honest treatment of any one of them requires noting the alternative explanations before awarding the point. When a left government retreats from its programme under market pressure, the structuralist claims the gradient; the pluralist replies that the government also faced voters who feared the consequences, allies who defected and promises that were never costed, and that democratic publics punishing economic chaos is not obviously a Marxist datum. When a left government is blocked by courts or central banks, the autonomy theorist claims the apparatus; the institutionalist replies that independent courts and banks were designed to block all governments impartially and regularly block right wing ones too. When ministers emerge from office into boardrooms, the instrumentalist claims the network; the sceptic asks whether the causation ran the other way, governments recruiting the already eminent. These counter readings are not pedantry. They are the difference between a theory that earns its confirmations and a story that collects them, and they are why this article has insisted, from the grid onward, that each position carry its falsifiability conditions on its face. The reader who applies the four narrations to a concrete case, and scores them by what they predicted in advance rather than what they can explain afterwards, is doing the thing the missing volume makes necessary: judging reconstructions as reconstructions, by their performance.

### Why can the fragments not be merged into a single doctrine?

Because they were written for different occasions and pull in different directions. The Manifesto's slogan, the Brumaire's autonomous apparatus, the Commune's counter form and the Gotha notes answer different questions, and reconstructions built from them, instrumentalist, structuralist, autonomy based and relational, contradict one another on mechanism and prediction. A merger has to demote some texts, which is a theoretical act.

## Boundaries: what this article does not settle

Four neighbouring questions press on this one and are deliberately answered elsewhere, and naming them protects the reader from expecting this article to do work it has assigned away. The meaning of the dictatorship of the proletariat, a phrase whose career runs from the Commune through the twentieth century's regimes and back, is separated at [dictatorship of the proletariat](/2013/01/01/dictatorship-of-the-proletariat/). Whether the state withers away, and what Engels and Lenin actually claimed about the fading of public power in a classless society, is treated at [withering away of the state](/2013/01/15/withering-away-of-the-state/). Whether Marxism possesses or lacks a democratic theory, the question the young Marx's distinction between political and human emancipation opens, is taken up at [marxism and democracy critique](/2013/03/15/marxism-and-democracy-critique/). And the application of these theories to the politics of later periods, the terrain on which this article has enforced a strict historical discipline, belongs to [marxist state theory today](/2013/04/01/marxist-state-theory-today/). Around the article's core the companion discipline of keeping a reader's own notes in order is served by the study tools collected at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), where the four positions and their texts can be tabulated in the grid's form for revision and seminar use.

That discipline, historical examples and named mechanisms rather than verdicts on the politics of the present, has governed the article throughout and is worth stating as its closing methodological claim. Any account of the state built from fragments is unusually easy to abuse, because the fragments can be quoted selectively to bless any position the quoter already holds: the committee for the pamphleteer, the Brumaire for the theorist of the strong executive, the Commune for the insurrectionist, Gotha for the scold. The protection against that abuse is the procedure this article has modelled. Name the reconstruction. Name its text. State what it predicts and what would refute it. Keep its rivals, Marxist and pluralist alike, in the room at full strength. And remember, when the argument grows too confident, the fact with which the article began: the volume that would have settled these questions was planned, announced and never written, and everyone who has pronounced on the Marxist theory of the state since 1883, the present article included, has been building in the space it left.

## Gramsci and Althusser: the state widened

Two further figures reshaped the field between the classical fragments and the Miliband and Poulantzas debate, and although each has a fuller treatment elsewhere in this series, no map of state theory is complete without their coordinates. Antonio Gramsci, writing his notebooks in a fascist prison in the late 1920s and 1930s, argued that the state in the developed West could not be understood as the coercive apparatus alone. Around the apparatus of force stood the institutions of civil society, schools, churches, newspapers, associations, in which the dominant class's worldview was organised into common sense and consent was manufactured daily. His formula of the state as political society plus civil society, dictatorship armoured by hegemony, explained what the committee formula could not: why the ruling order survived crises that a mere instrument would not have survived, because its rule was exercised as leadership before it was exercised as domination. The consequence for state theory is that the boundary of the state becomes a research question rather than a definition. Where does the state end and society begin, if the school's curriculum and the newspaper's framing do the work of the policeman by other means?

Louis Althusser, whose account of ideology and the ideological state apparatuses is treated in full at the article on Althusser and the ISAs in this series, formalised a version of Gramsci's widening in 1970. He distinguished the repressive state apparatus, army, police, courts, administration, functioning by violence, from the ideological state apparatuses, educational, religious, familial, legal, political, cultural, functioning by ideology, and argued that the school had replaced the church as the dominant ideological apparatus of capitalist societies. For state theory proper, Althusser's contribution is double-edged. He gave the structuralist generation its vocabulary of apparatuses and its insistence that the reproduction of labour power and of submission to the ruling ideology happens inside institutions that look private, which permanently enlarged the subject. But his functionalism was even more exposed to the objection pressed throughout this article: if every institution that reproduces the order counts as part of the state, and every outcome counts as reproduction, the concept of the state inflates until it explains everything and discriminates nothing. The strategic relational school can be read as an attempt to keep Althusser's enlarged map while restoring the discriminations his functionalism dissolved, which is one more instance of the field's characteristic motion: each generation inherits a widening and then pays for it with a narrowing.

## The fiscal skeleton: why dependence is not a metaphor

Structural dependence is often stated as a slogan and deserves to be stated as plumbing. A modern state does not produce what it consumes. Its revenue arrives as taxes on incomes, profits, sales and property generated by a privately organised economy, and as borrowing that lenders grant against expectations of that revenue's future flow. Its expenditure, on salaries, pensions, debt service, procurement, is continuous and politically rigid; its revenue is cyclical and behaviourally sensitive. When private investment falls, revenue falls with a lag, unemployment expenditure rises, the deficit widens, the currency and the bond market reprice the state's promises, and every one of these events arrives at the cabinet table as an objective emergency requiring no villain and no telephone call from a boardroom. The government that wishes to move against capital's interests must therefore finance its programme through the very process its programme proposes to discipline, and must persuade holders of mobile wealth to fund the state while it legislates against the conditions of their enrichment. This is not a conspiracy theory. It is an accounting identity with political consequences, and its strength varies with everything the strategic relational analyst studies: the openness of the capital account, the currency's reserve status, the depth of domestic savings, the structure of the debt.

Stating the mechanism as plumbing also states its limits, which the rhetorical versions obscure. Dependence is on investment in general, not on any investor; a state facing a fragmented business community, or commanding a resource rent, or borrowing in its own reserve currency from its own citizens, stands on a gentler gradient than a small open economy borrowing abroad in foreign currency. Dependence also runs in both directions over time. Capital depends on the state for the legal order of property and contract, for educated and healthy labour, for infrastructure, for the lender of last resort in its crises, dependencies that governments have periodically converted into leverage. The structuralist claim, disciplined by its best literature, is not that the state is capital's prisoner. It is that the two are locked in an asymmetric dependence in which one side's sanction, withholding the investment on which everyone's revenue depends, operates automatically and impersonally, while the other side's sanctions must be organised, legislated and defended. Asymmetry of that kind, graded and variable, is a long way from the committee. It is also a long way from nothing, and the distance between those two judgements is the space in which serious state theory now lives.

## Fractions, blocs and the organisation of a class that cannot organise itself

A recurring weakness of the committee formula is its picture of the bourgeoisie as a committee ready class, unified, self aware and capable of issuing instructions. The historical bourgeoisies of the record were nothing of the kind. They were divided by sector, industrial against financial, export against domestic, large against small; by region; by confession and party; by time horizon, the speculator against the dynast; and their members' rational pursuit of individual advantage routinely damaged the class's collective position, in wage cutting that destroyed its own markets, in competitive overproduction, in resistance to the regulation that would have stabilised its order. Poulantzas built his concept of the power bloc on this observation. The dominant class, he argued, does not and cannot organise its own political unity; the fractions' interests are too contradictory. The state performs the organisation for it, constituting an unstable equilibrium of compromises among the fractions under the leadership of a hegemonic fraction, while simultaneously disorganising the dominated classes. On this account the state's relative autonomy is not a favour granted to capital but the very condition of capital's political existence as a class, since a state captured by one fraction would shatter the bloc and a state glued to the class's average opinion would reproduce its incoherence.

The concept repays attention because it generates discriminating expectations. It predicts that state policy will systematically advantage the hegemonic fraction of a period, heavy industry in one settlement, finance in another, at the expense of other fractions that then complain, sincerely, that the state has been captured by their enemies; that crises of the state often register as crises of representation within the bloc, when no fraction can lead and the equilibrium of compromises breaks; and that the dominated classes appear in the state's calculations as a presence to be disorganised or partially incorporated, through concessions addressed to them as citizens, consumers or regions rather than as a class. Miliband's sociology meets this account on partly common ground, since his state elite can be studied as the personnel through which a bloc's equilibrium is staffed, and the two men's quarrel was sharpest precisely where their objects overlapped. The concept's weakness is the family weakness of structuralism: fractions are easy to identify after a policy succeeds and hard to specify before it does, and the analyst who can always find the fraction a policy served has purchased coherence at the price the pluralist objection names.

## A worked case: factory legislation and the education of a theory

Theories earn their keep on cases, and the factory acts of the nineteenth century remain the cleanest laboratory the subject possesses, because the documentary record is rich, the interests are legible and Marx himself worked on the evidence as an investigator of the system he theorised. Through the first half of the century, British mill owners resisted statutory limits on the working day with the argument that the last hour of labour was the source of all profit and its regulation would ruin the industry. The Ten Hours Act of 1847 and its successors were carried nonetheless, by a coalition that no simple class map predicts: landed aristocrats avenging themselves on the manufacturers who had defeated them over the corn laws, Tory philanthropists, medical officers, factory inspectors whose reports built the evidentiary record, and the organised pressure of the workers themselves in the short time movement. The state that resulted was not the manufacturers' committee; it had legislated against the manufacturers' declared and organised will, and the manufacturers' prophets of ruin were refuted by the industry's subsequent prosperity.

Now run the grid over the case. The instrumentalist notes the personnel: a parliament still dominated by land, a state elite for whom the millocracy were parvenus, inspectors recruited from a professional stratum with its own ethos, and explains the outcome by the composition of the governing world, in which the industrial fraction had wealth before it had standing. The structuralist notes the function: unlimited extraction was consuming the labour power on which the system depended, children stunted, workers dying at ages the inspectors tabulated, and the state, acting for capital as a whole against capital in particular, imposed the conditions of the system's reproduction, while the Act's very form, universal, legal, apparently above class, performed the ideological work of presenting a class settlement as the nation's health. The autonomy theorist notes the inspectors themselves, an apparatus acquiring expertise, jurisdiction and momentum, drafting the reports that made further legislation imaginable, a machine educating its masters. The strategic analyst notes the terrain: a state whose parliamentary form allowed an alliance of land and labour against industry that a different constitution of access would have filtered out. The case does not crown a winner. It does something better: it shows each theory illuminating a stratum of the same events and failing at the others, which is what a map of rival reconstructions is for, and it shows why the tradition's argument has lasted a century without the dishonesty of pretending the case settles it.

## How the executive of a parliamentary republic fits the committee formula

The committee formula speaks of the executive, and parliamentary republics are the case where its grammar is most often tested, since there the executive emerges from a legislature, answers to it and falls when it loses its confidence. The formula's defenders reply that this changes the wiring, not the current: a cabinet drawn from a parliament elected on a property weighted franchise, in the nineteenth century case, or dependent in the modern case on the confidence of markets and media concentrated in few hands, still manages the common affairs of the possessing class, and the need to manage them in common, settling the fractions' quarrels, disciplining the short sighted, funding the shared infrastructure of coercion and consent, is exactly what a committee exists for. The formula's critics reply that the reply proves too much. An executive that must win elections under universal suffrage, bargain with unions, survive a free press and answer for the price of bread is subject to pressures the word committee cannot see, and its frequent defeats of business on taxation, regulation and welfare are not committee work in any recognisable sense. Between these, the discriminating analyst asks the grid's questions of the specific republic: who staffs the executive and its surrounding apparatus, what fiscal and monetary constraints bind it, which fractions does its settlement advantage, what would it observably fail to do even when its sincerest members wish to. The formula survives the exercise as a question, which is perhaps the most that should ever have been claimed for an eleven word sentence from a pamphlet: not the theory of the state, but the first question a theory of the state must answer, why the executive so rarely surprises the class whose world it administers.

## Does the welfare state disprove the theory?

No question in the query traffic around this subject recurs more stubbornly, and it deserves a direct hearing because it is the pluralist objection in its most concrete dress. The welfare states of the postwar democracies taxed capital at rates the committee formula would not predict, decommodified health, education and income maintenance to a degree that plainly strengthened workers against employers, and were built and defended by parties that business frequently opposed. If the state is capital's instrument or capital's structure, the objection asks, why did it spend three decades building institutions its supposed master disliked? The structural answer has been given above and can be summarised honestly: welfare states also reproduced a healthy, educated labour force, stabilised demand, insured the system against its own casualties and were built under the pressure of organised labour and the shadow of an alternative system to the east, so that their construction is evidence of the state's room for manoeuvre under pressure, not of its independence from the order it administered; and their partial dismantling once the pressure relaxed is, on the same account, the gradient reasserting itself. The critic's rejoinder is equally available: a settlement that lasted a generation, transformed life chances and was defended by electorates is being absorbed into the theory as a detour, and the absorption is exactly the unfalsifiable move. The welfare state therefore stands in this article where the evidence puts it, as the strongest single exhibit for the pluralist and the most instructive hard case for the Marxist, a case on which undisciplined versions of the theory cheat and disciplined versions, the ones that predict asymmetry and variation rather than omnipotence, can be tested and have been.

## How reconstructions are built: a short method for readers

Since the missing volume makes every reader of this subject a builder whether they wish to be or not, the building operations deserve to be made explicit, because seeing them is the only defence against inheriting someone else's construction disguised as Marx's doctrine. There are five operations, and every theory in the grid performs all five, well or badly. Selection: the builder chooses which fragments are load bearing, the Manifesto or the Brumaire, the early critique or the late notes, and the choice is usually announced as fidelity. Weighting: within the chosen fragments, some sentences become the theory and others become rhetoric, occasion or juvenilia, and the demoted passages are rarely argued with, only classified. Extension: the fragments are applied to objects their author never faced, mass democracy, the welfare state, the central bank, the global firm, and each application is a fresh claim wearing an old name. Supplementation: the gaps are filled from other sources, Weber on bureaucracy, Gramsci on consent, Keynesian economics on the fiscal state, and the supplements do more work the further the object stands from 1875. And immunisation, the disreputable operation: the passages that contradict the construction are explained as irony, development or depth, until the construction can no longer lose.

None of the first four operations is avoidable, and none is discreditable when it is owned. The reader's discipline is to perform them consciously: to say, this account is built on the Brumaire weighted above the Manifesto, extended to the age of universal suffrage, supplemented from the fiscal literature, and its builder's name is attached. That sentence, which this article has tried to make easy to say about each row of its grid, is the practical form of the missing volume finding. It converts authority into attribution. Where a claim can no longer borrow the founder's prestige and must stand on its evidence, the theory of the state becomes an ordinary, arguable, improvable branch of social science, which is what its best practitioners always treated it as and what its worst defenders, clinging to the prestige, prevented it from becoming.

The same five operations audit the present article, and it is only fair to submit it to them in print. Its selection weights the Brumaire and the Commune address above the early manuscripts, on the stated ground that state theory needs the texts where an apparatus is actually analysed. Its weighting demotes the committee formula to a question, which is a contestable choice and has been argued rather than assumed, at the length the formula's fame demands. Its extensions run to the welfare state, the postcolonial state and the transnational firm, objects the fragments never faced, and those sections should be read as the article's own claims, not as Marx's. Its supplements are Weber, the fiscal historians and the responsiveness literature, each flagged where it enters. And its immunisations are for the reader to catch, armed with the grid and the falsifiability column, which exist in the article precisely so that its own constructions can be refused with the same ease as the ones it refuses. A pillar that teaches a method should expect to be measured by it, and this one is content to be. Readers who apply the operations elsewhere will find the exercise habit forming: textbooks, lectures and polemics on this subject sort themselves quickly once the questions are learned, whose fragments, weighted how, extended where, supplemented from what, protected against which evidence. The sorting does not settle the argument about the state. It restores the argument to the condition in which it can be settled, by evidence, one case and one mechanism at a time, which is all that any reader was ever entitled to ask of a book that does not exist.

## Studying a state: what counts as evidence

A closing word on evidence, since the article has demanded falsifiability from every row and owes the reader an account of what could supply it. The instrumentalist's evidence is prosopographical and documentary: biographies of office holders, network data on interlocking directorates, records of lobbying and appointment, and its characteristic failure is to mistake access for outcome, which is why the strongest instrumentalist work pairs the sociology with policy histories showing the access converting into decisions that would otherwise have gone the other way. The structuralist's evidence is comparative and counterfactual: matched cases where similar governments under similar pressures chose differently and paid measurably different prices, episodes where the corrective mechanisms can be timed, investment falling before the policy changed and in response to its announcement, and its characteristic failure is the post hoc redescription the pluralist objection targets. The autonomy theorist's evidence is institutional: the doctrines, budgets, recruitment and time horizons of apparatuses, the paper trail of friction between an elected government and its own machine. The strategic analyst's evidence is the archive of strategies themselves, party programmes, treasury submissions, the memoirs in which actors explain which doors they found open and which locked. A researcher entering the field could do worse than choose the case first, a single state in a single crisis, and interrogate it with all four kits in sequence, recording where they converge and where they force different archives open. Convergence across rival kits is the nearest thing this subject has to proof, and divergence is its research programme.

## What any theory of the state must explain

Before the rival accounts are judged, the job description they compete for should be fixed, because part of the field's confusion comes from theories answering different examinations while claiming the same title. Any adequate theory of the state in capitalist society must explain at least six things, and the grid's rows can be scored by how many of the six they handle without strain. It must explain the state's form: why political power in this kind of society is organised as a separate, specialised, apparently universal public authority rather than fused with economic power as it was when the lord held court on his own land and the guild regulated its own trade. It must explain the state's personnel and their recruitment, and how much difference the answer makes. It must explain the state's agenda, why some questions reach it as decisions and others never arrive. It must explain outcomes over time, the long continuities that survive changes of government and the genuine ruptures that occasionally break them. It must explain variation, why states embedded in similar economies differ so much in capacity, generosity and coerciveness. And it must explain change of form itself, the moments when the apparatus is rebuilt, expanded or, as in 1871, briefly imagined out of existence.

Scored against that list, the rows divide their labour in a way the polemics usually hide. Instrumentalism is strongest on personnel and agenda, weakest on form and variation, since similar elites preside over very different states and different elites over similar ones. Structuralism is strongest on form and long continuity, weakest on variation and rupture, since a constraint that everywhere operates explains nowhere in particular. The autonomy model is strongest on the apparatus's behaviour in crisis and weakest as a general account, being a theory of exceptional conditions that its admirers keep stretching into a theory of normal ones. The strategic relational approach is strongest on variation and change, since selectivities and strategies differ by construction from place to place and period to period, and weakest on the first question, the gross asymmetry of the whole field, which it can register case by case without ever quite naming as a single fact. Pluralism, the rival, is strongest on agenda and on the real dispersion of victories across issues, weakest on the boundaries of the agenda itself, on why the deepest questions about property so rarely reach decision at all. A reader who keeps the six questions in view will find the literature sorting itself: most celebrated refutations consist of showing that a theory fails a question it never attempted, and most genuine advances consist of a theory learning to answer one more question without losing the ones it had.

## The derivation debate: reading the state off the form of value

One further reconstruction deserves its place in the record, because it tried to solve the missing volume problem by the boldest available method: deriving the state from the economic categories themselves. In the West Germany of the 1970s, a school of theorists undertook what they called the derivation of the state, arguing from the logic of the commodity form. Where production is organised through exchange between formally free and equal owners of commodities, they reasoned, the guarantee of property and contract cannot be left to the exchangers themselves, since each is a competitor of the others; a separate instance, standing above the individual capitals and agents, must secure the general conditions of exchange, and that instance is the state, whose form, legal, general, apparently class neutral, mirrors the form of the value relation it protects. The Soviet theorist Evgeny Pashukanis had sketched a kindred argument from the legal form in the 1920s before perishing in the purges, and the derivation school, often without knowing him, rebuilt it with the mature Marx's categories.

The attempt matters here less for its conclusions than for what it reveals about the corpus. Derivation is the reconstruction that takes the six book plan most literally: if the state was to be treated after capital, landed property and wage labour, then perhaps its theory can be recovered by continuing the ascent from the abstract categories, letting the political form emerge where the economic analysis requires it. The critics, including sympathetic ones, identified the price. A derivation from the form of value yields a state of magnificent generality and almost no content: it explains why some separate public power must exist in a commodity society and is silent about parliaments and presidencies, welfare and warfare, the difference between Denmark and a dictatorship. History, which the Brumaire never forgets, drains out of the account, and with it every discriminating prediction. The school's own later work conceded much of this, moving toward the historical and institutional analysis it had hoped to bypass. Its episode stands in this article as the limiting case of textual fidelity: the reconstruction that follows the founder's plan most faithfully demonstrates, by its emptiness at the concrete level, why the plan could not have been executed from the categories alone, and why Marx's own attempts at state analysis were always, irreducibly, works of history.

## Lenin's State and Revolution: the reconstruction that made history

No survey of this field can omit the one reconstruction that changed the world, and its omission from most textbook grids is itself a datum about how the tradition edits its past. In the summer of 1917, in hiding from the provisional government that sought his arrest, Lenin wrote The State and Revolution, an attempt to recover what he took to be the authentic Marxist doctrine of the state from the fragments, against the orthodox social democracy that he believed had falsified it. His assembly choices are the clearest in the whole literature because he states them: the load bearing texts are the Commune address, with its verdict that the ready made state machinery cannot be wielded but must be broken, the Gotha notes, with the transitional dictatorship, and Engels on origins and withering. From these he built a doctrine of stark simplicity. The state is a machine for the suppression of one class by another. The bourgeois machine must be smashed, not captured. Its replacement is the Commune form generalised: officials elected and revocable, paid workmen's wages, the standing army replaced by the armed people, administration simplified until every cook could govern, and the whole apparatus already, in its design, a state in the process of ceasing to be one.

As scholarship the book is a partisan reconstruction and knows it; as politics it armed a revolution, and the history that followed is the hardest evidence this article has to handle. The state that emerged from that revolution did not follow the design. The revocable officials, the workmen's wages, the armed people, the withering: within a few years the apparatus had grown beyond anything the tsars maintained, a single party monopolised it, and the man who had written that the state must be smashed presided, under the pressures of invasion, famine and collapse, over the construction of the most comprehensive state machine in Europe. Defenders of the reconstruction distinguish the doctrine from the circumstances and the doctrine from its author's successors; critics reply that a theory of the state which cannot anticipate what its own seizure of power would build has failed at exactly the point where the missing volume's absence cost most. This article does not adjudicate that quarrel, which belongs to the history of the twentieth century. It registers the structural lesson its pillar exists to teach. Lenin's book is the missing volume problem in its most consequential form: a brilliant reader, under revolutionary pressure, assembled the fragments into the theory he needed, and the assembly, not any text of Marx, governed. Every reconstruction in the grid is the same operation performed at lower stakes, which is why the operation should always be visible.

## War, taxation and the making of states

The Marxist accounts in the grid share a blind spot that a rival tradition of scholarship, working quite outside Marxism, has made impossible to ignore, and a complete guide must let the blind spot show. States were not only, perhaps not even primarily, made by class relations in the domestic sense. They were made by war and the preparation for war. The fiscal military history of Europe runs on a rhythm that class analysis alone does not generate: rulers who needed armies needed taxes, taxes needed bureaucracies, bureaucracies needed records, credit and law, and the states that extracted most efficiently survived the competition of the battlefield while hundreds of others disappeared. The slogan associated with the historical sociologist Charles Tilly, that war made the state and the state made war, names a mechanism in which the state's growth is driven by interstate rivalry and coercion rather than by the needs of accumulation, and in which the classes enter as the taxed, the conscripted and the creditors rather than as the authors of the script.

The honest Marxist response is not denial, since the corpus contains the materials for absorption: Engels himself rooted public power partly in military organisation, and the Brumaire's apparatus is unthinkable without the wars that built it. The response is to ask what the fiscal military mechanism leaves unexplained. War making states still had to decide whose property the taxes fell on, which classes supplied the officers and which the ranks, what the apparatus did between wars in the regulation of industry, land and labour, and why, across centuries of similar military pressure, states settled into such different relations with their economies. Those are the questions on which the grid's theories work, and the two research traditions are better read as addressing different layers of the same object than as competitors for a single crown. But the encounter disciplines the Marxist side permanently. A theory of the state that begins with capital must explain why the state's skeleton was substantially built before capital ruled, and why its growth spurts track the battlefield as faithfully as the business cycle. The derivation school could not answer that. The Brumaire, with its inherited machine perfected by every regime, already half contains the answer, which is one more reason the autonomy row of the grid has aged better than the slogan row.

## The state beyond the heartlands: colony and periphery

The fragments were written about Europe, with America as an occasional comparison, and the grid inherits that geography unless it is deliberately corrected. Yet most states in the modern world were formed under conditions the classical texts never faced: as colonial administrations designed for extraction and control over populations denied citizenship, or as postcolonial inheritances in which the apparatus was built by an occupying power and bequeathed, intact, to governments ruling societies whose class structures had been deformed by the bequest. Writers in the Marxist tradition who confronted those states, among them Hamza Alavi with his account of the overdeveloped postcolonial state, argued that the inherited apparatus in such societies is disproportionately large and autonomous relative to the indigenous classes, which are too weak and divided to subordinate it, so that the military bureaucratic oligarchy mediates among several propertied classes, metropolitan capital included, from a position of strength no Brumairean balance quite predicts. Whatever the verdict on that specific thesis, the general correction stands. Relative autonomy in the heartlands was a discovery that had to be fought for against a slogan. In much of the world it is the obvious starting point, and the committee formula reads there not as an error but as an irrelevance, since the committee was never local.

The colonial case also returns the article to Engels with a complication he would have recognised. If states arise with class division and present themselves as standing above society, the colonial state presents the formula in caricature: a power frankly external to the society it rules, organised for another society's accumulation, moderating nothing. Its successor states inherited not only the apparatus but the problem of legitimacy that the apparatus embodied, and much of their subsequent history, the coups, the developmental dictatorships, the states that could coerce everything and administer nothing, is the history of that inheritance. No account of the state adequate to its object in the debates inherited by the twenty first century can treat the European parliamentary case as the norm and the rest as deviation. The grid survives the journey if its rows are read as questions rather than as European answers: who staffs this state, on what revenues does it rest, what balances among classes and foreign powers hold it up, what does its inherited form make easy and what impossible. Asked in Lagos or Lahore with the same discipline as in London, the questions are the tradition's genuine universalism, the part of the missing volume that later generations have had to write for themselves, in circumstances its author never saw.

## Law, courts and the form of legality

The state theories of the grid speak mostly of executives, bureaucracies and armies, and a reader could finish them without noticing the institution through which most citizens most often meet the state: the law. Marxist writing on law has its own home in this series, but the connection to state theory is too structural to leave implicit, because the legal form is one of the strongest exhibits in the structuralist case and one of the hardest for every account to digest. The law of a capitalist society addresses its subjects as abstract persons: free, equal, owners of themselves and of whatever else they own, entitled to contract and to the protection of the courts without regard to station. That abstraction is not a lie pasted over inequality. It is the real form in which the society's exchanges occur, since the labourer and the employer do meet as legal equals in the contract that joins them, and its reality is precisely what makes it effective. The young Marx's distinction returns here with legal dress: the equality is genuine in the heaven of the law and compatible with radical inequality on the earth of the factory, and a state that ruled by open class decree would actually serve its economy worse than one that rules through general norms impersonally applied.

This is the soil in which Pashukanis grew his theory, mentioned above, that the legal form itself corresponds to the commodity form, the subject of law mirroring the owner of commodities, so that law reaches its fullest development in bourgeois society and would fade with the relations that sustain it. The thesis has been battered, for its functionalism, for its silence about criminal law and the family, for its author's terrible fate lending it a pathos that substitutes for argument, and it remains the boldest attempt to derive a state institution from the economic form rather than from personnel or pressure. For the grid its lesson is double. To the instrumentalist it poses the question why capital should prefer a legal order that regularly rules against its members, protecting tenants, debtors, employees and even revolutionaries' speech with the same impersonal machinery, if the state were simply its instrument; the plausible answer, that predictable general rules are worth more to accumulation than any particular victory, is a structuralist answer wearing instrumentalist clothes. To the structuralist it poses the reverse question: if the legal form is functional for the system, why do its norms so often acquire a life of their own, invoked successfully against the powerful by movements the system would rather suppress, so that the form constrains its supposed beneficiaries as well as its victims? The rule of law, on the strongest reading the tradition has produced, is neither a sham nor a sanctuary. It is a terrain, to borrow Jessop's word, whose generality is both its service to the existing order and its permanent availability to that order's opponents, and any theory of the state that flattens it in either direction has failed to explain the institution citizens actually inhabit.

## Parties and elections: the transmission belts examined

Between the classes of the theory and the state of the grid stand the organisations that claim to connect them, the parties, and the mechanisms that periodically reassign office, the elections, and each theory reads these transmission belts in its own image. The instrumentalist reads the party system as a market in which capital holds the strongest currencies: the finance of campaigns, the ownership of media, the socialisation of candidates through careers that filter out the unreliable long before any voter is consulted, so that the choice offered at elections is real but pre cleared, a selection among managers of the same settlement. The structuralist is comparatively indifferent to who wins, and this indifference is his signature: elections decide the personnel of a constrained office, and their function in his account is as much legitimation as selection, periodically renewing the state's claim to embody a universal will that its structure prevents it from exercising against the order that sustains it. The autonomy theorist watches the moments when the belts slip: when a plebiscitary leader bypasses the parties altogether and rules through the apparatus and the referendum, which is the Bonapartist configuration in modern dress, or when the parties themselves become so fused with the state, funded by it, staffed from it, that they form a cartel defending the machine against society, a diagnosis developed by writers well outside Marxism that reads like a chapter of the Brumaire rewritten for democracies.

The strategic relational reading is the most patient with the detail: parties are strategies made durable, their internal rules and inherited loyalties part of the terrain, and an election is a moment when the balance of forces condensed in the institutions is partially recounted under rules that themselves select, districting, thresholds, campaign finance law, the timetable of parliaments against the timetable of markets. What all four readings share, and what distinguishes them jointly from naive democratic theory, is the refusal to treat the vote as the end of the analysis. The counting of heads settles who governs. The theories compete over what governing then consists in: whose world the governors inhabit, what the revenue permits, what the apparatus allows, what the terrain rewards. A citizen need not accept any of the four to find the shared refusal useful. It is the difference between asking whether an election was fair, a question with an answer, and asking what the fairly elected were then able to do, the question this article exists to keep open.

## Authoritarian statism: Poulantzas and the state in crisis

The last Poulantzas, the Poulantzas of State, Power, Socialism in 1978, confronted a configuration that none of the grid's original rows described, and his name for it has entered the general vocabulary of the field: authoritarian statism. Surveying the advanced democracies of his decade, he saw not the classical exceptional state, no coup, no plebiscitary emperor, but a transformation within the forms of legality: power concentrating in the executive at the expense of legislatures, administration displacing statute, parties declining into transmission belts for decisions taken elsewhere, liberties formally intact and practically narrowed, and the state intervening ever more deeply in economic and social life while the arenas in which its interventions could be contested were being thinned. His explanation was structural in his mature manner: the intensification of the state's role in managing a crisis ridden accumulation required insulating that management from the popular pressures the state's own forms admitted, so the forms themselves were being re engineered, and the materiality of the state, its institutions as a condensation of forces, registered the shift before any constitution announced it.

The diagnosis has had a long afterlife precisely because it names a tendency rather than a regime, and tendencies can be tracked. Its value for the reader of this article is methodological. Poulantzas is here doing the thing the structuralism of 1968 could not do: specifying a direction of institutional change, identifying the mechanisms, executive aggrandisement, the decline of the legislature's real function, the rise of parallel networks within the administration bypassing formal channels, and implying the observations that would count against him, a revival of legislative supremacy, a decompression of the executive, a renewed party mediation. Critics have pressed him on the tendency's supposed irreversibility and on the convenience of a concept that fits both interventionist and market liberal phases of the state, since the strong executive that plans and the strong executive that deregulates can both be filed under his heading. The criticism is the family's old one and it lands where it always lands. But the late work completes the arc this article has traced from the young Marx's bureaucracy to the relational state: a tradition that began by unmasking the state's claim to stand above society ended by producing, in its most sophisticated hands, an analysis of how the state's own material form changes under the pressure of the forces it condenses. That is not the committee. It is nearly its opposite in method, and the distance between them is the history this guide has tried to map.

## The first morning: inheriting the machine

Every theory in the grid is finally tested at a moment none of them describes often enough: the first morning of a government that intends transformation. Before it a minister inherits a building, a budget already three quarters committed by law and contract, a debt schedule, a central bank with its own doctrine, officials whose cooperation is formally owed and substantively discretionary, courts that will review the programme's legality, markets that will price its credibility by the hour, and a public whose patience is the scarcest resource in the building. The theories predict the morning's physics differently, and the predictions can be watched in real time whenever the moment occurs. The instrumentalist expects the machine to socialise the newcomers: the briefings, the courtesies, the gradual discovery that every radical option has been minuted as impossible by someone charming. The structuralist expects the indicators to do the work before the briefings finish. The autonomy theorist expects the permanent institutions to defend their doctrines as the state's own memory. The strategic analyst expects the morning to reveal the terrain's selectivities in their purest form, since a government with a programme is a strategy meeting the institutions that will favour parts of it and filter the rest.

The Commune model exists, one might say, because Marx contemplated that morning and refused its physics. His conclusion of 1871, that the ready made machinery cannot simply be laid hold of, is the first morning problem stated as a verdict: the machine is not neutral equipment awaiting a new driver but a formed institution that will form its drivers, and transformation therefore begins with the apparatus itself or does not begin. The century and a half since has not produced a settled alternative verdict, because the experiment has been run under such different conditions, and with such mixtures of the machine's capture, transformation and revenge, that each theory can find its morning in the record. What the article can leave the reader is the discipline of watching such moments with the grid open: noting which prediction was made in advance, which mechanism actually appeared, and which theory is quietly rewriting its account afterwards. States are most legible in their transitions, and the tradition that made a slogan of the state is best honoured by reading its transitions more carefully than its slogans.

## Measuring the tilt: what empirical research made of the quarrel

The Miliband and Poulantzas debate was conducted at the level of method, and a later generation of empirical researchers, many of them indifferent or hostile to Marxism, effectively ran parts of it as a research programme with data. Their work deserves a place in this article because it changed what the argument is about. Studies of policy responsiveness in the United States, of which Martin Gilens's Affluence and Influence is the best known, compared the preferences of citizens at different income levels with the policies subsequently adopted, and reported a pattern that neither camp of 1970 had quite predicted: when the preferences of the affluent and the preferences of the middle and the poor diverged, policy tracked the affluent closely and the others hardly at all, while on the many questions where preferences coincided across classes, policy followed the majority as pluralism expects. Business oriented research reached cognate findings from the other side: organised business loses often on particular measures, especially when it is divided or when public attention is high, and wins systematically on the structural questions, taxation of capital, the legal framework of the firm, the openness of markets, that decide the terrain on which particular measures are fought.

Read through the grid, this literature is a partial vindication scattered across the rows, which is what mature evidence usually looks like. The instrumentalists were right that access and resources tilt outcomes, and the mechanisms were measured rather than asserted. The structuralists were right that the tilt is deepest exactly where the accumulation process itself is at stake, and weakest on the symbolic and distributive margins where democratic politics enjoys real freedom. The pluralists were right that the affluent are not a class in the Marxist sense, being divided on most questions, and that majorities prevail when they agree, which is most of the time on most issues. And everyone was wrong about something: the early instrumentalists about how much personnel explains, the early structuralists about how little agency matters, the pluralists about the neutrality of the agenda, since the questions on which the tilt is total are selected before any vote is counted. A student who meets the subject through this literature first sometimes wonders why the older debate matters. It matters because the concepts in which the findings are stated, tilt, terrain, agenda, structural question, were forged in that debate, and because the findings have not dissolved the theoretical problem. They have given it measurements. What kind of fact the tilt is, a fact about people, about positions, about selectivities, remains exactly as contested as it was in the pages of New Left Review, and the contest is the theory.

## Questions from the forum: the deep state and other borrowings

The concepts of this field circulate in public argument in borrowed and degraded forms, and a guide that ignores the borrowings abandons the reader to them. The most successful borrowing of the period is the phrase deep state, and the question whether it is a Marxist idea deserves a direct answer: it is not, though the resemblance that prompts the question is real. The deep state of popular usage is a hidden directorate, intelligence services, permanent officials, unnamed networks, secretly steering the visible government against the people's will. The Marxist accounts in the grid posit almost the opposite kind of object. Their states are not hidden and their mechanisms are not secret instructions; they are visible institutions whose tilt arises from composition, dependence and form, documented in budgets, directories and statutes, and the tradition's whole point is that no conspiracy is required, since a machine built and staffed and financed in the open will reproduce the tilt while every individual inside it behaves honourably. Where the popular phrase does touch the tradition is at the autonomy row: the permanent apparatus's capacity to frustrate an elected government is a genuine phenomenon, analysed by Marx in 1852 and by the statist literature since. But the tradition analyses it as a public structure with interests, not as a cabal, and the difference matters practically, because a structure can be reformed by law and a cabal can only be hunted.

Two other forum questions recur often enough to deserve the article's discipline. The first asks why governments of the left so often disappoint their supporters, and is usually posed as a riddle with a single key. The grid supplies four keys and insists they be tried separately: the personnel were absorbed, the indicators intervened, the apparatus resisted, the terrain filtered, and in any concrete case the honest answer assigns weights among these and names the evidence, while also admitting the explanation no theory in the grid owns, that the programme itself was incoherent, unaffordable or unwanted by the majority whose name it invoked. Disappointment is overdetermined in politics, and a theory that claims all of it has confessed to claiming too much. The second question asks whether the state can be used to end the order that sustains it, the strategic question of the whole tradition. The fragments themselves divide on it: the Gotha notes treat the state as an organ to be subordinated, the Commune address as a machine to be broken, the Brumaire as an inheritance that smothers its inheritors. The grid's modern rows divide correspondingly, the strategic analyst answering that everything depends on the terrain and the strategy, the structuralist that the attempt will meet the gradient, the instrumentalist that it depends who holds the handles. The article's contribution to that question is its founding one. Since no volume settles it, the question must be argued from evidence and mechanism each time it is posed, and anyone who answers it by quoting a sentence of 1848, in either direction, has mistaken the tradition's most famous fragment for its missing book.

## One economy, many states: the problem of scale

The six book plan ended with the world market, and the state volume that precedes it in the plan was already, by that architecture, a partial object: a theory of individual states inside a system none of them contains. Later history sharpened the problem into the form in which it now dominates the field. Production is organised across borders by firms whose supply chains, finances and legal domiciles are chosen from a menu of jurisdictions, while political authority remains stubbornly national, so that each state confronts an accumulation process that can leave. The structural dependence described earlier acquires, in this setting, a mobility premium: the sanction of exit, once a slow matter of disinvestment, becomes a standing option priced into every negotiation between a government and a large firm, and competition among states for the location of investment presses their tax, labour and environmental settlements toward one another in the pattern popularly called a race to the bottom, though the evidence shows the race is run at very different speeds in different lanes and some states decline to run it. Instrumentalism acquires a matching problem of scale: the networks that staff a national state are increasingly formed in transnational circuits, in the universities, firms, funds and forums where a global managerial stratum is made, so that the sociology of the state elite has to be rewritten at a level above the nation the theory was built on.

The tradition's responses divide along the grid's seams, instructively. One line of argument holds that the mismatch is temporary in form: that capital requires political guarantees only states supply, that the largest firms remain anchored in home jurisdictions whose courts, currencies and navies underwrite them, and that reports of the national state's eclipse confuse its transformation with its disappearance, a reading the fiscal record supports, since the taxing and spending shares of the advanced states did not collapse in the decades when their eclipse was most confidently announced. Another line extends the relational approach upward, treating the emerging layer of international institutions, trade regimes, financial standards bodies, arbitration tribunals, as a terrain with its own selectivities, a partial state form above the states, in which the balance of forces is condensed with even less democratic mediation than the national apparatuses provide. A third, older line recalls that the founders expected the mismatch: a system whose productive organisation overflowed every national container was, in their analysis, generating the pressure that would eventually force political forms to its own scale, and the European experiment in pooled sovereignty is sometimes read in this key, as an attempt, halting and contradictory, to build a state sized to its market. The article takes no side among these, beyond noting what their existence proves for its pillar. What was unfinished for one national literature has become, by the growth of its object, a subject that must now also explain the relations among states and the institutions above them. The missing volume has acquired a missing sequel, and the reconstructions multiply accordingly, which is the strongest practical reason for the discipline this article has recommended throughout: name the reconstruction, state its scale, and refuse to let a theory built for one object borrow authority over another.

One consequence of the scale problem deserves emphasis because it reverses the usual direction of the debate. In the classical arguments, the state was the large object and the firm the small one, and the question was how the large object served the interests collected in the small. Where a single firm's investment decision can move a small state's currency, employment and credit rating within a quarter, the sizes have inverted, and the state's managers experience the firm the way an earlier century's firms experienced the state: as a weather system to be forecast, courted and occasionally defied at real cost. The theories survive the inversion better than might be expected, because none of them ever depended on the state's dignity. Instrumentalism becomes a sociology of a transnational elite. Structuralism becomes a claim about mobile capital's discipline over immobile polities, arguably its purest historical form. The autonomy model watches small apparatuses discover how little autonomy a balance of forces grants when one of the forces can emigrate. And the relational approach, built for terrains, finds the largest terrain of all in the layered institutions through which the world market is governed. The grid travels. What does not travel is any excuse for quoting its European evidence as though it were the world's, and the sections on colony and periphery above are this article's payment of that debt.

## Using the grid without merging it

The article has forbidden one move so consistently that the reader is owed the permitted alternative. If the four theories may not be merged into a composite Marxist view, how is anyone to think about an actual state, which will presumably exhibit personnel networks, structural constraints, an apparatus with its own will and a selective terrain all at once? The answer is sequence rather than synthesis. The theories are rival explanations of the same outcomes, but they are not rival descriptions of the same facts, and the working analyst uses them as successive interrogations, each of which may return a different share of the explanation for a given case. Begin with the description all four must accept: the institutions, the revenues, the personnel, the recent outcomes. Then interrogate in order of the questions fixed earlier in this article. The instrumentalist interrogation asks who staffs and who has access, and returns a quantity: the degree to which outcomes track the composition and pressure of identifiable people. The structural interrogation asks what would have happened to any personnel, and returns a second quantity: the width of the corridor within which the first quantity operates. The autonomy interrogation asks where the apparatus departed from every class's script, and the strategic interrogation asks which of the outcomes the terrain had pre selected before the actors arrived.

Reported honestly, the four quantities will rarely sum to a single story, and their failure to sum is information rather than embarrassment. Where the personnel quantity is large and the corridor wide, the case is a genuinely political one in which agency ruled, and the pluralist rival will score it too. Where the corridor is narrow enough to swallow the personnel, the structural claim has earned a confirmation of the disciplined kind. Where the apparatus departs from all scripts, the Brumaire has spoken. What the analyst must never do is average the four into a warm assertion that many factors matter, because that assertion, unlike each of its components, predicts nothing and can be refuted by nothing. The grid is a set of instruments, and instruments are used one at a time, by a hand that knows which one it is holding. That, in the end, is the whole of the missing volume's practical teaching. Marx left no doctrine of the state behind him. He left a set of analyses sharp enough to build rival doctrines from, and the intellectual honesty of everyone who has built since consists in keeping the rivals distinct, the mechanisms named, and the evidence in charge. A tradition that can do that does not need the volume it lost. A tradition that cannot would not have been saved by it.

## For students and exam candidates

The mark carrying move on every state question is the one this article is built around: name several Marxist theories of the state where the question invites one. An answer that opens by noting that Marx planned a book on the state and never wrote it, that the corpus therefore supports at least four distinct reconstructions, and that the committee formula is the most instrumentalist of them and not a summary of the rest, has announced a level of command that markers reward before the argument begins. Learn the grid by its rows, one claim, one mechanism, one weakness each, and learn the textual addresses: Manifesto for the committee, Brumaire for autonomy, the Commune address for the counter form, Gotha for the transition, Engels for origins. In evaluation, never leave a theory without its falsifiability condition, and never present instrumentalism and structuralism as complementary factors; they are rival mechanisms, and saying so, with the debate as your evidence, is worth more than a page of additional content.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The Miliband and Poulantzas exchange is the finest teaching instrument this topic possesses, because it lets students watch two writers who share a conclusion dismantle each other's methods, and a seminar built around the review, the reply and the rejoinder teaches more about social science than a term of methodological generalities. Set the committee formula early, let the class enjoy its clarity, then introduce the Brumaire and ask the formula to survive it; the discomfort that follows is the lesson. Guard against the composite textbook answer, in which instrumental and structural factors are stirred together, by requiring each student to defend one row of the grid against the others for a session, and to state, before any contemporary example is admitted, what evidence would change their mind. Where the Commune is taught, teach it with its problems, siege, improvisation, the gap between decree and implementation, since a model presented without its difficulties invites the cynicism that greets its first contact with the record.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the working legacy of this literature is a set of discriminating questions rather than a doctrine: personnel or structure, discretion or constraint, which fraction, through which institution, over what horizon, and the discipline of the falsifiability column applied to one's own preferred account before it is applied to others. Structural dependence, in its modelled forms, remains the tradition's most productive research frontier precisely because its claims are graded and variable. For historians, the corpus is a reminder that state apparatuses are inherited and path dependent, and that the Brumaire's questions, who built this machine, what does it do when no class can steer it, outlast the regime that prompted them. For journalists, one rule covers most occasions: the committee line is a bad citation. It is a slogan from a revolutionary pamphlet, rejected in its simple form by the major theorists of the tradition it is quoted against, and a report that attributes it to Marxism without qualification has told its readers less than nothing, since it has hidden the argument that constitutes the subject.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Marxist theory of the state?

There is no single doctrine bearing that name, because the book that would have stated it was planned and never written. In its place the tradition holds several reconstructions built from scattered writings: an instrumentalist account in which the state serves the class that staffs and pressures it, a structuralist account in which fiscal and institutional dependence on private accumulation constrains any government, an autonomy model drawn from the analysis of France under Louis Bonaparte, and a later relational account of the state as a terrain favouring some strategies over others. Responsible usage names which of these is meant, gives its textual source, and treats the famous committee phrase as one position among rivals rather than as the whole inheritance.

### Q: Did Marx write a book on the state?

He did not. His scheme for a critique of political economy in six books, drafted at the end of the 1850s, reserved the fourth place for the state, after capital, landed property and wage labour, and that volume was never begun. What survives is dispersed: an 1843 manuscript against Hegel, the pamphlet of 1848, journalism on France, an address defending the Paris Commune, notes on a German party programme, and remarks scattered through the economic manuscripts. Engels attempted the systematic treatment after Marx's death with a book on the state's origins. Every later theory in this field is therefore an assembly from fragments, and the choices made in assembling are part of what a reader is entitled to inspect.

### Q: Is the state just a committee of the ruling class?

The phrase comes from the Manifesto, where the executive of the modern state is called a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie, and it captures the instrumentalist pole of the tradition in its sharpest form. As a complete account it fails on the tradition's own evidence: Marx's analysis of France describes an apparatus that dismissed the bourgeoisie's parliament and ruled over its makers, and later theorists showed that states regularly tax, regulate and prosecute the class they supposedly serve. The sentence is best used as an opening question about why executives so seldom act against the possessing class's settled interests, not as a conclusion that forecloses the asking.

### Q: What is the difference between instrumentalist and structuralist theories of the state?

The two accounts locate the explanation in different places. Instrumentalism looks at hands: the social origins of ministers and officials, their schooling, their club and boardroom connections, the pressure organised interests bring to bear, and it expects outcomes to change when the personnel change. Structuralism looks at position: a government of any composition must raise revenue from an economy it does not own, preserve the confidence of those who decide investment, and operate institutions built around the separation of political citizenship from economic power, and it expects those constraints to reproduce similar outcomes through different hands. The celebrated quarrel between Miliband and Poulantzas turned on this contrast, and treating the two as one theory with two names dissolves the very disagreement that made the debate instructive.

### Q: What is relative autonomy of the state?

The expression describes a bounded independence. A capitalist state is not steered hour by hour by the class that benefits from it; its officials can refuse individual firms, impose unwelcome regulation, and pursue projects of the apparatus itself, sometimes for long periods. The bounds are set by dependence: revenue, employment and political survival all ride on an accumulation process in private hands, so autonomy that threatens that process summons sanctions which reassert the limit. Poulantzas argued the distance is functional as well as real, because a ruling class divided into competing fractions needs an organiser outside itself to compose its unity and protect its long term interests against its members' short term demands.

### Q: What is Bonapartism?

The term takes its pattern from Louis Bonaparte's seizure of power in France in 1851, analysed by Marx in The Eighteenth Brumaire. Where the contending classes have fought to exhaustion, where the possessing class fears its own parliament more than it fears a usurper, and where the largest class cannot organise its own representation, the executive machine of bureaucracy, army and police can lift itself above society and govern through a plebiscitary leader. The property order is preserved, indeed protected, while every class is politically humiliated. Later writers extended the label to other regimes resting on a similar paralysis, and careful usage demands that the balance producing the regime, and the apparatus's own purposes within it, be specified rather than the word serving as a synonym for strong government.

### Q: Can the state ever act against capitalist interests?

It does so constantly at the level of individual firms and sectors: taxes are levied over business protest, monopolies are dissolved, safety and environmental rules raise costs, executives are prosecuted. The theories diverge on what such episodes prove. Instrumentalists must explain them through divisions among the powerful or pressure from below strong enough to bend the instrument. Structuralists predict them, since the survival of the system can require disciplining its own beneficiaries, and reserve their claim for a narrower test: a state that turns against the conditions of accumulation itself, property security, profit expectation, sound money, should meet impersonal resistance in investment, credit and currency markets. Judged by that test rather than by anecdotes, the claim can be assessed, and has been, with mixed results.

### Q: What was the Miliband and Poulantzas debate?

It was an exchange in New Left Review beginning in 1969, when Poulantzas reviewed Miliband's The State in Capitalist Society and charged that its sociology of elites had adopted the standpoint of the pluralists, asking who governs instead of why the state serves capital regardless of personnel. Miliband replied the following year that Poulantzas had dissolved real decision makers into structures and built a theory no evidence could embarrass. Ernesto Laclau intervened on the question of how classes acquire political identity, and Poulantzas returned with a rejoinder refining his account of class fractions and the power bloc. The exchange remains the standard education in the subject because the two men shared a conclusion and exposed, in attacking each other, that a shared conclusion is not yet a shared theory.

### Q: Where does the state come from according to Engels?

Engels, in his 1884 study of the origin of the family, private property and the state, argued that public power is a historical product rather than a natural fixture. When production generated a surplus and society split into classes with irreconcilable interests, the older order of kinship could no longer hold the antagonism, and a specialised institution arose: officials organised over territory instead of blood, armed bodies distinct from the population, prisons and taxes to sustain them. This power presents itself as a moderator standing over society, and in Engels's judgement it generally secures the dominance of the economically strongest class, with exceptional periods of balance in which it gains wider independence. His ethnological detail has dated; his insistence that the state had a beginning remains the tradition's founding move.

### Q: Is there one Marxist theory of the state or several?

Several, and the number is not a temporary embarrassment awaiting a synthesis. The sources themselves pull in different directions: a pamphlet's slogan about a committee, a journalist's anatomy of an apparatus ruling above exhausted classes, a defence of an insurrectionary city government that abolished the separate machinery altogether, notes insisting the state is a class organ while refusing to describe its successor. Twentieth century writers built honestly from different shelves of that small library and arrived at instrumentalist, structuralist, autonomy based and relational accounts that contradict one another on mechanism and prediction. A synthesis that irons out the contradictions has to demote some texts to rhetoric, and that demotion is itself a theoretical act which should be declared, not smuggled into a textbook paragraph beginning with the words Marxists believe.

### Q: How should a student open an essay on the Marxist theory of the state?

Open with the absence, not with the slogan. A strong first paragraph states that Marx projected a systematic treatment of the state inside a six book plan and never wrote it, that the evidence therefore consists of scattered texts from different decades and genres, and that the essay will distinguish the reconstructions built from them before evaluating any. Then name the reconstructions to be compared, give each its founding text in a clause, and state the criterion the essay will apply, usually the quality of mechanism and the handling of unfriendly evidence. This opening earns credit three ways: it displays textual knowledge, it prevents the marker's most common complaint, the treatment of a contested tradition as a single doctrine, and it gives the remaining paragraphs a structure they can actually follow.

### Q: What evidence would show a state enjoying relative autonomy in a concrete case?

Look for outcomes that run against the declared preferences of the dominant class's organised representatives and persist. The clearest signatures are policies imposed over sustained business lobbying and maintained after the lobbying peaks: a tax reform the peak associations fought for years, a regulatory regime built despite a sector's campaign, a central bank or court repeatedly frustrating the governing class fraction that appointed it. Documentary evidence strengthens the case where officials record purposes of the institution itself, revenue maximisation, administrative order, international prestige, pursued against ministerial as well as business wishes. The claim weakens correspondingly if the supposed autonomy dissolves on inspection into service to a rival fraction, or if every autonomous episode ends the moment it touches investment, which suggests the limit was reached rather than independence demonstrated.

### Q: Why did Marx never finish his planned book on the state?

The reasons were cumulative rather than mysterious. The first book of the scheme, on capital, expanded beyond every estimate and consumed two decades, remaining unfinished itself; the second and third volumes existed only as manuscripts his executors later assembled. Journalism for a living, the work of the International, recurrent illness and exile's poverty all competed with the desk. There was also a difficulty internal to the design: the state volume stood fourth, after landed property and wage labour, and its method required the economic categories beneath it to be completed first, so each delay in the foundations postponed the superstructure of the plan. By the time of his death in 1883 the materials for several books existed as fragments and notebooks, but the volume on the state had never been begun as a continuous work.

### Q: How does the executive of a parliamentary republic fit the committee formula?

Imperfectly, and the imperfection is informative. A cabinet in a parliamentary republic is drawn from an elected assembly, falls when it loses confidence, and administers through officials it inherits, so the picture of a committee taking instructions from a board of proprietors fits badly at the level of procedure. Defenders of the formula shift its meaning: the executive manages the possessing class's common affairs in the sense of settling its fractions' disputes, guaranteeing property and contract, and maintaining the order within which accumulation proceeds, tasks no individual capitalist can perform. Critics answer that an executive which must survive elections, negotiate with unions and answer for prices is moved by pressures the metaphor cannot register, and that its regular defeats of business preferences are data, not disguises. The formula is most defensible read as a claim about the boundaries of executive action rather than about its daily agenda.

### Q: What should a researcher separate before calling a state policy instrumentalist?

Four things that casual usage merges. First, benefit: a policy may advantage capital without having been caused by capital's action, as weather advantages farmers without their arranging it. Second, access: meetings, donations and shared backgrounds show channels exist, not that anything flowed through them on this occasion. Third, intention: the documents may reveal officials pursuing revenue, order or re-election while telling themselves a public interest story, none of which is a class instruction. Fourth, counterfactual shape: the instrumentalist reading strengthens only where comparable cases with different personnel or weaker channels produced different outcomes. A researcher who logs benefit, access, intention and counterfactual separately, and grades the evidence for each, can say precisely how much of the instrumentalist claim a given episode supports, instead of letting a suggestive network diagram carry a conclusion it cannot bear.
