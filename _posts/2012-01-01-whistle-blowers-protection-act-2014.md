The Whistle Blowers Protection Act occupies a position no other Indian statute quite shares. It was debated and passed by both Houses of Parliament, it received the assent of the President on 9 May 2014, it was published in the Gazette of India on 12 May 2014 as Act 17 of 2014, and it has never operated for a single day. Its first section leaves its commencement to a date the Central Government may appoint by notification, and that notification has never been issued. A person searching for the law that protects someone who reports corruption by a public servant will find this Act described in textbooks, in examination guides and in government statements as though it were a working part of the legal system. It is not. It is a complete protective code, with designated authorities, an inquiry procedure, identity safeguards, anti-victimisation directions and criminal penalties, that exists entirely on paper.

![The Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014 and the protection it was designed to give a person who discloses corruption by a public servant - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-110.webp)

The distinction matters because the question a potential discloser actually asks is not what Parliament enacted but what protection exists when a disclosure is made. Those are different questions in India, and this Act is the reason they are different. The reader who finishes this article will be able to explain the mechanism by which a fully enacted statute can remain inoperative for over a decade, describe the scheme Parliament designed in 2014 and the genuine defects inside that scheme, trace the amendment bill that was offered as the reason for waiting and then allowed to lapse, and state accurately what protects a whistleblower in the Act's absence: an interim vigilance resolution from 2004, a set of sectoral mechanisms that cover different populations for different wrongs, a witness protection scheme that applies only inside criminal proceedings, and the writ jurisdiction of the constitutional courts. None of those substitutes is the statute. Saying so plainly is the beginning of accuracy on this subject, and most published accounts of the Act fail at exactly that point by describing it in the present tense as a functioning remedy.

## The problem the Act was built to solve

Corruption inside a public organisation is rarely visible from outside it. The files that would prove a rigged contract, a diverted consignment or a purchased posting sit inside the office that committed the wrong, and the people with access to those files are subordinates of the people who benefit from the wrong. Every anti-corruption system therefore depends, whether it admits it or not, on insiders and near-insiders deciding to speak: the engineer who knows the road was built thinner than the specification, the accounts clerk who processed the false bill, the officer who was ordered to sign. What such a person risks by speaking is not abstract. The retaliation available to a superior in the Indian administrative system is extensive and mostly lawful in form: a punitive transfer, an adverse annual confidential report, a vigilance inquiry opened in return, a charge-sheet on a stale pretext, suspension, blocked promotion, and in the extreme cases that gave this statute its political force, physical violence that the administrative system could not or did not prevent.

The two deaths that fixed this problem in the national imagination are a matter of settled public record and are stated here without embellishment. Satyendra Dubey was an engineer with the National Highways Authority of India working on the Golden Quadrilateral highway programme in Bihar. In 2002 he wrote to the Prime Minister's Office identifying corruption in the project and asked expressly that his identity be kept secret, because he understood the risk he was taking. His identity was not protected. He was murdered at Gaya in November 2003. Shanmugam Manjunath was an officer of the Indian Oil Corporation who had acted against petrol pumps selling adulterated fuel in Uttar Pradesh. He was murdered in November 2005. Neither man had a statute to invoke. Each depended on the discretion of the very administrative machinery he was reporting on, and in Dubey's case the single safeguard he requested, confidentiality, was the safeguard that failed.

The demand that followed those deaths was specific rather than rhetorical. It was not a demand for tougher punishment of corruption, which the statute book already supplied. It was a demand for a protected channel: a designated authority outside the discloser's own chain of command, a legal duty to conceal the discloser's identity, a prohibition on retaliating against the discloser with a power to undo retaliation when it happened, and penalties for the two acts that make disclosure dangerous, revealing who spoke and punishing them for speaking. That four-part demand is the template against which the Whistle Blowers Protection Act should be read, because the Act is recognisably an attempt to enact exactly those four elements. It is also the template against which the Act's substitutes should be measured, because each of them supplies some of the four elements and none supplies all of them.

The mechanics of retaliation repay a closer description, because they explain why a general exhortation to honesty was never going to be enough and why the protection had to be legal in form. A government servant's career is administered through instruments that are discretionary by design. Postings and transfers are executive decisions in which no reasons need ordinarily be recorded. The annual performance appraisal is written by the reporting officer, who may be the very person the disclosure concerns, and a single adverse entry can stall a promotion board for years. Departmental proceedings can be initiated on allegations that would never survive a criminal standard of proof, and the pendency of proceedings itself operates as a punishment, blocking promotion, foreign posting and vigilance clearance. Suspension is formally not a punishment and is practically a public disgrace with reduced pay. None of these instruments requires the retaliating superior to do anything unlawful on its face, which is why retaliation is so difficult to police through the ordinary service law: each act arrives dressed as routine administration, and the servant who challenges it in a tribunal must prove the motive behind an order the system presumes to be bona fide. A protection statute worthy of the name has to intervene in exactly that evidential structure, which is what the Act's reversal of the burden of proof in victimisation proceedings attempts. Without such an intervention, the rational insider calculates that silence costs nothing and speech may cost a career, and the calculation is usually correct.

There is a further asymmetry the problem statement should record. The wrongdoing a disclosure targets is frequently collective and monetised: a percentage structure on contracts, a syndicate around a licensing desk, a posting market. The proceeds give the beneficiaries both the motive and the resources to retaliate, to litigate, and to wait. The discloser, by contrast, acts alone, on a salary, with a family's housing and schooling tied to the posting he is risking. Any realistic account of whistleblowing in the Indian public services begins from that imbalance of organisation and resources, and any protection regime that ignores it, by protecting only against the formal acts of the employer and not against the informal economy of threats around the office, will protect the paperwork of disclosure rather than the discloser. The killings of 2003 and 2005 stand at the extreme end of that informal economy, which is why they, and not a committee report, remain the reference point of the public debate.

A second population belongs in this account because it performs the whistleblower's function without the whistleblower's proximity. Applicants under the Right to Information Act, 2005 obtain the records that expose a diverted ration allocation, a ghost muster roll or a road that exists only on paper, and they publish what they find. They are, functionally, the most productive disclosers of local corruption in India, and they do so under their own names, because the information law requires an applicant to identify himself. The violence and harassment directed at information applicants across the years since 2005 is the most direct continuing evidence of the gap an uncommenced protection statute leaves. The pillar treatment of the [Right to Information Act, 2005 and the disclosure regime it built](/2011/09/01/right-to-information-act-2005-guide/) covers that statute's own machinery; the point for this article is that the two laws were designed as complements, one to extract information and one to protect the person who acts on it, and only one of the pair was ever switched on.

## What existed before: a Law Commission draft and an interim resolution

The legislative idea is older than the deaths that made it urgent. N. Vittal, as Chief Vigilance Commissioner, pressed from the late 1990s for a law that would encourage disclosures of corruption and protect the persons making them, and at his instance the Law Commission of India took up the subject. The Commission, chaired by Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy, delivered its One Hundred and Seventy-ninth Report in December 2001, submitting it on 14 December 2001. The report did what Law Commission reports at their best do: it surveyed the disclosure regimes of other jurisdictions, concluded that a statute was essential if corruption was to be reported from inside the system, and attached a complete draft, the Public Interest Disclosure (Protection of Informers) Bill. The architecture that the eventual Act would adopt a decade later, a designated authority receiving disclosures in confidence, concealing identity, inquiring discreetly and protecting the informer, is already present in that draft. The lineage matters for a reason beyond antiquarian interest. When the Act is criticised, as it often is, for the design choice of funnelling disclosures through designated authorities rather than protecting disclosure at large, the answer is that the design was not improvised in 2010. It was the Law Commission's considered model, and the debate about it is a debate with a reasoned institutional proposal, not with a drafting accident.

What the executive did with the recommendation, for a decade, was nothing legislative. The draft bill circulated within government from 2003 without reaching Parliament. The substitute that emerged instead was administrative. After Dubey's murder, the Supreme Court, in the proceedings that followed, expressed the expectation that a machinery for acting on whistleblower complaints be put in place pending legislation, and on 21 April 2004 the Government of India issued the Public Interest Disclosure and Protection of Informers Resolution, usually shortened to the PIDPI Resolution, through the Department of Personnel and Training, amending it on 29 April 2004. The resolution designated the Central Vigilance Commission as the agency to receive written complaints or disclosures on allegations of corruption or misuse of office by employees of the Central Government and of bodies controlled by it. It promised confidentiality of the complainant's identity, empowered the Commission to seek reports and to recommend action, and contemplated protection for the complainant through directions to the authorities concerned.

The PIDPI Resolution deserves a fair description, because it is the mechanism that has actually carried the load for two decades and is the mechanism that carries it still. It is not a statute. It creates no offence, confers no right a court can enforce as such, and binds nobody beyond the executive that issued it and can amend or withdraw it. Its coverage is confined to the central sphere: employees of the Central Government, corporations established under central law, government companies, and societies and local authorities owned or controlled by the Centre. A disclosure about a state government servant falls outside it. Its procedure is deliberately spare: the complaint must be addressed to the Secretary of the Commission in a sealed cover, the complainant must identify himself to the Commission even though his identity is then withheld from the organisation complained against, and the Commission will not act on an anonymous complaint. Its protection promise is real but discretionary, resting on the Commission's authority to call for reports and to press the organisation for action. Measured against the four-element template, the resolution supplies a designated authority and a measure of identity concealment, offers protection as administrative practice rather than legal right, and carries no penalties at all. It was designed, expressly, as an interim arrangement to hold the position until Parliament legislated. The interim arrangement has outlasted the legislation that was supposed to replace it, because the legislation, though enacted, was never commenced, and the resolution's own repeal, written into Section 31 of the Act, is locked inside the same uncommenced statute. A repeal clause in an Act that has not begun cannot repeal anything, so the interim mechanism survives by the same omission that keeps the permanent one dormant.

The comparative material before the Law Commission showed that other democracies had answered the same problem in recognisably different ways, and the choice India eventually made is clearer when set against them. The United Kingdom's Public Interest Disclosure Act, 1998 protects a worker who makes a qualifying disclosure through a ladder of recipients: to the employer first, then to prescribed regulators, and more widely, including to the press, only on stricter conditions, with the remedy lying in employment tribunal proceedings for detriment and dismissal. The United States federal system, built around the Whistleblower Protection Act of 1989, protects disclosures by federal employees to an independent Office of Special Counsel and gives the employee an individual right of action before the Merit Systems Protection Board, and the later American securities legislation added financial rewards for information leading to enforcement recoveries. The Indian design differs from both in its centre of gravity. It is not built around the employment relationship and a tribunal remedy for detriment, as the British model is, nor around an independent counsel and an individual action, as the American federal model is. It is built around the vigilance inquiry: a disclosure is a trigger for an administrative investigation by a designated authority, and protection is an incident of that investigation, granted and policed by the same authority. That choice has a logic in a system where the vigilance machinery already existed and where service tribunals were not designed for protective intervention at the speed retaliation moves. It also has a cost, which the comparative view exposes: where protection is an incident of an inquiry, a discloser whose complaint is closed at the discreet inquiry stage may find the protection has ended with the file, whereas a detriment-based model protects the act of disclosure itself wherever it leads. The Law Commission's draft, and the Act after it, accepted that cost in exchange for institutional simplicity. A modern re-examination would have to price it again.

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission added its voice in its fourth report, on ethics in governance, in 2007, recommending legislation to protect whistleblowers as part of a wider integrity framework. India is also a party to the United Nations Convention against Corruption, which calls on states to protect persons who report corruption offences in good faith against unjustified treatment. By the time a bill finally reached Parliament, the proposal therefore carried the accumulated authority of a Law Commission draft, an administrative reforms recommendation, an international commitment and a decade of demonstrated interim inadequacy. The bill's slow passage and the Act's non-commencement cannot be explained by any absence of institutional support for the idea.

## The passage of the Bill and the renaming of the Act

The bill that became the Act was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 26 August 2010 by Prithviraj Chavan, the Minister of State for Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions, under the title of the Public Interest Disclosure and Protection to Persons Making the Disclosure Bill, 2010. The title announced the Law Commission lineage, and the statement of objects tracked it: a mechanism to receive disclosures of corruption, wilful misuse of power or discretion and criminal offences by public servants, with safeguards against victimisation. The bill was examined by the parliamentary standing committee attached to the ministry, and its journey through the Lok Sabha took over a year against the background of the anti-corruption mobilisation of 2011, in which the demand for the Lokpal occupied most of the public attention and this quieter bill travelled in its convoy. The Lok Sabha passed it on 27 December 2011, by which point its short title had become the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2011, the name by which the measure was publicly known.

The bill's passage through the Lok Sabha took place in the shadow of the Lokpal agitation, and the shadow is part of the explanation for both its passage and its subsequent neglect. The street mobilisation of 2011 was directed at the creation of a Lokpal, and the government's legislative response was a package of integrity measures of which this bill was one: it could be pointed to as evidence that the disclosure side of the anti-corruption architecture was being built, while attracting a fraction of the scrutiny directed at the ombudsman proposal. Measures passed as part of a package inherit the package's politics. They are debated less on their own text, amended less from the floor, and owned by no single constituency once the package dissolves, and all three marks are visible on this bill's record. Its text reached the statute book with the drafting imperfection in the appeal provision uncorrected and with the contested choices, the anonymity bar and the false-disclosure penalty, carried through in their introduced form.

The Rajya Sabha did not pass the bill until 21 February 2014, more than two years later, near the end of the Fifteenth Lok Sabha's life. The timing shaped everything that followed. Assent on 9 May 2014 came in the last days of the outgoing government's tenure, in the closing phase of the general election and days before the successor government was formed, a moment at which no ministry was positioned to prepare commencement rules, designate authorities and notify a date as an early act of administration. Commencement is the kind of unglamorous executive work that happens when a government adopts a statute as its own programme. This statute arrived at the precise point of transition when adoption was least likely, and the incoming government, which had not carried the bill and did not share its parentage, treated it from the start as a text to be corrected before use rather than a programme to be implemented. The 2015 amendment bill was the formal expression of that treatment. The two-year delay in the Rajya Sabha therefore did more than postpone the Act: it relocated the Act's birth to the one fortnight in the parliamentary cycle at which commencement was structurally improbable, and the consequences of that relocation have lasted a decade. Presidential assent followed on 9 May 2014, and the Act was published in the Gazette of India on 12 May 2014 as Act 17 of 2014. Two features of that chronology deserve emphasis. The first is that the enacted short title carried the year 2011, the year of Lok Sabha passage, while the Act number carried 2014, the year of assent. The second is what happened next. The Repealing and Amending Act, 2015, which is Act 17 of 2015 and took effect on 13 May 2015, amended the short title by its Second Schedule and substituted 2014 for 2011, so that the statute is correctly cited as the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014. The renaming was a tidying exercise to align the title with the year of enactment, not a re-enactment, and nothing in the statute's substance changed. But the episode has generated durable confusion in secondary writing, where the Act is variously dated 2011 and 2014 and occasionally described as though two statutes existed. There is one statute: Act 17 of 2014, passed under a 2011 title and renamed in 2015. Any account that gives a different Act number, or treats the renaming as a fresh law, is wrong, and any account that describes the Act as in force is wrong in the more consequential way this article keeps returning to.

One further amendment to the enacted text should be recorded, because it shows the statute being maintained in form while dormant in substance. As enacted, Section 1 confined the Act's extent to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, the standard exclusion of the period. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 omitted that exclusion with effect from 31 October 2019, so the Act as it stands extends to the whole of India. Parliament has therefore amended the territorial extent of a statute that has never operated anywhere. The detail is small but diagnostic: the Indian legislative system is entirely capable of processing amendments to this Act. What it has not done is the single executive act, a commencement notification under Section 1(3), that would give the Act effect.

## How the Whistle Blowers Protection Act is built

The Act has thirty-one sections arranged in seven chapters, and its architecture follows the sequence of a disclosure's life. The first chapter contains the short title, extent and commencement provision in Section 1, the exclusion of the Special Protection Group in Section 2, and the definitions in Section 3, of which two carry the whole scheme: the definition of the Competent Authority, which assigns each class of public servant to a receiving office, and the definition of disclosure, which fixes what may be reported. The second chapter, Sections 4 to 6, governs the making of a public interest disclosure and the bars on inquiry. The third chapter, Sections 7 to 10, gives the Competent Authority its powers, including the powers of a civil court, subject to the secrecy carve-out in Section 8. The fourth chapter is headed Superintendence in part and protection in substance: Sections 11 to 14 contain the safeguards against victimisation, the protection of complainants and witnesses, the duty to conceal identity and the power to pass interim orders. The fifth chapter, Sections 15 to 22, contains the offences and penalties. The sixth, Sections 23 to 24 in the Gazette's arrangement of the miscellaneous chapter, deals with annual reports and good faith protection for those administering the Act, and the final provisions, Sections 25 to 31, contain the rule-making and regulation powers, the laying requirements, the power to remove difficulties, and the repeal of the 2004 resolution in Section 31.

The miscellaneous provisions complete the picture of a statute designed for accountability in operation. Section 23 requires every Competent Authority to prepare a consolidated annual report of its activities under the Act, to be forwarded to the Central or State Government and laid before Parliament or the state legislature, so that the handling of disclosures would have been a matter of recurring public record, with the number of disclosures, their outcomes and the recommendations made visible to legislators and, through them, to the press. Section 24 protects the Competent Authority and every officer, employee, agency or person acting on its behalf for anything done in good faith under the Act, the standard shield without which officials administering a sensitive jurisdiction would expose themselves to personal litigation for doing their jobs. Sections 28 and 29 subject every notification, rule and regulation under the Act to laying before Parliament or the state legislature. Section 27 empowers the Competent Authority, with the previous approval of the government, to make regulations for giving effect to the Act. The cumulative effect is of a text that expects to be watched: by legislatures through reports and laying, by complainants through the outcome and hearing rights in Section 5, and by the accused through the hearing and appeal rights recorded elsewhere in this article. Dormancy defeats that expectation comprehensively, because a statute that never operates produces no reports, lays no rules and answers no questions about its working beyond the single question of why it has not begun.

Two structural choices define the Act's character. The first is that it is a channel statute, not a shield statute. It does not create a general right to disclose wrongdoing to anyone, to the press, to a legislator or to the public, with protection following the disclosure wherever it is made. It creates one protected route: a written disclosure, in the prescribed form, to the one authority designated for the class of public servant complained against. A disclosure made outside that route gains nothing from the Act. The second choice is that protection inside the route is administrative rather than judicial in the first instance. The Competent Authority inquires, recommends, directs and reports; it does not try the corruption allegation itself, and the offences the Act creates are tried by the ordinary criminal courts on the Authority's complaint. The Act is therefore best understood as a protected intake and supervision mechanism bolted onto the existing disciplinary and criminal machinery, not as a new anti-corruption court. Readers who expect it to resemble the American model of a freestanding claim for retaliatory dismissal, or the British model of protected disclosures made to a range of prescribed persons and tested in employment tribunals, will misread it. Its closest relative is the vigilance system it grew out of, given statutory form and sharper teeth.

## The disclosure: what may be reported, and on what terms

Section 3 defines a disclosure as a complaint relating to three classes of conduct. The first is an attempt to commit or the commission of an offence under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, the statute whose offences a disclosure will most often allege, and whose own scheme is treated in the [pillar article on the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988](/2011/12/15/prevention-of-corruption-act-1988/). The second is wilful misuse of power or wilful misuse of discretion by virtue of which demonstrable loss is caused to the government or demonstrable wrongful gain accrues to the public servant or to a third party. The third is an attempt to commit or the commission of a criminal offence by a public servant generally. The drafting repays attention. Mere error, negligence or an unpopular decision is not enough: the misuse of power or discretion must be wilful, and it must produce a demonstrable loss or a demonstrable wrongful gain. The definition thus screens for culpable conduct with a measurable consequence, and excludes the honest mistake and the policy disagreement. A disclosure may be made by any public servant or by any other person, including a non-governmental organisation, so the channel is not confined to insiders; a citizen who has assembled evidence of a corruption offence may use it. Section 4 then provides that the disclosure may be made notwithstanding anything contained in the Official Secrets Act, 1923, an override of real significance, because the secrecy statute is the instrument most often invoked to argue that an official document can never lawfully leave the office that holds it. Under the Act as enacted, a disclosure to the Competent Authority is lawful even where the material would otherwise attract the secrecy law.

The form of the disclosure is prescribed in Section 4(4): it must be in writing or by electronic mail or electronic mail message, in accordance with the procedure prescribed, contain full particulars, and be accompanied by supporting documents or other materials, if any. The electronic channel matters more than its brevity suggests. A disclosure that must travel as a physical letter through an office's dak system passes hands the discloser cannot count; an electronic submission to the receiving authority's designated address shortens the chain between the discloser and the one office entitled to know his name. The prescribed procedure itself, however, was left to rules under Section 25, and rules for a disclosure procedure are among the instruments that commencement would have generated and dormancy has not. The declaration requirement in Section 4(3) cuts both ways in the same fashion. Read protectively, it is a filter of seriousness that distinguishes the disclosure from the rumour and gives the Authority a signed foundation for its inquiry. Read from the discloser's side of the desk, it is a personal undertaking of the substantial truth of an allegation which the discloser, by the nature of inside knowledge, may believe completely and be able to prove only in part, since the documents that would complete the proof are held by the organisation accused. The Act asks for reasonable belief, not proof, and the distinction is the discloser's shelter. It is also a distinction that exists, so far, only as words in a Gazette that no inquiry has ever applied.

The terms on which a disclosure must be made are set out in the same section and contain the Act's most contested feature. Every disclosure must be made in good faith, and the person making it must give a personal declaration that he reasonably believes the information and the allegation to be substantially true. It must be in writing or by electronic mail, contain full particulars, and be accompanied by supporting documents or other materials where any exist. The Competent Authority may call for further information. Then Section 4(6) provides that no action shall be taken on a public interest disclosure if the disclosure does not indicate the identity of the complainant or the identity of the complainant is found to be incorrect or false. Anonymous complaints are therefore barred outright, and pseudonymous complaints with them. The discloser must name himself to the authority, in exchange for the authority's statutory duty, examined below, to keep that name from everyone else.

The case for the bar, made by the Law Commission's tradition and by administrators, is that anonymity invites vendetta: an unsigned denunciation costs its author nothing, can be manufactured by a rival in a promotion contest, and leaves the authority unable to test the source or seek the particulars only the source holds. The case against, made by the Act's critics including those who campaigned for the statute, is that the bar misunderstands the risk structure of disclosure. The person with the best information is usually the person most exposed, a subordinate in the same office, and requiring him to register his identity with any office at all, however conscientious, asks him to stake his career and safety on the integrity of a file movement he cannot observe. Satyendra Dubey's history is the standing rebuke to complacency on this point: he identified himself to the highest office in the executive and asked for secrecy, and the secrecy failed. A system that conditions all protection on prior identification, critics argue, will receive the disclosures that are safe to sign and not the disclosures that matter. Both arguments are serious, and the Act simply chose one side of them. What can be said with confidence is that the choice was deliberate and debated, and that a Parliament revisiting the Act would have to decide the question again rather than inherit it silently.

Who may use the channel is broader than the word whistleblower suggests, and the breadth is deliberate. Section 4(1) opens the disclosure to any public servant or to any other person, expressly including a non-governmental organisation. A journalist who has assembled documentary evidence of a corruption offence, a contractor who was solicited for a bribe and refused, a pensioner who discovers that a work certified in his village was never executed, a civil society organisation that has tracked a procurement across its paper trail: each is a competent discloser under the text, provided the allegation fits the definition of disclosure and the declarant signs the declaration of reasonable belief in its substantial truth. The inclusion matters because a large share of provable corruption is visible to persons outside the service, suppliers, applicants, beneficiaries and auditors among them, whose evidence an insider-only regime would waste. It also changes the risk analysis in a way the debate rarely notes. An outside discloser faces no reporting officer and no transfer, but faces defamation suits, criminal complaints in return, and the withdrawal of the licences and contracts on which his livelihood depends, and the Act's victimisation chapter, drafted around proceedings and restoration to a post, fits the insider's retaliation better than the outsider's. The statute's protection is therefore widest at its entrance and narrowest where the outsider's danger lies, an imbalance that operation would quickly have exposed and that a dormant text never has to answer for.

Section 6 adds the remaining limits on what the machinery will touch. The Competent Authority is not to inquire into a disclosure to the extent it seeks to reopen an issue already determined by a court or tribunal, nor into a matter already the subject of a formal and public inquiry under the Public Servants (Inquiries) Act, 1850, or referred for inquiry under the Commissions of Inquiry Act, 1952. It is not to investigate a disclosure made after the expiry of seven years from the date on which the action complained against is alleged to have taken place. And nothing in the Act empowers the Authority to question a bona fide action or a bona fide exercise of discretion in the discharge of duty. The seven-year bar is the provision most likely to matter in practice, because corruption schemes frequently surface late, through an audit objection, a successor's discovery or a falling-out among beneficiaries, and a disclosure assembled after seven years would be outside the Act however well evidenced. The bona fide action saving, by contrast, largely restates what the definition of disclosure already achieves through the word wilful.

## The Competent Authority: a map of who receives what

Because the Act channels every disclosure to a single designated recipient, the definition of Competent Authority in Section 3 is its operating core, and the design principle is visible at once: the recipient is always an authority situated outside and above the hierarchy complained against, matched to the constitutional position of the person complained against. For a member of the Union Council of Ministers, the Competent Authority is the Prime Minister. For a member of Parliament who is not a minister, it is the Chairman of the Council of States for a member of that House and the Speaker of the House of the People for a member of that House. For a minister in a state or a union territory, it is the Chief Minister. For a member of a state legislature who is not a minister, it is the Chairman of the Legislative Council or the Speaker of the Legislative Assembly, according to the House. For the subordinate judiciary and for persons discharging adjudicatory or court-connected functions, including judges other than judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts, court-appointed liquidators, receivers and commissioners, and arbitrators to whom a cause has been referred, the Competent Authority is the High Court.

For the great mass of public servants, the designations are institutional. For persons in the service or pay of the Central Government, and for the long statutory catalogue that follows them, employees and office-bearers of central corporations, government companies, societies and local authorities controlled by the Centre, election officials, office-bearers of aided co-operative societies, members and employees of central service commissions and their selection committees, teachers and employees of centrally established or funded universities, and office-bearers and employees of institutions receiving central financial assistance, the Competent Authority is the Central Vigilance Commission or any other authority the Central Government notifies for the purpose. The state mirror image assigns the corresponding classes of state public servants to the State Vigilance Commission, where one exists, or to an officer or authority the State Government notifies. For members of the armed forces and the other services whose fundamental rights Parliament may restrict under Article 33 of the Constitution, the Competent Authority is whichever authority the Central or State Government, as the case may be, notifies as having jurisdiction over them.

The definition of public servant is borrowed from the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, and carries one express exclusion: a judge of the Supreme Court or of a High Court is not a public servant for the purposes of this Act. The senior judiciary therefore sits outside the disclosure channel altogether. The exclusion was defended on the ground that judicial accountability at that level is governed by the constitutional removal process and was the subject of separate legislation on judicial standards, and criticised on the ground that a protection statute which cannot receive a complaint about the most powerful adjudicators leaves a conspicuous silence at the top of the system. Both points are recorded here because the exclusion is structural and deliberate, and any reader using the Act's map should know where the map ends.

The Central Vigilance Commission's position within the map deserves a separate note, because it is the recipient for the overwhelming majority of disclosures the Act contemplates and because its character shapes what commencement would have meant in practice. The Commission is a statutory body under the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003, with superintendence over the vigilance administration of the central sphere and a largely advisory relationship to the disciplinary authorities who actually punish. Under the Act of 2014 it would have acquired a second, distinct jurisdiction: not advice on vigilance administration generally, but the conduct of disclosure inquiries with civil court powers, the making of recommendations on a statutory timetable, the protection jurisdiction in Chapter IV, and the power to set the criminal provisions in motion by complaint. The two jurisdictions would have reinforced each other, since the Commission's disclosure work would have fed its vigilance superintendence and its annual reporting. They would also have concentrated, in one institution, the functions of recipient, inquirer, protector and reporting authority for the central sphere, a concentration the Law Commission model accepted and which the interim resolution had already normalised. Critics of concentration make the obvious point: a complainant dissatisfied with the Commission's handling of his disclosure has, within the Act, nowhere else to go with it, since Section 21 bars the civil courts and the appeal in Section 20 does not lie against a closure. The design trusts the Commission completely or not at all. That trust has been reasonably placed in the resolution era, on the evidence of the Commission's own published handling standards; the Act would have made the trust structural and irrevocable by the complainant, which is a stronger commitment than the resolution ever demanded.

The design has an obvious strength and an obvious weakness, and honest analysis should name both. The strength is jurisdictional clarity joined to constitutional propriety: a complaint against a minister goes to the head of the government, a complaint against a legislator goes to the presiding officer of his House, a complaint against a subordinate judge goes to the High Court that supervises him, and a complaint against an official goes to a vigilance body with no line authority over him. Nobody is asked to inquire into himself, and no disclosure is routed through the office it accuses. The weakness is that the map imports the conflicts of each receiving office. A Prime Minister receiving a disclosure against his own minister is the political superior of the accused, with a government's collective interest in the outcome. A presiding officer receiving a disclosure against a member of his House is the guardian of that House's privileges and reputation. The Central Vigilance Commission, for the ordinary official, is the cleanest recipient, and it is no accident that the interim resolution had already settled on it. The Act's wager is that a duty-bound authority with statutory obligations of confidentiality and reporting is safer than a conflicted one might appear, because its handling of the disclosure is structured by the Act's procedure and its record is reportable to Parliament. That wager was never tested, because the procedure it structures has never run.

The map is best tested by tracing two disclosures through it. Take first an engineer in a central public sector undertaking who discovers that measurements in a works register have been inflated at the instance of his general manager, and who holds copies of the register and the payment certificates. His disclosure concerns a person in the service or pay of a corporation established under central law, so it goes to the Central Vigilance Commission. The Commission must ascertain that he is the discloser, conceal his identity, and make a discreet inquiry. If it proceeds, it seeks the undertaking's comments from its head without naming him, and it may reveal his name to that head only with his written consent, a consent he will weigh knowing the head and the general manager inhabit the same headquarters. If the allegation is substantiated, the Commission recommends proceedings; the undertaking must decide within three months and record reasons if it disagrees. If, meanwhile, he is transferred to a remote depot, he applies under Section 11, and the undertaking must prove the transfer was not victimisation. Every stage of that trace depends on provisions that have never been in force.

Take, by contrast, a clerk in a state public works department who reports the same inflation by her executive engineer. Her disclosure goes to the State Vigilance Commission if her state has one, and if it does not, to whichever officer or authority her state government has notified under the Act, which presupposes a notification no state has had occasion to issue for a statute that has never commenced. Under the interim arrangements she has no equivalent of the PIDPI Resolution at all, because that resolution is confined to the central sphere. Her protection is the general law of the state she serves. The two traces, identical in facts, diverge entirely in route and in protection, and the divergence is the federal shape of the gap: the Act was designed as an all-India channel with a state storey, the interim substitute is a central mechanism only, and the storey of the building where most public servants work was never built even in substitute form.

### Does the Act apply to the Special Protection Group and the armed forces?

Section 2 provides that its provisions shall not apply to the armed forces of the Union, being the Special Protection Group, so the force constituted under its 1988 Act is excluded outright. Other members of the armed forces are not excluded by name, but their coverage depends on a government notification designating the authority to receive complaints concerning them.

The fuller position is this. Complaints concerning members of the armed forces and the other services listed in Article 33 of the Constitution are routed, on the text, to an authority the Central or State Government notifies as having jurisdiction over them, which leaves their coverage dependent on a further executive act rather than settled by the Act itself. The Special Protection Group exclusion was justified by the nature of the force's work, which is the physical protection of a small number of protectees and depends on operational confidentiality of an unusual order. The general position of the defence services, the paramilitary forces and the police forces to which Article 33 applies is channelled rather than excluded: a Competent Authority exists for them in the text, but only in the form of an authority to be specified by notification, which is a second commencement-type gap inside the first. The intelligence and security organisations, it should be noted, are not excluded from this Act at all, in pointed contrast to the Right to Information Act, 2005, whose Section 24 takes the organisations in its Second Schedule substantially outside that statute subject to exceptions for corruption and human rights violations. A disclosure alleging corruption by a public servant in such an organisation is, on the text of the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, a disclosure the Act can receive. What the Act does instead is control the material inside the inquiry, through the Section 8 secrecy carve-out examined in the next part, rather than exclude the organisation from the channel. Defenders of the design regard that as the better balance, since corruption inside a security organisation is not less corrosive for being secret. Critics reply that a channel is only as real as the inquiry it can conduct, and that Section 8 gives the government a certificated veto over the evidence. That disagreement, too, was never tested in operation.

## The inquiry: discreet first, adversarial later

The procedure in Section 5 is staged, and the stages reflect the statute's central anxiety, which is that the act of inquiring can itself expose the discloser. On receiving a disclosure, the Competent Authority must first ascertain from the complainant whether he is in fact the person who made it, and must conceal his identity unless he has already revealed it himself elsewhere. It then makes a discreet inquiry, in the manner and within the time prescribed, to decide whether there is any basis for proceeding further. Discretion here is a procedural value, not a courtesy: the preliminary stage is designed to test the allegation without alerting the organisation complained against to the existence of a complaint or the identity of its source. If the Authority concludes that investigation is warranted, it seeks comments, explanation or a report from the head of the organisation concerned. In doing so it must not reveal the complainant's identity, and it must direct the head not to reveal it either. Only where revelation becomes necessary for the purpose of seeking the organisation's response may the Authority disclose the identity, and then only with the complainant's prior written consent. A complainant who refuses consent must instead supply the documentary evidence supporting the complaint, so that the inquiry can proceed on the papers. Where the comments received substantiate corruption or wilful misuse of power or discretion, the Authority recommends measures to the public authority: proceedings against the public servant, administrative steps to redress the loss caused to the government, a recommendation for criminal proceedings where the facts warrant them, corrective measures, or any other necessary measure. The public authority must decide on the recommendation within three months, extendable by a further three months at the Authority's allowance, and if it disagrees it must record its reasons. The complainant must be informed of the action taken and the final outcome, and if the Authority proposes to close a case it must first give the complainant an opportunity of being heard if he desires it.

The powers behind that procedure are in Sections 7 to 10. The Competent Authority may require any public servant or any other person to furnish information or produce documents relevant to the inquiry, and it has all the powers of a civil court trying a suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 in respect of summoning and examining persons on oath, requiring discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records and issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses or documents. Its proceedings are judicial proceedings for the purposes of the perjury provisions. Section 7(4) then disarms the standard official defences: subject to Section 8, no obligation of secrecy, whether imposed by the Official Secrets Act, 1923 or by any other law, and no privilege in respect of documents or evidence, may be claimed by the government or any public servant in proceedings before the Competent Authority. Every public authority must create an appropriate machinery for dealing with disclosures sent to it, under the Competent Authority's superintendence and directions. And under Section 10 the Authority may take the assistance of the Delhi Special Police Establishment, the police authorities or any other authority it considers necessary to complete the inquiry within the prescribed time.

Two features of that procedure deserve separate assessment. The first is the closure hearing. Before ordering the closure of a case after inquiry, the Competent Authority must give the complainant an opportunity of being heard if he desires it. The provision answers the most common grievance in complaint systems of every kind, the file closed in silence on reasoning the complainant never sees, and it converts closure from an administrative disappearance into a decision that must survive the complainant's answer. Its limits are equally visible: the hearing is the complainant's if he asks, the Authority's reasoning is not required to be published, and a closure after hearing is, within the Act, final, with no appeal provided for the complainant against it, the appeal in Section 20 being confined to penalty orders. The second feature is the timed decision. The three-month period for the public authority to accept or reject a recommendation, extendable once by three months, with reasons recorded for disagreement, is stricter than the corresponding discipline in most Indian recommendatory regimes, several of which impose no time limit at all and receive action-taken reports years late or never. Had the Act operated, the recorded-reasons requirement would have created a documentary trail of refusals, reasoned in writing by named authorities, which is the raw material of parliamentary and press accountability. The trail does not exist, because the recommendations were never made.

The recommendatory character of the outcome deserves a clear-eyed sentence, because it is the point on which admirers and detractors of the design divide. The Competent Authority cannot itself dismiss, prosecute or convict. What it can do is inquire with compulsory powers, form a view on evidence, recommend with the weight of a constitutional or statutory office behind the recommendation, require a timed decision, require reasons for disagreement, report annually to Parliament or the state legislature under Section 23, and protect the complainant while all of that happens. That is the vigilance model statutoryised, and its defenders say, with some force, that in the Indian system a recommendation from the Central Vigilance Commission, formally recorded and reported, is a heavier instrument than its label suggests, and that the alternative of a parallel prosecuting authority would have collided with the disciplinary jurisdiction of governments and the sanction regime of the corruption law. Its detractors answer that a determined department can absorb a recommendation, record reasons and outwait the complainant, and that the Act gives the Authority no power to compel acceptance even where its inquiry has substantiated the allegation. The disagreement is genuine and structural, and it is a disagreement about an inquiry that has never been held.

One structural tension inside the inquiry should be named before the carve-out is reached, because it would have dominated the Act's practice. The discreet inquiry is conducted by the Competent Authority, but the first substantive response is sought from the head of the organisation complained against, and the organisation's own internal machinery, created under Section 9 under the Authority's superintendence, does much of the factual work. The head is directed not to reveal the complainant's identity, and the Authority withholds the name, but the organisation is in substance being asked to investigate itself and report upward, which is the standard vigilance method and carries its standard risk: the inquiry's framing, its choice of records and its characterisation of the allegation are all in the hands of the institution with the strongest interest in a quiet outcome. The Act's answers to that risk are the Authority's independent powers under Section 7, its ability to take the assistance of the Delhi Special Police Establishment or the police under Section 10, the penalty in Section 15 for a false or obstructed report, and the duty to tell the complainant the final outcome with a hearing before closure. Whether those answers suffice depends on the Authority's willingness to distrust the first report and use its own powers, which is a question about institutional culture that no text can settle. The vigilance system's long experience under the 2004 resolution suggests the risk is managed actively in some organisations and absorbed passively in others, and a commenced Act would have inherited both habits along with the machinery.

### What information can never be demanded in a whistleblower inquiry?

Section 8 of the Act provides that no person shall be required to furnish information or assist an inquiry if doing so is likely to prejudicially affect the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, or relates to contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence.

The section also protects the proceedings of the Union and state Cabinets and their committees, and a certificate from the Secretary to the government concerned, certifying that material is of the protected nature, is declared binding and conclusive on the point.

The carve-out has a respectable core and a questionable edge. The respectable core is that an inquiry into a disclosure will sometimes reach material, a Cabinet discussion of a defence procurement, for example, whose circulation would cause harm out of proportion to its evidential value, and a legal system is entitled to wall such material off. The questionable edge is the certificate mechanism. The question whether a document falls within the protected categories is settled not by the Competent Authority examining the document but by a certificate of the very government whose servant is complained against, and the certificate is declared binding and conclusive. The Authority is thus disabled from testing the claim, in contrast to the Right to Information Act's appeal structure, in which an Information Commission can examine the record before accepting an exemption. The categories themselves track the language of the information law's exemptions, which made them familiar to the drafters, but the transplant changes their effect: in the information law they justify refusing a citizen a document, while here they justify refusing the inquiring authority the evidence of the wrong it is inquiring into. A secrecy carve-out that a respondent department can invoke by certificate is a standing temptation to classify the embarrassing as the sensitive, and the Act contains no counterweight beyond the Authority's general reporting power. This is one of the design questions, along with the anonymity bar, that any serious attempt to commence the Act would have to reopen, and it forms part of the honest case, addressed later in this article, that the statute as passed is not the statute its supporters wish existed.

## The protection provisions: identity, victimisation and safety

Chapter IV of the Act is where its promise is concentrated, and Section 11 is its centre of gravity. The Central Government is placed under a duty to ensure that no person or public servant who has made a disclosure is victimised by the initiation of proceedings or otherwise merely on the ground that he made the disclosure or rendered assistance in the inquiry. A person who is being victimised, or is likely to be victimised, on that ground may apply to the Competent Authority for redress, and the Authority may give suitable directions to the public servant or public authority concerned to protect him or avert the victimisation. Before directing, the Authority must hear both the complainant and the authority complained against, and in that hearing the burden of proof lies on the public authority to show that the action alleged to be victimisation is nothing of the kind. That reversal of the burden is the single most practically significant sentence in the chapter, because the standard official defence to a retaliation claim is that the transfer, the adverse report or the inquiry was an ordinary administrative decision taken for recorded reasons, and an unassisted complainant can rarely prove the retaliatory motive behind a facially regular order. Under Section 11 the authority must prove the negative. Directions given under the section are binding on the public servant or authority against whom victimisation is proved. The power includes, expressly and notwithstanding any other law, the power to direct the restoration of the public servant who made the disclosure to the status quo ante, which is the undoing of the punitive transfer, the withdrawn charge or the reversed posting rather than a declaration that it ought not to have happened. Wilful non-compliance with a direction carries a penalty of up to thirty thousand rupees under Section 11(5).

Identity protection is layered through three provisions that should be read together. Section 5 opens the inquiry with a duty to conceal the complainant's identity, examined above. Section 13 then provides generally that the Competent Authority shall, notwithstanding any law for the time being in force, conceal the identity of the complainant and the documents and information furnished by him for the purposes of the inquiry, unless the Authority itself decides otherwise or a court orders revelation. Section 16 supplies the criminal sanction, in the offences chapter, for any person who negligently or mala fidely reveals a complainant's identity. Section 14 adds a power of a different kind: at any time after a disclosure is made, if the Competent Authority is of the opinion that a corrupt practice requires to be stopped during the continuance of the inquiry, it may pass interim orders to prevent its continuance. That power is aimed at the ongoing scheme rather than the discloser's position, and it recognises that an inquiry which takes months while the complained conduct continues undisturbed has failed at half its purpose.

The interim power deserves more attention than it usually receives, because it is the Act's only forward-looking instrument. Everything else in the statute is retrospective or protective: the inquiry reconstructs what happened, the recommendations respond to it, the victimisation chapter repairs what was done to the discloser. Section 14 alone addresses the scheme in progress, the tender still being steered, the adulteration still being sold, the appointments still being made on the compromised list. Its presence in the text shows the drafters understood that a disclosure is often a report of a continuing enterprise rather than a completed event, and that the value of the information decays with every month the enterprise runs. The same understanding, applied to the discloser's position, is what a commencement notification delayed by a decade most conspicuously lacks: the retaliation the Act exists to prevent is also a continuing enterprise, conducted in postings and proceedings across the very years the notification has been awaited.

### Can the Competent Authority protect witnesses as well as the complainant?

Section 12 of the Act answers this directly. If the Competent Authority is of the opinion that the complainant, the witnesses or any person rendering assistance in the inquiry needs protection, it shall issue appropriate directions to the government authorities concerned, including the police, which shall take the necessary steps to protect the persons concerned.

The opinion may be formed on the application of the complainant or of the witnesses themselves, or on the basis of information the Authority has gathered in the inquiry, so protection does not depend on the endangered person finding the door and knocking. Physical protection of this kind is the provision whose absence from the substitutes is most keenly felt, because the threats that follow a disclosure are not confined to the discloser. The colleague who confirms the file noting, the contractor's employee who saw the measurement book, the family member who answers the door: retaliation in Indian administrative life has never respected the boundary of the formal complainant. A direction power that reaches witnesses and assistants, backed by the police authorities of the area, is the statute acknowledging that protection is a perimeter, not a personal privilege. It is also, like every other provision discussed in this article, a power that has never been exercised by anyone, because the chapter containing it has never been in force.

Where victimisation takes the form of a violation of human rights, a threat to life, custodial violence, or harassment that crosses into the territory of the National Human Rights Commission, a parallel constitutional and statutory route exists independently of this Act, and the [account of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 and the commissions it created](/2010/09/01/protection-of-human-rights-act-1993/) sets out what that route can and cannot deliver. The overlap is worth noticing because it illustrates the pattern of the substitutes: the human rights machinery can inquire and recommend where a discloser's rights are violated, and it needs no commencement notification because it was commenced in 1993, but it was not designed for disclosures, it has no power to conceal a complainant within a disclosure procedure, and its recommendations carry the familiar recommendatory limits. A whistleblower piecing together protection in the Act's absence is assembling it from institutions each built for another purpose. That assembly is described in full later in this article; the point here is that Section 11's integrated design, one authority holding the disclosure, the identity, the retaliation claim and the restoration power together, is precisely what the assembly cannot reproduce.

## The offences and penalties

The Act's fifth chapter distributes its sanctions across the three ways the system can be sabotaged: by the organisation that will not answer, by the person who exposes the discloser, and by the complainant who abuses the channel. Section 15 addresses the first. Where the Competent Authority finds that an organisation or official, without reasonable cause, has failed to furnish the report sought from it within the time specified or has mala fidely refused to submit it, a penalty may be imposed of two hundred and fifty rupees for each day the default continues, subject to a ceiling of fifty thousand rupees. Where the organisation or official has knowingly given an incomplete, incorrect, misleading or false report, or has destroyed the record or information that was the subject of the disclosure, or has obstructed the furnishing of the report in any manner, the penalty may extend to fifty thousand rupees. No penalty may be imposed without an opportunity of being heard. The destruction limb matters more than the modest figures suggest: the records are usually the case in a disclosure about corruption, and a statute that prices the destruction of the subject record at a fine comparable to a traffic penalty for a department is relying on the inquiry's other consequences, disciplinary and reputational, to do the deterring.

The two hundred and fifty rupees a day figure has a lineage worth noticing, because it shows the drafters borrowing from the one Indian disclosure statute that does operate. Section 20 of the Right to Information Act, 2005 empowers an Information Commission to impose on a defaulting public information officer a penalty of two hundred and fifty rupees for each day of delay, up to a ceiling of twenty-five thousand rupees. The whistleblower Act adopts the same daily rate for an organisation or official that fails to report to the Competent Authority, doubling the ceiling to fifty thousand. The borrowing is instructive in both directions. It gave the provision a tested administrative pedigree: the daily penalty under the information law is the sanction that information commissions actually impose and officers actually fear, out of proportion to its amount, because it is personal, recorded and difficult to explain upward. And it underlines, by contrast, the difference between the two statutes' fates. The information law's penalty has been imposed in thousands of orders and has generated a jurisprudence of reasonable cause. The whistleblower Act's identical mechanism has never been imposed once, because there has never been an inquiry to default in. Two statutes, one template, opposite histories: the comparison is the notification gap stated in miniature, in a single borrowed figure.

Section 16 addresses exposure. Any person who negligently or mala fidely reveals the identity of a complainant is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and with a fine which may extend to fifty thousand rupees. The inclusion of negligence alongside bad faith is deliberate and important: the paradigm failure in this field, the leaking of Satyendra Dubey's letter through the ordinary movement of official paper, was not necessarily a malicious act, and a sanction confined to deliberate revelation would leave the careless file movement that actually kills people outside the offence. Section 17 addresses abuse of the channel from the other side. Any person who makes a disclosure mala fidely and knowingly that it was incorrect, false or misleading is punishable with imprisonment which may extend to two years and a fine which may extend to thirty thousand rupees. Sections 18 and 19 extend liability to the heads of government departments and to companies and their officers in charge where an offence under the Act is committed by a department or a company, subject to the standard defences of absence of knowledge and due diligence. Section 22 confines prosecution: no court may take cognizance of an offence under the Act except on a complaint made by the Competent Authority or an officer authorised by it, and no court inferior to a Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or a Chief Judicial Magistrate may try such an offence.

The departmental and company liability provisions in Sections 18 and 19 follow the standard Indian pattern for offences by organisations and repay a sentence of analysis. Where an offence under the Act is committed by a department of government, the head of the department is deemed guilty unless he proves the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it; where it is committed with the consent, connivance or attributable neglect of another officer, that officer is deemed guilty as well. For companies, the persons in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the business are liable on the same deemed-guilt and due-diligence structure. In a statute whose gravest offence is the revelation of a complainant's identity, the departmental provision is the one with teeth directed at the right level: the leak of a discloser's name is characteristically an institutional failure, a letter copied too widely, a file sent to the wrong desk, and a rule that fixes responsibility on the head of the department unless diligence is proved creates an incentive for exactly the handling protocols, restricted circulation, sealed custody, named custodians, that confidentiality in practice consists of. The fine levels in the Act are low in absolute terms, and fines are not the real sanction for a department head; prosecution itself, on the Competent Authority's complaint, is.

Section 17 is the penalty that has attracted the most sustained criticism, and the criticism deserves its strongest form. The offence requires bad faith and knowledge of falsity, so on its face it does not punish the honest disclosure that turns out to be mistaken, and its defenders, including the government during the bill's passage, presented it as a necessary deterrent against the weaponised complaint, the disclosure filed to stall a rival's promotion or to settle a score inside the office. The critics' answer is that the formal elements are not where the deterrence operates. A discloser deciding whether to sign his name to an allegation cannot know in advance how an authority will characterise a disclosure that fails, because many true disclosures fail for want of proof rather than want of truth, and the distance between "not substantiated" and "mala fide and knowingly false" is a distance the discloser must trust an institution to observe while the institution holds his name. The Law Commission tradition accepted a penalty for false complaints as part of the design, and the international instruments are not uniform on the point, since several respected regimes penalise knowingly false reports while protecting honest error. The Indian debate is therefore not between a penalty and no penalty in the abstract. It is about whether a two-year imprisonment provision, in a system where the protective half of the bargain has never been commenced, can be defended by pointing to the safeguards in a statute that does not operate. That is the uncomfortable circularity at the centre of this Act, and it recurs below.

A drafting defect in the appeal provision should be recorded, because it illustrates the care with which an uncommenced statute escapes the corrections that operation forces. Section 20 gives a person aggrieved by an order of the Competent Authority relating to the imposition of a penalty a right of appeal to the High Court within sixty days. As enacted, the section refers to penalties under Sections 14, 15 and 16. Section 14, however, is the interim orders provision and imposes no penalty at all, while Section 17, which creates the imprisonment and fine for a false or frivolous disclosure, is not mentioned, and neither is the penalty for defying a victimisation direction under Section 11(5). The cross-references do not map cleanly onto the penalty provisions the Act actually contains. In a working statute such an error is usually found by the first appellant and repaired by an amending Act. In a dormant one it simply sits in the text, a small fossil of the scrutiny the Act never received because it never had to work.

## The position of the person complained against

A protection statute that ignored the position of the accused official would be indefensible, and this Act does not ignore it, a point worth making in a debate that usually treats safeguards for the complained-against as obstruction. The disclosure must carry a signed declaration of reasonable belief in its substantial truth, which fixes the complainant with responsibility for the allegation from the outset. The inquiry begins discreetly, so an unsubstantiated allegation is tested before the organisation is formally engaged, and Section 5(6) requires the Authority to close the matter where the allegations are frivolous or vexatious or where grounds are insufficient. No penalty under the Act may be imposed on any person without an opportunity of being heard, a proviso stated expressly in Section 15 and reflected in the hearing requirement built into the victimisation procedure in Section 11, where both sides are heard before a direction issues. Section 21 bars the jurisdiction of the civil courts over matters the Competent Authority is empowered to determine, and Section 22 ensures that prosecution for the Act's own offences begins only on the Authority's complaint, so the criminal provisions cannot be set in motion by a private vendetta bypassing the Authority's filter. Section 24 protects the Authority and those acting for it for anything done in good faith under the Act, and Section 20 provides an appeal to the High Court against penalty orders, with the drafting imperfection in its cross-references already recorded.

One boundary of the Act's exclusivity should be marked, since it determines where a dissatisfied party would actually have gone. Section 21 bars the civil courts from matters the Competent Authority is empowered to determine and forbids injunctions against action under the Act, but a bar of that kind has never excluded the constitutional writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court under Articles 226 and 32, which cannot be ousted by statute. A complainant whose disclosure was closed, an official penalised, or a discloser whose protection application was refused would therefore not have been remediless outside the Act's own appeal provision: the constitutional courts would have reviewed the Authority's decisions for legality, procedural fairness and proportionality, as they review every statutory authority. The Act's internal balance, often described as though the Competent Authority were a law unto itself, would in practice have operated under that standing judicial supervision, and several of the design's contested features, the certificated secrecy veto in Section 8 and the closure of complaints on a discreet inquiry, would have acquired their real meaning from writ litigation rather than from the text alone. That they have acquired no meaning at all is, once again, a function of dormancy: doctrines develop only when decisions exist to challenge.

The balance struck is therefore not one-sided, and saying so strengthens rather than weakens the case for commencement, because the stock objection that the Act would license a regime of denunciation does not survive a reading of its text. The Act requires a named complainant, a signed declaration, a discreet preliminary filter, hearings before penalties, an appellate route and a bar on collateral litigation. What it does not give the complained-against, significantly, is the complainant's name: identity is revealed to the head of the organisation only with the complainant's written consent, and the accused official must answer the allegation without knowing its source. That asymmetry is the deliberate heart of the design, justified by the power imbalance described at the start of this article, and it is the feature a respondent department would have tested first in litigation had the Act ever operated. Indian courts, applying the principles of natural justice, would have had to decide how an official can fairly answer a charge whose maker he cannot question, and the Act's answer, that the Authority itself tests the source and the evidence on his behalf, would have been the battleground. The question remains entirely theoretical, one more entry in the catalogue of doctrine this statute was supposed to generate and has not.

## The appointed day that never came

Section 1(3) of the Act provides that it shall come into force on such date as the Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, appoint, and that different dates may be appointed for different provisions. Appointed-day commencement is a common and usually innocent device: it lets the executive prepare rules, designate authorities and ready machinery before a statute's obligations bite, and most Acts that use it are commenced within months. The device places the final step of lawmaking in executive hands, however, and what the executive does not do cannot be compelled by the text. No notification appointing a date for any provision of the Whistle Blowers Protection Act has ever been issued. Successive governments have confirmed the position in Parliament in terms that do not vary: the Act requires amendments, aimed at safeguarding against disclosures affecting the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State and allied interests, before it is brought into force. A statement to that effect, recording the assent of 9 May 2014, the Gazette publication of 12 May 2014 and the absence of any commencement notification, was placed before Parliament in December 2025 in a written answer of the Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions. The position is therefore not an oversight awaiting discovery. It is a stated, maintained and repeatedly defended executive decision.

The consequences cascade through the Act's own text in ways worth tracing. Section 31 repeals the PIDPI Resolution of 21 April 2004, with a saving for anything done under it, but a repeal contained in an uncommenced Act has no effect, so the interim resolution continues to govern the field it was meant to vacate, and the Central Vigilance Commission continues to receive disclosures under a 2004 executive resolution rather than under the statute that designates it. Section 30 empowers the Central Government to remove difficulties in giving effect to the Act by order, but only within three years from the date of commencement, a clock that has never started and a power that presupposes an operating statute. The rules that Sections 25 and 26 contemplate, prescribing the procedure for disclosure and the manner and time of the discreet inquiry, have no function to perform until there is an Act in force to make them under. The annual report to Parliament under Section 23 reports on a nil return by definition. Every working part of the machine is present and every part is still, and the stillness is produced by the absence of a single one-page notification.

This Act is the third instance of a pattern this series has already met twice, and naming the pattern is the analytical point the article exists to make. Section 3 of the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978, which would have written safeguards into the preventive detention provisions of Article 22, was left to an appointed day and has never been brought into force, as the [article on the 44th Amendment Act, 1978](/2010/03/01/44th-amendment-act-1978-explained/) records in detail. The Freedom of Information Act, 2002 was enacted, never commenced, and then repealed by Section 31 of the Right to Information Act, 2005 without operating for a day, as the [legislative history of the RTI Act](/2011/09/15/rti-act-legislative-history/) sets out. The whistleblower statute completes the triad: a constitutional amendment safeguard withheld at the commencement stage, a transparency statute passed and never activated before repeal, and a protection statute unnotified for over a decade. Non-commencement is therefore not an accident that occasionally befalls Indian legislation. It is a recognisable and repeated instrument of legislative practice, a way for the political system to claim the credit of enactment while retaining the reality of the status quo, and it is nearly costless to use because commencement is invisible. A bill defeated is news; an assent withheld is news; a notification never issued is a silence, and the statute books, the examination guides and the government reports go on listing the Act among the laws of India.

The silence persists partly because non-commencement hides inside a crowd of respectable descriptions. The Act appears in the bare-Act compilations and on the statute databases without any marker distinguishing its status from that of operating law, and it is reproduced in full, because the text is complete and authentic. Examination guides and coaching material list it among the instruments of anti-corruption law in the present tense. Government reports on governance count the enactment among the measures taken against corruption. Journalists writing on a fresh retaliation case reach for the statute as the legal frame and describe a discloser as protected, or as having failed to use his protection, under a law that no discloser has ever been able to invoke. Each description is a small error with a common direction: all of them convert an enactment into an operation. The conversion matters beyond pedantry because it misdirects the remedy. A citizen who believes the Act works will ask why vigilance bodies are not enforcing it; a citizen who knows it was never commenced will ask the only question that leads anywhere, why the notification has not issued. Accuracy about commencement status is therefore not a bibliographical nicety but the difference between two politics, one of implementation complaint and one of enactment completion, and this series treats the point as foundational enough to repeat across the three articles that carry the three instances of the pattern.

The legal system's tools against that silence are weaker than a reader might expect, and the weakness has a precedent at the pattern's first instance. When the uncommenced Section 3 of the Forty-fourth Amendment was challenged in A.K. Roy v. Union of India in 1982, the Supreme Court declined to direct the government to bring the provision into force, treating the commencement decision as one the Constitution had left to the executive and not as a duty a court could enforce by mandamus. The reasoning was institutional: a court cannot itself assess the administrative readiness a commencement judgment embodies, and a direction to commence would make the court the author of the provision's operation. Whatever the force of that reasoning for a constitutional amendment in 1982, its effect across the pattern is cumulative and disabling. It means that for every appointed-day provision, the only remedies for indefinite non-commencement are political: parliamentary questions, public campaigns, and the pressure of accurate reporting. No writ has brought this Act into force, and on the authority of A.K. Roy none is likely to. The executive that benefits from the silence is also the only institution empowered to end it, which is the structural definition of the notification gap and the reason this series treats it as a technique of governance rather than a mishap of administration. The contrast with the Act's own subject matter is pointed. The statute was written for people whose complaint is that the system will not act on what it knows. Its own history is a demonstration of the complaint.

## The amendment that was offered as the reason, and then allowed to lapse

The government's explanation for non-commencement has always pointed to a defect in the Act as passed: it contained no bar on disclosures touching the security and sovereignty interests of the State, and the Official Secrets Act override in Section 4 meant a disclosure could lawfully carry classified material to the Competent Authority. The remedy proposed was the Whistle Blowers Protection (Amendment) Bill, 2015, introduced in the Lok Sabha on 11 May 2015 and passed by that House two days later, on 13 May 2015. The bill amended Section 4 to prohibit disclosures falling within ten categories of information, modelled, by the government's own statement of objects, on the exemptions in Section 8(1) of the Right to Information Act, 2005: information prejudicially affecting the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security, strategic, scientific or economic interests of the State, or relations with a foreign State, information leading to incitement of an offence, and the remaining heads covering matters such as Cabinet proceedings, information received in a fiduciary capacity, commercial confidence and intellectual property. A disclosure touching a prohibited category would not be inquired into; the Competent Authority would refer it to an authority authorised by the government, whose decision on the matter would be binding. The bill amended Section 5 so the Authority could not inquire into such a disclosure, amended Section 8 so no person could be required to assist an inquiry in a way that disclosed such information, and reversed the Act's relationship with the Official Secrets Act, so that disclosures covered by the secrecy statute would no longer be protected by the override.

The government's case, stated in Parliament by the Minister of State for Personnel, Dr Jitendra Singh, was that the amendments aligned the whistleblower law with the balance the information law had already struck between disclosure and security, and that a protection statute which could be used to move classified material without any screening was a risk no government could commence responsibly. Presented in that form, the argument is not frivolous. States do protect classified information, and a disclosure channel is a channel along which documents move.

The critics' case was stated most forensically in the clause-by-clause analysis published by Venkatesh Nayak of the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative in May 2015, and its analytical core was reinforced by PRS Legislative Research, whose examination of the bill made the decisive structural point. A disclosure under this Act is not a publication. It is made in confidence to a high constitutional or statutory authority, the Prime Minister, a presiding officer, a High Court or the Vigilance Commission, for the purpose of inquiry. To import into that confidential channel the exemption categories of a statute governing what a citizen may be given for public use is a category error, because the harms the information law's exemptions guard against arise from release into the open, not from transmission to the very authorities charged with guarding the State's interests. PRS pointed out two further differences the borrowing suppressed. Under the information law, exempt information may still be disclosed where the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm, and a refusal is subject to a two-stage appeal; the amendment bill contained neither a public interest override nor an appeal, and it routed the prohibited disclosure instead to an authority authorised by the government itself, whose qualifications and appointment process the bill did not specify and whose decision was made binding. The referral design drew a distinct objection that should be separated from the general outcry, because it goes to who decides. A disclosure touching a prohibited category was not to be destroyed or ignored; it was to be referred by the Competent Authority to an authority authorised by the Central Government, whose decision would be binding. The receiving authorities under the Act include constitutional offices, the Prime Minister, presiding officers and High Courts, and statutory vigilance bodies. The amendment proposed that where a disclosure concerned the most sensitive material, the decision would pass from those offices to an unnamed authority of the executive's own choosing, appointed by a process the bill did not describe, applying criteria it did not publish, and deciding finally. PRS Legislative Research flagged the absence of any specified qualifications or appointment process for that authority as a defect in itself. The design's tendency is plain: the categories of disclosure most likely to embarrass the State at its highest levels are exactly the categories routed away from the independent recipients to a recipient the State appoints. Defenders of the bill answered that security material cannot be left to authorities without security clearance and expertise, which is a genuine administrative point; but the bill did not answer it by clearing the existing authorities or by constituting a security-cleared bench within them. It answered by transfer to the executive, and the transfer, not the security concern, is what the criticism fastened on.

The critics added the point about the secrecy statute: the Act's override of the Official Secrets Act was the protection that made an official document usable as evidence of corruption, offences under that Act carry imprisonment of up to fourteen years, and removing the override while keeping the disclosure's documentary requirements would leave the discloser to choose between an unprovable allegation and a prosecution. On the critics' account, the ten categories, which include fiduciary information and commercial confidence, between them cover the documentary substance of most corruption in procurement, contracting and public finance, so the amendment did not trim the Act's edge so much as remove its substance.

The years between the bill's introduction and its lapse are documented in an unusual way, through the parliamentary question. Session after session, members of both Houses asked the government when the Act would be brought into force, and the Ministry of Personnel's written answers, of which the statement given by Dr Jitendra Singh on 30 November 2016 is representative, repeated a formula that did not change in substance across the period: the amendment bill had been introduced to safeguard against disclosures affecting sovereignty, security and allied interests, it had passed the Lok Sabha, it was pending in the Rajya Sabha. The formula was accurate at every point and empty in aggregate, because it described a process that was not moving. A bill pending in a House whose business the government substantially controls is pending by choice, and the Rajya Sabha was never asked to vote on the measure, neither to pass it nor to reject it. The Upper House's composition in those years gave the government genuine reason to expect difficulty with contentious legislation, and that difficulty is part of the political record; but a government that treats a bill as indispensable does not ordinarily leave it unlisted for four years and then let dissolution take it. The questions and the formulaic answers are, in the end, the most complete official history of the amendment: a precondition announced, a bill introduced, a House not asked, a dissolution accepted.

The bill was never enacted. It passed the Lok Sabha but was not passed by the Rajya Sabha, and it lapsed on the dissolution of the Sixteenth Lok Sabha in 2019. The sequence is the fact on which any verdict about the decade of non-commencement must rest. The government's stated position was that the Act could not be commenced without the amendment. The amendment was within the government's power to pursue through both Houses for four years after its introduction, and it was allowed to lapse instead. The result is the position the country has: neither the amendment the government said was necessary nor the commencement the government said depended on it. Whatever view is taken of the amendment's merits, the sequence refutes the suggestion that non-commencement was a pause in an active legislative process. It was, and remains, the settled outcome.

## What actually protects a whistleblower in India

Because the statute does not operate, the practical question the brief for this article identified as the one that matters, what protection a person who discloses corruption actually has, must be answered from the substitutes. They are set out in the table below and then examined in prose, because each substitute protects a different population against a different harm by a different mechanism, and the differences are where the gap shows.

| Protection | In force | Who it covers | What it provides | Practical limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014 | No, never commenced | Public servants complained against across the Union and the states, and any person making a disclosure | A designated Competent Authority for each class of public servant, a duty to conceal identity, protection against victimisation with restoration to the status quo ante, and penalties for revealing identity or making a false disclosure | Exists only as enacted text; no authority has ever received a disclosure under it and no protection under it has ever been given |
| The PIDPI Resolution, 2004 | Yes, as an executive resolution | Employees of the Central Government and of central corporations, government companies, societies and local authorities controlled by the Centre | The Central Vigilance Commission as designated agency to receive disclosures, confidentiality of the complainant's identity, inquiry through reports from the organisation, and recommendations for action and protection | Not a statute and creates no enforceable right or offence, covers the central sphere only, takes no anonymous complaints, and protects by administrative practice rather than by legal entitlement |
| The Companies Act, 2013 vigil mechanism | Yes | Directors and employees of listed companies and of prescribed classes of companies | A statutory duty under Section 177 to establish a vigil mechanism for reporting genuine concerns, with safeguards against victimisation and direct access to the chairperson of the audit committee in appropriate cases | Protects the corporate reporter about corporate wrongdoing; it does not reach disclosures against public servants or the machinery of the state |
| The SEBI informant mechanism for insider trading | Yes, from December 2019 | Any informant with original information about insider trading in securities | A confidential channel to the regulator's Office of Informant Protection, confidentiality of identity, protection against retaliation by market entities, and a reward of ten per cent of the monetary sanctions recovered, capped at one crore rupees | Confined to insider trading; the reward depends on recovery, and identity must be disclosed before a reward is paid |
| The Witness Protection Scheme, 2018 | Yes, as law declared by the Supreme Court until legislation is enacted | Witnesses in criminal proceedings, graded by threat perception | Protection measures ordered by a competent authority on a threat analysis, including concealment of identity, escort, relocation and related measures, funded through a witness protection fund | Protects a person as a witness inside a criminal case; it is not a channel for reporting wrongdoing and gives no remedy against service retaliation |
| Constitutional remedies and court directions | Yes | Any person whose rights are violated | Writ jurisdiction under Articles 32 and 226, through which courts have ordered protection in individual cases and laid down interim arrangements pending legislation | Case-specific and after the event; there is no general judicially created whistleblower protection, and a court acts on the facts placed before it, not as a standing receiving authority |

The PIDPI mechanism in practice is worth describing at ground level, because it is the route a central government discloser actually travels. The complainant writes to the Secretary of the Central Vigilance Commission, or submits the complaint through the Commission's complaint channels, in a sealed cover, identifying himself to the Commission and setting out the allegation with the supporting material he holds. The Commission does not act on anonymous complaints, and it warns complainants that a complaint in the PIDPI stream must not be copied to other authorities, since circulation is how identities escape. The Commission screens the complaint, and where it discloses a vigilance angle it calls for a report from the chief vigilance officer of the organisation concerned or refers the matter for investigation, including, in suitable cases, to the Central Bureau of Investigation. The complainant's identity is withheld from the organisation throughout. On the report, the Commission advises the disciplinary authority on the course to follow, and where the complainant reports victimisation the Commission can take up the matter with the organisation and seek protection for him. The Commission publishes aggregate figures for the complaints it receives and disposes of under the resolution in its annual reporting, and the answer placed before Parliament in December 2025 annexed the data for the years 2020 to 2024, which is evidence that the interim channel carries a substantial and continuing traffic a decade after the statute meant to replace it was enacted. What the description should make plain is the source of the mechanism's authority at every step: the Commission's statutory vigilance jurisdiction and its administrative standing, not any right of the complainant. The complainant is a beneficiary of a practice. Practices can be excellent, and by the standards of Indian administrative arrangements this one is conscientiously run. A practice is nonetheless revocable, non-justiciable in its particulars and bounded by the resolution's central-only reach, and those three qualities are the whole difference between it and the Act.

The corporate mechanisms deserve a fuller word, because they are the part of the landscape that actually functions and because their functioning proves the design questions are answerable. Section 177(9) of the Companies Act, 2013 requires every listed company and such other classes of company as are prescribed to establish a vigil mechanism for directors and employees to report genuine concerns, and Section 177(10) requires the mechanism to provide adequate safeguards against victimisation of the persons who use it and direct access to the chairperson of the audit committee in appropriate or exceptional cases. The securities regulator's listing regulations impose a parallel requirement on listed entities. The Securities and Exchange Board of India went further in 2019, inserting an informant mechanism into its insider trading regulations with effect from 26 December 2019: an informant with original information about insider trading may submit it to a dedicated Office of Informant Protection, the informant's identity is held in confidence, market entities are forbidden to retaliate, and a reward of ten per cent of the monetary sanctions collected, up to one crore rupees, rewards information that leads to disgorgement of at least one crore rupees. The mechanism's operating detail shows how deliberately it was engineered around the informant's exposure. Information is submitted on a prescribed form to the Office of Informant Protection, a unit separated from the regulator's enforcement divisions for the purpose. An informant may file through a legal representative, in which case his identity need not be revealed even to the regulator at the time of filing, and is disclosed only before a reward is paid; the regulations forbid listed companies, intermediaries and fiduciaries from subjecting an employee to harassment, demotion, suspension or termination for reporting, and require their internal codes of conduct to say so. The reward is calibrated to outcome: ten per cent of the monetary sanctions actually collected, where the information has led to disgorgement of at least one crore rupees, with an interim reward of up to ten lakh rupees available at the stage of the final order. Set beside the public sector design, two choices stand out. The securities mechanism pays for information, accepting that the informant's motive may be mixed, where the whistleblower Act, following the Law Commission, protects only and pays nothing. And the securities mechanism solved the identification dilemma in the opposite direction from the Act: instead of requiring the informant to identify himself to the authority as the price of action, it permits the identity to be withheld from the authority itself until the reward stage, on the reasoning that a channel nobody dares use yields no information to protect. The contrast does not prove the securities design right for public administration, where the accused is the State and the material is its files. It proves that the identification question has more than one workable answer in Indian regulatory practice, and that the Act's answer was a choice, not a necessity.

Here, in the securities market, the Indian state operates a disclosure channel with confidentiality, anti-retaliation protection and a financial incentive, the three features whose absence from public administration is explained by their supposed impracticality. The population covered is different, an informant about market abuse rather than an official reporting his own department, and the comparison should not be pushed past that. But the mechanism's existence disposes of the argument that confidential, protected, rewarded disclosure is administratively impossible in India. It is done, routinely, where a regulator wanted it done.

The Witness Protection Scheme, 2018 answers a different need and should not be confused with whistleblower protection, though the two are often run together in public discussion. The scheme, prepared by the Union Government with the National Legal Services Authority and approved by the Supreme Court on 5 December 2018 in Mahender Chawla v. Union of India, was declared by the Court to be law under Articles 141 and 142 of the Constitution until Parliament or the state legislatures enact suitable legislation, and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 obliges every State Government to prepare and notify a witness protection scheme. It grades witnesses by threat and orders measures proportionate to the threat. A whistleblower whose disclosure matures into a prosecution may be protected under it as a witness in that case. What it does not do is receive the disclosure, inquire into the allegation, conceal the reporter within an administrative process, or undo a punitive transfer. It begins where the criminal process begins, which is usually after the events that destroy a discloser's career.

One more institution belongs in the map, because citizens frequently assume it fills the gap. The Lokpal, the anti-corruption ombudsman created by the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 and operational since its first chairperson and members took office in 2019, receives complaints alleging offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 against public servants, including, with safeguards, the Prime Minister, ministers and members of Parliament. A person with evidence of corruption by a public servant therefore has a standing national institution to complain to, and that is a real addition to the landscape the 2004 resolution left. But the Lokpal is a receiving and prosecuting institution for the allegation, not a protection regime for the complainant. Its statute is organised around inquiry and prosecution of the accused, and it was not designed to conceal a complainant inside a disclosure procedure, to reverse the burden in a retaliation claim, or to restore a transferred clerk to his desk. Complaint and protection are different functions, and the Indian system, having built fragments of each in different statutes, has commenced the complaint fragments and left the protection statute dormant. A whistleblower is thus better supplied with places to send the allegation than with law to stand behind him when he sends it, which is precisely the inversion a protection Act exists to correct.

What remains is the constitutional route, and its limits must be stated exactly, because overstating it is the mirror image of describing the Act as operative. There is no general judicially declared protection for whistleblowers in India. What exists is the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and the High Courts under Articles 32 and 226, through which a person facing victimisation or threat can seek, and in individual cases has received, directions for protection, for the quashing of retaliatory proceedings, or for an investigation to be transferred or monitored. It was the Supreme Court's insistence, in the proceedings after Satyendra Dubey's murder, that an interim machinery exist pending legislation, which produced the PIDPI Resolution. Those are real powers exercised in real cases, but they are reactive, case-specific and dependent on the discloser reaching a constitutional court with counsel, usually after the retaliation has occurred. A writ petition is a remedy for a person already in trouble. It is not a channel, it confers no confidentiality by design, and it imposes no duty on any authority to receive and inquire into disclosures as a routine function. The service-law route beneath the writ jurisdiction tells the same story at lower altitude. A government servant punished after a disclosure may challenge the transfer, the charge-sheet or the adverse entry before the administrative tribunal or the High Court on the ordinary grounds of service law, mala fides among them, and occasionally wins. But the tribunal proceeding is about the service order, not about the disclosure: the corruption allegation is not inquired into, the complainant's identity enjoys no procedural protection within it, the burden of proving mala fides rests on the servant, and the litigation runs for years while the posting consequences are lived in real time. Nor do the government's general grievance channels alter the position. A complaint about corruption sent through a departmental grievance portal or to a minister's office is an administrative representation, forwarded in the ordinary course to the very hierarchy it concerns, with no confidentiality discipline, no inquiry powers and no protection attaching to its maker. The landscape, surveyed whole, has institutions for punishing corruption, institutions for complaining about it, and institutions for litigating its consequences for the complainant. The missing institution is the one the Act designed: a body whose statutory business is the disclosure itself and the safety of the person who made it. The honest summary of the substitutes is therefore short. For wrongdoing inside a listed company or a securities market, working mechanisms exist. For wrongdoing inside the state, a central government employee has an administrative resolution of 2004 with a conscientious vigilance commission at its head, a state government employee has less, an information applicant has his own name on his application, and everyone has the courts after the fact. The statute that would have replaced that patchwork with a designed system is the one on the shelf.

### What should a person do before making a disclosure?

A person considering a disclosure should first preserve the evidence outside the office that controls it: keep dated copies of the documents, orders and correspondence that establish the wrongdoing in a private and secure place, and build a written chronology while memory is fresh.

The practical reasoning behind that answer is worth setting out, because the statute's absence changes the preparation a prudent person makes. The office file can be weeded, transferred or reconstructed once an inquiry begins, and memory untested by contemporaneous notes degrades quickly under questioning, so the copies and the chronology are not administrative tidiness but the substance of the case in portable form. A disclosure under the PIDPI Resolution goes to the Central Vigilance Commission in a sealed cover, and a disclosure about a state servant goes to whatever vigilance machinery the state maintains, and in neither case does a statute guarantee the handling. Documents in a personal file should therefore be organised so they can be read by someone who knows nothing of the office: a short statement of what happened, the chronology, and the records in the order the chronology cites. A disclosure file is usually a bundle of office documents in mixed formats, and a person assembling one can open and check spreadsheet, document and presentation files before anything is sent using [the ReportMedic office file viewer for Excel, Word and PowerPoint files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which keeps the working set readable without altering the originals. Legal advice before disclosing, from a lawyer unconnected with the organisation, is the second precaution the record of retaliation justifies, since the choice of channel determines what protection, if any, attaches, and a disclosure made to the wrong recipient, to the press or to a superior inside the chain, acquires no protection from any of the mechanisms described here. This general account of the position is legal information about how the mechanisms work, not advice on any individual case, and a person whose livelihood or safety is at stake should take advice on his or her own facts before acting, because the consequences of a disclosure cannot be recalled once the envelope is opened.

## The state storey that was never built

The Act's design is federal in a way its substitutes are not. The definitions assign state public servants to State Vigilance Commissions or notified state authorities, Section 26 empowers State Governments to make rules for carrying out the Act, and Section 29 requires state notifications and rules to be laid before the state legislature. On paper, therefore, commencement would have activated a two-storey structure: the central channel through the Vigilance Commission and the other designated authorities, and a state channel in every state, each with its own rules and its own legislature watching the annual reports. Most public employment in India is state employment, and most of the corruption a citizen encounters, in the ration shop, the police station, the municipal office, the public works subdivision, is state corruption. The Act's centre of practical gravity was always going to be its state storey.

That storey exists in even worse repair than the central one, because the interim arrangements never pretended to cover it. The PIDPI Resolution is a central instrument for central employees. State vigilance arrangements are a patchwork of executive bodies of varying age, staffing and independence, created by resolution or, in a minority of states, operating alongside a Lokayukta institution of the state's own design, and no state framework was built as the state half of the whistleblower scheme. The Act itself anticipated unevenness: its designation for state servants runs to the State Vigilance Commission, if any, a parenthesis that concedes some states have no such commission to designate, and falls back on an officer or authority the State Government notifies. Commencement would therefore have begun, in several states, with a designation exercise and a rule-making exercise that the states had no institutional reason to prepare for a central statute whose own commencement date was unknown. The notification gap reproduces itself down the federal structure: a central omission licenses state inaction, and state inaction is then available as evidence that the machinery is not ready, in a circle that only a commencement date can break. For the citizen whose wrong is a state wrong, which is to say for most citizens, the practical content of the decade of non-commencement is not that a Delhi authority is dormant. It is that the channel nearest his grievance was never even provisionally constructed.

## The honest assessment: a defective Act, and no defence for the shelf

Two propositions about this statute are true at once, and commentary on it usually fails by asserting only one of them. The first is that the Act as passed has real defects, acknowledged by many of its own supporters. It bars the anonymous disclosure, which excludes the most exposed informants at the threshold. It imprisons the maker of a mala fide false disclosure for up to two years, in terms a cautious official will read as a risk attaching to any disclosure that fails. It leaves the inquiry's outcome recommendatory. It allows the government to wall off evidence from the inquiry by its own certificate under Section 8. It excludes the senior judiciary. A Parliament commencing the Act without revisiting at least the anonymity bar and the certificate mechanism would be commencing a protection statute of middling strength, and the scholars and campaigners who pressed for the law, the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative prominent among them in its commentaries on both the Act and the amendment bill, have said as much in print.

The second proposition is that none of that justifies the outcome that exists. The choice the executive faced across a decade was never between this Act and a better one. It was between this Act and nothing, and nothing is what a discloser in a state government office, or an applicant under the information law, or a subordinate in a central ministry whose complaint falls outside the vigilance resolution's comfort, actually has. A middling statute in force generates the things a dormant one cannot: designated authorities obliged to act, a body of practice, reported figures, litigation that exposes the weak provisions to judicial and public scrutiny, and a standing text that amendment can improve incrementally, as Indian legislatures routinely improve working statutes. The argument that the Act should wait for perfection has the direction of legislative learning backwards. Statutes are not perfected on the shelf. They are perfected, if at all, in operation, and a decade on the shelf has produced exactly one amendment to this Act: the correction of its year in the title.

The government's implicit position, that the 2015 amendment was needed first and that commencing an Act about to be amended would be wasteful, fails on its own terms for reasons already recorded in this article: the amendment was the government's to carry, it was carried through one House in two days when the government wished, and it was then left unpressed in the other House until dissolution extinguished it. A government that regarded the amendment as the precondition of a working protection regime had four years to enact it and a commencement notification to issue the day after. It did neither, and after the bill lapsed it introduced no successor. The inference the record supports is that protection of whistleblowers commands assent as a proposition and no priority as a programme, from governments of both the coalitions that have held office since 2014, the first of which passed the Act in its final parliamentary weeks and the second of which inherited the notification pen. That distribution of responsibility should be stated, because accounts of the delay that assign it to one party misdescribe a bipartisan institutional preference for the credit of the statute without the inconvenience of its operation.

What commencement would actually set in motion is worth spelling out, because the demand is sometimes dismissed as symbolism, as though notifying the Act would change nothing on the ground. A commencement notification under Section 1(3), which may appoint different dates for different provisions, would be followed by obligations the text already contains. The Central and State Governments would make rules under Sections 25 and 26 prescribing the procedure for disclosure and the manner and time of the discreet inquiry. The Competent Authorities would make regulations under Section 27, with governmental approval, and would specify the offices to receive disclosures on their behalf under Section 4(2). Every public authority would have to create the appropriate machinery Section 9 requires for dealing with disclosures sent to it. The authorities the Act designates by notification, for the Article 33 services at the Centre and for state servants in states without a vigilance commission, would have to be notified. The Central Vigilance Commission would acquire, in place of its resolution-based role, a statutory jurisdiction with compulsory powers, an offences chapter behind it and an annual reporting duty to Parliament under Section 23 that includes its work under the Act. None of that requires fresh primary legislation, and all of it is the ordinary implementation traffic that follows any commenced statute. The barrier has never been capacity. The machinery clauses were drafted precisely so that commencement would generate the machinery, and the argument from unreadiness inverts the design.

It should also be recorded that commencement and amendment are not alternatives, and never were. Indian legislative practice routinely commences a statute and amends it in parallel as operation exposes defects; the statute book is full of Acts whose first amending Act followed within a few years of commencement for exactly that reason. A government persuaded that the Act's security carve-out is too narrow had both instruments in its hands throughout: notify the Act, so that its protection machinery begins and its disclosure channel opens, and carry the amendment bill on its merits, so that the carve-out debate is held about a working system whose records would show what disclosures actually contain. The 2015 bill's critics, it is worth noting, did not defend the enacted text as perfect; the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative's analysis criticised the bill while the wider campaign literature had long criticised the Act's own anonymity bar and false-disclosure penalty. The reform conversation, in other words, had material for a better statute from both directions. What it never had was an operating statute to reform. Presented with the sequence, the plea that amendment had to precede commencement reads less as legislative method than as the mechanism by which neither happened.

The verdict this article defends is therefore narrow and evidentiary. Passed is not the same as in force, and the distance between the two is not a technicality but the substance of the matter for the only readers whose interests are existential here, the people deciding whether to report what they know. The Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014 should be cited, taught and reported with its commencement status attached, as a statute enacted and never brought into force, and the recurring demand to notify it, made by the campaigners who drafted its ancestry and by the parliamentary questioners who extract the government's repeated answer, should be understood as the most concrete and achievable demand in Indian anti-corruption politics: it requires no majority, no drafting and no money, only a signature on a notification that has been waiting since 12 May 2014. Until that signature exists, the substitutes mapped in this article are the whole of the law on the subject, and they should be used with a clear understanding of how much thinner they are than the design Parliament approved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Whistle Blowers Protection Act provide?

The Act provides a complete mechanism for receiving and inquiring into public interest disclosures against public servants. A disclosure may allege an offence under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, wilful misuse of power or discretion causing demonstrable loss to the government or wrongful gain, or the commission of a criminal offence. Each disclosure goes to a Competent Authority designated for the class of public servant concerned. The Authority inquires with the powers of a civil court, must conceal the complainant's identity, may direct protection against victimisation including restoration to the position held before retaliation, and the Act creates penalties for revealing a complainant's identity and for making a false disclosure. None of this operates, because the Act has never been brought into force.

### Q: Can a whistleblower make an anonymous complaint under the Act?

No. Section 4(6) of the Act provides that no action shall be taken on a public interest disclosure if the disclosure does not indicate the identity of the complainant, or if the identity given is found to be incorrect or false. The discloser must name himself to the Competent Authority and must also give a personal declaration that he reasonably believes the information and the allegation to be substantially true. In exchange, the Authority is placed under a statutory duty to conceal his identity from the organisation complained against and from everyone else, and revealing it negligently or in bad faith is a criminal offence. The bar on anonymity is the Act's most criticised feature, because the informants with the most valuable information are usually those with the most to lose from identification.

### Q: Who can receive a whistleblower complaint under the Act?

The recipient is the Competent Authority designated in Section 3 for the class of public servant complained against. For a Union minister it is the Prime Minister; for a member of Parliament, the Chairman of the Council of States or the Speaker of the House of the People according to the House; for a state minister, the Chief Minister; for a state legislator, the presiding officer of the House concerned; for subordinate judges and others discharging adjudicatory functions, the High Court. For the great body of central government servants and employees of central public sector bodies, it is the Central Vigilance Commission or another authority the Central Government notifies, and for state government servants it is the State Vigilance Commission or a notified state authority.

### Q: What powers does the Competent Authority have during an inquiry?

Under Sections 7 to 10, the Competent Authority may require any public servant or any other person to furnish information or produce documents relevant to the inquiry. It has all the powers of a civil court trying a suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: summoning and examining persons on oath, requiring discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records and issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses or documents. No claim of secrecy under the Official Secrets Act, 1923 and no privilege over documents may be raised against it, subject to the Section 8 carve-out for security and Cabinet material. It may also take the assistance of the Delhi Special Police Establishment or the police to complete an inquiry.

### Q: Who counts as a public servant under the Whistle Blowers Protection Act?

The Act borrows the definition of public servant from clause (c) of Section 2 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, which is a wide definition covering persons in the service or pay of the government or of bodies owned, controlled or aided by it, persons holding offices involving public duty, judges within its terms, employees of universities and aided institutions, and office-bearers of co-operative societies receiving government aid. The Act then makes one express exclusion of its own: a judge of the Supreme Court or of a judge of a High Court is not a public servant for its purposes, so no disclosure under the Act can be made against a judge of a constitutional court. The Special Protection Group is also taken outside the Act entirely by Section 2.

### Q: How does the Act protect a whistleblower from victimisation?

Section 11 obliges the Central Government to ensure that no person who has made a disclosure is victimised by the initiation of proceedings or otherwise merely for making it. A person who is being victimised, or is likely to be, may apply to the Competent Authority, which can give suitable directions to the public servant or authority concerned after hearing both sides. In that hearing the burden of proof lies on the public authority to show that its action was not victimisation. Directions are binding, and the power expressly includes directing the restoration of the discloser to the status quo ante, undoing the punitive transfer or proceeding. Wilful disobedience of a direction carries a penalty of up to thirty thousand rupees.

### Q: What is the punishment for revealing a whistleblower's identity?

Section 16 of the Act makes it an offence for any person negligently or in bad faith to reveal the identity of a complainant. The punishment is imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and a fine which may extend to fifty thousand rupees. The inclusion of negligence is deliberate: the historic failure in this field was the leaking of a discloser's identity through the ordinary movement of official correspondence rather than a deliberate betrayal, and the offence is drafted to catch careless handling as well as malicious exposure. Prosecution can only begin on a complaint by the Competent Authority or an officer it authorises, and the offence is triable by a court not inferior to a Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or a Chief Judicial Magistrate. The provision has never been used, because the Act is not in force.

### Q: What happens if a whistleblower's disclosure is found to be false or frivolous?

Two consequences follow, and they should be distinguished. If the Competent Authority, after inquiry, finds the allegations frivolous or vexatious, or finds insufficient grounds to proceed, it closes the matter under Section 5, having first given the complainant an opportunity of being heard if he wishes. Separately, Section 17 makes it an offence to make a disclosure in bad faith and knowing that it was incorrect, false or misleading, punishable with imprisonment of up to two years and a fine of up to thirty thousand rupees. An honest disclosure that cannot be proved is not, on the text, an offence: the prosecution must establish bad faith and knowledge of falsity. Critics argue that a discloser cannot rely on that distinction being observed in practice, which is why the provision is contested.

### Q: Is there a time limit for making a disclosure under the Act?

Yes. Section 6(3) provides that the Competent Authority shall not investigate any disclosure involving an allegation if the complaint is made after the expiry of seven years from the date on which the action complained against is alleged to have taken place. The limit runs from the conduct, not from its discovery, which makes it stricter than a limitation rule that starts when the wrong comes to light. Corruption schemes are often uncovered late, through an audit objection or a change of incumbency, and a disclosure assembled after the seven years have run falls outside the Act however strong its evidence. Section 6 also bars inquiry into matters already determined by a court or tribunal, matters under formal public inquiry, and matters referred to a commission of inquiry.

### Q: Can a whistleblower be prosecuted under the Official Secrets Act?

Under the Act as enacted, a disclosure made to the Competent Authority is protected against that risk in two ways. Section 4 opens with the words notwithstanding anything contained in the Official Secrets Act, 1923, so making the disclosure through the statutory channel is lawful even where the material is an official document. Section 7(4) adds that no secrecy obligation or privilege may be claimed against the Competent Authority in the inquiry, subject to Section 8. The Whistle Blowers Protection (Amendment) Bill, 2015 would have reversed this position and disallowed disclosures covered by the secrecy statute, which critics identified as one of its most damaging changes, since offences under that Act carry imprisonment of up to fourteen years. The bill lapsed, so the override stands in the enacted text, in a statute that is not in force.

### Q: Has the Whistle Blowers Protection Act ever been brought into force?

No. Section 1(3) leaves commencement to a date the Central Government may appoint by notification in the Official Gazette, and no such notification has ever been issued for any provision of the Act. The Act received presidential assent on 9 May 2014 and was published in the Gazette of India on 12 May 2014 as Act 17 of 2014, but publication is enactment, not commencement. The government has confirmed in written answers in Parliament, including an answer of the Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions in December 2025, that no commencement notification has been made, giving as the reason that amendments concerning sovereignty, security and allied interests were considered necessary first. The Act has accordingly never operated for a single day.

### Q: Why was the Whistle Blowers Protection Act never notified?

The reason the government has consistently given in Parliament is that the Act as passed contained no safeguard against disclosures affecting the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State, strategic and economic interests and relations with foreign States, and that it first required amendment on those lines. The Whistle Blowers Protection (Amendment) Bill, 2015 was introduced for that purpose. Whether that explanation accounts for the whole delay is contested. The amendment bill passed the Lok Sabha in May 2015 but was never passed by the Rajya Sabha and lapsed when the Sixteenth Lok Sabha was dissolved, and no successor bill was introduced. Critics therefore argue that non-commencement became a settled preference rather than a pause, since the government neither completed the amendment it said was necessary nor commenced the Act without it.

### Q: What did the 2015 whistleblower amendment bill propose?

The Whistle Blowers Protection (Amendment) Bill, 2015, introduced in the Lok Sabha on 11 May 2015 and passed by that House on 13 May 2015, proposed to amend Section 4 so that a disclosure could not be made on ten categories of information modelled on the exemptions in Section 8(1) of the Right to Information Act, 2005, covering sovereignty and security, strategic, scientific and economic interests, foreign relations, Cabinet proceedings, fiduciary information and commercial confidence among them. A disclosure touching a prohibited category would be referred to an authority authorised by the government, whose decision would be binding, and the Competent Authority would be barred from inquiring into it. The bill also reversed the Act's override of the Official Secrets Act, 1923. It was not passed by the Rajya Sabha and lapsed on the dissolution of the Sixteenth Lok Sabha.

### Q: How can a person complain to the Central Vigilance Commission under the PIDPI Resolution?

The Public Interest Disclosure and Protection of Informers Resolution, 2004 designates the Central Vigilance Commission as the agency to receive disclosures about corruption or misuse of office by employees of the Central Government and of corporations, government companies, societies and local authorities owned or controlled by the Centre. The complaint is sent in writing to the Secretary of the Commission in a sealed cover, with the complainant's identity stated to the Commission, which then withholds that identity from the organisation complained against. The Commission does not act on anonymous complaints. It seeks reports from the organisation, examines them and recommends action, and it may press for the complainant's protection. The resolution is an executive instrument, not a statute, so it creates no offence and no enforceable right, and it does not extend to state government servants.

### Q: What protection does an RTI activist actually have?

An applicant under the Right to Information Act, 2005 has no dedicated statutory protection. The information law itself contains no provision shielding an applicant from retaliation, and the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, which was designed to protect persons who disclose wrongdoing by public servants, has never been brought into force, so it supplies nothing either. What exists is the general law: the criminal law against assault, intimidation and murder, police protection sought in an individual case, the Witness Protection Scheme, 2018 where the applicant becomes a witness in a criminal proceeding, and the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court, through which protection directions have been given on particular facts. An information applicant also applies under his own name, since the information law requires the applicant to identify himself, which is the exposure the missing statute was meant to answer.

### Q: Does the Whistle Blowers Protection Act protect employees in private companies?

No. The Act is concerned with disclosures against public servants, a term it takes from the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, and its machinery of Competent Authorities, vigilance commissions and presiding officers belongs entirely to the public sphere. An employee of a private company who reports wrongdoing by his employer is outside it. Protection for corporate reporters comes from a different body of law that is in force: Section 177 of the Companies Act, 2013 requires listed companies and prescribed classes of companies to maintain a vigil mechanism for directors and employees to report genuine concerns, with safeguards against victimisation, and the securities regulator's regulations impose a corresponding requirement on listed entities. The contrast is instructive, because the corporate mechanism operates while the public sector statute does not.

### Q: What is the vigil mechanism under the Companies Act, 2013?

Section 177(9) of the Companies Act, 2013 requires every listed company, and such other classes of companies as are prescribed, to establish a vigil mechanism for directors and employees to report genuine concerns in the manner prescribed. Section 177(10) requires the mechanism to provide adequate safeguards against victimisation of persons who use it, and to provide for direct access to the chairperson of the audit committee in appropriate or exceptional cases. Details of the mechanism must be disclosed on the company's website and in the board's report. The securities regulator's listing regulations require listed entities to maintain a whistleblower policy on similar lines. The mechanism is an internal reporting channel supervised by the audit committee, not an external authority, and it addresses wrongdoing within the company rather than by public servants.

### Q: Does the Act give any reward to a whistleblower?

No. The Whistle Blowers Protection Act contains no reward, bounty or share of recoveries for the person making a disclosure. Its inducements are protective rather than financial: concealment of identity, a bar on victimisation with a power to restore the discloser to the position held before retaliation, and penalties against those who expose or obstruct. The absence of a reward was a deliberate feature of the Law Commission model on which the Act rests, which treated disclosure as a civic act to be protected rather than a service to be purchased. The contrast within Indian law is the securities regulator's informant mechanism for insider trading, introduced in 2019, under which an informant whose original information leads to disgorgement may receive ten per cent of the monetary sanctions collected, up to a ceiling of one crore rupees.

### Q: Which cases led to the whistleblower law being drafted?

The demand is associated above all with two killings. Satyendra Dubey, an engineer with the National Highways Authority of India working on the Golden Quadrilateral programme, wrote to the Prime Minister's Office in 2002 identifying corruption in the project and asking that his identity be kept secret; his identity was not protected and he was murdered in November 2003. Shanmugam Manjunath, an Indian Oil Corporation officer who had acted against petrol pumps selling adulterated fuel in Uttar Pradesh, was murdered in November 2005. Alongside those events stand the institutional steps: the Law Commission's One Hundred and Seventy-ninth Report of 2001, which supplied a draft bill, the Supreme Court's insistence on an interim machinery, which produced the 2004 vigilance resolution, and the Second Administrative Reforms Commission's recommendation of 2007.

### Q: What happens to a whistleblower in India today?

A person who reports corruption by a public servant has no operative protection statute. If the complaint concerns a central government employee, it may be made to the Central Vigilance Commission under the Public Interest Disclosure and Protection of Informers Resolution, 2004, an executive arrangement that promises confidentiality and inquiry but creates no enforceable right and no offence. A state government employee's complaint depends on the vigilance machinery of the state. If retaliation follows, the remedies are those of the general law: service-law challenges to a punitive transfer or proceeding, the criminal law where threats or violence occur, the Witness Protection Scheme, 2018 if the person becomes a witness in a prosecution, and a writ petition to a constitutional court seeking protection on the facts of the case. Each is reactive, and none replaces the uncommenced Act.
