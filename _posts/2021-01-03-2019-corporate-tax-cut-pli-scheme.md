The 2019 corporate tax cut and the Production Linked Incentive scheme that followed it are usually filed under tax policy and industrial promotion, as if the first were a technical adjustment to a rate schedule and the second a subsidy programme with better branding. The record resists that filing. On 20 September 2019, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman stood in Goa, where the Goods and Services Tax Council was meeting, and announced that an ordinance signed that morning had cut the base rate of corporation tax from 30 percent to 22 percent for domestic companies willing to surrender their exemptions, and to 15 percent for new manufacturing companies incorporated after 1 October 2019. Fourteen months later, on 11 November 2020, the Union Cabinet extended a production subsidy design first tested on mobile telephones to ten more sectors at a stroke, taking the committed incentive envelope to Rs 1.97 lakh crore. Between those two decisions sits a third, Prime Minister Narendra Modi's Atmanirbhar Bharat address of 12 May 2020, which supplied the doctrine under which the subsidy programme would be sold to the country. This article defends one claim about that sequence: the 2019 corporate tax cut and the post-2020 PLI scheme operate as political architecture for the post-2019 economic order, and a five-feature map of the sequence captures content that the technical-tax-policy framing and the policy-implementation framing each leave out.

## The Goa Announcement: A Decisive Moment Staged Outside the Budget

The setting was the first fact. India's corporation tax rate had been set, adjusted and promised away inside budget speeches for three decades, and the budget of 5 July 2019, Sitharaman's first, had left the rate structure for large companies untouched at 30 percent while extending the concessional 25 percent rate to companies with turnover up to Rs 400 crore. Eleven weeks later the rate fell anyway, and it fell in Goa, at a press conference held alongside the thirty-seventh meeting of the GST Council, through an ordinance rather than a finance bill. Every element of that staging carried a political message that a budget paragraph could not have carried: the decision was presented as an act of the whole government at a moment of economic alarm, announced by the Finance Minister in person, effective immediately, and deliberately detached from the parliamentary calendar that would have delayed it to February 2020.

The market verdict arrived within the trading day. The Bombay Stock Exchange Sensex closed 1,921 points higher on 20 September 2019, a rise of 5.32 percent and the index's largest single-day gain in a decade, as recorded in the exchange's own session data and reported across the financial press that evening. Government bond yields moved the other way, rising on the fiscal arithmetic the Finance Ministry itself supplied: Sitharaman put the revenue forgone from the rate cut and the accompanying reliefs at Rs 1.45 lakh crore in a year, roughly 0.7 percent of gross domestic product. A finance minister announcing a revenue loss of that size as the centrepiece of a stimulus was making a claim about what the state could afford to surrender, and the size of the figure was part of the announcement's force. A smaller, quieter adjustment would have been read as tinkering. Rs 1.45 lakh crore was read, by markets, by industry federations and by the opposition, as a government staking its second-term economic reputation on a single instrument.

The sequence framing the announcement matters as much as the day itself. The second Modi government had been formed on 30 May 2019, after the verdict declared on 23 May 2019 returned the Bharatiya Janata Party with 303 seats, and [the post-2019 election political-architectural context](/2019/08/16/2019-election-verdict-explained/) gave the cabinet an unusual asset: a fresh five-year mandate with no coalition arithmetic to manage. The tax cut was the first great economic decision taken with that asset, and it was taken under pressure, after six quarters of decelerating growth, a collapsed shadow-banking sector and a public argument about whether the slowdown was cyclical or structural. Reading the decision inside [the post-2014 PMO architecture context](/2019/08/11/pmo-centralisation-question-india/) sharpens the point: the announcement bore the marks of a decision cleared at the apex and executed through the Finance Ministry as a single move, the ordinance route chosen precisely because it required no one's consent outside the executive.

### Why was the tax cut announced in Goa during a GST Council meeting?

The Goa venue was where the Finance Minister already was: the GST Council, which Sitharaman chairs, held its thirty-seventh meeting in Panaji on 20 September 2019. The choice converted a federal fiscal gathering into the stage for a Union tax decision, four months ahead of the February budget, which was the political point.

The ordinance form deserves a paragraph of its own, because form was content. An ordinance under the Constitution takes effect on promulgation and lapses unless Parliament converts it into an Act, a device the founding generation designed for recess emergencies. The government used it in September, with Parliament due to sit in November, which means the urgency was constructed rather than imposed by the calendar: the executive judged that the signal value of immediacy outweighed the deliberative cost, and it accepted the obligation to defend the measure twice, once to the markets in September and once to Parliament in the winter session. When the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Bill reached the Lok Sabha, Sitharaman defended the revenue figure on the floor, telling members in the debate of early December 2019 that approximately Rs 1.45 lakh crore could be forgone and that the government accepted the charge knowingly, as the price of reviving investment. The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act received assent on 11 December 2019, converting the ordinance into permanent law with retrospective effect from the ordinance's date.

## Feature One: The Ordinance Read by Its Text

The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Ordinance of 20 September 2019 worked by inserting two new sections into the Income-tax Act of 1961, and the architecture of the post-2019 corporate tax order is legible in those two sections alone. Section 115BAA offered every domestic company a base rate of 22 percent, available on one hard condition: the company had to surrender the exemptions, deductions and incentives the code had accumulated over decades, from accelerated depreciation preferences to the area-based and sector-based holidays of the 1990s and 2000s. With the 10 percent surcharge and 4 percent health and education cess applied, the effective rate for a company electing the section came to 25.17 percent. The election, once made, could not be withdrawn. Section 115BAB addressed the future rather than the stock of existing firms: a domestic company incorporated on or after 1 October 2019, set up to manufacture, and beginning production before a statutory deadline (31 March 2023 in the ordinance, later extended to 31 March 2024) would pay 15 percent, an effective 17.01 percent with surcharge and cess, on the same condition of surrendering exemptions.

Three further provisions completed the instrument, and each had a named constituency. Companies that stayed inside the exemption regime had their Minimum Alternate Tax reduced from 18.5 percent to 15 percent, while companies electing the new sections were freed of the Minimum Alternate Tax altogether, which removed the anomaly under which a firm could owe a floor tax in a year of book profit and cash loss. The enhanced surcharge on capital gains that the July 2019 budget had imposed, and which had driven foreign portfolio investors to withdraw funds through August 2019, was withdrawn for gains on securities and derivatives. The tax on share buybacks, introduced in the same July budget, was disapplied to buybacks announced before 5 July 2019. Read as a whole, the ordinance did three things at once: it cut the headline price of being a company in India, it began closing the exemption economy that had made the headline rate largely fictional for well-advised firms, and it repaired, inside ninety days, two wounds the government's own first budget had inflicted on market sentiment.

The domestic base rates against which the cut operated explain its scale. Before September 2019, a domestic company with turnover above Rs 400 crore paid 30 percent at base, which with the maximum surcharge and cess produced an effective rate near 35 percent for the largest firms; companies below the turnover threshold paid 25 percent after the phased reductions of the late 2010s. The 22 percent base therefore cut the statutory burden on the largest companies by more than a quarter, and the comparison the Finance Ministry invited was international. When Sitharaman presented the figures in Goa, the ministry's accompanying material placed the effective 25.17 percent alongside the rates of the Asian economies with which India competes for manufacturing investment, a comparison the government repeated in Parliament and in investor meetings through the following year.

The ordinance also completed a promise that an earlier finance minister had made and not kept. In his budget speech of 28 February 2015, Arun Jaitley had announced that the corporation tax rate would be reduced from 30 percent to 25 percent over four years, with exemptions withdrawn in parallel. The four years passed with the reduction delivered only to smaller companies, and the exemption withdrawal stalled against the resistance of every sector holding a holiday. The September 2019 instrument achieved in one ordinance what the 2015 schedule had failed to achieve by instalments, and it did so by inverting the method: instead of withdrawing exemptions from firms that held them, it offered firms a clean rate in exchange for walking away from the exemptions themselves. That inversion is the first piece of evidence for the article's claim. A purely technical tax reform could have taken either route. The route chosen distributed the political costs so that no named beneficiary lost a named benefit by state action; firms surrendered their holidays by election, at a price the state set. The design was a political settlement dressed as a rate schedule.

A final textual feature belongs in the record, because it governed everything that followed. The 15 percent rate for new manufacturing was fenced with dates: incorporation on or after 1 October 2019, commencement of production by the deadline, manufacture as the qualifying activity. The fence converted the tax code into a recruitment instrument aimed at a defined class of investor, the firm deciding in 2019 or 2020 where to place a new plant in Asia. When the Production Linked Incentive scheme arrived for mobile telephones in the following spring, it used the same grammar of windows, thresholds and deadlines. The ordinance and the subsidy were drafted in one dialect, and the dialect was industrial policy conducted through fiscal law.

## The Prehistory of the Fence: Make in India and the Long Road to September 2019

The ordinance of September 2019 was a rupture in method, not in intention, and its prehistory runs through the first Modi government's industrial programme. Make in India, launched on 25 September 2014, had promised to raise manufacturing's share of national output to 25 percent and to create 100 million manufacturing jobs by 2022, targets the programme's own documents set and the decade declined to meet. The instruments of that first phase were facilitation and tariff protection rather than fiscal architecture: an investment portal, sector committees, and, from the budget of 2016-17, a Phased Manufacturing Programme for mobile telephones that raised customs duties in steps, first on finished handsets, then on chargers, battery packs and printed circuit board assemblies, forcing assembly onshore by price rather than purchasing it by subsidy. The programme worked at its own scale: finished handset imports collapsed as a share of the market, and the assembly base the PLI scheme would later pay to expand was built under the tariff wall the programme raised. The tax side of the first phase moved by inches. The budget of 2018 extended the 25 percent rate, until then confined to the smallest companies, to firms with turnover up to Rs 250 crore, and the budget of July 2019 extended it to Rs 400 crore, which the Finance Ministry calculated covered 99.3 percent of companies by number while leaving the largest manufacturers, the firms whose location decisions the government most wanted to move, inside the 30 percent schedule with its thicket of exemptions.

The intellectual warrant for the final step had also been laid in advance, and it came from inside the state's own advisory machinery. NITI Aayog's Three Year Action Agenda of August 2017, covering 2017-18 to 2019-20, recommended that the corporate tax rate be brought down toward the levels of competing Asian economies and that exemptions be withdrawn in parallel, the same pairing Jaitley had announced in 2015. The Economic Survey of 2018-19, presented by Chief Economic Adviser Krishnamurthy Subramanian on 4 July 2019, built its central argument around private investment as the economy's engine and tax buoyancy through lower rates and wider compliance. India's rise in the World Bank's ease of doing business rankings, from 142nd in the 2015 report to 63rd in the report published in October 2019, supplied the government with a parallel narrative of administrative reform, and the tax cut was presented in investor forums as the fiscal completion of that narrative: the state had simplified its procedures, and at Goa it simplified its price. Read against this prehistory, the September decision loses its appearance of improvisation. The destination had been announced in 2015, recommended by the government's own think tank in 2017, argued by its Chief Economic Adviser in 2019, and blocked for four years by the exemption interests the ordinance's election device was designed to bypass. The political content of the moment lies in the bypass, and the bypass required the mandate arithmetic of May 2019 to be spendable. Feature Two takes up the conditions that made the spending necessary; the prehistory establishes that necessity met a design already waiting in the drawer.

## Section by Section: The Fine Print That Allocated the Gift

The conditions inside the two new sections allocated the ordinance's benefits with a precision that the headline rates conceal, and the allocation is further evidence for the political reading. Section 115BAA's 22 percent was not a rate for being a company; it was a rate for being a company of a particular kind, one whose profits arose without the state's prior assistance. A firm electing the section surrendered not only future exemptions but the accumulated stock of them: losses and depreciation attributable to the surrendered incentives could not be carried into the new regime, and Minimum Alternate Tax credit accumulated under the old one lapsed on election. The election was irrevocable, which converted a tax choice into a one-way door, and the one-way design served the settlement's permanence: every large company that walked through the door became a constituency against restoring the exemption economy, since restoration could not return what the firm had surrendered. Within two assessment cycles, the Finance Ministry's statements to Parliament recorded that the overwhelming majority of companies by number, and a large majority by income, had migrated to the concessional sections, a migration the ministry cited as the reform's ratification by its subjects.

Section 115BAB's fence was drafted tighter, and its exclusions map the government's anxieties. The qualifying company had to be genuinely new: it could not be formed by splitting up or reconstructing an existing business, it could not take over a going concern, and plant and machinery previously used could not exceed 20 percent of the total value of its plant and machinery, a threshold designed to admit relocated lines from a group's existing operations while barring the re-badging of old factories as new ones. The section's exclusions named businesses the government refused to subsidise at the preferential rate, running from mining and the bottling of gas cylinders to cinematograph production, with a residuary power to notify further exclusions. Limited liability partnerships stood outside both sections altogether, a boundary Sitharaman defended in the Lok Sabha on the ground that the partnership form raised separate questions, which confined the gift to the Companies Act universe of incorporated firms and excluded the professional and trading partnerships through which much Indian business actually runs. Each boundary was a decision about which capital counted as the capital the settlement wanted: incorporated, manufacturing, auditable, and new. The fine print is where the architecture shows its load-bearing walls, and the walls were drawn by politics, not by tax theory, since tax theory has no view on whether a film producer's profits deserve a higher rate than a battery maker's.

## Feature Two: The Slowdown That Made the Cut a Political Necessity

No account of the September 2019 decision works without the deceleration that preceded it, because the deceleration supplied both the pressure and the argument. On the Central Statistics Office series in use at the time, gross domestic product growth had stepped down from 8.3 percent in 2016-17 to 6.8 percent in 2018-19, and the quarterly prints through 2019 turned a step-down into a slide: 5.0 percent in the quarter from April to June 2019, a six-year low when it was published on 30 August 2019, and 4.5 percent in the quarter from July to September. The full year 2019-20 closed at 4.0 percent on the revised series, before the pandemic quarter had begun. Behind the aggregates sat named failures and named queues. The Infrastructure Leasing and Financial Services collapse of September 2018 had frozen credit through the non-bank financial sector that financed vehicle purchases, small business working capital and housing. Passenger vehicle sales fell for eleven consecutive months into the autumn of 2019, a contraction the Society of Indian Automobile Manufacturers documented month by month, and the automobile industry's public appeals for a goods and services tax reduction became a running feature of the pre-September debate. The Periodic Labour Force Survey, whose 2017-18 report the government released on 31 May 2019 after a leak-driven controversy, recorded an unemployment rate of 6.1 percent, the highest in the survey series the National Statistical Office maintains.

The scholarly argument about the slowdown was running in parallel, and the government knew its terms. Arvind Subramanian, who had served as Chief Economic Adviser from 2014 to 2018 and had published Of Counsel: The Challenges of the Modi-Jaitley Economy in 2018, argued in a June 2019 research paper that India's growth in the years after 2011 had been overestimated by roughly two and a half percentage points, a claim the government contested through the Economic Advisory Council to the Prime Minister. In December 2019, Subramanian and Josh Felman published their diagnosis of what they called the great slowdown, locating its cause in a second, unresolved balance-sheet crisis spreading from the shadow banks into real estate and small industry. The significance of that paper for the September decision is chronological: the diagnosis the paper formalised in December was the diagnosis circulating in the Finance Ministry's consultations through August, when Sitharaman held a sequence of meetings with industry associations, market participants and sector representatives, and announced staged relief packages on 23 August, 30 August and 14 September 2019. The tax cut was the capstone of that sequence, and it was deliberately unlike the earlier instalments, which had addressed liquidity, exports and housing in administrative language. The capstone addressed the price of capital itself. The instalments deserve their own record, because they show the escalation the capstone completed. On 23 August 2019, Sitharaman withdrew the budget's enhanced surcharge on foreign portfolio investors, promised that pending goods and services tax refunds to small enterprises would be cleared within sixty days, announced that government departments would release three-quarters of arbitration awards to contractors, and front-loaded Rs 70,000 crore of bank recapitalisation into the public sector banks. On 30 August she announced the merger of ten public sector banks into four, a consolidation presented as balance-sheet repair. On 14 September she addressed exports and housing, with duty remission for exporters and a Rs 10,000 crore window for stalled affordable housing projects. Each package was received by the market commentary of its week as insufficient, and the pattern of insufficiency was the ministry's problem: administrative measures were being priced as housekeeping. The ordinance broke the pattern by changing kind, from housekeeping to architecture, and the break was visible in the instrument's price, its permanence and its venue. A government that had spent three Fridays administering relief spent the fourth rewriting the tax code, and the escalation ladder is preserved in the Finance Ministry's own press releases for any reader who wishes to test the claim that September 20 was designed as a decisive moment rather than stumbled into as one.

The foreign investment ledger framed the same decision from the other side. Gross foreign direct investment inflows had reached 64.4 billion US dollars in 2018-19 on Reserve Bank of India figures, a record, yet the composition troubled the ministry: the inflows ran to services, software and e-commerce rather than to the export manufacturing that the government's Make in India programme, launched in September 2014, had promised to expand. Manufacturing's share of gross value added had sat near 16 to 17 percent for a decade against the programme's stated ambition of 25 percent. Inside [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), the manufacturing shortfall was not a statistic but a broken undertaking given to the electorate, and the tax cut's 15 percent manufacturing fence was drafted as the undertaking's repair. Feature Two of the five-feature map is therefore the positioning itself: a second-term government, holding a fresh mandate and facing a deceleration it had not forecast, chose to answer a demand-side and credit-side crisis with a supply-side instrument of record size. That choice, and not the rate arithmetic, is what requires a political reading.

### How did India's effective corporate tax rates compare with competing economies after the ordinance?

After the ordinance, an existing Indian company electing the new regime faced an effective rate of 25.17 percent including surcharge and cess, and a new manufacturing company 17.01 percent. The Finance Ministry's Goa presentation set those figures against rival economies competing for the same factories: China's standard rate stood at 25 percent, Vietnam's at 20 percent.

The comparison also had a transatlantic origin that the ministry's documents acknowledged. The United States had cut its federal corporate rate from 35 percent to 21 percent in the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of December 2017, and the United Kingdom had legislated a path toward 17 percent before abandoning it. The global floor under corporate taxation had dropped through the late 2010s, and India's pre-2019 effective rate near 35 percent for large companies had left the country at the expensive end of every site-selection table the investment promotion agencies compiled. The government's stated case rested on closing that gap. Whether closing it was sufficient to move factories is a separate question, taken up in the adjudication below; that the case was made in these terms, in the ordinance's own week, is a matter of the Finance Ministry's record.

## The Government's Case and the Critics' Case, Each at Its Strongest

The government's case, stated by Sitharaman in Goa and defended by her in Parliament through the winter session, ran on three rails. The first was competitiveness: at an effective 25.17 percent, India had placed its corporate rate, in the Finance Minister's phrase at the Goa press conference, at par with the lowest in the region for established companies, and below them for new manufacturing. The second was investment revival: the ministry argued that retained earnings are the first source of industrial finance, and that Rs 1.45 lakh crore left with companies would fund capacity, retire expensive debt and restore the animal spirits whose absence ministers had lamented since 2017. The third was structural honesty: the cut was paired with the surrender of exemptions, so the state was trading a corruptible schedule of negotiated privileges for a uniform price, completing the 2015 Jaitley design. Industry federations ratified the case in public. The Confederation of Indian Industry and the Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry issued statements on 20 September 2019 welcoming the announcement as bold and as a signal that India intended to compete for the supply chains then moving under the pressure of the United States and China trade conflict.

The critics' case, at its strongest, was made on the opposition benches and in the economic press within the same week, and it also ran on three rails. P. Chidambaram, the former Finance Minister, writing from custody in his published columns and through statements issued by the Congress party, charged that the government had diagnosed a demand collapse and prescribed a supply-side remedy: companies sitting on unsold inventory would use the tax saving to cut debt and pay dividends, not to build capacity for customers who did not exist. The revenue arithmetic drew a second line of attack. The Hindu BusinessLine's analysis of 26 September 2019 examined the tax paid by Indian companies in 2018-19 and argued that the Rs 1.45 lakh crore figure overstated the true annual loss, since the average effective rate paid across the corporate sector was already well below the statutory maximum; on that reading the ministry had inflated the sacrifice to inflate the signal, and the fiscal cost would compound against a deficit already under strain from weak goods and services tax collections. A third line, developed by economists who supported lower rates in principle, held that timing had inverted the logic: a rate cut delivered as emergency stimulus would be banked as windfall by the existing stock of companies, which is why the 22 percent rate for incumbents absorbed most of the Rs 1.45 lakh crore, while the genuinely new investment the government said it wanted was confined to the narrow 15 percent fence.

The evidence available in the following eighteen months let both cases claim partial vindication, and the honest summary names the measurers. Corporate results for the quarters after September 2019 showed the savings flowing first to balance sheets: deleveraging and dividend payouts rose faster than capital expenditure through 2019-20, as analyses by the Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy and by rating agency research recorded. The Reserve Bank of India's studies of company finances for the period found interest coverage improving on lower tax outgo. The government's defenders, including Vivek Dehejia and Rupa Subramanya in their essays on the post-2019 trajectory, answered that a balance-sheet repair was the precondition for an investment cycle rather than its substitute, and that judging a structural rate cut by two quarters of expenditure data committed exactly the category error the technical framing invites: the instrument was built for a decade, and its first measurable effect was supposed to be the restoration of corporate solvency after the shadow-banking freeze. What neither side disputed, because the Finance Ministry's documents settled it, was the distribution of the sacrifice: the state gave up a sum equal to roughly 0.7 percent of gross domestic product in annual revenue, permanently, in a single ordinance, with Parliament asked to ratify the gift after the markets had already celebrated it. That distribution is a political fact before it is a fiscal one.

## The Opposition's Alternative: The Demand Prescription Stated Whole

A settlement is defined by the alternative it defeats, and the alternative to the post-2019 architecture was stated coherently enough in 2019 and 2020 to deserve its own section. Its premise was that India's slowdown was a crisis of purchasing power before it was a crisis of production cost: rural wages had stagnated, the informal sector had absorbed two shocks in demonetisation and the goods and services tax transition, and the consumption data, including the withheld 2017-18 consumer expenditure survey whose leaked findings showed falling real consumption, pointed to households rather than boardrooms as the binding constraint. The prescription followed. Put income into households first, through personal tax relief, expanded rural employment guarantee funding and direct transfers of the kind the Congress manifesto of April 2019 had promised under its minimum income proposal; cut the goods and services tax on the consumption goods whose sales had collapsed, automobiles among them; and treat any corporate relief as conditional, exchanged for contracted investment rather than granted against hope. Economists outside the party system supplied the argument's technical form. The demand prescription's advocates noted that the fiscal multiplier on transfers to liquidity-constrained households exceeds the multiplier on retained corporate earnings in a slack economy, and that the government's chosen instrument would raise the fiscal deficit without raising the expenditure that enters the national accounts as demand. Inside Parliament and in his columns, Chidambaram pressed the distributional edge of the case: a state that could find Rs 1.45 lakh crore for companies in a single morning had pleaded poverty before every claim from farmers, state governments and the employment guarantee for a year.

The government's answer to the alternative was never only economic, and its political form is what the framework records. Ministers replied that demand measures had been taken and would continue, pointing to the income support instalments paid to farmers under the scheme announced in the interim budget of February 2019 and to the housing and export packages of the August and September 2019 sequence, and they characterised the corporate cut as the structural complement those measures lacked. But the deeper answer lay in the architecture's asymmetry, which the opposition's critique identified correctly and could not reverse: the corporate instrument was permanent law within twelve weeks, the production subsidy was a cabinet decision with a five-year fuse, and the household instruments remained annual budget lines, revisable at every February. Permanence is the currency in which settlements are denominated. The opposition proposed expenditure; the government enacted structure. The pillar's framework, which has tracked this asymmetry from the licence schedules of 1948 to the council arithmetic of 2017, reads the 2019 contest as the sequence's clearest modern instance of structure defeating expenditure in open competition, and it records the demand prescription here at full strength because a synthesis that cannot state the losing case cannot claim to have adjudicated anything.

## The Winter Ratification: Parliament Debates What the Markets Had Already Priced

The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Bill's passage through Parliament in the winter session of 2019 supplies the ratification chapter of Feature One, and it matters to the article's claim because ratification after the fact is a distinctively architectural act: the legislature was asked not to decide but to confirm, with the Sensex's verdict and the industry federations' applause already on the record as the cost of refusal. The Lok Sabha passed the bill on 2 December 2019 and the Rajya Sabha returned it on 5 December, with assent following on 11 December. In the debates, the opposition's speakers concentrated on the demand argument and the fiscal argument that the critics' section above recorded, and Congress members moved amendments to condition the rate cut on investment commitments, which the treasury benches defeated on division. Sitharaman's reply held the government's three rails together: competitiveness, investment revival through retained earnings, and the structural trade of exemptions for a clean rate. Replying separately to members who pressed the revenue figure, she confirmed that approximately Rs 1.45 lakh crore could be forgone and treated the figure, as the Goa presentation had treated it, as the measure of the government's seriousness rather than as an embarrassment to be minimised.

The debate's most revealing exchange concerned what the cut would not do. Members asked why a government confronting a consumption collapse had left personal income tax untouched, and the Finance Minister answered, in the terms her parliamentary replies of that session record, that benefits to individuals are reviewed periodically and would be taken at the appropriate time, a promise the budget of 1 February 2020 partially redeemed with an optional lower-rate personal regime. The exchange fixes the settlement's order of operations in the government's own words: capital first, by ordinance, in September; households later, by budget, in February, and then only as an option fenced with surrendered exemptions on the 115BAA pattern. Sequence is the confession of priorities that speeches exist to conceal, and the winter ratification put the sequence into the parliamentary record where the pillar's framework can cite it. A technical reading of the ordinance sees a rate. The record of its ratification sees a government ranking its constituencies in public and daring the opposition to rank them differently.

## Feature Three: The PLI Scheme, From One Notification to Fourteen Sectors

The Production Linked Incentive design entered Indian law and administration in the spring of 2020, in the last fortnight before the pandemic lockdown reorganised the state's priorities. On 20 March 2020 the Union Cabinet approved three schemes from the electronics and pharmaceutical ministries: a production incentive for large-scale electronics manufacturing, aimed at mobile telephones and specified components; a scheme for bulk drugs and active pharmaceutical ingredients; and a scheme for medical devices. The electronics scheme was notified on 1 April 2020. Its mechanics defined the template every later sector scheme would copy. A manufacturer earned an incentive of 4 to 6 percent on incremental sales of goods made in India, measured against a base year, for five years, provided the firm crossed thresholds of incremental investment and incremental production that the notification fixed in advance. The state paid only after output existed and had been sold. The instrument thus inverted the older subsidy grammar of Indian industrial policy, under which capital subsidies and tax holidays had been paid against promises and plant construction; PLI paid against verified performance, and it paid in cash from the budget rather than through the tax code.

The spring schemes carried their own outlays, smaller than what followed but not trivial: Rs 6,940 crore for the bulk drugs and active pharmaceutical ingredients scheme and Rs 3,420 crore for medical devices, against the electronics scheme's envelope for mobile telephones and specified components. The bulk drugs scheme answered a dependence the Commerce Ministry had quantified for years, the import of roughly two-thirds of the active ingredients Indian formulators consumed, the bulk of them from China, and its eligibility list named fifty-three critical bulk drugs whose domestic production the state would pay to restart. The medical devices scheme addressed a market in which imports supplied the large majority of high-end equipment. Both schemes were approved on 20 March 2020, four days before the national lockdown began, which fixed their place in the sequence: the production-subsidy instrument was designed for a supply-chain argument that predated the pandemic, and the pandemic then supplied the argument's proof in the form of interrupted ingredient shipments and ventilator shortages that the Health Ministry's procurement record documented through the spring.

The political scaling came on 11 November 2020. The Union Cabinet, chaired by the Prime Minister, approved the extension of the PLI design to ten further sectors in a single decision, and the Press Information Bureau release of that day itemised the outlays: Rs 57,042 crore for automobiles and auto components, Rs 18,100 crore for advanced chemistry cell batteries, Rs 15,000 crore for pharmaceutical drugs, Rs 12,195 crore for telecom and networking products, Rs 10,900 crore for food products, Rs 10,683 crore for textile products in the man-made fibre segment and technical textiles, Rs 6,322 crore for specialty steel, Rs 6,238 crore for white goods, Rs 5,000 crore for electronic and technology products, and Rs 4,500 crore for high-efficiency solar photovoltaic modules. The ten sectors carried Rs 1,45,980 crore over five years. Added to the spring schemes, the envelope reached the figure Sitharaman announced in her budget speech of 1 February 2021: an outlay of Rs 1.97 lakh crore for PLI schemes across thirteen sectors, beginning 2021-22, which the speech projected would produce minimum additional production worth more than 500 billion US dollars over five years. The Cabinet's approval of a fourteenth sector, drones and drone components, followed in September 2021, and in December 2021 the government approved the semiconductor programme with an outlay of Rs 76,000 crore, which the brief for this article counts as the fourteenth sector's companion launch inside the same manufacturing architecture.

The sector list is itself a political document, and it repays reading as one. Mobile telephones represented the proven case, a sector where assembly had already scaled under the Phased Manufacturing Programme. Automobiles and components represented the largest single cheque, written to an industry that employed millions and had spent 2019 pleading distress. Pharmaceuticals and bulk drugs carried the China-dependence argument in its rawest form: Indian formulators imported roughly two-thirds of their active ingredients, most of them from China, a vulnerability the pandemic had converted into a security argument that the Commerce and Health ministries pressed in cabinet. Telecom networking products belonged to the same security ledger, written after the June 2020 border clash had made Chinese equipment a question of state. Solar modules, batteries and specialty steel addressed the energy transition's supply chain. Textiles and food products were the employment sectors, the two entries on the list whose justification ran to jobs per rupee rather than to strategic depth. A scheme whose sectors were chosen by a single cabinet sitting, spanning security, employment, energy and export logics at once, cannot be read as a technician's artefact. The list is the coalition the government intended to build, itemised.

### How is a PLI incentive calculated on incremental sales?

The incentive is a percentage, fixed by each sector notification and running between 4 and 6 percent in the electronics scheme, of incremental sales: sales of goods manufactured in India above the base year, which the electronics notification fixed as 2019-20. It is paid annually for five years, after the applicant crosses thresholds for incremental investment and production.

Administration carried the same signature as design. Each sector scheme ran through its own ministry, with the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology administering electronics, the Department of Pharmaceuticals administering drugs and devices, the Department of Heavy Industries administering automobiles and batteries, and so on down the list, while the Empowered Group of Secretaries and the Cabinet Secretariat monitored the programme as a whole. The selection of applicants was discretionary and competitive: for mobile telephones, sixteen companies were approved in October 2020, including the contract manufacturers Foxconn, Wistron and Pegatron, which assembled for Apple, alongside Samsung and five Indian firms. The approvals made the scheme's wager explicit. India would pay the world's largest electronics assemblers a percentage of their Indian sales to relocate production depth into India, and it would publish the payments. The PLI state did not hide its hand inside a tax expenditure statement; it wrote cheques in the open and defended the cheques as strategy.

## What the PLI Record Shows, as Its Named Measurers State It

The brief for this article fixes the scheme's evidence scope past the article's locked date, through the sector notifications of 2021, and the record those notifications built is best summarised by attribution. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology stated in its programme reviews that mobile telephone production in India had risen from Rs 1.89 lakh crore in 2018-19 to Rs 2.75 lakh crore in 2021-22, with mobile exports rising several-fold across the same years, and that India stood as the world's second-largest producer of mobile handsets by volume. The government's budget documents converted the programme into forward commitments: the budget speech of 1 February 2022 stated that the PLI schemes across fourteen sectors held the potential to create 60 lakh new jobs and additional production of Rs 30 lakh crore over five years. Those are the administering state's figures, and the article reports them as such.

The India Cellular and Electronics Association's programme reporting added the export series to the production series: mobile telephone exports rose from roughly 1.6 billion US dollars in 2018-19 to roughly 5.5 billion dollars in 2021-22, a near three-and-a-half-fold rise across the scheme's first two operating years, with the association attributing the inflection to the approved assemblers reaching their incremental thresholds. The association is an industry body and its figures are the industry's own, a caveat the article records; the direction of the series is corroborated by the Directorate General of Commercial Intelligence and Statistics trade data for the telephone sets classification over the same years.

The independent assessors the brief names, Pranjul Bhandari and Aurodeep Nandi of HSBC in their published research on the scheme, gave the design high marks on precisely the feature this article has stressed: payment against output, sector selection tied to revealed global demand, and eligibility thresholds that filtered for scale. Their caution ran to execution: approvals in several sectors ran ahead of disbursals, guidelines for some schemes were renegotiated after announcement, and the realised incentive outgo in the programme's first years ran well below the headline envelope, which they read as evidence that the constraint on Indian manufacturing sat in land, logistics and supplier depth rather than in the price of the subsidy. The Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry and the Confederation of Indian Industry, in their programme assessments, pressed a complementary complaint from the applicant side, asking for faster claim settlement and for the inclusion of component ecosystems that the original thresholds had excluded. The semiconductor decision of December 2021, taken with an outlay larger than most sector schemes combined, extended the design to the one input whose absence the pandemic shortages of 2021 had made vivid in the automobile industry's production losses. By the end of 2021, the architecture stood complete on paper: a corporate tax rate cut in permanent law, a doctrine named Atmanirbhar Bharat, and fourteen sector schemes paying for production. What the doctrine contributed, and why it counts as a separate feature of the map, is the subject of the next section.

## Extensions and Revisions: The Architecture Under Construction Through 2021

The brief's evidence scope extends through the sector notifications of 2021, and those notifications show the architecture being revised in the open, which is itself a datum for the political reading: programmes that exist to be administered do not require repeated cabinet attention, while architectures do. The white goods scheme for air conditioners and LED lighting, approved in principle in the November 2020 decision at Rs 6,238 crore, received its detailed cabinet approval in April 2021 with component-depth conditions written into eligibility, a direct administrative answer to the assembly objection. The specialty steel scheme followed with cabinet approval on 22 July 2021 at Rs 6,322 crore. In September 2021 the Cabinet approved the textiles scheme for man-made fibre and technical textiles at Rs 10,683 crore, restructured the automobile and auto components scheme at Rs 25,938 crore against the larger November 2020 envelope, added a champion-companies window for advanced automotive technology, and approved the drones scheme at Rs 120 crore as the fourteenth sector. The advanced chemistry cell battery scheme moved to competitive bidding in October 2021. Each revision renegotiated the bargain with a sector federation in public, and the renegotiations accumulated into a pattern the implementation reading records as delay: the automobile industry's original allocation was nearly halved in redesign, the textiles scheme was confined to the fibre segments where India lacked depth rather than spread across the cotton chain where it held strength, and the battery bidding drew the Reliance, Hyundai and Ola Electric groups into a single auction for gigafactory capacity. Delay and design are the same events read at different altitudes. At the altitude of the pillar, the revisions are the settlement being fitted to its constituencies sector by sector, which is what settlements require and what rate schedules do not.

One further extension belongs to the tax side of the architecture. The commencement deadline inside Section 115BAB, fixed at 31 March 2023 by the ordinance, was extended to 31 March 2024 in the budget legislation of 2023, and the sunset on the PLI windows was managed sector by sector through guideline amendments rather than by any single closing act. The pattern matches the ordinance's own history: permanence for the rate, flexibility for the fences. A government that had built a technical instrument would have defended its parameters. A government that had built a settlement defended the settlement and traded the parameters, which is the behaviour the record shows from Goa in September 2019 to the last notification inside this article's scope.

## The Bureaucracy of the Cheque: How a PLI Claim Travels

The scheme's administrative machinery deserves a section, because the machinery is where the architecture's theory of the state becomes visible. Each sector notification appointed a project management agency to run the scheme's files: the Industrial Finance Corporation's successor institutions, the Small Industries Development Bank and similar public financial bodies appear across the notifications as the verifying layer between the applicant and the ministry. An applicant filed projected investment and production, received approval against the scheme's thresholds, manufactured and sold, and then claimed the incentive on audited incremental sales, with the claim verified against goods and services tax records and statutory audit certificates before the ministry sanctioned payment. Above the ministries sat the Empowered Group of Secretaries, constituted to monitor the programme across sectors, with the Cabinet Secretariat reviewing progress. The design's premise was a distrust of the older subsidy state's discretionary habits: thresholds in the notification rather than discretion in the section office, payment after performance rather than before construction, and verification against a tax database that the goods and services tax had, for the first time, made national and machine-readable. The paperwork is public throughout: every notification, guideline and approval list sits on the administering ministry's website, and the programme's summaries circulate through the industry associations as slide decks, the sort of file an applicant's finance team opens in a [PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) before the board meeting that votes on the application. The GST's data infrastructure, built by the pillar's ninth moment for revenue purposes, turned out to be the enabling condition for the tenth moment's subsidy design, a dependence the synthesis records because no account of the sequence explains it as coincidence.

The machinery also generated the record by which the scheme will be judged, and its frictions are documented in the industry's representations to the administering ministries through 2021. Applicants in several sectors pressed for the base year and threshold conditions to be recalibrated where the pandemic year had broken the production baseline the notifications assumed, and the guideline revisions recorded in the extensions section above were the administrative trace of that pressure. The claim cycle's annual rhythm meant that the first substantial disbursals in most sectors fell due after this article's locked date, which is why the outcomes section of this article is written by attribution to projections and production series rather than to payment data. The discipline matters: an architecture is not its disbursals, and the pillar's framework has never confused the two, but the reader is owed the admission that the cheque's journey from notification to bank account is the part of the record the locked date truncates.

## The Assembly Question: Depth, Value Addition and the Fence's Critics

One objection followed both instruments from their first week, and the article's claim survives only if the objection is stated at full strength. The objection holds that India had learned to assemble other countries' manufacturing rather than to manufacture: mobile telephone plants in Noida and Sriperumbudur imported displays, chipsets and camera modules, added labour and logistics worth a modest fraction of the handset's value, and exported the finished product, so that a subsidy on incremental sales rewarded the shallowest segment of the chain while the trade deficit with China in components widened behind it. The objection was legible in the trade data the Directorate General of Commercial Intelligence and Statistics published through 2020 and 2021, which recorded component imports rising alongside handset exports, and it was pressed in the economic press by analysts who calculated domestic value addition in electronics assembly in the range of a tenth to a fifth of final value. On this reading, the 15 percent tax fence and the PLI cheque were purchasing a statistic, production booked in India, while the commanding heights of the chain remained in Shenzhen, Taipei and Seoul.

The government's answer was also stated on the record, and it had a sequence rather than a denial. Officials of the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology argued, in the scheme's defence and in the phased manufacturing logic that preceded it, that assembly is the entry rung of every electronics ladder climbed in East Asia: the assembler arrives for the incentive, the supplier park follows the assembler, and component depth is purchased in the second round, which is what the specified electronic components window inside the electronics scheme, the separate scheme for semiconductor fabrication announced in December 2021, and the advanced chemistry cell battery scheme were each designed to do. The Phased Manufacturing Programme for handsets, which had raised duties on finished telephones and then on components in steps from 2016 onward, supplied the precedent the ministry cited: by 2019, imports of finished handsets had fallen to a small share of the market the programme had once conceded. Whether the sequence completes is an empirical question the record of 2021 cannot settle, and the article takes no position on the outcome. The political fact stands apart from the outcome. Faced with the assembly objection, the government did not retreat to a purer instrument; it widened the architecture, adding scheme upon scheme until the map of subsidies traced the whole chain it was accused of missing. The widening, not any single scheme, is the behaviour the political reading predicts and the implementation reading cannot explain.

## The World the Fence Was Built For: Supply Chains in Motion

The 15 percent fence and the PLI cheque were addressed to a moving object, and the object's motion is documented. From 2018, the United States and China exchanged tariff rounds that covered several hundred billion dollars of traded goods, and every boardroom site-selection exercise in Asia acquired a China-plus-one column. Vietnam was the column's first beneficiary, absorbing electronics and footwear lines at a pace its industrial parks struggled to service. In April 2020, Japan's government earmarked 243.5 billion yen inside its emergency package to subsidise Japanese firms moving production out of China, the first time a major economy had written decoupling into a budget line, and the precedent was cited in Indian policy discussion through that year. India's pitch to the same boardrooms rested on the assets the tax cut was designed to advertise: a domestic market no rival location could match, a corporate rate aligned to the region by the ordinance, and, from the spring of 2020, a subsidy that paid in cash for output. The pitch had a name in the investment promotion literature of the period, the China-plus-one strategy, and the government's documents adopted the frame without embarrassment.

The pitch's early returns were recorded in the approvals themselves. When the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology announced sixteen approved applicants for the mobile telephone scheme in October 2020, the list joined Samsung with the three contract manufacturers serving Apple, and with five Indian firms including Lava and Dixon, which placed the wager's both halves on one page: the foreign assembler for scale and export credibility, the domestic champion for the political claim that the architecture built Indian firms and not only Indian factories. State governments competed inside the frame the Union had built, with Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka signing memoranda and allotting land for the approved plants, a federal competition for factories that the PLI design deliberately provoked by making the incentive portable across state lines. The provocation is a further datum for the synthesis. The 1948 settlement allocated industry between the Union and the states by schedule. The 1991 settlement set the states competing within a national market the Union had opened. The post-2019 settlement made the states competing sales agents for a Union subsidy, inside a doctrine announced by the Prime Minister. The federal form of Indian industrial policy had been rewritten a third time, and the rewriting, like its predecessors, was a political act wearing administrative dress.

## Feature Four: Atmanirbhar Bharat and the Doctrine That Named the Architecture

On the evening of 12 May 2020, in a televised address to the nation delivered during the national lockdown, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced that his government would build an Atmanirbhar Bharat, a self-reliant India, and that the economic package attached to the project would total Rs 20 lakh crore, approximately 10 percent of gross domestic product, assembled from the Reserve Bank of India's liquidity measures and the fiscal announcements the Finance Minister would detail across five press conferences from 13 to 17 May 2020. The address named five pillars for the project: economy, infrastructure, a technology-driven system, vibrant demography, and demand. It instructed the country to be, in the Prime Minister's phrase, vocal about local, converting local products into global brands. Delivered eight weeks into a pandemic that had collapsed quarterly output by a magnitude no post-1947 government had faced, the address functioned as the doctrine-launch for everything the post-2019 architecture had been assembling since Goa: the tax fence of September 2019 acquired a philosophy, and the production subsidies of March 2020 acquired a name under which the Cabinet would scale them in November.

The doctrine's content was argued about immediately, and the argument is part of the record the article must carry at full strength on both sides. The Prime Minister glossed self-reliance in the address itself and in his subsequent statements as a posture toward the world rather than a wall against it: a self-reliant India, in his formulation, would integrate with global supply chains from a position of domestic strength, and the five pillars named openness-compatible instruments, infrastructure and systems among them. The Swadeshi Jagran Manch, the economic affiliate of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, welcomed the address as the vindication of a position it had held since 1991, and its officeholders said so in print. The critics answered from two directions. Trade economists warned that the vocabulary of self-reliance, joined to the tariff increases of the 2018 to 2021 budgets, revived the licence-era instinct to protect first and compete later, and that the Rs 20 lakh crore headline overstated the fresh fiscal content of a package composed largely of credit guarantees and central bank liquidity, a decomposition the rating agencies and the opposition's finance spokesmen published within days of the May tranches. The government's defenders replied that the guarantee-heavy design was deliberate statecraft under a revenue collapse, and that the doctrine's test was the production architecture it authorised, not the accounting of a single week. The border clash in the Galwan valley on 15 June 2020, five weeks after the address, then fused the doctrine to the security state: the emergency procurement, the application bans and the investment screening that followed belong to [the post-China architectural-substantive-decoupling-pattern framework](/2020/11/09/2020-galwan-moment-reanalysed/), but the fusion mattered for PLI, whose telecom and pharmaceutical sectors were argued in cabinet as instruments of the same decoupling.

The package that the address announced was detailed by the Finance Minister across five press conferences from 13 to 17 May 2020, and its composition is the doctrine's first test. The tranches combined collateral-free automatic loans for micro, small and medium enterprises backed by a credit guarantee envelope of Rs 3 lakh crore, a subordinate debt provision for stressed small firms, a partial credit guarantee for non-bank lenders, liquidity for power distribution companies against state guarantees, a reduction in provident fund contribution rates for three months, and free foodgrain for migrant workers outside the public distribution system's ration card net. The Reserve Bank's contribution, rate cuts and targeted liquidity operations conducted since March 2020, was counted inside the Rs 20 lakh crore headline. Analysts at the rating agencies and in the financial press decomposed the total within days and estimated the fresh budgetary outlay at a fraction of the headline, a decomposition the opposition adopted as its central charge against the package. The government's reply, given by the Finance Minister across the tranche briefings, held that a liquidity crisis in a financial system is treated with guarantees and credit lines rather than with expenditure, and that counting only the cash lines misunderstood the instrument. Both the decomposition and the reply are recorded here because the argument over the package's arithmetic is the doctrine's political content in miniature: the headline was built to be argued about, and the argument carried the doctrine into every newsroom in the country for a week, which no budget speech in the government's tenure had achieved.

Feature Four's place in the five-feature map rests on this fusion. The tax cut lowered the price of producing in India; PLI paid a premium for producing in India; Atmanirbhar Bharat told the country why producing in India was a national obligation and told the bureaucracy which of its files the obligation covered. A doctrine that can be cited in a cabinet note is an institution, and the November 2020 cabinet decision cited it in its title: the Press Information Bureau release of 11 November 2020 announced the ten-sector approval under the heading of enhancing India's manufacturing capabilities and exports, Atmanirbhar Bharat. The phrase in the heading was not decoration. It was the authorising citation, the same role that [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/) assigns to the doctrines of the Nehru years, which governed files for decades after the speeches that launched them were forgotten as oratory.

## The Fiscal Ledger: What the Cut Cost and What the Collections Showed

The permanent character of the September 2019 sacrifice is measurable in the revenue series the Income Tax Department publishes. Gross corporation tax collections stood at Rs 6.64 lakh crore in 2018-19, the last full year of the old rate structure. In 2019-20, with the new rates in force for half the year and the slowdown cutting profits, collections fell to Rs 5.57 lakh crore, a decline of roughly a sixth, the first absolute fall in the series in a decade. In 2020-21, under pandemic conditions, corporation tax yielded Rs 4.57 lakh crore against a personal income tax yield that surpassed it for the first time in the history of the two taxes, an inversion the budget documents of February 2021 recorded without commentary and the financial press noted at length. The fiscal deficit for 2019-20 closed at 4.6 percent of gross domestic product against a budgeted 3.3 percent, a slippage the Finance Ministry attributed in its budget documents to the revenue shortfall and the slowdown together. Attribution between the rate cut and the cycle cannot be made exact from the published aggregates, and the article does not pretend otherwise. What the aggregates settle is the order of magnitude the government accepted: a permanent reduction in the state's largest single revenue line, taken in a year when revenue was already failing, in exchange for an investment response whose arrival the ministry's own statements placed years in the future.

The ledger has a second column, and it belongs in the same paragraph as the first. Company incorporations, which the Ministry of Corporate Affairs counts monthly, did not collapse with the collections: the ministry recorded approximately 1.24 lakh new company registrations in 2020-21, a rise on the year before despite the lockdown, and the count of limited liability partnerships rose alongside. The government's defenders cited the incorporation series as the fence working at the margin the fence was built for, the decision to found a new entity in India rather than elsewhere. The critics answered that incorporation is cheap and production is dear, and that the series counted letterheads. Both statements are true, and the gap between them is the gap the PLI architecture was built to close: the tax instrument rewarded existence, the subsidy instrument rewarded output, and the doctrine told the two instruments they were one policy. The five-feature map records the gap rather than resolving it, because the resolution belongs to the production data of the middle 2020s, outside this article's evidence scope.

## The States' Share: A Union Cut Inside a Federal Fiscal System

Corporation tax in India is not the Union's money alone. It sits inside the divisible pool of taxes that the Finance Commission shares between the Union and the states, at 42 percent for the states under the Fourteenth Finance Commission's award and 41 percent under the Fifteenth, whose report for 2021 to 2026 was tabled with the budget of February 2021. A permanent cut in the corporation tax rate therefore distributed its cost across every state budget in the country without any state legislature voting on the gift, a structural fact that gives the Goa venue a second, sharper edge: the Finance Minister announced a decision that reduced the shared pool while chairing, a few rooms away, the council in which the states bargain over the other great shared tax. State finance ministers made the point in the Council's subsequent meetings and in the compensation dispute that consumed the Council through 2020, when Kerala's Finance Minister Thomas Isaac, among others, argued in public that the Union had developed a pattern of shrinking the divisible pool through cesses, surcharges and rate decisions while expanding its own discretionary space. The corporation tax cut is a rate decision inside the pool rather than a cess outside it, and the distinction matters to the accounting; it did not matter to the politics, where the cut entered the states' standing brief against the Union's fiscal centralisation.

The PLI architecture ran the federal current the other way, and the contrast completes the federal picture. The subsidy was the Union's money, spent by Union ministries, on plants the states competed to host, which made every chief minister a supplicant for Union industrial policy and every state industrial department a franchise of a national scheme. The GST settlement of 2017, the pillar's ninth moment, had pooled sovereignty in a council where the Union holds a third of the vote and the states two-thirds. The post-2019 settlement recentred the initiative in New Delhi while distributing the factories across the map, a combination the synthesis must record plainly: the architecture was federal in its geography and unitary in its authorship. That combination, and not the rates, is what a future government would have to renegotiate, state by state and sector by sector, to undo it.

### How does the pillar-closing synthesis read the September 2019 decisions against the 1991 reforms?

The synthesis reads 2019 as the mirror image of 1991 inside one framework: the Rao government liberalised under external compulsion, hiding a revolution inside a crisis budget, whereas the Modi government cut rates and paid production subsidies from electoral strength, advertising a revolution inside an ordinance. Both were political settlements resetting the state's bargain with capital.

## Feature Five: The Pillar-Closing Synthesis Across Ten Moments

This article closes the pillar that [the economic-policy-as-politics framework](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/) opened, and the closing obligation is synthesis: to state what the pillar's ten moments, read in sequence, establish about the way economic decisions function in Indian politics, and to place the 2019 and 2020 decisions at the sequence's end with their full weight. The sequence begins with the Industrial Policy Resolution of 6 April 1948 and the planned economy that [the Nehruvian settlement](/2020/11/20/nehruvian-planned-economy-reanalysed/) built on it, in which the state reserved the commanding heights to itself and licensed private capital into the remainder, a settlement that was a coalition document before it was a growth strategy. It continues through [the nationalisations as political acts](/2020/11/26/indira-nationalisations-political-acts/) of 1969 to 1980, when the ownership of banks, insurance, coal and oil became the medium in which a split party and a plebiscitary leader rewrote the state's relation to property. The third and fourth moments are the hinge: the crisis budget of 24 July 1991 and [the detailed-political-architectural-substantive-content framework](/2020/12/01/1991-reforms-detail-reanalysed/) for reading it, in which devaluation, delicensing and trade opening were executed as political theatre management under a minority government. The fifth moment, [the sanctions response of 1998](/2020/12/07/1998-nuclear-sanctions-response/), showed the state's economic machinery conscripted into a sovereignty demonstration, diaspora bonds and all. The sixth, the Right to Information Act of 12 October 2005, proved the framework's reach by extending it beyond macroeconomic instruments: a transparency statute functioned as anti-corruption politics with measurable effects on the state's own files. The seventh, the crisis response of 2008 to 2009, showed a government buying social peace with stimulus and farm loan relief at the edge of an election. The eighth and ninth are the present government's first-term signatures, [the political-economy framework](/2020/12/23/demonetisation-political-economy/) for the currency withdrawal of 8 November 2016 and [the cooperative-federalism-political-architectural-substantive-content framework](/2020/12/29/gst-cooperative-federalism-reanalysed/) for the tax unification of 1 July 2017, one a centralising shock, the other a federal bargain ratified by a council of states.

The tenth moment is the subject of this article, and its position in the sequence is what the five features, taken together, reveal. Each earlier moment had been a single instrument asked to carry a settlement: a resolution, a nationalisation, a budget, a statute, a demonetisation. The post-2019 moment is the first in the sequence to arrive as a system, three instruments deep before the pillar closed: a rate structure rewritten by ordinance, a production subsidy operating across fourteen sectors, and a doctrine launched by the head of government in a national address, each element citing the others in official documents. The system character is visible in the cabinet paper trail the brief directs attention to, where the November 2020 PLI approval travels under the Atmanirbhar heading and the tax fence's manufacturing deadline is aligned with the subsidy window. In the terms of [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), which ranks founding decisions by the weight of what later actors must work around, the post-2019 settlement bids for the economic equivalent of that rank: a future finance minister who wishes to raise the corporate rate or close the subsidy envelope must first repeal a doctrine the country was told its survival required. That is what political architecture means in this pillar's vocabulary. A rate can be revised. An architecture must be dismantled, and dismantling has authors, costs and opponents.

The pillar's analytical contribution can be stated in one sentence, and the sentence is the synthesis the closing article owes the reader. Across ten moments and seven decades, the economic decisions that endured in India were the ones designed as political settlements, with constituencies named, costs distributed by choice and doctrines attached, while the decisions remembered as technical triumphs or technical failures were, on inspection, settlements whose political design had been concealed or neglected. The 1948 resolution endured because it settled the claims of the Bombay Plan industrialists and the Congress socialists in one text. The 1991 reforms endured because Rao and Manmohan Singh settled the claims of a frightened cabinet, a sceptical party and an watching opposition in the sequence of their announcement. The post-2019 architecture was designed, openly, on the same principle, and the five-feature map set out below, the findable artifact of this article, is the instrument by which a reader can test that claim against the record feature by feature. ## Adjudication: Technical Tax Policy, Implementation, and the Political Reading

Three readings of the post-2019 sequence compete, and the article's brief requires a verdict among them. The technical-tax-policy reading treats the ordinance as an exercise in rate design: a broad base purchased with a lower rate, an exemption economy retired, a new-manufacturing fence aligned with international practice. It is the reading of the Finance Ministry's own explanatory memoranda, and its strength is real, because the ordinance's text genuinely is elegant rate design, as Feature One recorded. The policy-implementation reading treats the sequence as an execution problem: PLI guidelines issued and revised, claims settled slowly, sectors renegotiated, disbursals trailing approvals. Its strength is also real, and Bhandari and Nandi's HSBC research, cited earlier, is its most careful statement. Each reading captures a layer of the record. The claim of this article is that neither layer explains the decisions' shape, and that the unexplained residue is systematically political.

Consider what each rival reading cannot digest. The technical reading cannot explain the venue, the timing or the instrument: rate design did not require a Goa press conference in September, an ordinance eleven weeks after a budget that had held the rate, or a revenue sacrifice announced as a boast rather than conceded as a cost. The implementation reading cannot explain the scaling pattern of November 2020, in which a design tested on one sector for seven months was extended to ten more sectors in a single cabinet sitting before the first scheme's disbursals had begun, an act that makes sense as architecture and none as administration. And neither reading can explain the doctrine: a mid-pandemic prime ministerial address has no place in a rate schedule or a payment manual, yet the November cabinet decision travelled under the address's name. The political reading explains all three residues with one mechanism. A government holding a fresh mandate, facing a slowdown that threatened its central undertaking, used fiscal instruments to build a settlement: incumbent capital received a permanent rate, prospective manufacturers received a fence and a cheque, the state received a doctrine that made the gifts legible as national strategy, and the opposition received a target it could attack only by arguing against investment itself. The settlement's terms are the five features. The map is the claim.

The strongest counter deserves its paragraph, stated as its holders state it. The counter holds that the post-2019 content is exhausted by technical decisions taken inside technical constraints: the rate had to fall because rival jurisdictions had cut theirs, the subsidy had to pay for output because capital subsidies had leaked for decades, and the doctrine was rhetoric laid over arithmetic by a government that communicates by slogan in every domain. Dehejia and Subramanya's essays on the post-2019 trajectory, sympathetic to the government's direction, nonetheless concede the design intelligence the counter describes, and the concession cuts against the purely rhetorical dismissal. The counter fails at the point the brief's complication identifies. The technical decisions were available to every finance minister since 2015, when Jaitley announced the destination and missed every milestone on the road to it. What changed in September 2019 was not the arithmetic but the political conditions: a majority of 303, a slowdown that had become the government's central vulnerability, and a Prime Minister's Office willing to spend revenue at a scale the fiscal rules were written to prevent. Technical content operated through political conditions, and the reading that foregrounds the conditions captures the operationalisation the counter leaves unexamined. The verdict follows the brief's namable claim, and the article rests on it: the 20 September 2019 corporate tax cut and the post-2020 PLI scheme operate as political architecture for the post-2019 economic order, and the five-feature framework captures that architecture as the pillar-closing synthesis of the economic-policy-as-politics argument.

## The Inheritance: What the Architecture Asks of Its Successors

A closing article owes its pillar a statement of what the settlement leaves behind, and the post-2019 architecture leaves three inheritances that any successor government, of any party, must work around. The first is the rate itself. A corporation tax schedule whose effective rate for compliant companies stands at 25.17 percent, with the exemption economy dismantled by the companies' own elections, cannot be raised without a finance minister standing in Parliament to announce that India has become a more expensive place to manufacture than the neighbours the Goa presentation invoked. Rates cut in calm times are revised in budgets; rates cut as national strategy, under a doctrine, acquire the protection the doctrine gives them. The second inheritance is the subsidy book. Fourteen sector schemes with five-year payment tails create a calendar of obligations that runs across at least one general election, owed to named companies against verified production, in sectors whose federations have organised themselves around the schemes' continuation. Closing the book early would be a sovereign default in miniature against the state's own published terms, and every successor knows the price of being seen to pay it. The third inheritance is the doctrine's vocabulary. Atmanirbhar Bharat entered the language of cabinet headings, state industrial policies and corporate annual reports within a year of the May 2020 address, and doctrines that enter the headings outlive the governments that coin them, as the pillar's first articles showed for the vocabulary of the planned economy, whose terms structured files for a decade after the planning apparatus itself had thinned.

The inheritance includes a household wing that the headline instruments overshadow. The budget of 1 February 2020 inserted Section 115BAC into the Income-tax Act, offering individuals a schedule of lower slab rates in exchange for surrendering the exemptions and deductions, housing loan interest and the standard investment allowances among them, that the old personal regime had accumulated. The device was Section 115BAA translated to the personal side: the same grammar of a clean rate purchased with surrendered privileges, the same irrevocable-by-default election, the same bet that taxpayers would trade a negotiated schedule for a legible one. The Finance Ministry presented the personal regime as simplification and choice, and its critics, including the tax bar's published commentaries of February 2020, noted that the surrender price fell hardest on the salaried households whose deductions were the old regime's most used provisions. For the pillar's synthesis the wing matters beyond its revenue weight, which was small beside the corporate instrument. It shows the architecture propagating across the tax code within five months of its invention, from companies to individuals, which is the behaviour of a governing design rather than a single decision. A successor government inherits not a rate but a template, and templates, once two generations of taxpayers have filed under them, develop the same resistance to repeal that the rate itself enjoys.

None of this settles the wager's outcome, and the article's discipline requires the distinction to be drawn one final time. Production may or may not deepen; exports may or may not compound; the revenue forgone may or may not return as buoyancy. Those questions belong to the production statistics and the tax data of the years after this article's scope, and the named measurers, the ministries, the Reserve Bank's company finance studies, the HSBC research programme and the monitoring institutions, will answer them in their own publications. What the record inside the scope already settles is the character of the act. Between September 2019 and December 2021, the Indian state rebuilt the terms on which it hosts manufacturing capital, using an ordinance, a doctrine and fourteen subsidy schemes, at a stated price of Rs 1.45 lakh crore a year in revenue and Rs 1.97 lakh crore in committed incentives, and it did so as a single connected design whose elements cite one another in the official record. The pillar that began with a resolution in April 1948 ends with an architecture in January 2021, and the framework that connects them, economic policy as politics, has carried the weight the pillar's opening article assigned it. The five-feature map stands as the closing exhibit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 20 September 2019 corporate tax cut?

On 20 September 2019, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman announced in Goa, during the thirty-seventh meeting of the Goods and Services Tax Council, that the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Ordinance had cut India's corporation tax. The base rate for existing domestic companies fell from 30 percent to 22 percent for firms surrendering their exemptions and deductions, an effective rate of 25.17 percent after surcharge and cess. New manufacturing companies received a 15 percent base rate. The ordinance also reduced Minimum Alternate Tax from 18.5 percent to 15 percent, withdrew the enhanced surcharge on capital gains that the July 2019 budget had imposed, and disapplied the new buyback tax for buybacks announced before 5 July 2019. The Finance Ministry estimated the revenue forgone at Rs 1.45 lakh crore a year. Parliament converted the ordinance into the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act in December 2019, with effect from the ordinance's date.

### Q: What is the 22% corporate tax rate?

The 22 percent rate is the base corporation tax rate created by Section 115BAA of the Income-tax Act of 1961, inserted by the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Ordinance of 20 September 2019. It is available to any domestic company that elects it, on the condition that the company surrenders the exemptions, deductions and incentives available under the older regime, including the accumulated benefit of losses attributable to those incentives and any Minimum Alternate Tax credit. With the 10 percent surcharge and the 4 percent health and education cess, the effective rate is 25.17 percent, and companies under the section owe no Minimum Alternate Tax. The election is made in the return for the relevant year and, once exercised, cannot be withdrawn, which means a company that migrates to the 22 percent schedule cannot return to the exemption regime in a later year. The Finance Ministry informed Parliament that the large majority of companies migrated to the concessional sections within the first assessment cycles.

### Q: What is the 15% rate for new manufacturing?

The 15 percent rate is the base corporation tax rate created by Section 115BAB of the Income-tax Act, available to a domestic manufacturing company incorporated on or after 1 October 2019 that begins production before the statutory deadline, set at 31 March 2023 in the ordinance and later extended to 31 March 2024. With surcharge and cess, the effective rate is 17.01 percent. The fence around the rate is deliberate: the company must be genuinely new, not formed by splitting up or reconstructing an existing business, previously used plant and machinery cannot exceed 20 percent of the total value of its plant and machinery, the company must surrender exemptions on the Section 115BAA pattern, and notified categories of business are excluded. The rate was designed as a location instrument, aimed at the firm deciding where in Asia to place a new plant, and the Finance Ministry presented the effective 17.01 percent in investor forums as placing India below the headline rates of the competing manufacturing economies of the region.

### Q: What is the PLI Scheme?

The Production Linked Incentive scheme is a budgetary subsidy under which the Union government pays manufacturers a percentage of their incremental sales of goods produced in India, measured against a base year, for a fixed period of years, provided the manufacturer crosses investment and production thresholds fixed in advance by notification. The design was first approved by the Union Cabinet on 20 March 2020 for large-scale electronics manufacturing, bulk drugs and medical devices, and the electronics scheme was notified on 1 April 2020 with incentives of 4 to 6 percent of incremental sales over five years. On 11 November 2020 the Cabinet extended the design to ten more sectors at a combined outlay of Rs 1,45,980 crore, and the budget of 1 February 2021 announced a total envelope of Rs 1.97 lakh crore. The scheme pays after output is produced and sold, verified against tax records, which distinguishes it from the capital subsidies and tax holidays of earlier industrial policy.

### Q: When was the PLI Scheme announced?

The Production Linked Incentive scheme was announced in stages rather than on a single date. The Union Cabinet approved the first three schemes, for large-scale electronics manufacturing, bulk drugs and active pharmaceutical ingredients, and medical devices, on 20 March 2020, and the electronics scheme was notified on 1 April 2020, which is the date the design entered operation. The scheme's expansion to ten further sectors was approved by the Cabinet chaired by Prime Minister Narendra Modi on 11 November 2020, the announcement from which the programme's Rs 1.97 lakh crore envelope descends, since the budget speech of 1 February 2021 carried that total for thirteen sectors. A fourteenth sector, drones and drone components, was approved in September 2021, and the semiconductor programme followed in December 2021. The staged announcement is part of the article's argument: the scheme was scaled by successive cabinet decisions under the Atmanirbhar Bharat heading, each stage enlarging the architecture rather than merely administering the previous one.

### Q: Which sectors are covered under PLI?

The Production Linked Incentive architecture covers fourteen sectors. The first three, approved in March 2020, are mobile telephones and specified electronic components, critical key starting materials, drug intermediaries and active pharmaceutical ingredients, and the manufacture of medical devices. The ten sectors approved on 11 November 2020 are automobiles and auto components, pharmaceutical drugs, specialty steel, telecom and networking products, electronic and technology products, white goods covering air conditioners and LED lighting, food products, textile products in the man-made fibre segment and technical textiles, high-efficiency solar photovoltaic modules, and advanced chemistry cell batteries. Drones and drone components became the fourteenth sector in September 2021, and the semiconductor programme approved in December 2021, with an outlay of Rs 76,000 crore, extends the design to chip fabrication and design. Outlays vary sharply by sector, from Rs 57,042 crore originally assigned to automobiles and components down to Rs 120 crore for drones, reflecting the capital intensity each sector's expansion required.

### Q: How does PLI shape post-2019 manufacturing architecture?

The article argues that the Production Linked Incentive scheme shapes the post-2019 manufacturing architecture in three ways that earlier subsidy designs did not. First, it pays for output rather than for capacity, so the state's money follows verified production and sales instead of plant construction, and the entitlement is earned annually against audited incremental sales. Second, it is allocated sector by sector through cabinet decisions, which makes the list of favoured industries a published political document spanning security sectors such as telecom equipment and bulk drugs, energy-transition sectors such as batteries and solar modules, and employment sectors such as textiles and food processing. Third, it interlocks with the other instruments of the period: the 15 percent tax rate for new manufacturing rewards the decision to incorporate a plant in India, the production incentive rewards the decision to run it at scale, and the Atmanirbhar Bharat doctrine supplies the public language under which both payments are defended. The three instruments together form the architecture this article maps.

### Q: What revenue did the Finance Ministry estimate the government would forgo each year after the tax cut?

The Finance Ministry estimated the revenue forgone from the September 2019 rate cut and its accompanying reliefs at Rs 1.45 lakh crore in a year, approximately 0.7 percent of gross domestic product. Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman announced the figure at the Goa press conference of 20 September 2019 and confirmed it in Parliament during the winter session, telling members that approximately Rs 1.45 lakh crore could be forgone and that the government accepted the charge as the price of reviving investment. The figure covered the reduced corporation tax rates, the Minimum Alternate Tax reduction and the surcharge and buyback reliefs announced the same day. Critics contested the estimate from both directions: The Hindu BusinessLine argued on 26 September 2019 that the true annual loss was smaller, since the average effective rate companies actually paid stood well below the statutory maximum, while opposition speakers argued that the permanent character of the rate cut made the cumulative sacrifice far larger than a single year's figure suggested.

### Q: What happened to Minimum Alternate Tax in the September 2019 ordinance?

The September 2019 ordinance changed Minimum Alternate Tax in two directions at once. For companies that remained inside the exemption regime, the rate of Minimum Alternate Tax on book profits fell from 18.5 percent to 15 percent, a reduction the Finance Ministry presented as relief for firms still using their incentives. For companies that elected the new concessional sections, Section 115BAA at 22 percent or Section 115BAB at 15 percent, Minimum Alternate Tax ceased to apply altogether, since the sections' conditions already required the surrender of the exemptions the floor tax existed to police. The change carried a cost that the fine print allocated to the electing firm: Minimum Alternate Tax credit accumulated under the old regime could not be carried into the new sections and lapsed on election. The dual treatment illustrates the ordinance's method. The state did not abolish the floor tax by decree; it priced the exit from the regime the floor tax guarded, and let each company's board decide whether the price was worth paying.

### Q: Why did the government use an ordinance in September 2019 instead of waiting for the annual budget?

The ordinance route placed the rate cut in force on 20 September 2019, more than four months before the budget of February 2020 could have carried it, and the government defended the timing on the ground that investment decisions and market sentiment would not wait for the parliamentary calendar. Growth had fallen to 5.0 percent in the April to June 2019 quarter, a six-year low published on 30 August, and the Finance Ministry had spent August and early September announcing staged relief packages that market commentary received as insufficient. An ordinance under the Constitution takes effect on promulgation and requires parliamentary conversion within weeks of the next session, so the device also staged the politics the article describes: the executive acted alone in September, the markets delivered their verdict the same day, and Parliament debated the measure in December with the verdict already recorded. The opposition criticised the route as ordinance governance of a permanent fiscal change. The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act received assent on 11 December 2019.

### Q: How did the stock market respond on the day the tax cut was announced?

The Bombay Stock Exchange Sensex closed 1,921 points higher on 20 September 2019, a gain of 5.32 percent in a single session and the index's largest one-day rise in a decade, as the exchange's session data recorded and the financial press reported that evening. The rally began within minutes of Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman's Goa press conference and carried across banking, automobile and capital goods shares, the sectors whose earnings the rate cut most directly raised. Government bond yields moved in the opposite direction, rising as traders priced the Rs 1.45 lakh crore revenue figure the Finance Ministry had itself supplied. The article treats the session as evidence rather than decoration: the market response was the first public verdict on the decision, delivered before Parliament had seen the bill, and the government cited the verdict through the following weeks as ratification of the ordinance route. The divergence between the equity market's celebration and the bond market's caution captures the settlement's two faces, a gift to corporate earnings financed by the state's balance sheet.

### Q: What did the opposition parties argue the tax cut neglected?

The opposition argued that the tax cut neglected demand. P. Chidambaram, the former Finance Minister, writing in his published columns and through statements issued by the Congress party, charged that the government had diagnosed a collapse of purchasing power and prescribed a supply-side remedy, and that companies holding unsold inventory would apply the tax saving to debt reduction and dividends rather than to new capacity for customers who did not exist. The alternative prescription, stated by Congress speakers in the winter session debates and by the party's economic resolutions, ran to personal income tax relief, higher funding for the rural employment guarantee, direct income support of the kind the Congress manifesto of April 2019 had proposed, and goods and services tax reductions on the consumption goods whose sales had fallen. Opposition members moved amendments in the Lok Sabha to condition the rate cut on investment commitments, and the treasury benches defeated them. The government's reply held that household measures had been taken in the August and September 2019 packages and that the corporate instrument addressed the structural side those measures could not reach.

### Q: How do Sections 115BAA and 115BAB of the Income-tax Act differ?

The two sections inserted by the September 2019 ordinance address different populations of companies. Section 115BAA sets the 22 percent base rate and is open to every domestic company, existing or new, that elects it and surrenders the exemption regime; its subject is the standing stock of Indian firms, and its effective rate with surcharge and cess is 25.17 percent. Section 115BAB sets the 15 percent base rate, an effective 17.01 percent, and is fenced to a narrow class: a company incorporated on or after 1 October 2019, engaged in manufacture, beginning production before the statutory deadline, not formed by splitting or reconstructing an existing business, and holding previously used plant and machinery below 20 percent of the total. Both sections require the surrender of exemptions and free the electing company from Minimum Alternate Tax, and both elections are effectively permanent. The difference is the difference between a settlement with the present and a recruitment offer to the future, which is why the article reads the pair as one instrument with two faces.

### Q: What did Prime Minister Narendra Modi announce on 12 May 2020 under Atmanirbhar Bharat?

In a televised address to the nation on the evening of 12 May 2020, delivered during the national lockdown, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced the project of an Atmanirbhar Bharat, a self-reliant India, and attached to it an economic package of Rs 20 lakh crore, approximately 10 percent of gross domestic product, assembled from the Reserve Bank of India's liquidity operations and the fiscal measures Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman detailed across five press conferences from 13 to 17 May 2020. The address named five pillars for the project, economy, infrastructure, a technology-driven system, vibrant demography and demand, and it instructed the country to be vocal about local products, carrying them to global markets. The Prime Minister glossed self-reliance as strength for engagement with the world rather than withdrawal from it. The address functioned as the doctrine-launch for the post-2019 architecture: the Production Linked Incentive expansion of November 2020 was approved under the Atmanirbhar Bharat heading, and the November cabinet release carried the phrase in its title.

### Q: What are the five pillars named in the Atmanirbhar Bharat address?

The five pillars named in Prime Minister Narendra Modi's address of 12 May 2020 are economy, infrastructure, system, vibrant demography and demand. Economy stood for a quantum jump in output rather than incremental growth. Infrastructure stood for the physical networks the address described as the identity of a modern India. System stood for a technology-driven administrative order, rules and processes rebuilt around digital platforms rather than inherited files. Vibrant demography stood for the country's population as the source of its energy, the demographic asset the address placed at the centre of the self-reliance project. Demand stood for the full use of the domestic market's purchasing power, the home economy treated as the base from which Indian production would address the world. The five pillars were elaborated in the Finance Minister's tranche announcements of 13 to 17 May 2020 and repeated in the government's programme documents through the following year, where they served as the classification scheme under which measures as different as credit guarantees, foodgrain distribution and production subsidies were filed as one package.

### Q: How large was the economic package attached to the Atmanirbhar Bharat address?

The package attached to the Atmanirbhar Bharat address totalled Rs 20 lakh crore, approximately 10 percent of gross domestic product, as Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced on 12 May 2020. The total combined the fiscal measures the Finance Minister detailed from 13 to 17 May 2020, among them a credit guarantee envelope of Rs 3 lakh crore for collateral-free loans to micro, small and medium enterprises, liquidity for power distribution companies and non-bank lenders, provident fund relief and free foodgrain for migrant workers, with the Reserve Bank of India's rate cuts and liquidity operations conducted from March 2020. The composition was contested from the week of announcement. Rating agency analysts and opposition finance spokesmen decomposed the headline and estimated the fresh budgetary outlay at a fraction of the total, arguing that guarantees and central bank liquidity are not expenditure. The Finance Minister replied across the tranche briefings that a liquidity crisis is treated with credit instruments rather than cash, and that the package's design matched the emergency it addressed.

### Q: What share of incremental sales does the PLI scheme pay as an incentive?

The Production Linked Incentive schemes pay an incentive calculated as a percentage of incremental sales, meaning sales of goods manufactured in India above the level recorded in a base year fixed by each sector notification, with 2019-20 serving as the base year in the electronics scheme. In the electronics scheme for large-scale manufacturing, the rate runs from 4 to 6 percent of incremental sales, paid annually over five years. Rates differ across the sector schemes according to each notification's schedule, and payment is conditional: the applicant must cross the notification's thresholds for incremental investment and incremental production, the sales must be of goods manufactured in India, and the claim is verified against goods and services tax records and audited accounts before the administering ministry sanctions disbursement. The design's governing idea is that the state pays only for output that exists, which separates the scheme from the capital subsidies of earlier industrial policy, under which money moved against plant construction and promises rather than against verified sales.

### Q: How much money did the Union Cabinet commit to the PLI schemes in total?

The Union Cabinet committed approximately Rs 1.97 lakh crore to the Production Linked Incentive schemes over five years, the total Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman announced in her budget speech of 1 February 2021 for thirteen sectors beginning in 2021-22. The total assembles two cabinet decisions: the approvals of March 2020 for electronics manufacturing, bulk drugs and medical devices, and the decision of 11 November 2020 covering ten further sectors at Rs 1,45,980 crore, within which automobiles and auto components carried the largest original allocation at Rs 57,042 crore and high-efficiency solar modules the smallest of that round at Rs 4,500 crore. The budget speech projected that the envelope would support minimum additional production worth more than 500 billion US dollars over five years, and the budget speech of 1 February 2022 added the programme's employment projection of 60 lakh new jobs and additional production of Rs 30 lakh crore. A fourteenth sector, drones, and the semiconductor programme of December 2021 extended the architecture beyond the original envelope.

### Q: How does the pillar-closing synthesis connect the 2019 tax cut to the reforms of 1991?

The pillar-closing synthesis reads the 2019 tax cut and the 1991 reforms as the same kind of act performed in opposite postures. The reforms of July 1991 were executed by a minority government under external compulsion, in the middle of a balance of payments crisis, with the revolution concealed inside a crisis budget and defended as necessity. The decisions of September 2019 were executed by a government holding 303 seats, under no external compulsion, with the revolution advertised in a press conference and defended as strategy. Both reset the state's bargain with capital for a generation: 1991 by withdrawing the state from licensing and ownership, 2019 by repricing the state's terms for hosting manufacturing and paying for production in cash. The framework the pillar applies to both, economic policy as politics, holds that the durable content of each settlement lies in its political design, the constituencies it named, the costs it distributed and the doctrine it attached, rather than in the technical merits that the rival framings debate. The 2019 settlement is the sequence's tenth moment and its closing exhibit.

### Q: What must a future government do to reverse the post-2019 tax and subsidy architecture?

A future government seeking to reverse the architecture would have to act on all three of its layers, and the article argues that the layering is the settlement's protection. On the tax layer, it would have to raise the corporation tax rate in Parliament against the Finance Ministry's own published comparison with competing Asian economies, and against a corporate sector that surrendered its exemptions permanently when it elected the concessional sections, so that a rate rise could not be softened by restoring the old holidays the firms no longer hold. On the subsidy layer, it would have to close fourteen sector schemes whose payment obligations run for five years from each approval and whose beneficiary federations have organised around continuation. On the doctrine layer, it would have to displace the vocabulary of self-reliance that cabinet headings, state industrial policies and the November 2020 approvals carry. Each layer is reversible in law. The synthesis's claim is that the three layers together raise the political price of reversal above what a single budget can casually pay, which is what distinguishes an architecture from a measure.
