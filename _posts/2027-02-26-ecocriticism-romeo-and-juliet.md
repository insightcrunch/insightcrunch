Ecocriticism, the youngest of the critical schools to reach this play, arrived with a large claim and a small shelf. The claim was that nature in Romeo and Juliet is not the painted backdrop of the tragedy but part of its machinery: the heat that primes the fatal brawl, the herbs that time the feigned death, the pestilence that stops the letter, the season that compresses the whole action into a few July days. The shelf, measured honestly, is thin. The school's standard books on Shakespeare pass the play by almost entire, and the one monograph chapter that takes the play's climate as its whole subject appeared only in 2019, more than two decades after the school had named itself and reorganized the study of King Lear and The Tempest. A verdict-led account of ecocriticism and this play therefore has to do two things at once: state what the approach genuinely reveals, and mark, without embarrassment, where the record is a reconstruction built from the school's instruments rather than a shelf of settled readings.

![The Friar gathering herbs at dawn in Verona - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict of this study is that ecocriticism earns its place on this play, but on narrower and stranger terms than its founders advertised. Verona has no wilderness. Every green thing in the tragedy is grown, walled, dosed, timed, or sold: a friar's basket of weeds and flowers, an orchard behind a wall, a nightingale on a cultivated pomegranate tree, a stuffed alligator in a poor man's shop. The school's founding gesture, which was to recover the wild as literature's neglected subject, finds nothing wild to recover here. What it finds instead is more valuable: a tragedy whose causation runs through administered nature. The lovers are not struck down by an indifferent cosmos in the manner of King Lear's storm. They are killed by a calendar, a climate, a pharmacy, and a quarantine, each of them a system by which human beings had organized the nonhuman world and then submitted to the organization. Ecocriticism, applied with discipline, reframes fate in this play as scheduling. The stars get the Prologue's credit; the dog days, the herb garden, and the plague house do the work.

The evidence base for that verdict should be visible from the start, because it is the kind of evidence that older accounts of the play walked past on their way to character and fate. It consists of a calendar recited by a nurse, a weather forecast spoken by a peacemaker, a botanical lecture delivered over a basket, a bird identified on a garden tree in the dark, a government order sealing a door, a shop inventory of dead animals, and a tomb figured as a stomach. None of those passages is obscure. All of them are quoted in every classroom. What the green reading adds is not new text but a new filing system, under which the passages stop being local colour and become the connected machinery of a single argument about how a city organizes the nonhuman world and is then governed by its own organization. Where a reader suspects the filing system of manufacturing the connections, the test is mechanical: remove the heat, the dose, or the quarantine from the plot, and the plot stops. Remove the metaphors of fate, and nothing changes at all.

That reframing survives only if the approach also survives its trial, because the objections to green reading are serious and are staged at full strength in the closing sections. Timothy Morton argues that the school's central word, nature, is itself the obstacle, a screen built to human scale that separates people from the world it claims to honour. Rebecca Ann Bach, reviewing the school's Shakespeare books in Shakespeare Quarterly, shows its signature inference failing in print, as social alienation is renamed ecological alienation without the argument that would earn the new name. Against those charges stands the work that holds up: Sophie Chiari's reconstruction of the play's early modern climate, in which July is not a metaphor but a dated, feared, medically theorized season, and the play's own doctrine, spoken by the Friar over a basket at dawn, that the same earth breeds poison and medicine and that the difference lies in use. The school's tools explain that speech, and the weather over it, better than any rival school has done. The school's slogans do not. The sections that follow build the case in that order: the method and its delayed arrival, the climate, the pharmacy, the wall, the animals, the market, the tomb, and then the trial.

## What Is Ecocriticism, and Why Did It Reach This Play Last?

Ecocriticism began as a word in search of a field. William Rueckert coined the term in 1978, in an essay titled "Literature and Ecology: An Experiment in Ecocriticism," proposing that ecological principles be applied to the study of literature and treating poems as stores of energy released again in each reading. The coinage lay almost dormant for over a decade. The field organized itself in the early 1990s around the Association for the Study of Literature and Environment, founded in 1992, and acquired its charter volume in 1996, when Cheryll Glotfelty and Harold Fromm published The Ecocriticism Reader. Glotfelty's working definition, kept deliberately broad, remains the standard citation: ecocriticism is "the study of the relationship between literature and the physical environment." Lawrence Buell, whose The Environmental Imagination of 1995 had given the approach its first major monument, added the condition that separated the school from older nature study: the inquiry was to be conducted, in his much-quoted phrase, "in a spirit of commitment to environmentalist praxis." The school thus defined itself from the start by two commitments, one descriptive and one political: attend to the physical world in texts, and do so in order to change how the physical world is treated outside them.

The second commitment explains the school's late and uneven arrival in Shakespeare studies. Feminist and Marxist readings of the plays had obvious early modern subjects waiting for them: women, property, rank. Ecology had to be excavated, because the word environment in its modern sense did not exist in the period, and the play texts offered no manifesto to quote. British usage often prefers the plainer label green studies for the same enterprise, which is why the school's Shakespeare manifesto carries green rather than eco in its title; the naming quarrel is superficial, but it records a real difference of temperament between a movement that began in American nature writing and one that began in British cultural materialism, and the Shakespeare work has drawn on both. Simon C. Estok sounded what Todd Borlik, reviewing the resulting books, called the first call for a parley between early modernists and ecocritics, in a PMLA forum piece of 1999, and returned in 2001 with "A Report Card on Ecocriticism," arguing that the school had distinguished itself first by "the ethical stand it takes" toward the natural world and second by "its commitment to making connections." A decade of monographs followed. Robert N. Watson's Back to Nature (2006) argued that the culture of the late Renaissance was already anxious about losing contact with the real, and read the comedies as that anxiety's theatre. Gabriel Egan's Green Shakespeare (2006) supplied the field's manifesto for the plays, with detailed ecocritical readings of Henry V, Macbeth, As You Like It, King Lear, Coriolanus, and the late romances. Estok's Ecocriticism and Shakespeare: Reading Ecophobia (2011) proposed ecophobia, an irrational and groundless fear or hatred of the natural world, as the school's master diagnostic term. Lynne Bruckner and Dan Brayton's Ecocritical Shakespeare (2011) opened its collected essays with the editors' founding question: "Can reading, writing about, and teaching Shakespeare contribute to the health of the planet?"

The school that produced those books was broader than its Shakespeare shelf, and its internal map matters for judging what it can do with a walled city. Jonathan Bate's The Song of the Earth (2000) gave the British tradition its manifesto, proposing an ecopoetics in which the poem itself is an act of dwelling rather than a description of a place. Buell, surveying the field in The Future of Environmental Criticism (2005), distinguished a first wave of ecocriticism, devoted to wilderness, nature writing, and the defence of a nature separate from culture, from a second wave that turned to cities, environmental justice, and the environments people actually inhabit; a play walled inside a city belongs to the second wave's territory, which is one reason the first wave's tools fit it so poorly. Greg Garrard's Ecocriticism (2004), the school's standard map, sorts its concerns into tropes: pastoral, wilderness, apocalypse, dwelling, animals, and the earth. Verona activates the list by inversion. Its pastoral is an orchard under lock, its wilderness is a reported grove, its apocalypse is a sealed house, its dwelling is a tomb. A reading that works through Garrard's map and marks each trope present in negative form has already learned the play's ecological shape.

The school's ecofeminist branch adds the one body of period evidence that this play most conspicuously suppresses. Rebecca Laroche's Medical Authority and Englishwomen's Herbal Texts (2009) documents how much of England's practical plant knowledge circulated in women's hands and women's books, in stillrooms and kitchens rather than in universities, and Laroche and Jennifer Munroe's Shakespeare and Ecofeminist Theory (2017) carries that scholarship into the plays. Set against that record, the Friar's monopoly of herbal expertise reads as a professional enclosure of a knowledge that the culture also held domestically and in female hands; the play gives its one plant book to a celibate male professional and sends its women to him as patients. Juliet never gathers, doses, or names a single herb. She drinks what she is handed, and her one moment of pharmacological reasoning, the suspicion in her soliloquy that the vial might be a poison meant for her, is conducted in the dark, alone, with no herbal of her own to consult. The green reading that notices the Friar's expertise should also notice its distribution.

One fact governs everything a green reading of this play can claim, and the surveys tend to glide past it. Romeo and Juliet is missing from nearly all of that shelf. Egan's ten detailed readings in Green Shakespeare contain no chapter on the play. Estok's case studies run to Lear, Coriolanus, the Henry plays, Othello, and Pericles. Randall Martin's Shakespeare and Ecology (2015), the first book to reconstruct the topical environmental contexts of the plays, builds its case studies on The Merry Wives of Windsor, As You Like It, the Henry IV plays, Henry V, Macbeth, Cymbeline, and Hamlet. The play does appear at the margins of the school's work, and the margins matter: Karen Raber's opening essay in the Bruckner and Brayton collection takes the animals of Romeo and Juliet and Hamlet as its subject, and Dan Brayton's Shakespeare's Ocean (2012), which argues that ecocriticism suffers a terrestrial bias and neglects the global ocean, ranges across the canon to plays whose seas are as figurative as this one's. But the sustained, chapter-length green treatment of the tragedy is a single one: the second chapter of Sophie Chiari's Shakespeare's Representation of Weather, Climate and Environment (2019), titled "'[T]he fire is grown too hot!': Romeo and Juliet and the Dog Days." The honest summary is that ecocriticism reached this play last because the play resists the school's instincts. It has no heath, no forest of Arden, no island, no storm. It has a city in high summer, and the city is the problem the school had to learn to read.

The school's most consequential encounters with the play may in the end prove to be pedagogical rather than monographic. The Bruckner and Brayton collection closes with an assessment of the challenges and necessities of teaching Shakespeare ecocritically, and the choice of test case matters: this tragedy is, by a wide margin, the Shakespeare play most often placed in young readers' hands, which means its Verona is the environment in which most students first meet the idea that a text has a physical world at all. A generation taught to ask after the play's weather, its plants, and its epidemics before asking after its theme has been given a different play, and the school's long delay in reaching the tragedy is partly redeemed by the size of the classroom it reached when it arrived.

### What does the word nature mean inside the play itself?

Inside the play, nature is not scenery and not an environment. The word names a personified mother and a moral order: the earth that bears every living thing and receives every dead one, a power that sets a virtue inside each plant and creature and leaves the use of that virtue to human hands.

Nature, for Verona, is a parent, a pharmacy, and a judge, and the tragedy's people appeal to that power as children, patients, and defendants, never as tourists in a landscape and never as stewards of a wilderness. The power waits on human use to decide whether each virtue heals or kills, and that waiting, the subordination of natural potency to human handling, is the tragedy's ecological premise.

That period sense is the discipline the whole reading must keep. The play's nature is capitalized by its speakers into a parent: the Friar's earth is "nature's mother," and the phrase is genealogy, not metaphor for soil. The same speech sets the operating law of the play's natural world, a law of use rather than of essence. Nothing on earth is so vile, the Friar teaches, that it gives the earth no special good, and nothing is so good that, "strained from that fair use," it does not revolt from its true birth into abuse (2.3). An ecocritical reading that imports a modern wilderness ethic into that speech will misread it; the speech is about management, proportion, and the human hand. Yet the speech is also the strongest evidence that the play thinks ecologically in its own idiom, because it states interdependence as doctrine: the earth is womb and tomb in one body, plants and persons house the same two opposed kings, and a flower carries poison and medicine in a single rind. The method's task is translation without theft, rendering that early modern system into terms a modern reader can weigh while leaving its otherness intact. Where translation slides into theft, the resisters examined later in this study have their case, and part of it stands.

Read down the third column, the primer states the study's thesis in pieces. The play's nature is a set of systems under administration, and the tragedy occurs when the administration fails at three points in sequence: the heat escapes management and the brawl follows, the drug performs exactly as designed and the design fails on information, and the quarantine performs exactly as designed and kills by succeeding. Nothing in the table is a backdrop. The rows are the plot, described from the side of the nonhuman world.

The primer has a hidden column, and naming it sharpens every row: time. Each of Verona's natural systems is a timetable. The dog days are a season, a span of weeks in the year when heat and disease were expected on schedule. The Friar's gathering is a dawn appointment, kept because the virtues of plants were held to ride on the hour and the dew. The potion is a dose measured to two and forty hours, a pharmacological clock set ticking in a girl's body. The quarantine is a detention of days, renewable on suspicion. The bud of love is a forecast, weeks of ripening between the planting and the flower. Even the tomb is a schedule, the three days Paris promises to strew with flowers and the nightly water he vows to bring. The lovers' tragedy is famous for its haste, and the green reading explains where the haste lives: every natural system in the play runs on a clock, the clocks are all slightly wrong for one another, and the catastrophe is what happens in the gaps between them.

## The Dog Days: Heat, the Calendar, and the Fated Sky

Sophie Chiari's chapter on the play is the one sustained ecocritical treatment the tragedy has received, and its strength is that it begins from the calendar rather than from a theory. The play is set in July. The Nurse's arithmetic in the opening act fixes Juliet's fourteenth birthday to Lammas Eve, the night before the harvest festival of the first of August, a fortnight ahead, and remembers the child's weaning by a public natural event: "'Tis since the earthquake now eleven years" (1.3). Private biography in Verona is dated by the agricultural year and by seismic memory, and Chiari's reading presses the implication that the whole action unfolds inside the most feared stretch of that year, the dog days, the period when the Dog Star, Sirius in Canis Major, rises with the sun and was held to breed heat, fever, and madness. Her chapter's title phrase, the fated sky, names her central finding: the incidents of the tragedy seem to occur first on a celestial stage before they unfold on the earth, so that Juliet's life depends as fully on Verona's sky as on its streets, and the month of July gathers a particular charge through images that bind the heroine's name to the month and to the stifling heat that hangs over the city.

Benvolio states the climate doctrine in the play's own words at the head of the fatal act: "The day is hot, the Capulets abroad, / And if we meet, we shall not scape a brawl, / For now, these hot days, is the mad blood stirring" (3.1). The sequence repays slow attention. Heat is named first, before enemies, before honour. The humoral physiology behind the line, in which summer heat thins and heats the blood and disposes a body toward choler, is the period's medicine rather than a poet's fancy, and the companion account of [hot weather and hot blood in Verona's climate](/2024/10/04/hot-weather-hot-blood-verona/) sets out that medical framework in full. What the ecocritical reading adds is causal rank. Readings governed by character treat the weather as an accelerant of a feud that would have ignited anyway. The green reading notices that Shakespeare compressed his source: Arthur Brooke's poem lets the affair run across months and seasons, while the play packs courtship, marriage, brawl, banishment, and tomb into a single short week of the dangerous season. Compression is interpretation. The tragedy is built to happen in the dog days the way a fire is built to happen in a drought, and Mercutio's death, Tybalt's death, and Romeo's banishment all fall inside the hours when the blood is understood, by everyone onstage, to be mad.

### What are the dog days, and why do they hang over Verona?

The dog days are the stretch of high summer when Sirius, the Dog Star, rises and sets with the sun, a season that early modern calendars and medical writers treated as the year's most dangerous, breeding fevers, pestilence, and derangement in overheated bodies. They hang over Verona because the whole action is dated inside them.

A July of stifling heat in which blood, tempers, drugs, and disease all behave as the season's physicians predicted makes the city's climate the tragedy's first and most constant pressure, felt by every person in it before any sword is drawn. It is that pressure, dated and documented, that Chiari's reading recovers.

Chiari's argument, followed carefully, also corrects the laziest version of a green reading, the one that treats the play's sky as a climate allegory for a warming world. Her Verona is not a prophecy. The early modern sky she reconstructs was a fated sky in a precise sense: a machinery of astrological influence and seasonal humours that educated people of the 1590s were learning to doubt and ordinary practice continued to obey. Carla Mazzio, quoted in Chiari's chapter, calls the play a "crushing tragedy of astrological determinism" unfolding at a moment when predictive astrology was being seriously challenged, and the challenge matters as much as the determinism. Romeo's defiance of the stars in the fifth act is spoken by a man who has consulted that machinery for news and found it silent; the heavens of this play are beautiful, starry, and unhelpful, a clear sky under which every disaster is terrestrial.

The season also explains the tragedy's famous haste better than the usual appeal to youthful impetuosity. The action's five days run from the Sunday feast to the Thursday tomb, a single working week, and every agent inside them behaves as people behave in a heat wave and a harvest: quickly, because things spoil. Capulet, who had counselled Paris to let two summers pass, abruptly advances the wedding by a day in the fourth act, a husbandman's decision taken over a ripe crop, and the Friar's plan is built to the same clock, its dose measured in hours because a body in July will not keep. Brooke's poem had let the lovers enjoy months of stolen meetings across a turning year. Shakespeare's compression is usually praised as dramatic economy. The climate reading adds that it is also meteorology: in the dog days, fruit, tempers, corpses, and secrets all have short keeping times, and a plot that must ripen and rot inside a fortnight before Lammas is a plot obeying the season's arithmetic. The lovers are not merely young and hasty. They are perishables in a hot week, and everyone around them, father, friar, nurse, and prince, handles them at the speed that perishables demand. The celestial frame of stars, heavens, and astrology is mapped in [the companion study of the stars and astrology imagery in the play](/2024/06/14/stars-astrology-imagery-analysis/), and its findings stand behind this section without being repeated here. The ecocritical point is the junction: in the period's cosmology the sky was part of nature, its upper storey, and the play's tragedy runs on the whole column of that nature at once, from the Dog Star at the top to the pestilence at the bottom, with the heated human blood in between.

The climate of the play was also, for its first audiences, a climate of experience rather than of fiction. The middle 1590s in England were years of failed harvests and dear bread, a run of wet summers and spoiled grain that the period registered as dearth and that climate historians treat as a sharp episode inside the long cooling now called the Little Ice Age; Chiari's book works within that frame, and Rebecca Totaro's research on meteorology and physiology maps the same culture of weather from the side of the body. Shakespeare's company played in open air, under the same sky the fiction consults. A tragedy whose Chorus blames the stars, whose heroine is born at Lammas, and whose fatal act opens with a servant class forecast of hot blood was performed before spectators who had buried neighbours in plague years and queued for bread in dearth years. The ecocritical reading does not need to imagine the play's weather as a metaphor for anything. It was the medium the audience had walked through to reach the theatre.

The play's own sky, surveyed closely, is stranger than the storm centred canon led the school to expect. There is no tempest in Verona, no thunder, no rain; the weather of the tragedy is a clear, hot, starry calm, and the heavens malfunction not by raging but by shining. Juliet identifies the flaw in the machinery when she refuses Romeo's oath: do not swear by the moon, she warns, "the inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb" (2.2). Nature's largest visible body is a byword for alteration, and Romeo's answering image has already staged the sky as a court of rivals, the fair sun bidden to "kill the envious moon, / Who is already sick and pale with grief" (2.2). The dawn that the Friar greets in order, grey eyed morn smiling on frowning night, recurs as the play's one repeated meteorological event, and each recurrence is worse: the dawn that chequers the clouds for the gatherer, the dawn whose lark divides the lovers, the dawn of the wedding that finds a corpse, and the final dawn on which the sun itself declines to appear. A criticism formed on Lear's storm expects nature to answer the tragedy in kind. Verona's nature answers by keeping perfect, beautiful, lethal time, which is why a climate reading, patient with calendars, finds more in this play than a storm reading would.

One more entry in the play's climate record belongs here, because it shows the earth itself joining the calendar as a keeper of time. The Nurse dates her great memory, the weaning of Juliet, by the earthquake: the child was taken from the breast on the day the earth shook, and the interval is counted from the shaking. Editors have long connected the reference to the earthquake that struck the Channel coast in 1580 and was felt across England; the dating arguments built on the line belong to another study, but the ecological fact does not depend on them. In the Nurse's memory, a private milestone of the body is filed under a public convulsion of the ground, exactly as Juliet's birthday is filed under a harvest feast. Verona's people keep their lives in nature's ledger because no other ledger is large enough to hold both a weaning and an earthquake, and the play's audience, which had its own earthquake in living memory, heard the entry as fact rather than as colour.

## The Friar's Basket: Nature as Pharmacy

The play's nature has a headquarters, and it is a cell with a garden attached. Friar Laurence enters at dawn carrying what the stage direction and his own words describe as a basket, an osier cage woven from willow withies, and the speech he delivers over it is the most complete statement of natural philosophy in any Shakespeare play of the decade. An ecocritical reading treats the speech not as a decorative soliloquy but as the tragedy's operating manual, the document that explains in advance how every later natural system in the play will behave. The Friar announces his errand in terms of harvest and timing: before the sun advances its burning eye, he must upfill the basket "with baleful weeds and precious-juiced flowers" (2.3). Gathering is skilled work done at a skilled hour, and the two categories into which the harvest falls, baleful and precious-juiced, are not two kinds of plant. They are two outcomes of plants, waiting on preparation and dose.

The doctrine follows in a chain of couplets that editors have long mined for the period's botany. The gathering itself is timed by the same natural philosophy the speech will state. The Friar works before sunrise for a reason: "Now, ere the sun advance his burning eye, / The day to cheer and night's dank dew to dry, / I must upfill this osier cage of ours / With baleful weeds and precious-juiced flowers" (2.3). The virtues he is after ride in the plants with the night's dew on them and are spent by the day's heat, so the pharmacy keeps the cool hours, and the dog days that govern the city's violence govern the gatherer's timetable too. Even the basket is a piece of the argument, a cage of osier, of willow withies cut from the riverside and woven, a cultivated container for a cultivated harvest, so that the scene's first image of nature is of nature already worked into a tool for carrying nature in. "The earth that's nature's mother is her tomb; / What is her burying grave, that is her womb" (2.3). Generation and decay are one circulation, and the children of diverse kind that suck on the earth's natural bosom each carry a virtue: "O, mickle is the powerful grace that lies / In plants, herbs, stones, and their true qualities" (2.3). Then the law of use: "For nought so vile that on the earth doth live / But to the earth some special good doth give; / Nor aught so good but, strained from that fair use, / Revolts from true birth, stumbling on abuse" (2.3). The famous flower concentrates the whole system in a single organism: "Within the infant rind of this weak flower / Poison hath residence and medicine power" (2.3), and the closing figure universalizes it: "Two such opposed kings encamp them still / In man as well as herbs, grace and rude will" (2.3). The full account of [the Friar's herbs and the poison and cure pattern in Verona](/2022/10/28/friar-laurence-herbs-poison-cure/) gives the speech its own article; the green reading's business here is what the speech establishes ecologically. Nature in this speech is neither benevolent nor malevolent. It is potent, indifferent to outcome, and legible to trained attention. The Friar is the play's one professional reader of that potency, and the tragedy is, in large part, the story of his reading being correct and his information being wrong.

The period record supports the speech's authority. John Gerard's Herball, the great English plant book published in 1597 in the play's own moment, organizes the same conviction at encyclopedic length: plants carry virtues and harms that the practitioner sorts by kind, part, season, and preparation. Gerard's folio was a working book as much as a learned one, compiled by a practising gardener and barber surgeon out of the gardens he tended and the continental herbals he adapted, and its hundreds of woodcut plants represent the same conviction the Friar versifies, that the created world is a stocked pharmacy whose contents are legible to trained attention and dangerous to the untrained. The maxim associated with Paracelsus, that the dose alone makes a thing poison or remedy, circulates through the same medical culture, and the Friar's distinction between smelling the flower, which cheers each part, and tasting it, which arrests the senses, is that doctrine in verse. The editions' commentary, René Weis's Arden notes most fully, stocks the Friar's basket from the period's herbals and glosses the speech against them; an ecocritic inherits that editorial labour and adds the systemic question the editors leave alone. Asked as botany, the speech is a commonplace of Renaissance natural philosophy. Asked as ecology, it is a theory of a managed world: every substance in Verona, plant, drug, gold, love, and feud alike, behaves according to the law of the weak flower, harbouring cure and poison together and yielding whichever the use extracts. Romeo will say as much in Mantua, holding his gold and calling it the worse poison of the two, and Juliet will test the doctrine in her own body, wondering in the potion soliloquy whether the Friar's distilled liquor is a subtle poison meant to kill her rather than a medicine meant to save her. The play keeps auditing its pharmacist, and the audit is the plot.

That is the reframing the green reading contributes here, and it is a genuine one. Older criticism filed the herb speech under imagery, a purple preface to the Friar's introduction. The ecocritical account files it under causation. The tragedy's central device, the sleeping potion, is not a romance contrivance bolted onto a realistic feud story; it is the direct application of the play's stated natural philosophy, prepared by the one man who has explained the philosophy aloud, measured to a span of hours, and defeated not by any failure of the natural system but by a failure of the postal one. Nature, in the Friar's handling, works perfectly. It is the human courier network, interrupted by an epidemic, that fails. The school's larger claim about the play, that its natural order is a frame for the action rather than its wallpaper, stands or falls on passages like this one, and on this passage it stands.

The speech itself refuses to end in the serenity it is usually quoted for. Its last movement turns from balance to blight: "And where the worser is predominant, / Full soon the canker death eats up that plant" (2.3). The canker, the gnawing worm or blight that hollows a bud from inside, is nature's internal corruption, rot as a resident rather than an invader, and the Friar plants it at the close of his doctrine as the fate of every system, plant or person, in which the rude will outgrows grace. The play then exhibits the doctrine three times. The feud is Verona's canker, eating the city from inside its two great houses. The potion is a managed predominance, rude will held at bay for two and forty hours by exact dosage. The tomb is the canker's final meal. A speech that began as a herbal ends as a theory of decay, and the green reading is the first to insist that the decay is not a falling away from the system but one of its stated operations.

The potion scene in the fourth act shows the doctrine at work as a technology. The Friar's "distilled liquor" is a product of the still, the period's chemical art, in which a plant's virtue is concentrated by fire and water into a few drops; what he hands Juliet is not a flower but an extract, nature processed to a timetable. The promise he attaches to it is a schedule of physiological effects: no warmth, no breath, the roses of the lips and cheeks fading to wan ashes, a borrowed likeness of death lasting "two and forty hours" (4.1). Juliet, alone with the vial, conducts the patient's own audit of the pharmacy, asking what guarantee she has that the substance is medicine rather than the poison a frightened friar might administer to erase his part in the marriage. Her fear is the doctrine's shadow side given a voice: if poison and medicine share a rind, the drinker cannot tell them apart by looking, and must trust the gatherer's intention across an absolute asymmetry of knowledge. The tragedy exploits exactly that asymmetry. Everyone who uses the play's natural systems, from the Friar with his clockwork dose to Romeo with his purchased poison, is operating a potency whose outcome the system guarantees and whose meaning it does not.

## The Orchard Wall: Cultivation Without Wilderness

If the Friar's garden is the play's pharmacy, the Capulet orchard is its other enclosed green, and the pair of them define the limits of Verona's nature. Between them they exhaust the outdoor world of the tragedy: a professional's herb plot and a rich household's fruit garden, both walled, both entered by permission or by climbing, both governed by ownership. The survey of [nature and the garden in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/06/28/nature-garden-imagery-analysis/) treats that green world in its own right; the ecocritical argument here concerns what kind of nature an orchard is. Romeo reaches the play's most famous love scene by trespass over a boundary: "With love's light wings did I o'erperch these walls; / For stony limits cannot hold love out" (2.2). The perch is a falcon's word as much as a lover's, and the wall is the scene's first fact. Nothing beyond it is wild. The trees are orchard stock, the ground is a great house's private ground, and the girl who appears above is the estate's heir, introduced by her father a scene earlier in the language of land itself: "the hopeful lady of my earth" (1.2). The love that the cliché calls natural, spontaneous, and free is staged inside a property regime, growing on cultivated trees, and the play knows it.

### Is there any wilderness in Verona?

No. The play contains no wilderness at all, and the absence is systematic rather than accidental. Its outdoor spaces are a street, a churchyard, a friar's garden, and an orchard, each one built, owned, planted, or consecrated, and its single grove, the sycamores west of the city, is reported rather than shown and serves a mood, not an action.

Verona's nature is entirely administered, and an ecocriticism built for forests and heaths must rebuild its tools for walls before it can read a word of the play.

Charlotte Scott's Shakespeare's Nature (2014) supplies the period framework that makes sense of the absence. Scott demonstrates that the dominant practice through which early modern England met the natural world was husbandry, and that the language of cultivation, of intervention, management, prudence, and profit, reshaped the period's imagination of social and personal life. Verona's greens are husbandry's spaces. Even the play's one gesture toward an untended nature, the sycamore grove where Benvolio reports that Romeo was seen "with tears augmenting the fresh morning's dew" (1.1), is a literary landscape, the conventional grove of the melancholy lover, described rather than entered and abandoned by the action within a dozen lines. The tragedy simply has no outside. Mantua, the place of exile, is presented through a shop. The road between the cities is crossed by a servant and a letter. A school that began by championing the wild has to learn, from this play, to read cultivation itself as an ecological subject: walls, grafts, seasons, and doses as the forms in which the nonhuman world actually enters most human lives.

Juliet's own figures for the affair confirm the cultivated register. She imagines the love as a plant under management, not as a force of the wild: "This bud of love, by summer's ripening breath, / May prove a beauteous flower when next we meet" (2.2). The hope is horticultural and conditional, a bud whose flowering depends on season, shelter, and time, and her anxiety is a gardener's anxiety, that the contract was "too rash, too unadvised, too sudden," like lightning that ceases to be before it can be named. Her bounty, when she reaches for the largest natural measure she knows, is the sea: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep" (2.2). Dan Brayton's oceanic reading of the canon finds its limit case here, a landlocked tragedy in which the global ocean exists only as a standard of measure inside a walled garden, invoked by a girl who has never seen it. The pattern repeats across the imagery. The nonhuman world enters the lovers' speech abundantly, but it enters as resource: as calendar, as measure, as medicine, as omen. An ecocriticism honest about this play reports that finding whole, because it overturns the pastoral assumption the play is usually wrapped in. This is not a tragedy of nature invaded by society. It is a tragedy inside a nature that society had already fenced, planted, and priced.

The orchard also feeds the plot in the most literal sense available to it, through the great household's provisioning machinery. On the wedding morning the Capulet kitchen is heard calling for the feast's ingredients, "dates and quinces in the pastry" (4.4), imported fruit and orchard fruit together, while the master of the house jokes that the bakers and cooks have kept him up all night. The same estate that grows the garden where the love was sworn also produces the banquet at which the lovers met, the feast to which Paris is invited as a prospective son, and the wedding breakfast that becomes a funeral. Cultivation in this play is not a metaphor for the household; it is the household's daily labour, the conversion of gardens, markets, and kitchens into alliances. The wall Romeo climbs is the boundary of that labour. Inside it, everything grows toward a marriage the fathers intend; the trespass consists of harvesting the one crop, the daughter, that the garden was enclosed to keep. Read through husbandry, the orchard scene's erotic charge is also an economic one, and the ecocritical account is the first to hold both charges in a single reading.

The phrase with which Capulet introduces that crop deserves its own sentence of attention, because it prices the whole system. Juliet is "the hopeful lady of my earth" (1.2), the heir figured as the land itself, sole tenant of the estate's future. The marriage negotiations that follow are conducted as conveyancing: Paris sues for the earth, is told to wait two summers for it to ripen, and is then granted it in haste when the market in heirs shifts. Her mother's book speech, wooing by proxy with Paris described as a volume to be read, belongs to a companion study; the green point is narrower. In Verona a daughter is a field whose crop is an alliance, and the orchard in which she swears herself to Romeo is not a refuge from that economy but its headquarters, the enclosed ground where the estate's most valuable growth is kept under wall and key. The tragedy of the orchard is that the crop consents to be harvested by the wrong house, and the wall, built to prevent exactly that loss, is what makes the theft delicious enough to attempt.

## The Body as Weather: Humours, Sleep, and the Senses

The last environment in the play is the one every character carries. Early modern physiology treated the human body as a small weather system, its four humours answering the four elements, its blood heated and thinned by the same sun that heated the street, its temper a forecast a friend could read. The green reading takes that doctrine seriously as the play's internal ecology, the point where the outer climate and the inner life are not compared but continuous. Benvolio's warning about the mad blood stirring is the doctrine in street dress; Montague's account of his son is the same doctrine in a father's: Romeo has been seen at dawn in the sycamore grove "with tears augmenting the fresh morning's dew, / Adding to clouds more clouds with his deep sighs" (1.1). Grief, in that report, is precipitation. The boy's inner weather augments the outer one drop for drop, and Benvolio finds him by reading the landscape the sorrow has made. No boundary is drawn where the climate ends and the melancholy begins, because the period's medicine drew none.

Estok's Ecocriticism and Shakespeare supplies an instrument for this section that the play fits with unusual exactness, and the join should be marked as an application rather than a citation: his book closes with a study of sleep as an interstitial space between the human and nature, a daily surrender in which the body's government lapses and the creature rejoins the unguarded world. Verona's plot is built from sleeps and their counterfeits. Romeo sleeps in Mantua and dreams that Juliet came and found him dead and breathed life back into him with kisses, so that he revived and was an emperor; he wakes calling the dream a presage of joyful news, and within minutes Balthasar rides in with the tidings that make the dream true in its letter and false in its comfort. Juliet's potion sleep is the period's most famous false death, a pharmacological imitation so exact that it deceives the watchers, the family, and nearly the audience, and the Friar's description of it is written in the language of a landscape in winter: the roses of the lips and cheeks fading to wan ashes, the windows of the eyes falling, every part deprived of supple government. Sleep, in Estok's term the go-between, is where this play's bodies cross into nature's jurisdiction and are administered there, by dream, by drug, and in the end by the worms.

The senses are the border crossings where the administration happens, and the Friar's speech had already mapped them. Smelling the weak flower cheers each part; tasting it arrests all the senses with the heart. The distinction is an ecology of contact: at a distance, through the air, the plant is a medicine of the spirits; ingested, taken across the body's border, it becomes a power that can stop the machine. Every decisive transaction in the tragedy follows one of those two routes. The lovers meet through the distance senses, sight and hearing, eye and ear across a hall and through a night, and their language strains to keep love in the cheering register, all bounty and boundlessness. The deaths all come by ingestion and penetration: the distilled liquor drunk, the poison drunk, the dagger taken into the breast. Even the feud conducts its business at the border, by the bitten thumb, the pricked skin, the sword's entry. A green reading that attends to routes of contact finds the play's moral physics written in them: what Verona admires at a distance it worships, and what it takes into the body, plant, drug, gold, or hatred, it dies of. The doctrine of the weak flower was never only about flowers.

## The Animal Chain: Queen Mab, Cats, Falcons, and Birds at Dawn

Karen Raber's essay in Ecocritical Shakespeare is the one piece of the school's Shakespeare work that takes this play as a primary subject, and its choice of ground is instructive. Raber reads the animals of Romeo and Juliet and Hamlet together, arguing for the thematic significance of the plays' creatures and for their material function in the world the first audiences inhabited: the animals are signs, and they are also stock, tools, food, sport, and neighbours in a city where human and animal life shared streets, walls, and air. An ecocritical reading of the play's creatures builds on her foundation and finds the same double register everywhere. Verona's animals are never merely emblems. Each one arrives with a job in the period's economy of creatures, and the tragedy keeps assigning those jobs to its people until the distinction between the human cast and the animal chain thins to a membrane.

Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, delivered in the street before the feast, is the play's densest piece of natural history, and it is a miniature. Mab's chariot is "an empty hazel-nut," made by "the joiner squirrel or old grub," its spokes "of long spinners' legs," its cover "of the wings of grasshoppers," its traces "of the smallest spider's web," its collars "of the moonshine's watery beams," its whip "of cricket's bone" and its wagoner "a small grey-coated gnat" (1.4). The speech builds a complete working world at insect scale, with craftsmen, materials, draught animals, and transport, and then drives it across the faces of sleepers to plant the dreams that the waking play proceeds to live out: lovers dream of love, courtiers of curtsies, soldiers of throats cut in ambush. The ecocritical observation is that Shakespeare imagines even fantasy as an ecology, a system of small creatures labouring in their materials. The speech is usually read as Mercutio's fireworks, a bravura irrelevance. Read materially, it is the play's scale model of its own method: large fates assembled from very small lives.

Mab's errand deserves one further ecological notice, offered as the reading's suggestion rather than the record's fact. She is a vector. Her circuit runs from nose to nose and lip to lip among the sleeping, and what she delivers is fitted to the host: fees to the lawyer, a benefice to the parson, a kiss to the lady, a blister, in one famous instance, to the lips that have been tainted with sweetmeats. The play's other great invisible transmission works the same way, carried through the air of a sealed house and arriving before its effects are understood. A tragedy whose plot turns on a microscopic delivery system twice over, dreams by insect and death by infection, has a finer sense of scale than its cosmic machinery advertises. The stars are the largest things in its universe and do nothing; the smallest things in it do everything.

### Who speaks for the animals of Verona?

No character speaks for them. The nightingale sings from the pomegranate tree and is argued over as evidence in a dispute about the hour; the lark is blamed for a parting it merely announces; cats lend a prince his nickname; falcons supply the terms in which a girl calls her lover back; horses draw the sun across the sky.

The play's creatures are heard, used, ridden, eaten, and buried rather than represented, and they have functions everywhere and advocates nowhere, which is the period's ordinary arrangement and the fact an animal reading of the play has to start from.

The aubade of the third act gives the pattern its most consequential instance, because there a bird becomes a clock on which two lives depend. Juliet argues for the nightingale, which sings nightly on the pomegranate tree, Romeo answers for the lark, "the herald of the morn," and the scene's agony is conducted as an ornithological dispute: "It is the lark that sings so out of tune, / Straining harsh discords and unpleasing sharps. / Some say the lark makes sweet division; / This doth not so, for she divideth us" (3.5). A pomegranate is itself a cultivated exotic, a garden tree of the great house, so even the wild voice of the night reaches the lovers from inside the enclosure, perched on planted stock. Around these principals the chain extends in every direction. Tybalt is "Prince of Cats" to Mercutio, a nickname drawn from the beast fable's cat, and dies in a fight conducted like a cat's quarrel over territory and precedence. Juliet, calling Romeo back, wishes for a falconer's voice "to lure this tassel-gentle back again" (2.2), imagining her husband as a trained hawk and herself as the keeper whose call he is conditioned to answer. The sun is drawn by "fiery-footed steeds" in her wedding-night invocation, and Phoebus's horses do the day's work of hauling light across the sky. Raber's material point holds at every station: falconry, horsemanship, orchard husbandry, and the keeping of cats were practices, not metaphors, for the first audience, and the play's animal language draws its force from handling the creatures, not from contemplating them.

The chain's lower links are as deliberate as its noble ones. The play's first dialogue about the feud is conducted in the language of the kennel and the street: Sampson, boasting to Gregory, declares that "a dog of that house shall move me to stand" (1.1), and the servants' whole code of honour is a dog's code, to take the wall, to stand, to bite. The houses keep their young men as fighting animals are kept, fed on insult and let out into public ground, and the Prince's ordinances fall on the brawls the way a magistrate's orders fall on a bear garden, repeatedly and without effect. Falconry supplies the register's other pole. The tassel gentle Juliet names is the tiercel, the male peregrine, prized in the period's manuals for its size and tractability, and her wish is precise: to have the falconer's voice, and the lure, that recall a flown hawk to the fist. Her next line names the condition of the keeper, whose bondage is hoarse and may not speak aloud. She casts the marriage, in its second hour, as an act of animal training conducted by a girl who is herself confined, and the figure's tenderness does not cancel its accuracy about the world both lovers inhabit, a world in which every living thing is somebody's animal, stock, or quarry.

The chain also runs downward into the tomb, and the descent is the section's last observation. Caroline Spurgeon's census of the play's imagery, conducted generations before the school existed, counted its suns, moons, flowers, and creatures in their clusters; the census as a whole belongs to another study in this series, but its premise is the ecocritic's inheritance, that the imagery forms a system rather than a decoration. Within that system, the animal register darkens as the plot tightens. The mandrake of Juliet's terrified soliloquy, a plant with a creature's legend, "shrieks like mandrakes torn out of the earth" in her imagination of the vault (4.3), a root said in the herbals' folklore to scream when pulled and to drive the hearer mad. By the final scene the figurative chain reaches the floor of the animal world, and Romeo, resolved to remain in the vault, names the company he expects: "Here, here will I remain / With worms that are thy chambermaids" (5.3). The worms are the last animals of the play and the only ones who keep their appointment. An ecocriticism that follows the creatures from Mab's team to the tomb's worms has traced the tragedy's true food chain, and the trace is the school's cleanest local result.

## Pestilence and the Price of Nature: The Apothecary's Shop

The tragedy's fate is usually assigned to the stars, occasionally to the feud, and almost never to its most efficient agent, which is a public health measure working exactly as intended. Friar John, sent with the letter that would have told Romeo of the potion plan, reports the failure in the fifth act with bureaucratic precision: seeking a brother of the order to accompany him, he was found by "the searchers of the town" in a house "where the infectious pestilence did reign," and the doors were sealed (5.2). The device descends from Brooke's poem, where the messenger is likewise held in a house shut up against suspected infection, and Shakespeare sharpens it into the tragedy's hinge. No villain intercepts the letter. No storm delays it. A quarantine, the period's considered defence against its most feared environmental fact, stops it, and the entire catastrophe of the final act unfolds inside the space the sealed door creates. Ecocriticism, which asks after the material systems inside a text's weather of fate, identifies this as the play's deepest environmental mechanism: epidemic disease is Verona's true occult power, the invisible agent that no character can defy, bribe, or outrun, and the city's rational administration of it is what kills the lovers.

### What stopped the Friar's letter?

A quarantine stopped it. Friar John was sealed inside a house where the pestilence was suspected to reign, detained with a fellow friar who had been visiting the sick, by town searchers doing precisely what plague orders required. The letter failed through a public health system functioning correctly, and the ending is unintelligible without that administrative fact.

The letter failed, that is, not through malice, not through chance weather, and not through any missed appointment, but through the collision of a private errand of mercy with a public machinery of survival, and the collision is the tragedy's hinge.

The plague was not a distant colour for the first audience. London's theatres had been closed by pestilence in the years just before the play was written, and the bills of mortality were a familiar civic publication. The ecocritical reading of the fifth act therefore recovers an experience of environmental vulnerability that modern accounts of the play, organized around fate and character, have allowed to fade: the sense that the city's air itself was a medium of death, that houses were sealed on suspicion, and that a letter's journey across forty miles of Italy could be annulled by the state of a single room. Rebecca Totaro's work on meteorology and physiology in the period traces how thoroughly early modern culture joined the state of the air to the state of the body, and the play's catastrophe is built at that junction. The pestilence never appears onstage. It has no lines and no agent. It is the only power in the tragedy that operates exactly like fate is supposed to operate, invisibly, irresistibly, and without appeal, and it does so as a fact of the environment administered by a committee.

The machinery that seals Friar John was, in the play's world, a recent and Italian invention, and the detail dignifies the green reading's claim that the play attends to systems. The Italian city states and their Adriatic neighbours had built the period's most elaborate defences against epidemic disease: health boards with powers to inspect houses and goods, searchers appointed to view the sick and the dead, lazarettos for the isolation of the infected, and at Ragusa, as early as 1377, the formal detention of travellers for a period that lengthened into the forty days that give quarantine its name. Shakespeare's Verona runs a provincial version of that system, searchers, suspicion, sealed doors, and the system works. Nothing in the scene suggests corruption, panic, or error. The Friar's errand fails against the period's best environmental governance operating at full competence, and that is the tragedy's bleak joke about administration: the city survives by the same act that dooms the lovers, and no appeal lies from a sealed door to any court in Verona. Even the feud's language knows the mechanism. When Mercutio, dying between the two camps, calls down a plague on both houses, the curse chooses the city's true sovereign as its instrument, the one power that visits Montague and Capulet with perfect impartiality.

The medium of that sovereignty was the air itself, in the period's understanding, and following the air through the play completes the section's circuit. Pestilence was held to travel in corrupted air, exhaled by marshes, graves, crowds, and sickrooms, which is why the defences against it were defences of air: sealed doors, shut windows, fires, perfumes, flight to cleaner ground. Verona's tragedy can be retold as a history of its airs. The street air of the first act, hot and stirring the blood; the orchard air of the second, full of nightingale song and the breath of vows; the close air of the plague house, sealed by law; and finally the vault's air, the one atmosphere in the play that no one administers and everyone fears, the ancient receptacle where the bones of the ancestors are packed and where Juliet, rehearsing her waking, imagines suffocation among the loathsome smells and the shrieks of mandrakes. The lovers begin in open air and end in the worst air in Italy, and the gradient is the plot. An ecocriticism that maps the play's atmospheres has hold of something no account organized by stars can see: the tragedy is, among its other natures, a story of breathing, and of who controls the air in which other people have to breathe.

Mantua supplies the complementary scene, the market where nature is sold. The apothecary's shop Romeo visits is a cabinet of the nonhuman world converted to stock: "a tortoise hung, / An alligator stuffed, and other skins / Of ill-shaped fishes," with "green earthen pots, bladders, and musty seeds, / Remnants of packthread, and old cakes of roses" thinly scattered to make up a show (5.1). The Friar's garden had gathered living plants for their virtues; the shop displays dead creatures for their price, and the difference between the two establishments is the difference between the play's two economies of nature. Romeo understands the transaction in the Friar's own terms and inverts them: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world, / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1). Gold is the true toxin in the scene, the manufactured substance that corrupts every system it enters, and the poison itself, the distilled compound of the natural world, is almost innocent beside it. The green reading draws the columns together. Verona's nature is gathered, dosed, timed, sealed, and sold, and every one of those administrations is double edged in the Friar's sense: the quarantine saves the city and kills the lovers, the drug saves the marriage and enables the suicides, the gold buys the poison and is named the worse poison of the pair. The tragedy's ecology is a system of correct procedures producing a catastrophe, which is a more uncomfortable finding than any villain could supply.

## The Tomb as Ecosystem: Nature's Womb and the Womb of Death

The Friar's doctrine promised a circulation: the earth that is nature's mother is also her tomb, and the burying grave is also a womb, so that death feeds generation in one continuous economy. The last act tests the doctrine at its limit, and the ecocritical reading of the ending is the school's reckoning with whether the play's nature is finally a system of renewal or a system of consumption. Romeo enters the Capulet vault speaking the Friar's language reversed. Where the Friar's earth was a mother whose grave was a womb, Romeo's vault is "thou womb of death, / Gorged with the dearest morsel of the earth" (5.3), a maw that eats and does not bear. The reversal is exact and deliberate, the same two nouns coupled and their direction inverted, and it marks the tragedy's ecological verdict: the circulation has jammed. What the earth receives in this play, it does not return. The children of diverse kind who were to suck on the natural bosom end as food in the womb of death, and the golden statues the fathers promise are the city's attempt to pay the debt in metal, since the earth will not pay it in life.

The churchyard scenes prepare the collapse in the play's flower language, which the green reading follows as a single thread from the Friar's basket to the grave. Flowers in Verona keep arriving at the wrong ceremony, a pattern their own article in this series unfolds; the ecological form of the pattern is a broken season. Capulet, finding Juliet apparently dead on her wedding morning, reaches for weather to say it: "Death lies on her like an untimely frost / Upon the sweetest flower of all the field" (4.5), and then states the system's failure outright: "Our bridal flowers serve for a buried corpse" (4.5). Paris, at the tomb, performs the rite the calendar should have made unnecessary, strewing the bridal bed with flowers and promising to dew it nightly with sweet water or with tears. The Friar had taught that the earth sorts every substance into medicine or poison by use; the ending shows the sorting machinery intact and the uses deranged, flowers for weddings dressing corpses, water for gardens distilled into tears, the marriage bed become the bed of death Romeo names as he takes the poison. Even his figure for the suicide is drawn from the natural world's violent motions: the body is a "seasick weary bark" and he its "desperate pilot," running it at once onto the dashing rocks (5.3). The sea that Juliet had made the measure of infinite giving returns, in the same mouth that first praised her bounty, as the medium of a wreck.

Against the jammed circulation the play sets one image of the system working, and it is worth naming because it defines what the tragedy loses. The Friar's dawn, with the grey-eyed morn smiling on the frowning night and the eastern clouds chequered with streaks of light (2.3), is the one moment when Verona's nature appears as an ordered alternation, darkness yielding to day in its proper turn, and the man who watches it understands his place inside it as a gatherer. Every subsequent dawn in the play is a disaster: the lark's dawn that divides the lovers, the wedding morning that finds a corpse, the final morning on which the Prince announces that the sun, for sorrow, will not show its head. The gloom of that last line is the city entering the earth's economy on the wrong side, a community that has fed its children to the womb of death and receives, in place of renewal, a peace purchased with statues. The ecocritical reading does not soften that ending into a lesson about stewardship. It sharpens it: the play's nature is a system that humans administer brilliantly and inhabit catastrophically, and the catastrophe is theirs, scheduled by their calendars, dosed by their pharmacies, and sealed by their quarantines.

Even the ground above the vault is planted. Paris, posting his servant outside the churchyard, orders him to lie "under yond yew trees" (5.3), and the yew is the churchyard's own tree, dark, long lived, and poisonous in leaf and seed, planted in English burial ground for centuries before the play and freighted in folklore with the chemistry of the grave. The detail is small and exact: the tragedy's last watch is kept beneath a toxic tree, in a consecrated enclosure where the living plants feed on the same earth the Friar called a mother. The churchyard is Verona's third garden, after the Friar's plot and Capulet's orchard, and it is the one garden whose crop is never harvested upward. What grows there stays there, and the play's final image of cultivation is a boy with flowers standing in the dark among the yews, watering a grave with the fidelity the gardeners of the earlier acts applied to things that were still alive.

Paris himself is that last gardener, and his gardening is the play's final study in cultivation miscarried. The Friar gardened the living, timing his harvests to dawn and his doses to hours. Paris gardens the dead, promising the tomb a ritual of strewing and watering, sweet water nightly or tears distilled by moans, a husbandry of grief applied to a crop that cannot respond. His rite is tender, exact, and useless, and in its uselessness it completes the play's survey of the human hand in nature: the same care that ripens fruit and cures fevers continues, by habit, after the object of care has passed out of the cycle altogether. Between the Friar's basket and Paris's watering can, the tragedy displays the whole career of cultivation, from a knowledge that works on life to a devotion that works on stone, and the distance between the two implements is the distance the plot has travelled.

The fathers' answer to the jammed circulation is the ending's coldest ecological fact, and it has been hiding inside the reconciliation's generosity. Montague will raise Juliet's statue in pure gold, and Capulet will match it with Romeo's, so that the children enter the city's permanent record as metal. Gold in this play is the one substance that never circulates through the Friar's economy: it does not grow, ripen, rot, heal, or poison by dose, and Romeo had already named it the worse poison precisely because it works on souls without the decency of a rind. The statues are the anti garden, flesh refused to the earth that was promised it, the cycle of womb and tomb interrupted by a foundry. Verona, unable to let its children return to the mother the Friar described, casts them instead in the material that exempts itself from nature altogether, and calls the exemption a monument. The green reading closes the tomb section on that substitution: the city that administered every living system in the play ends by choosing the one substance that is not alive, and the choice is its confession.

## The Anachronism Trial: Morton, the Resisters, and the Verdict

The brief against green reading comes in three strengths, and a verdict worth having must answer each at its own force. The mildest is the charge of fashion: the school reads a modern crisis into old texts and calls the projection discovery. The middle charge is methodological, and it has been made in print by a reviewer of the school's own books. The strongest charge is philosophical: the school's founding noun is a mirage. Each is taken in turn.

The fashion charge is answered by the school's best work rather than by its manifestos. Chiari's chapter does not find a warming planet in Verona; it finds a period climate, reconstructed from calendars, medical theory, astrology, and the Little Ice Age context of the 1590s, in which a July setting carried documented meanings that a modern audience no longer feels. That is historicism with an ecological archive, and it recovers something real that character criticism had left unread: why the play is set when it is set. Egan's own later survey, Shakespeare and Ecocritical Theory (2015), concedes the discipline's growing pains by organizing the field as a set of rival theories to be tested rather than a creed to be applied, and Estok's insistence that ecocriticism is distinguished by its ethical stand is, on inspection, a statement about the critic's obligations, not a licence to falsify a text. Where a green reading announces that the play is about a modern emergency, the fashion charge lands. Where it shows that the play is about an early modern one, the charge dissolves, and the dog days, the plague house, and the herbal are early modern emergencies attested in the period's own records.

### Where does a green reading of this play strain hardest?

It strains hardest at the word environment itself. The play has no term and no concept for a surrounding natural world distinct from the human one, because its cosmos is a single household of correspondences in which stars, humours, herbs, and souls answer one another. A reading that hunts Verona for an environment finds furniture it carried in.

The strain is survivable only when the reading follows the play's own systems, season, dose, quarantine, and enclosure, and lets the modern vocabulary arrive second, as translation rather than as discovery.

The methodological charge is Rebecca Ann Bach's, delivered in Shakespeare Quarterly in 2013 across reviews of Estok's Ecocriticism and Shakespeare and of the Bruckner and Brayton collection, and it deserves quotation in substance because it identifies the school's characteristic failure. Bach grants Estok his premise that the social is embedded in the natural, and then declines his inference: it does not necessarily follow, she writes, that a character's rejection of social ties is a rejection of the natural world, or that a play's punishment of extreme individualism is ecophobic. The connective tissue of the argument is asserted rather than demonstrated, and ecophobia, the master term, is stretched until it names every estrangement the critic dislikes. The charge generalizes, and an honest green reading of Romeo and Juliet must plead its own case under it. This study's defence is that its central claims are not renamings but mechanisms. The quarantine that stops the letter is not a symbol of estrangement from nature; it is a plague regulation, dated, staffed, and effective, and its consequences are the plot. The heat of the third act is not a metaphor for passion; it is a medical cause the characters themselves cite before a blow is struck. Where the ecocritical claim is a mechanism, Bach's objection misses. Where the claim is a mood, her objection convicts. The line between the two is the line this study has tried to hold, and it is the correct test for the whole school.

A second methodological charge predates the Shakespeare work and has never been fully answered. Dana Phillips, in The Truth of Ecology (2003), audited the school's founding vocabulary and found the science wanting: ecocriticism, he argued, borrows the prestige of ecology while using its central terms loosely, treating ecosystems as metaphors for textual communities and interconnection as a sentiment rather than a set of testable relations. The charge matters here because this play tempts exactly that looseness; Verona's correspondences, stars answering humours answering herbs, look like an ecology and are in fact a different system, a chain of signatures and sympathies that makes no claim about energy, population, or habitat. The defence available to the disciplined green reading is the one this study has practised: use the play's systems in the play's terms first, season, dose, wall, and door, and let the modern science arrive only where it genuinely translates, as it does for epidemic disease and for the physiology of heat. Phillips's audit stands as the school's permanent reminder that a vocabulary is not a method.

The school's own later conduct partly answers both charges, and fairness requires entering the answer in the record. Egan's Shakespeare and Ecocritical Theory (2015) is not a manifesto but an audit, a survey that sets the field's rival accounts of life, animals, and crowds against one another and against the science they borrow, and finds several of them wanting in the open. Chiari's climate work, arriving after both Bach's review and Phillips's book, reads as a method reformed by its critics: period terms first, mechanisms before moods, claims sized to the archive. A school that can be corrected in print and then produce corrected work has passed a test that many critical movements fail, and the green reading of this play is among the corrected work's products.

Candour about this study's own evidence belongs in the same paragraph as the charges, because the trial format is worthless if it exempts the accused in the dock beside the school. The sea argument rests on two speeches and an editor's survey; it is the thinnest plank in the structure, and it has been presented at the weight it can bear, as a reading of figures rather than of systems. The animal chain is Raber's ground, developed here rather than discovered. The claim that compression of Brooke's months into a July week is an interpretation is an inference from the texts, strongly supported by the calendar the Nurse recites, but an inference still. Against those admissions stand the three mechanisms, heat, dose, and quarantine, each attested in the play's lines and the period's records, and the verdict that follows is built on them and on nothing softer.

Timothy Morton's objection cuts deeper, because it is aimed at the noun the briefs of this school are written in. Ecology Without Nature (2007) argues that the very idea of nature, the capitalized Nature of the green tradition, will have to wither away before ecological thinking can begin: the concept is scaled to human measure, a picture hung between people and the nonhuman world that lets its viewers feel close to the world while keeping it at the distance of an image. Applied to this play, Morton's cut is precise and partly fatal. Verona's nature is indeed a picture of that kind for its inhabitants, a mother with a bosom, a pharmacy with a keeper, a sky with intentions, and the lovers die inside those pictures as much as inside the feud. The Friar's serene doctrine is itself the play's great act of framing: he looks at the circulation of poison and cure and sees a sermon, and the sermon consoles him into the confidence that the system can be ridden. Morton's lesson, learned properly, does not abolish the green reading of the play. It corrects its direction. The object of study is not a nature behind the text waiting to be honoured; it is the frames, mother, pharmacy, calendar, shop, through which Verona administered the nonhuman, and the cost of the frames when they were mistaken for the world.

The verdict can now be given in its final form. Ecocriticism reveals, in this play, the machinery of administered nature: a tragedy distributed across a season, a pharmacy, an epidemic, a market, and a tomb, in which the powers that kill are the powers that were supposed to protect, operating correctly. It reframes the familiar imagery, as its practitioners promised, and the reframing holds on the heat, the herbs, and the pestilence, the three systems where the play itself supplies a mechanism rather than a metaphor. It strains, and at points fails, when it imports the modern environment as a concept, when it renames social facts as ecological ones without earning the inference, and when it claims for this play a shelf of green authority that the record does not contain: one chapter, one essay, and a set of instruments. Raber's animals, Chiari's climate, and the play's own doctrine of use are enough. A school that arrived last to this play turns out to have been needed for it, because no earlier school thought to ask what stopped the letter, why the brawl needed July, or what the Friar's basket explains. Those are now permanent questions of the play, and they are the green reading's bequest, entered on the record with the anachronism caution attached and the joins showing, as they should be.

## What Changes in the Reading of the Play

One measure of the reading's force is what it does to the play's first fourteen lines, the passage every account of the tragedy must somehow digest. The Prologue assigns the catastrophe to the sky before a single character has spoken: a pair of star crossed lovers, a death marked love, a course of misadventured piteous overthrows conducted under the management of the heavens. Read in the older way, the sonnet is the play's thesis and the action its proof. Read after the green account, the sonnet is the play's official story, the version the city itself would file, and the five acts that follow are an audit that quietly transfers the causation downward, from the stars to the season, from the season to the blood, from the blood to the drug, and from the drug to a sealed door. The heavens in the event do nothing at all: no portent interrupts a scene, no storm answers a crime, no comet crosses the feast. Everything that the Prologue credits to the sky is performed, on inspection, by the earth and its administrations. That reversal, fate relocated from the firmament to the pharmacy, is the single largest change the school works in the play, and it is available to any reader who follows the mechanisms rather than the metaphor. The Chorus is not lying. It is doing what choruses and cities do, blaming the largest available power for what the smallest ones accomplished.

Three habits of reading change once the green account is admitted, and they can be stated plainly as the study's residue. The first is that the play's exposition is revalued: the Nurse's calendar, Benvolio's forecast, and the Friar's lecture, three passages routinely hurried through on the way to the love scenes, become the tragedy's explanatory core, the places where its causal system is stated by the people who operate it. The second is that the villain-shaped hole in the plot stops looking like a defect. Generations of accounts have apologized for the ending's machinery, the stopped letter, the mistimed waking, as though chance were a blemish on a design that should have run on motive. On the green reading the machinery is the design: a tragedy of systems administers its catastrophe through procedures, and procedure, unlike villainy, cannot be argued with, bribed, or killed in a duel. The third change is in the play's consolation, or rather in where readers are sent to look for it. The Friar's earth still promises that the grave is a womb, and the play still declines to keep the promise in any form the characters can use; the renewal, such as it is, happens only in the audience's recognition that the systems were legible all along. Ecocriticism's final service to this tragedy is to make that recognition available in the play's own vocabulary of seasons, doses, walls, and doors, which is the vocabulary the next reading of the play, whatever school it flies, will now have to pass through.

The school's own frontier, finally, has moved toward the place where this play began: performance under a real sky. Evelyn O'Malley's Weathering Shakespeare (2020) studied what open air production does to audiences and actors when the weather is a collaborator rather than a condition of the fiction, and the volumes that followed it, among them Katherine Steele Brokaw and Elizabeth Freestone's Performing Shakespeare on an Endangered Planet (2025) and Alyse Daroy and Paul Prescott's Shakespeare, Ecology, and Adaptation (2025), treat staging itself as an ecological act. For most plays that turn is a programme; for this one it is a homecoming. A tragedy written for daylight performance in a plague conscious, harvest governed city, set across five days of feared heat and starry calm, recovers a layer of its meaning whenever it is played outdoors in summer, with the audience's own blood stirring in the same season Benvolio warned about. The green reading's bequest therefore includes a practical one: it tells directors and teachers which facts of the play are load bearing, the heat, the hours, the dose, the door, and warns them that a production that cools Verona down has cut the tragedy's first cause.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch eco primer, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch eco primer is a table of the play's natural systems, element by element, pairing each passage where a system operates with the finding an ecocritical reading draws from it. Its rows cover the dog days, the Lammas calendar, the Friar's basket and his doctrine of the weak flower, the orchard wall, the bud of love, the lark and nightingale, Queen Mab's team, the sea of bounty, the plague house, the apothecary's shop, and the womb of death. The pattern it exposes is that nothing in Verona's nature functions as backdrop. Every element reappears as infrastructure: the season schedules the violence, the herbs schedule the sleep, the quarantine schedules the catastrophe, and the tomb closes the cycle the Friar announced.

### Q: What is ecocriticism, and when was it named?

Ecocriticism is the critical approach that studies the relationship between literature and the physical environment, asking how texts imagine, use, and are shaped by the nonhuman world. The term was named in 1978, when William Rueckert published "Literature and Ecology: An Experiment in Ecocriticism" and proposed applying ecological principles to the study of literature. The word then waited for its field: the Association for the Study of Literature and Environment was founded in 1992, and the approach acquired its charter anthology in 1996. Unlike older nature study, ecocriticism defined itself with an ethical commitment, treating the physical world as a subject of the criticism rather than as its scenery.

### Q: Who coined the term ecocriticism, and what did it first describe?

William Rueckert coined the term in his 1978 essay "Literature and Ecology: An Experiment in Ecocriticism." What it first described was not a survey of nature imagery but an application of ecology itself to literary study: Rueckert treated poems as stores of creative energy, released again each time a poem is read or taught, and asked how literature functions inside the larger system of a culture the way organisms function inside an ecosystem. The coinage lay largely dormant for over a decade, cited by few, until the scholars who founded the Association for the Study of Literature and Environment in the early 1990s revived it as the name of a movement.

### Q: What definition of ecocriticism did Cheryll Glotfelty give the field?

Cheryll Glotfelty defined ecocriticism as "the study of the relationship between literature and the physical environment." She gave the definition in her introduction to The Ecocriticism Reader of 1996, the anthology she edited with Harold Fromm that gathered the approach's scattered early work into a single charter volume. The definition was kept deliberately broad, on the model of feminist criticism's examination of literature from a gender conscious perspective: where that school took gender as its category, this one took the physical environment, from wilderness to city street to human body, as the ground every text stands on whether the text acknowledges the ground or not.

### Q: Why did ecocriticism reach Shakespeare studies later than feminism or Marxism?

Ecocriticism reached the plays later because its subject had to be excavated rather than found. Feminist and Marxist critics arrived to find women, property, and rank waiting in the texts under recognizable names; the environment of the early modern world did not exist under that name, since the period thought in terms of nature, the elements, the humours, and God's creation, and no play offers a speech in praise of an ecosystem. Simon Estok's call for a parley between early modernists and ecocritics came only in 1999, and the first monographs followed in 2006. The approach also carried an activist commitment that made historically minded Shakespeareans wary of imported agendas.

### Q: Which ecocritical books give Romeo and Juliet a chapter of their own?

Only one ecocritical monograph gives the play a chapter of its own: Sophie Chiari's Shakespeare's Representation of Weather, Climate and Environment of 2019, whose second chapter reads the play through the dog days and the fated sky. The play is otherwise a marginal presence in the school's Shakespeare shelf. Gabriel Egan's Green Shakespeare treats ten other plays at length, Simon Estok's Ecocriticism and Shakespeare builds on Lear, Coriolanus, and the histories, and Randall Martin's Shakespeare and Ecology takes its case studies elsewhere. The exceptions are Karen Raber's essay on the play's animals in the collection Ecocritical Shakespeare of 2011 and the play's place in Dan Brayton's oceanic survey of 2012.

### Q: What does Sophie Chiari argue about the play's dog days?

Sophie Chiari argues that the play's July setting is a governing condition of the tragedy rather than a detail of its calendar. Her chapter in Shakespeare's Representation of Weather, Climate and Environment shows the action unfolding inside the dog days, the high summer period when the Dog Star rises with the sun and was held to breed heat, fever, and madness, and she binds the heroine's very name to the month. The stifling heat that hangs over Verona in her account paralyses the city and heats its blood, so that the brawl, the haste, and the catastrophe are the season's work as much as the feud's, and the play's short, compressed duration is a summer's duration.

### Q: What is the fated sky in Chiari's account of the play?

The fated sky is Chiari's name for the play's governing heaven: a machinery of stars, seasons, and humours under which events seem to be staged in the sky before they unfold on the earth. In her account, early modern people read weather and climate through astrology and religious tradition at once, treating the heavens as the upper storey of nature, whose influences descended into bodies as heat, choler, and disease. Juliet's life, on this reading, depends as fully on that sky as on Verona's streets. Chiari stresses that the determinism was already contested in the 1590s, which is why the play can stage a sky that everyone consults and no one can use.

### Q: What does Simon Estok mean by ecophobia?

Simon Estok means by ecophobia an irrational and groundless fear or hatred of the natural world, modelled deliberately on terms such as homophobia and xenophobia so that contempt for nature can be studied as a structured prejudice rather than a private taste. He proposed the term as the master diagnostic of his Ecocriticism and Shakespeare of 2011, arguing that the plays repeatedly dramatize a dread of losing control to nature, in storms, diseases, appetites, and sleep, and that this dread connects to the period's treatment of women, foreigners, and the poor. Reviewers accepted the term's usefulness while questioning how much Estok allowed it to explain without further argument.

### Q: What did Gabriel Egan propose in Green Shakespeare?

Gabriel Egan proposed that Shakespeare studies adopt an openly ecological politics. Green Shakespeare of 2006 surveyed the concept of ecocriticism, read plays including Henry V, Macbeth, As You Like It, King Lear, and The Tempest for their constructions of nature and human society, their food, their weather, and their biological life, and closed by arguing for a contemporary EcoShakespeare alert to globalization and to the ownership of texts and seeds alike. Egan's wager was that understanding Shakespeare and understanding green politics have overlapping concerns and can sustain each other. His later Shakespeare and Ecocritical Theory of 2015 restated the field as a set of competing theories, a sign of the school's maturing self criticism.

### Q: How does an ecocritical reading change the Friar's herb speech?

An ecocritical reading moves the Friar's herb speech from the category of imagery to the category of causation. Read as imagery, the soliloquy at dawn, with its earth that is nature's mother and its flower holding poison and medicine in one rind, is a decorative preface to the Friar's entrance. Read ecocritically, it is the tragedy's operating manual: a statement of the natural philosophy that the whole plot then applies, since the sleeping potion is the speech's doctrine prepared and dosed, the apothecary's poison is the same doctrine sold, and the catastrophe follows when the natural system performs perfectly and the human information system fails. The speech stops being a pause in the action and becomes its explanation.

### Q: Why does the plague quarantine in Act 5 matter to an ecocritical reading?

The quarantine matters because it is the point where the play's fate is revealed as an environmental mechanism. Friar John's letter, carrying the one fact that would have saved the lovers, fails because the searchers of the town sealed him inside a house where the infectious pestilence reigned, exactly as plague orders required. No villain, storm, or star intervenes; an epidemic and its rational administration do. For an audience whose theatres had been closed by pestilence within living memory, the sealed door was the most familiar image of an invisible power governing human plans. The ecocritical reading restores that power to the centre of the ending, where fate based accounts leave a gap.

### Q: What does Karen Raber argue about the animals of the play?

Karen Raber argues that the play's animals are material presences before they are symbols. Her essay opening the collection Ecocritical Shakespeare of 2011 examines the animals of this play and of Hamlet together, weighing their thematic significance against their function in an early modern world where beasts were labour, food, sport, clothing, and neighbours. A tassel gentle was a trained hawk before it was a compliment, a cat was a creature of the household and the fable at once, and the insects of Queen Mab's team were the small labour force of the visible world. Raber's reading grounds the play's animal language in handling and use, which is why it survives the charge of anachronism better than most green criticism.

### Q: Why is there no wilderness in Romeo and Juliet, and why does the absence matter?

There is no wilderness in the play because its world is an administered city and its outskirts are other people's property. The outdoor spaces are a street, an orchard behind a wall, a friar's garden, and a churchyard; the one grove mentioned, the sycamores west of the walls where Romeo walks at dawn, is reported in a few lines and serves a lover's mood rather than any action. The absence matters because it disciplines the green reading. A school formed on forests, heaths, and islands must rebuild its method for walls, grafts, doses, and seasons, and in doing so it discovers the play's actual subject: nature as cultivation, the form in which the nonhuman world enters most human lives.

### Q: What does Timothy Morton object to in the idea of nature itself?

Timothy Morton objects that the idea of nature is a human scaled picture hung between people and the world it claims to honour. In Ecology Without Nature of 2007 he argues that the capitalized Nature of the green tradition will have to wither away before ecological thinking can begin, because the concept lets its holders feel intimate with the nonhuman world while keeping that world at the distance of an image. Applied to this play, the objection bites on the Friar's serene doctrine itself: his mother earth, with her bosom and her womb, is precisely such a picture, and the confidence it gives him is one of the tragedy's instruments.

### Q: Is an ecocritical reading of a play from the 1590s anachronistic?

An ecocritical reading is anachronistic only when it imports the modern environment as a concept and congratulates the play for sharing it. The school's strongest practitioners avoid that error by reconstructing the period's own ecological knowledge: the dog days and their medicine, the herbal tradition behind the Friar's basket, the plague orders behind the stopped letter, and the humoral link between climate and blood. Sophie Chiari's work on the play proceeds on exactly that discipline, recovering a climate the first audience feared rather than a crisis it could not have imagined. Where a reading keeps to mechanisms the text itself supplies, the charge of anachronism misses; where it settles for modern resonance, the charge is just.

### Q: What happens to the sea in a landlocked tragedy?

In a landlocked tragedy the sea survives as measure and metaphor, having lost its water. Verona is an inland city, and no character in the play has seen the ocean that Dan Brayton's ecocritical survey of the canon places at the centre of Shakespeare's imagination. The sea enters as Juliet's standard for the infinite, her bounty boundless as the sea and her love as deep, and it returns inverted at the end, when Romeo figures his own body as a seasick weary bark and himself as the desperate pilot who runs it on the dashing rocks. The element that signified inexhaustible giving becomes the medium of the final wreck, and the whole oceanic world of the play is contained in those two speeches.

### Q: How does the apothecary's shop differ from the Friar's garden as an economy of nature?

The shop differs from the garden as a market differs from a practice. The Friar gathers living plants at dawn for their virtues, sorting baleful weeds from precious juiced flowers by knowledge, and gives his compounds away inside a scheme of care. The apothecary of Mantua displays dead nature as stock, a tortoise hung, an alligator stuffed, skins of ill shaped fishes, musty seeds, and old cakes of roses scattered to make up a show, and sells his poison for gold to a man whose purpose he can guess. The garden administers nature by use; the shop administers it by price, and Romeo's verdict that the gold is the worse poison of the two states the difference in the play's own terms.

### Q: What can ecocriticism not explain about this play?

Ecocriticism cannot explain the play's language, its erotic charge, or its theatrical life, and a verdict led account should say so plainly. The approach has nothing distinctive to offer on why the shared sonnet at the feast is beautiful, on how the balcony scene manufactures intimacy in an open air theatre, or on the timing of Mercutio's wit, and its master terms turn vague exactly where the play turns verbal. Its competence is causal and material: seasons, substances, epidemics, enclosures, and the traffic between the human and nonhuman systems of a city. Claiming that territory honestly, and no more, is what separates the school's durable work on the play from its pamphlets.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch eco primer to argue that nature administers the tragedy?

An essay should use the primer as a causal map rather than as a catalogue of images. Begin from the rows where nature operates as a mechanism: the dog days heating the blood before the brawl, the Friar's doctrine supplying the potion and its timetable, the plague quarantine stopping the letter, and the apothecary's market supplying the poison. Argue from those rows that the tragedy's fate is administered, scheduled by a season, dosed by a pharmacy, and sealed by a public health order, and let the remaining rows, orchard, birds, sea, and tomb, extend the system. Concede the anachronism objection where it applies to vocabulary, and rest the case on mechanisms the play itself names.
