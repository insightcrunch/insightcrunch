Every May, millions of AP exams are written, graded, and converted into numbers on a 1 to 5 scale. Then, for a surprisingly large share of students, the record simply stops moving. The scores sit inside an account while applications are assembled, deadlines pass, and enrollment deposits are paid, because nobody in the family treated the act of sending as a decision with its own timing, its own costs, and its own strategy. A score that never reaches a college earns no credit, supports no placement request, and strengthens no application. The exam created the value. Sending is what delivers it.

Sending looks like a clerical step, and that appearance is exactly why it goes wrong so often. Hidden inside the clerical step sits a free report with a deadline that arrives before the scores themselves are known, a set of paid reports whose fees are set cycle by cycle, a genuine choice about which scores each college sees, and a timing puzzle that runs from the spring of junior year through the first advising meeting after enrollment. Students who handle those four elements deliberately protect the value of strong scores and avoid paying to publicize weak ones. Students who treat sending as an afterthought routinely do the opposite: they miss the free send, pay for reports that a college never needed, or send a borderline result to the one institution where it could only hurt.

## Sending is a decision, not a formality

The first correction this article makes is about agency. A score report is not an automatic consequence of sitting an exam. With one structured exception, the free send discussed below, nothing travels to any college until a student opens an account, names an institution, selects which scores the report will carry, and submits the request. The system is built around deliberate transmission. That design is a gift to the strategic student, because it means the flow of information toward colleges is controllable in both directions: strong results can be pushed forward at the moment they do the most good, and weak results can simply stay home.

Control also means responsibility. Because nothing moves automatically, every failure mode in this territory is a failure of omission or timing rather than of paperwork quality. A report sent to a college a student never attends is a fee spent for nothing. A report withheld out of vague anxiety, when the receiving college would have granted credit for exactly that score, is money quietly left in an account and later spent again on a repeated course. A free send designated toward a college chosen carelessly in the spring, before the applicant list has settled, is a benefit consumed by the wrong institution. Each of these errors is preventable, and each is prevented by the same habit: deciding the sending plan with the same care that went into choosing the exams.

The series thesis runs straight through this topic. AP outcomes are won on strategy, sequencing, and scoring literacy rather than raw effort, and sending is the purest sequencing problem in the entire program. The work is already done. The scores exist. What remains is a set of choices about destination, selection, and timing, and those choices determine whether years of preparation convert into credit and placement or evaporate into an unread record. A reader who absorbs the [complete guide to AP exams](/2008/11/08/ap-exams-complete-guide/) understands how the program is built; this article covers the final mile, where the program's value is either collected or forfeited.

## What a score report actually is

A score report is best understood as a transmission of a cumulative record, not a copy of a single result. When a student designates a college and selects scores, the receiving institution gets an official report from the testing organization carrying the chosen scores together with the identifying and exam information needed to match them to a person and, where relevant, to an application or an enrolled student record. The report is cumulative in an important sense: scores earned across several exam years live in one record, and a report ordered in a later year can carry results from every earlier administration the student selects. One student, one record, many possible transmissions.

The cumulative character of the record explains several behaviors that confuse families. A senior sending scores for credit is not limited to the exams written that spring; the report can gather the whole history, including the sophomore and junior results that were never sent anywhere at the time. A student who tested in three separate years does not hold three separate accounts that must be merged by hand. The record consolidates automatically as exams are written under the same identity, which is why the identity details attached to an account matter so much, and why a report that fails to match a college file is almost always an identity problem rather than a score problem.

### How does a score report differ from a high school transcript?

A transcript is issued by a school and records courses and grades, while a score report is issued by the testing organization and records exam results on the 1 to 5 scale. Colleges file the two documents separately and use them for different decisions. Only the official report can certify a score itself.

That separation carries a practical consequence families often miss. A strong course grade on a transcript does not deliver a score to a registrar, and a strong score in an account does not appear on a transcript unless a school chooses to note it. Credit and placement decisions are made from the official report, because registrars award credit against certified results rather than against a school's internal grade. Students who assume the transcript does the whole job discover the gap in the first term, when a credit they expected is missing and the report has to be ordered in a hurry from a position of weakness.

The report also differs from the score release, and confusing the two events causes a steady stream of errors. Release is the moment results become visible to the student, an event governed by the grading calendar and covered in the guide to [when AP scores are released](/2008/10/12/ap-score-release-2026/). Sending is a separate, later act directed at a college. Scores can be released and remain unsent for years. Scores can also be committed to a destination before release, through the free-send designation, and then travel automatically when the release occurs. Keeping the two events separate in the mind is the foundation of every timing decision in this article.


## The free send, and why its deadline shapes everything

Each exam cycle offers one score send without charge, and the conditions attached to it explain most of the strategy in this territory. The mechanism works by designation. Within the student's account, a single college can be named as the recipient of the free report, and the designation must be made by a deadline that falls in the period after the May exams and before the scores are released. When release arrives, the report travels to the designated college carrying the student's scores, including the results from that spring that the student has not yet seen when the choice was made.

Read that sequence again, because it is the whole puzzle. The free send must be claimed blind. A student designates the recipient before knowing the scores the report will contain. Every other send in the system happens with full information: the student sees a result, weighs it against a college's policy, and decides. The free send reverses the order, and that reversal is the price of the waived fee. The testing organization is effectively trading certainty for economy, offering a free transmission to students willing to commit a destination in advance.

Used well, the free send is genuinely valuable. A senior who has accepted an enrollment offer by the spring knows the destination with certainty, and for that student the blind commitment costs nothing: the college named is the college that will need the scores for credit evaluation, and the scores, whatever they are, will have to reach that college anyway for the credit conversation to happen. Designating the enrolled college converts a paid errand into an automatic one and starts the credit evaluation as early as the system allows. Used carelessly, the same mechanism sends an unknown set of results to a college the student may never attend, or worse, to a highly selective institution where a weak score arrives uninvited and unsendable-back, because a transmitted report cannot be recalled.

Two disciplines protect the free send. First, the designation deserves the same scrutiny as an application decision: the named college should be one the student is confident about attending or one where the arrival of the full record carries no downside. Second, the deadline deserves a place on the family calendar with the same status as an exam date. The deadline is set cycle by cycle and published within the account environment; it is missed constantly by students who assumed the free send could be claimed after seeing their scores, discovered in the release rush that the window had closed, and paid for a report that planning would have made free. Fees in this system are per-cycle figures rather than permanent ones, and the honest way to describe the cost of a missed free send is simple: whatever the standard report costs in that cycle is the price of the missed deadline.

## The InsightCrunch score-send decision flow

Strategy in this territory reduces to a sequence of questions asked in the right order. Destination first, because a send without a committed or credit-relevant destination is usually a send wasted. Selection second, because the choice of which scores ride in a report matters as much as the choice to send. Timing third, because the same report sent in different weeks of the cycle does different work. The table below, the InsightCrunch score-send decision flow, sets out the sequence as a reference a family can walk through for every college and every score, one row at a time.

| Decision point | Send when | Hold or wait when | Governing fact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Free-send designation | The college is the enrolled choice or carries no downside | The destination is still a guess or the record is uneven | The designation must be made by the per-cycle deadline, before scores are known |
| Score of 5 | A college is evaluating credit, placement, or an application | Almost never held when credit is in play | A top score is the record's strongest asset at every institution type |
| Score of 4 | The college grants credit or placement at 4 | A specific policy ignores 4 entirely | Most credit conversations begin at 4 or below |
| Score of 3 | The college's policy awards value at 3 or self-reporting helps | A selective reader may weigh a bare pass against the applicant | Policy, not pride, decides the borderline band |
| Score of 2 or 1 | Almost never sent voluntarily | Held by default in every scenario | An unsent low score remains invisible to the college |
| After admission | Sent to the enrolled college for credit evaluation | Held when the college awards nothing for the subject | Credit, not admission, is the audience after enrollment |
| Rush processing | A hard institutional deadline is days away | The ordinary window still reaches the deadline comfortably | Speed fees buy processing priority, not a better outcome |

The flow is deliberately unsentimental. Notice that pride appears nowhere in it. Scores are not sent because they feel good or withheld because they feel embarrassing; they are sent when the receiving institution's known behavior converts them into credit, placement, or a stronger file, and withheld when it does not. The framework also explains why generic advice about sending fails. Whether a 3 should travel depends entirely on which college is receiving it and what that college does at 3, which is a question about [which scores earn credit under college policies](/2008/10/07/ap-credit-policies-colleges/), not about the number itself.

## Paid reports and the standard order

Beyond the free designation, every transmission is a paid order placed through the student's account. The standard order follows a plain sequence: sign in, choose the institution from the directory by name or code, select the scores the report will carry, review the identity details the report will bear, and submit. Each college requires its own order. A report is addressed to one institution, so a student sending to four colleges places four transmissions, and the fee, set per cycle, applies per report rather than per score. One report can carry many scores to one college for the same charge as a report carrying a single score, which is why batching every intended score into the order matters: adding a forgotten result later means a second paid report to the same college.

The per-report structure quietly punishes incremental sending. Families who send one score in the fall, another in the winter, and a third after admission pay the cycle's fee three times to move information that could have traveled once. The disciplined alternative is to decide the full set for each destination before ordering, using the college's policy to determine which scores earn their place in the report, and then to transmit the complete set in a single order. Where a genuinely new score appears later, in a subsequent exam year, a further order is justified; where the set was knowable all along, the second order is a tax on indecision.

Processing and delivery occupy days, not minutes. A standard order moves through preparation and transmission, and the receiving college then has to match the report to a file, a step that sits outside the sender's control entirely. The realistic planning unit is weeks. Most sending failures attributed to slowness are in fact failures of starting position: the order was placed a few days before a deadline that the college had published months earlier, and the ordinary machinery, working exactly as designed, arrived after it. Students who internalize the lead time early almost never need the expensive alternative described next.

### What should a student verify before submitting a paid order?

Before submitting, verify three things: the institution named is the intended one, since the directory holds many colleges with similar names; the scores selected are the ones that college should receive; and the name and birth date on the account match the application record exactly.

Verification deserves emphasis because a submitted order is difficult to unwind. A report addressed to the wrong institution travels, gets filed somewhere it does not belong, and cannot be pulled back from a system that has already ingested it. The remedy for a wrong destination is corrective rather than retractive: order the correct report, contact the institution that received the stray, and accept that the information sent cannot be unsent. The three-minute review before submission is the cheapest insurance in the entire AP program, and the students who skip it are usually the ones who learn the difference between similar college names across a state border.


## Rush reports: what speed actually buys

Rush processing exists for the student who discovers a deadline late, and its economics deserve a clear head. A rush order pays an additional per-cycle charge to move the report through preparation faster. What the charge buys is priority in a queue. What it does not buy is influence at the receiving end: the college still has to match, file, and act on the report on its own schedule, and no fee changes how a credit policy reads or how an admissions file is weighed. Rush is a remedy for a sender's emergency, never a strategy.

The legitimate rush scenario is narrow and recognizable. A college has set a firm date for credit evaluation, orientation placement, or a scholarship file; the student learns of it with days to spare; the standard processing window would plausibly miss it. There, the premium is rational, because the value at stake, a semester of repeated coursework or a placement lost, dwarfs the fee. Every other rush order is a donation. Students rush reports to colleges that had not asked for them by any deadline, rush reports that duplicate a free send already in motion, and rush reports in the spring of junior year for applications that will not be read for half a year. In each case the money purchased speed toward a destination that was in no hurry at all.

A useful discipline is to ask what event, dated and documented, the speed is serving. If the answer names a real deadline, rush calmly and once, with the complete score set in the order. If the answer is anxiety, wait. The standard window exists precisely because colleges build their calendars around it, and an admissions office or registrar that expects AP reports expects them on the ordinary timetable. Panic is the most expensive emotion in the sending system, and it is almost always unnecessary.

## Score choice: sending is selective by design

The structural fact that surprises families most is that sending is selective. A student chooses which scores a report to a given college will contain, and the choice is made college by college. The report addressed to one institution can carry the full record while the report addressed to another carries a single subject. Nothing in the architecture requires symmetry, and nothing broadcasts a selection to other recipients. Each college sees the scores sent to that college, in the reports that college received, and no window into what was withheld or what other institutions were sent.

Selective sending reframes the whole ethics and strategy of the act. Withholding a score from a report is not concealment of a document a college has demanded; unless a college explicitly requires the full record, and a small number do state such a requirement, the selection is the student's to make under the system's own design. The honest reading of the rules is that the program built score choice on purpose, expecting students to present their records deliberately rather than dump them wholesale. Students should still read each college's stated requirements carefully, because a college that asks for all scores is entitled to all scores, and treating a stated requirement as optional is a different and serious matter.

Choice also operates across time. A score withheld from an admissions-season report can be sent after enrollment for credit purposes, when its audience changes from a reader weighing an application to a registrar executing a policy. Many borderline results should travel exactly that route: invisible during the judgment, useful during the accounting. The reverse journey does not exist. A score sent during the admissions season cannot be withdrawn when the student later wishes the file had been cleaner, which is why the default posture toward borderline sends during application season is patience.

Understanding how the underlying numbers are produced sharpens selection further. A 3 earned against a demanding composite threshold in a rigorous subject and a 3 earned narrowly in a lighter one arrive at a college wearing the same digit, and the mechanics behind the digit, covered in the explanation of [how AP scoring works](/2008/11/03/ap-scoring-explained/), explain why colleges lean on their own policies rather than on the digit alone. The student cannot annotate a report. Selection is the only commentary the system allows, which makes selection the skill worth learning.

## The borderline score problem, band by band

Every sending decision eventually becomes a question about a specific number, so the bands deserve separate treatment. A 5 is not a borderline case in any scenario that matters. It leads every report where credit, placement, or an application is in play, and withholding it is defensible only where a college awards nothing for the subject and the application is stronger without any AP presence at all, a rare configuration. The 5 also travels well across audiences: selective readers respect it, registrars monetize it, and no framing is required to make it legible.

A 4 sits in the broad productive middle. Most credit policies that mean anything at all begin paying at 4, and many pay the same award at 4 as at 5, which makes the 4 the workhorse of the credit system. During application season a 4 supports the story a strong transcript tells, and after enrollment it converts under most policies without argument. The occasions to hold a 4 are narrow: a policy that demonstrably ignores the subject at any score, or an application aimed at the small set of institutions where the applicant's other results are uniformly at the top band and a single 4 would be sent purely from habit. Even then, the harm theory is weak. Send the 4 in nearly every realistic case.

The 3 is where strategy earns its keep, because a 3 is worth different amounts at different addresses. At institutions whose policies award credit or placement at 3, the answer is mechanical: send it, at minimum after enrollment when the registrar can act on it. At institutions that award nothing below 4, sending a 3 for credit is pointless, and the only remaining question is the application-season one, where the calculus shifts. A 3 reported during admissions tells a reader that the student attempted college-level work and reached the qualified band. At a college where most admitted students present rows of top scores, the same 3 may dilute a file that was otherwise uniformly strong. Both readings are real, which is why the 3 must be decided per college, with the institution's selectivity and policy open on the desk, rather than by a family rule invented once and applied everywhere.

Scores of 2 and 1 should almost never be sent voluntarily. They earn no credit under any mainstream policy, they cannot help an application, and their only route to a college is the route the student controls. The narrow exceptions involve colleges that explicitly require the complete record, where honesty about the requirement governs, and situations where a score is being sent as part of a required full set for a credit-bearing sibling result under a college's stated all-scores rule. Absent a requirement, the low score stays in the account, where it costs nothing, risks nothing, and remains available if a future retake replaces the story it tells. A student who later retests and earns a stronger result can send the stronger record and leave the early attempt in permanent obscurity.


## Sending inside the application calendar

Timing questions in this territory are really questions about audience. The same report does different work in different seasons because the person reading it, and the decision it feeds, changes across the cycle. In the spring and summer after junior-year exams, almost no sending should happen at all. Applications do not exist yet, colleges are not assembling files for a class that has not applied, and a report sent into that vacuum arrives at an institution with nothing to attach it to. The junior-year free send is the single most wasted resource in the system for exactly this reason: it is claimed by students with no enrolled destination, naming colleges from an early and unstable list, and it delivers results to files that will never be opened.

The exception proves the rule. A junior with a genuinely settled destination, an early commitment, a recruited athlete with a signed path, a student certain of a particular state university and its policy, can use the free send productively, because the destination test, not the age test, is what matters. For the broad majority, the correct junior-year move is to let the free send go or to designate a college where the full record's arrival is harmless, and to save the deliberate transmissions for when they have an audience.

Senior fall is the season families imagine sending belongs to, and the reality is more nuanced. During application reading, many colleges work from scores students report on the application itself, a practice treated in the article on [AP scores on the Common App](/2006/12/29/ap-scores-on-the-common-app/), and require the official report only later, when an admitted student enrolls and credit is evaluated. Under that arrangement, ordering paid official reports to every college on an application list is premature spending: the reports will be re-requested in effect by the credit process, or rather the single report to the enrolled college will do all the work the dozen speculative reports were meant to do. Other colleges do want official reports during the application season, and their instructions say so. The rule is to send during application season exactly where a college's stated process asks for official scores, and to rely on self-reporting exactly where the college's process allows it.

After admission, the audience changes and the strategy simplifies. The enrolled college needs the complete credit-relevant record, sent once, promptly, so that credit evaluation, placement, and first-term advising can proceed with real information. This is the send that matters most in dollar terms, and it is also the send most often delayed by celebration and summer drift. An advisee whose scores arrive after orientation advising has been scheduled with placeholder assumptions may spend a first term repeating a course the record had already cleared. The post-admission send should happen as soon as the enrollment decision is final, using the free send where the designation window still allows and a standard paid order where it does not.

### Why can a correct score fail to reach the right file?

A report fails to match when the identity on the score record and the identity on the college file disagree: a shortened first name against a legal name, a transposed birth date, an application filed under a new surname. Receiving systems match text fields, and small disagreements strand reports until a person reconciles them.

Matching failures explain a large share of supposedly lost reports. The score traveled; the file never absorbed it. Prevention lives at the account level, where the name and birth date attached to the record should mirror the legal identity used on applications, character for character where the systems allow. A student who tested under a nickname in sophomore year and applied under a legal name in senior year has built a matching failure into the record and should reconcile the account details before ordering anything, rather than after a college reports the scores missing.

## Sending before an application exists

A recurring worry asks whether scores can be sent to a college before an application has been filed there. Mechanically, they can. The ordering system addresses reports to institutions, not to applications, and a college that receives a report for a person with no file simply holds the material pending a possible match. Nothing about an early send obligates the student to apply, harms a later application, or signals anything an admissions office acts upon. The report waits. If an application arrives, the match is made; if none arrives, the report ages quietly in storage.

Mechanical possibility is not the same as wisdom. Reports sent far ahead of applications buy nothing that a later order would not buy, and they introduce the matching risk described above, because the file they must eventually meet does not exist yet and cannot be checked against. The sensible use of early sending is narrow: a student with a settled destination using the free designation, or a family documenting scores for a specific scholarship or program file that has explicitly requested official results ahead of application. Every other case is better served by patience, because the scores do not expire in the account while the student waits for the application list to become real.

There is one more audience for early official scores, and it is not a college at all. Certain scholarship programs, summer programs, and school districts ask for official documentation during their own processes. Where a program's instructions request an official report, the same ordering machinery serves it if the recipient can be designated; where the program accepts the student's own score view or a school-verified copy, paying for an official transmission is unnecessary. Reading the recipient's actual requirement, rather than assuming the most formal document is always required, saves fees across a long application season.

## One order, several colleges, and the art of batching

Because each report is addressed to a single institution and charged per transmission, the multi-college season rewards planning. A student applying to eight colleges does not need eight reports. The student needs reports at the colleges whose processes require official scores during application season, which is often none of them, and one complete report at the enrolled college after the decision, which is always the essential one. Between those poles sits a middle group: colleges where an official report strengthens a file the student cares about, a scholarship deadline requires documentation, or a credit promise needs verification before enrollment can be evaluated intelligently. Sending to that middle group is a judgment call made college by college, with the policy and the stakes in view.

Batching also operates within a single destination. The instinct to send a strong score the moment it appears, then send another when a second result posts, doubles fees for no benefit at a college that files reports rather than acting on each arrival. A destination should receive its complete intended set in one order wherever the calendar permits, assembled after release when every result in the set is known. The free send complicates batching in one direction only: it carries what it carries, as one transmission to one college, and any later supplement to that same college is a paid order, which is another reason the free designation should be spent on the destination most likely to need the full record.

Record keeping ties the whole practice together. A family that tracks which scores went to which colleges, on which dates, under which order confirmations, can answer a registrar's question in one message and diagnose a missing report in one sitting. A family that sends from memory discovers gaps in the worst week of the summer. The tracking habit costs a single page of notes maintained across the season, and it converts sending from a scatter of forgotten transactions into an auditable plan, the same discipline the study side of the program rewards.


## The cumulative record across exam years

A student's record is a single accumulating file, and reports draw from it across years. The practical meaning is generous: a senior ordering a report for credit can include results from every prior administration in the same transmission, presenting the enrolled college with the complete history in one filing. The college evaluating credit wants exactly that completeness, because credit decisions interact; a department awarding credit for a sequence needs to see both halves, and a registrar applying a cap on total AP credit needs the whole inventory before ruling on any part of it.

Completeness cuts both ways, and the selection step is where the student shapes it. A cumulative report is cumulative only over the scores selected. The history exists in the account in full, while each report carries the subset chosen for that destination. A student whose sophomore attempt went badly and whose junior and senior work is strong can send the enrolled college the strong subset for admissions-adjacent purposes and still, after enrollment, reconsider whether the early score belongs in the credit conversation, where at some institutions a passing early result in a different subject may be worth money. The record remembers everything. The reports present what strategy selects.

Age changes the record's accessibility in one specific way. Results from the most recent administrations live actively in the online account, while older results are eventually moved to an archive, after which ordering follows an archive request route rather than the ordinary online order, with its own per-cycle handling. Archived does not mean destroyed or expired; alumni returning for a graduate or professional purpose, or adults whose employers or licensing paths ask for documentation, can still retrieve official reports of old scores. The lesson for families is modest: sending during the active years is simpler and cheaper in effort, and a student who knows a score will matter later should send it while the ordinary machinery handles it, rather than rediscovering the archive process years afterward.

## Confirming arrival, and repairing a wrong destination

An order confirmation proves submission, not delivery, and the distinction matters for any send tied to a deadline. Confirmation that a college received and filed a report comes from the college side: a checklist mark inside an applicant or student portal, a credit line appearing on a degree audit, or a direct answer from the registrar or admissions operations office when asked. Students sending against a dated requirement should verify through the recipient's channel after a reasonable processing interval, the same way a traveler confirms a booking rather than trusting a departure board. Where the portal shows nothing after the ordinary window, the first diagnostic is matching, the identity problem described earlier, and the second is a polite inquiry carrying the order details, which resolves most cases without any new fee.

The wrong-college send is the mistake families fear most, and its repair is undramatic. A report that traveled to the wrong institution cannot be recalled; the receiving system has it, and deletion requests are handled by that institution under its own practices. The corrective sequence is to order the correct report immediately, to notify the intended college if a deadline is implicated, and, where the stray destination matters, to contact the institution that received the report in error and ask how it handles misdirected score material. In practice, an unrequested report for a non-applicant is inert paper in a filing system. It creates no application, triggers no review, and follows no one. The real cost of the error is the duplicated fee and the lost days, both of which trace back to the skipped verification step before submission.

A subtler error sends the right scores to the right college under the wrong identity, a transposed birth date or a name variant, producing a report that arrives and matches nothing. The repair in that case runs through the account holder's support channel, where identity details on the record can be corrected and the report re-matched or reissued under the repaired identity. Families should treat identity hygiene as part of sending rather than as a separate chore: one legal name, one birth date, used identically on the account, the applications, and the enrollment paperwork, prevents the entire class of failures.

## Sending beyond borders

AP scores travel internationally more readily than many families expect. Universities in dozens of countries recognize AP results for admission consideration, placement, or credit under their own published frameworks, and the ordering system can address reports to institutions abroad in much the same way it addresses domestic ones. The strategic difference lies in interpretation rather than transmission. A foreign university may read a row of strong scores as evidence of readiness for a demanding curriculum, may map scores to entry requirements in specific subjects, or may treat them as supporting material beside a national qualification. The send itself is ordinary; the research into what the destination does with the record is not, and it should happen before the order, not after.

International applicants working in the other direction face a mirror-image problem. A student educated abroad who holds AP results and applies to colleges in the United States sends through the same account machinery as any domestic student, and the report carries the same weight. Where the record was built across schools or testing arrangements in more than one country, identity consistency becomes even more important, because matching systems have less local context to rescue a near-match. The score record is one of the most portable documents in international admissions, and treating its transmission with care preserves that portability.

## Sending, withholding, and cancelling: three different levers

Vocabulary confusion in this territory causes real damage, so the three levers deserve separation. Sending is transmission: a selected set of scores moves to a named college. Withholding is a hold placed on a specific score so that it is excluded from reports until the hold is released; the score continues to exist, invisible to recipients while the hold stands. Cancelling is deletion: a score is permanently removed from the record, under the deadlines and conditions the program sets, and cannot be recovered or sent afterward. These are escalating interventions, from presentation, to temporary concealment, to destruction.

This article's territory is the first lever. The other two belong to their own guides, and conflating them with sending leads students to destroy scores they merely needed to hold back, or to imagine a hold protects a score that a free send has already committed. The detailed treatment of holds and deletion lives in the guide to [AP exam cancellation and withholding](/2006/11/10/ap-exam-cancellation-and-withholding/), and the comparison between the two protective levers is drawn precisely in [score cancellation versus withholding](/2006/04/26/ap-score-cancellation-vs-withholding/). Read those before touching either lever. For sending purposes, the operative principle is that ordinary selection, choosing what each report carries, accomplishes most of what families want from the heavier machinery, without deadlines or irreversibility.

One interaction deserves a plain statement here. The free send transmits the record to the designated college when scores release. A student who intends to withhold or cancel a specific score must reckon with that transmission timing, because a report that has already traveled cannot be edited by a later hold on the account. Students carrying a known weak result into a spring where a free send is designated should think through the interaction before the release, not after it. Deliberate sending includes knowing what each mechanism will do to the record before any of them is used.

## What colleges do with a report

Understanding the receiving end improves every sending decision. At most institutions, an incoming report is matched to a record and then routed according to the season. During application season, at colleges that require or accept official scores, the report joins the file that readers consult beside the transcript and the self-reported results. After enrollment, the report takes a different path, toward the registrar and the departments, where its scores are read against published policy and converted into credit, placement, or a polite nothing. The same envelope of data serves two bureaucracies with two different questions: whether to admit, and what to award.

The credit path is where the money is. A report that arrives before orientation advising feeds degree planning from the first meeting; a report that arrives afterward forces revisions to a schedule already built. Departments, not admissions offices, judge most borderline credit questions, which is why the award for a score can depend on the student's declared direction and why a registrar may ask for a syllabus or direct a student to a departmental contact. None of that machinery can start on a score the college has not received. Students who grasp the routing send early after enrollment as a matter of course, and they send to state universities with the same care as to private ones, since the credit tables at large public institutions, examined in the guide to [AP credit at state universities](/2006/12/20/ap-credit-at-state-universities/), are often the most generous in the entire system.

The admissions path rewards restraint in the opposite direction. A reader's time with a file is short, and the scores that help are the scores that corroborate the record's strengths. Flooding the file with every result, including the weak and the irrelevant, does not project thoroughness; it projects an absence of judgment, in a process that is substantially about judging judgment. The selective architecture of the report system exists so that the student's presentation can be as considered as the application itself, and students should use the architecture rather than surrender to the superstition that more paper is always better.


## Who decides: the student, the family, and the counselor

Score records belong to the student, and the sending decisions sit in the account only the student operates. That legal simplicity hides a practical negotiation. Parents who funded the exams often feel entitled to direct the traffic; counselors who manage a school's advising calendar often hold the best local knowledge about which colleges on a student's list actually require official reports; the student holds both the credentials and the consequences. The functional arrangement is a division of labor. The counselor contributes process knowledge. The family contributes the policy research and the calendar discipline. The student executes the orders and owns the selection decisions, informed by both.

Disagreements usually concern the borderline band. A parent may want every score sent everywhere as proof of effort, while the student wants a disappointing result buried. The decision flow settles most of these arguments without reference to pride: what does this college award at this score, and what does this audience do with this information? Questions framed that way have answers that can be looked up, and lookup beats advocacy. Where the policy awards value, send. Where the audience can only be harmed or unmoved, hold. The counselor's office, which has watched the same argument across many cohorts, is the natural referee, and a short conversation there is worth more than a long one at the kitchen table.

Schools vary in how actively they support the sending process. Some build the free-send designation into a spring advising session for seniors; others leave the entire mechanism for families to discover. A student should learn which model the school operates early, because the discovery that no one was minding the deadline tends to arrive in the same week the deadline does. Ownership of the calendar cannot be outsourced to an institution whose attention is divided among hundreds of records. The account, the deadline, and the orders belong to the student, whatever support surrounds them.

## Strategy by student position

Position changes emphasis even when the mechanics stay fixed. The first-generation applicant, whose family has no inherited map of the process, is most exposed to the twin errors of assuming scores travel automatically and assuming every college needs an official report during application season. For that student, the highest-value moves are learning that the account exists and must be operated, capturing the free send with a settled destination in senior spring, and asking each college directly whether official scores are wanted with the application or after enrollment. None of those moves costs anything. All of them protect real money.

The homeschooled or independent candidate, whose exams were arranged through a host school, faces an identity and matching environment with more seams, addressed partly in the registration guide's treatment and worth double care here. The record may carry the host school's context while the applications carry a home address and a parent-built transcript. Sending early enough to surface and repair a matching problem, rather than in the final week before a deadline, converts a fragile arrangement into a routine one. The independent candidate should also confirm that all results written under arrangements in different years consolidated into one record, visible in the account, before relying on a single report to carry them.

The student aiming at the most selective colleges faces the presentation problem in its sharpest form. At institutions where admitted students present deep rows of top scores, selection is a real editorial act, and the weakest results in a record deserve the hardest scrutiny before traveling during application season. The same student, after enrollment, should reverse posture entirely and send the registrar everything with credit potential, because the audience has changed from judges to accountants. Holding a credit-bearing score back from an enrolled college out of leftover application-season caution is a pure financial loss, repeated silently across thousands of first-year schedules every year.

The student whose list is anchored by large public universities should weight the entire strategy toward credit capture. Public systems often publish the clearest credit tables and award the most units, which makes the post-enrollment report the single most valuable transmission of the whole process and the free send, aimed at the likely destination, a genuinely bankable benefit. For that student, the application-season questions simplify: self-report where allowed, send officially where required, and make certain the complete credit-relevant set reaches the enrolled institution before advising begins.

## The long life of an unsent score

Scores that are never sent do not evaporate. They persist in the record, visible to the student, available for transmission in later seasons and, after the active window, through the archive route described earlier. A score can be sent for the first time years after it was earned: by a transfer student whose new institution reads the record differently, by a returning adult whose program asks for documentation, by a graduate whose employer or professional pathway requests certified results. The absence of a deadline on the record itself is a quiet mercy of the system. Sending decisions can be revisited whenever a new audience appears, and a score held in one season loses none of its value for a later one.

Persistence also disciplines regret. A student who chose not to send a borderline score during application season has lost nothing if the enrolled college's policy later makes that score valuable; the report can be ordered then, complete and current. The asymmetry between the two error types should govern every close call. A score sent in error cannot be retrieved from a college that has filed it. A score held in error can be sent the week the error is discovered. When a family is genuinely torn about a borderline transmission during application season, the tie-breaker is structural: hold, because holding is reversible and sending is not.

The record's longevity carries one maintenance obligation. Accounts are accessed with credentials created in adolescence and needed, potentially, a decade later. The student who loses access to the account faces a recovery process before any sending can happen, usually at the moment of greatest hurry. Keeping the account credentials with the family's other durable records, and the identity details on the record consistent with legal documents, keeps the long-lived record usable across its whole life.

## Common mistakes that cost money or value

The mistake catalogue in this territory is short, stable, and almost entirely self-inflicted. Missing the free-send deadline stands first, an error of pure calendar that converts a free transmission into a paid one and is repeated by enormous numbers of students every cycle. Assuming scores are sent automatically stands second; no mechanism moves a record to a college outside the free designation and the student's own orders, and students learn this in the worst settings, at orientation or in a registrar's queue. Sending every score to every college stands third, a strategy of volume that pays the most fees for the least discrimination and hands weak results to audiences that never asked for them.

Fourth comes the premature junior-year send, reports dispatched to colleges on an early list that the final list will not resemble, arriving at files that will never exist. Fifth, the duplicated report, where a family orders a paid report to a college already holding the same scores from the free send, paying to deliver information twice because no record of the first transmission was kept. Sixth, the fragmented order, several partial reports to one destination across a season where one complete order would have served, each fragment carrying its own fee. Seventh, the identity mismatch, in which a perfectly good order fails to file because the name on the record and the name on the application differ by a nickname, a hyphen, or a transposed digit in a birth date.

Eighth, and most quietly expensive, is the omitted post-enrollment send: the senior who never transmits the record to the college actually attended, and who discovers the omission when a first-year schedule contains a course the scores had already cleared. Against that catalogue stands a single counterweight, the decision flow near the front of this article and a one-page family record of destinations, dates, and confirmations. Sending errors are not knowledge problems. They are administration problems, and administration yields to checklists.

### What makes one destination easier to send to than another?

Some colleges make sending simple because their instructions state plainly whether official scores are wanted with an application or after enrollment, and their portals show a received report visibly. Ease is mostly a measure of how clearly an institution explains its own process, which a student can assess before ordering anything.

## A sending plan, season by season

Assembled in order, the whole practice fits on one calendar. During the school year, the work is research and hygiene: confirm the account identity matches legal documents, learn which subjects and results are in the record, and read the credit policies of the colleges rising on the student's list so the eventual selections will be informed rather than improvised. In the spring of the exam year, the free-send decision arrives. A student with a settled destination designates it inside the account before the per-cycle deadline; a student without one makes the harder, often wiser choice to let the benefit pass rather than aim it at a guess.

At release, restraint governs. The results become visible, the emotional weather is strong, and the correct number of paid orders in the first days is usually zero. The application list is unfinished, the policies are the research that matters, and every send can wait for an audience. Across the autumn application season, sending follows each college's stated process: official reports where required, self-reporting where allowed, selection applied per destination with the borderline band decided against policy and selectivity rather than feeling. Through the winter, confirmations are checked where a dated requirement exists, and the family record of transmissions is kept current.

In the spring of the decision, the plan converges on one essential transmission. Once enrollment is chosen, the complete credit-relevant record goes to that college, by the free designation where the window serves and by a standard order where it does not, early enough for credit evaluation and advising to work with it. After arrival at college, the student verifies the award against the published policy, because a report received is not always a report fully applied, and discrepancies are far easier to repair in the first term than in the third. That sequence, run once with attention, captures nearly all the value the sending system holds.

## Inside the order: how a transmission actually happens

The mechanics deserve a walk-through, because students who picture the machinery make fewer errors with it. An order begins inside the student's account, the same environment where scores are viewed after release. The student selects the score-sending function, identifies the recipient from the institution directory, and confronts the first real decision: the directory is vast, and it contains institutions with near-identical names in different states, branch campuses filed separately from flagship campuses, and professional schools listed apart from their parent universities. Choosing the recipient is a research act disguised as a search box. The college code, where a student knows it from application materials, resolves ambiguity better than any name fragment, and the confirmation screen, which restates the institution and its location, exists to be read rather than clicked through.

Selection follows destination. The order presents the record and asks which scores the report will carry, and the student applies the per-college judgment built from policy research. A detail worth internalizing is that selection happens fresh for every order. The system does not assume that the set sent to one college defines the set for the next, and it does not warn a student who includes a score that a previous order withheld. Consistency across orders is the student's responsibility, which is another argument for the family record of what went where.

Submission closes the order and starts a process the student cannot watch in detail. The report is prepared from the record, transmitted to the institution through the channel that institution uses, and received into a workflow built around matching. Confirmation from the ordering side establishes that the request was accepted and charged or designated correctly. Everything after that point happens inside the recipient's systems, which is why verification, the portal check or the registrar inquiry, is a separate step performed later and through the college's own channels. Students who understand the handoffs stop expecting the sending side to certify events on the receiving side, and they stop treating a confirmation page as proof of delivery.

Designation and ordering are siblings, not twins. The free send is a designation: a standing instruction placed before release, executed by the system when scores post. A paid order is an instruction placed after results exist, executed on submission. The difference explains their different risk profiles. The designation's risk is blindness, committing before the results are known. The order's risk is error, choosing wrongly among known results and known destinations. A complete sending plan uses each tool in the season where its risk is smallest: designations for settled destinations in the spring, orders for everything deliberate that follows.

## The free send, class year by class year

The value of the free designation depends heavily on where the student stands, and walking the class years exposes the pattern. A freshman or sophomore with AP results holds scores that will matter eventually and a college list that is mostly imaginary. For that student the free send is nearly worthless as a transmission, because no destination can pass the settled test, and its only real function is as an early lesson in how the account works. Letting it lapse costs nothing, since the same reports can be ordered years later with full knowledge of the scores and the destinations. Families sometimes treat the unused designation as waste. It is better understood as an option that expired unexercised because exercising it would have been worse.

The junior year is the contested case. A junior's list has begun to form, a few colleges may feel like destinations, and the temptation to use the free resource on the favorite is strong. The discipline from earlier sections applies with full force: unless the destination is settled by commitment, geography, or an unmistakable policy fit, the designation is a guess dressed as thrift. A junior who does designate should choose the college where the arrival of the complete record, strong or mixed, can do no harm, which usually means a college whose process the family understands well rather than the most selective name on the list.

The senior year is where the designation comes into its own. A senior who has been admitted and has chosen, or who holds a clear first choice with an enrollment deadline in the spring, can name the destination with the confidence the mechanism assumes. The report then travels at release, weeks before a paid order placed in the summer drift would arrive, and the credit machinery starts early. Seniors without a settled destination by the designation deadline face the junior's problem one last time, and the same answer applies: a free send aimed at a guess is worth less than a paid send aimed at a certainty, because the paid send arrives with the scores chosen and the destination real.

One more senior scenario deserves attention, the student enrolling at a college that awards nothing for the subjects tested. There, the free designation has no credit work to do, and its value shrinks to documentation the college may never request. Even a certain destination does not make a useless transmission valuable. The designation should follow usefulness, not merely certainty, and a student whose enrolled college ignores the entire record can let the deadline pass with a clear conscience and keep the record in reserve for a transfer application or another future audience.

## The economics of a sending season

Fees in this system are per-cycle and per-report, and while this article states no figures, the arithmetic of a season can still be planned. The variables are the number of destinations receiving official reports, the number of separate orders placed per destination, and the number of rush premiums purchased under pressure. A disciplined season holds the first variable to the colleges whose processes require official transmission, the second to one complete order per destination wherever the calendar allows, and the third to zero. An undisciplined season multiplies all three, and the total, whatever the cycle's prices, lands several times higher for a record that arrives in worse condition.

Families should also budget the invisible costs. A misdirected report costs a duplicate order. A fragmented order costs its fragments. An archive request years later costs more effort than a timely order, and rush charges cluster where calendar discipline failed earliest. Against those costs, the free designation stands as the season's one structural saving, worth exactly one standard report at the cycle's price, available to any student whose destination is settled in time. Viewed coldly, the sending season is a small exercise in operations management, and the families who run it well spend the least money for the most reliable delivery.

Fee reductions belong to the wider exam program's equity architecture rather than to sending specifically, and students eligible for reductions on exam fees should ask whether any parallel provision touches their orders in a given cycle rather than assuming one exists or assuming none does. The honest posture is inquiry: provisions change, cycles differ, and the account environment and the school counselor are the authoritative sources for what a specific student's orders will cost in a specific season. What does not change is the structure, per report, per cycle, with speed and age as premium dimensions, and structure is what planning needs.

## Why official reports exist at all

It is reasonable to ask why a student cannot simply show a college the score screen and be done. The answer is integrity, and understanding it builds respect for the machinery. A score is a credential with cash value: it converts into tuition saved, requirements waived, and placements granted. Credentials with cash value attract fabrication, and a screenshot is the easiest document in the world to alter. The official report solves the problem by moving the information through a channel the college trusts, from the testing organization's record to the institution's file, with no student handling in between. The fee, the account, and the processing time are the price of a transmission a registrar can act on without verifying anything by hand.

Self-reported scores fit into the integrity system at a different level of trust. When a college accepts scores reported on an application, it is extending provisional trust, sufficient for an admissions judgment that will be audited later against the official report when credit is at stake. That two-tier arrangement explains the otherwise puzzling pattern families meet: a college content to read self-reported numbers in the autumn and insistent on the official transmission in the summer is not being inconsistent. It is matching the strength of the evidence to the weight of the decision, provisional for judgment, certified for money.

The same logic governs the treatment of scores reported by schools. A counselor's note or a school profile mentioning results may support an application narrative, and some transcripts carry scores a school has chosen to print, yet none of those substitutes for the official channel when credit is evaluated. Students who grasp the distinction stop asking schools to perform a transmission schools cannot perform, and they stop being surprised when a registrar asks for a report the student believed the transcript had already delivered.

## Sending again: transfers, second degrees, and new audiences

The first sending season feels final, and for many students it is. For a substantial minority, though, the record gets transmitted again under new circumstances, and the system's design accommodates the encore gracefully. The transfer student is the most common case. A student who enrolled at one college, sent the record there, and later moves to another institution needs the record sent again to the new destination, where a different credit policy will read the same scores and may award a substantially different result. A score the first college ignored may be valuable at the second; a subject the first college capped may be uncapped at the second. The sending decision must be rebuilt from the new policy rather than copied from the old one, and the old transmissions are simply irrelevant history, visible to no one at the new institution.

Transfer sending has its own timing trap. Credit evaluation for transfers often happens against enrollment and course-registration deadlines that are tighter than the first-year calendar, and a record that arrives after registration may leave a transferred student repeating material for a term before the award posts. Sending at the moment of transfer admission, rather than after arrival, is the protective habit. The student should also expect the receiving institution to re-evaluate from the official report rather than accepting the first college's transcript notation of credits granted, since transfer credit is judged from primary evidence wherever the stakes are coursework waived.

Later audiences appear across adult life. A graduate returning for a second degree may find an institution willing to clear general requirements with an old record. An employer in a field with formal credentialing may request certified results. A professional program may ask for documentation of prerequisite-adjacent coursework. Each request is answered by the same channel, the account or its archive successor, and each validates the maintenance advice given earlier: keep the credentials, keep the identity consistent, and the record stays usable for decades. The student who sent nothing anywhere in high school has lost nothing irrecoverable; the record waited, and waiting is one of the things the system does best.

## Names, identities, and repairing the record itself

Records are matched on identity, so identity changes deserve a section of their own. Students change names, families correct spellings, and documents issued years apart can carry variations that made sense at each issuance and chaos in combination. A score record created under one form of a name and an application filed under another will meet the matching problem described earlier every time a report is sent, until the underlying record is repaired. The repair route runs through the testing organization's support process, which can amend identity details on documentation, and it should be completed before a deadline-driven season begins rather than during one.

The same discipline covers birth dates entered wrongly at some hurried registration, accounts duplicated when a student created a second profile instead of recovering the first, and results scattered across two records that should be one. Duplicated accounts are the nastiest variant, because each holds part of the history and a report from either is incomplete. Merging and correction are support-channel work, slow relative to ordering, and they belong in the quiet months. A student entering application season should be able to open one account, see the entire record under one consistent identity, and order from it with confidence. Achieving that state is a sending task, even though no report moves while it happens.

There is a forward-looking lesson here for younger students. The identity used at the first exam registration tends to persist for the whole record, so the name form chosen at fourteen, perhaps a nickname offered casually to a proctor's roster, can echo into every transmission at eighteen. Using the full legal name from the first registration, spelled as it appears on the documents applications will use, is the cheapest strategic act in the entire sending system. It costs one careful minute and prevents a category of failure that otherwise has to be repaired at professional pace under deadline pressure.

## Placement without credit: the other reason reports travel

Credit monopolizes the sending conversation, yet a second institution function consumes reports just as eagerly: placement. Many colleges award no credit for a subject and still use its score to decide where a student begins. A mathematics department may excuse a student from an introductory sequence on the strength of a score while granting no units for the privilege. A language program may sort every entering student by certified proficiency, AP results among the evidence accepted. A writing or science program may use scores to waive a prerequisite while requiring the full number of courses afterward. In each case the award is invisible on a credit table, and a family researching only credit columns would conclude the score is worthless at that college and never send it.

Placement value changes several decisions in this article. The borderline band widens: a score too low for credit may still clear a placement threshold, which can rescue a first-term schedule from a course pitched below the student's preparation. The post-enrollment send becomes more urgent, since placement decisions cluster around orientation and advising, the very meetings an unarrived report misses. The free designation gains value at colleges with rich placement uses even where credit is stingy. Research, accordingly, has to ask two questions of every destination: what does this score earn, and where does this score start me? A college's answers live in different offices and sometimes in different documents, and the complete picture is the one the sending plan should be built on.

Placement also explains why some colleges request official reports from every enrolled student with AP results regardless of the credit table. The request is not bureaucratic greed; the institution is feeding an advising system that sorts students into starting points, and it needs certified inputs to do the sorting defensibly. A student who receives such a request should read it as a sign the scores will be used, and should send the complete relevant set promptly, including results in subjects where no credit exists anywhere in the catalogue.

## Reading a college's instructions, and asking when they hide

Every destination publishes a sending process somewhere; the skill is finding it. The trail usually begins on the admissions pages, where an application checklist states whether official scores are required, welcomed, or unnecessary alongside the self-reported alternative. It continues on the registrar or academic advising pages, where the credit and placement policies specify what enrolled students must transmit and by when. The two sources answer different seasonal questions, and a family that reads only the admissions checklist may miss an enrollment deadline sitting in a registrar's document, while a family that reads only the credit policy may pay for application-season reports nobody required.

When the published process is ambiguous, and ambiguity is common, the remedy is a direct question to the office that owns the decision. Admissions operations answers for application-season requirements; the registrar answers for credit transmission. A short inquiry naming the student's situation, an applicant or an admitted student, the scores in hand, and the specific question, whether official reports are wanted and by what date, produces an answer that can be filed with the family record and relied on. Colleges answer such questions constantly. The students who suffer from ambiguity are the ones who guessed instead of asking, and guessed in the expensive direction.

One reading skill deserves emphasis: distinguishing required, recommended, and accepted. A required official report is a condition of a complete file, and missing it has consequences. A recommended report is an invitation with judgment attached; a strong record should accept the invitation. An accepted report is merely permitted, and sending becomes a pure strategy question governed by the decision flow. Collapsing those three categories into one vague sense that colleges like scores leads to over-sending at the permitted end and under-sending at the required end, the worst combination available.

## Scholarships and special files

A final set of recipients sits outside the admissions and registrar channels. Scholarship programs, honors colleges, and special opportunity programs sometimes maintain their own files and their own documentation requirements, and a score record may be requested by a committee that never sees the registrar's copy. Merit programs pegged to academic distinction occasionally ask for certified evidence of the results they are rewarding. Departmental scholarships may ask for the specific subject record relevant to their field. Each such request should be answered on its own terms, through the channel the program names, and recorded in the family log like any other transmission.

The discipline matters because program files run on their own deadlines, often earlier than the enrollment calendar and sometimes disconnected from application dates entirely. A student who learns of a documentation requirement from a program's fine print in the same week it falls due will be buying rush processing or missing the file. Reading program conditions with the same care as college instructions, at the start of the season rather than the end, is the same checklist habit wearing different clothes. Where a program accepts the student's own documentation or a school-verified copy, the family record should note that too, so the cheaper evidence is used with confidence when the file is assembled.

## Three seasons, three students: the flow at work

Abstract rules become usable when they survive contact with actual records, so consider three representative students and watch the decision flow handle each. The first holds a settled destination. Admitted in the winter and enrolled by the spring, this senior names the college in the free designation before the per-cycle deadline, and the record travels at release without further effort or expense. Because enrollment is certain, the blindness of the designation carries no risk: the registrar needs the record regardless of its contents, the credit-bearing scores will be applied, and the weaker ones will be read by an office executing a policy rather than an audience weighing a candidacy. One decision, made once, completes the student's entire sending obligation, and the summer can be spent verifying the award rather than initiating it.

The second student is a junior with strong scores and an unstable list. Six colleges are under consideration, none committed, and the favorite changes with the weather. The free designation tempts, because a benefit unused feels like money discarded. The flow rules otherwise. No destination passes the settled test, so the designation either lapses or goes to the safest name on the list, a college where the strong record's arrival is harmless under any outcome. No paid orders follow, because no audience exists yet. A year later, with applications filed, the strategy activates: self-reporting where colleges allow it, official reports where they require them, and the borderline 3 in one subject decided separately for each destination against policy and selectivity. After enrollment, one complete order carries the credit set to the college that matters. Total spending across two years is a fraction of what an anxious family might have laid out in the junior spring alone, and every transmission arrived in front of an audience prepared to use it.

The third student carries a mixed record into a transfer. Two strong scores, one middling, one weak, earned across three years and sent once, partially, to a first college whose policy rewarded little. The new institution's table is more generous, and its placement system consumes scores the first college ignored. The correct move is a fresh, complete analysis: the strong scores and the middling one all carry credit or placement value at the new destination and travel together in one order at the moment of transfer admission; the weak score, valueless under the new policy as under the old, stays home. Notice what the example teaches. Prior sending history at another institution neither helps nor hinders, no permission is needed to transmit a record again, and the receiving college's policy, consulted fresh, writes the entire plan.

## Coordinating sending with a retake

Some students sit an exam more than once, and the existence of two results in one subject complicates presentation in a specific way. Both results live in the record; a report's selection determines which of them a given college sees. The natural strategy sends the stronger outcome and leaves the weaker in the account, and under the selective architecture that is a legitimate use of the tool the system provides. The exception, as always, is the college that requires the complete record, whose instruction converts selection from a strategy into a compliance question. The mechanics and ethics of repeating an exam belong to the analysis of [whether AP retakes are worth it](/2008/08/14/ap-exam-retakes-worth-it/); the sending consequence is simply that a retake upgrades what a later report can carry, and a student planning one should time paid transmissions with the new result in mind.

Timing interacts with retakes in one more place. A senior awaiting a spring retake result while an enrolled college waits for the credit record should usually send the established record on schedule and supplement it after release if the new result earns a place, rather than holding the entire transmission hostage to one pending number. Credit evaluation proceeds in stages at most institutions, and a late supplement joins a file more easily than a late start. The fragmented-order warning from earlier sections applies to careless repetition, not to a planned supplement with a genuine new result behind it.

## Gap years and deferred enrollment

A student who defers enrollment inserts a year into every timeline, and sending adapts in two ways. Where the college holds the student's admission and expects the credit record by a stated point, the transmission should follow the college's instruction for deferred students rather than the family's assumption; some institutions evaluate credit at the original enrollment point and carry the award forward, while others process the record when the student actually arrives. The difference decides whether the report should travel in the first summer or the second, and only the college's own answer settles it. A short inquiry at the moment deferral is granted prevents a record from sitting in a file an extra year or arriving after a deadline nobody mentioned.

Where no enrollment is settled and the gap year precedes applications, the student's position resembles the junior's: no audience, no orders, and a record that waits patiently. Gap-year applicants sometimes feel pressure to make the record do something during the interval, as if activity were evidence of seriousness. Transmission is not activity a college can see or reward outside a process, and the disciplined answer remains to hold everything until applications create files for reports to join. The archive horizon is the only clock worth watching in a long deferral, and even that operates in years rather than months.

## Records built across schools and systems

Not every record is built in one building. Students move between schools, test through host institutions, spend a year in another system, or assemble exams across a school career that spans districts and occasionally countries. The score record consolidates those results under the student's identity, indifferent to where each exam was administered, which is one of the system's quiet strengths. Before relying on that consolidation, though, the student should open the account and confirm that every expected result is present under the single identity. A missing result usually traces to an identity variation at one administration, a different name form or a separate account created by a host school process, and the repair belongs to the support channel, completed in a quiet month.

Host-school testing deserves a particular note. A student who sat exams at a school other than the one attended may find the administrative trail, who ordered what and which code was used, thinner than a home-school student would. None of that affects the record itself, which belongs to the student, but it raises the premium on direct verification: the student, not any institution, confirms the record's completeness and executes every transmission. Independent candidates and frequently moved students should treat the account as their registrar of record and audit it the way a registrar would, result by result, year by year, before the first order is placed.

## An end-of-season audit

When the applications are filed, the enrollment is chosen, and the essential transmissions are complete, one closing ritual repays its small effort. The family opens its record of sends and walks it once: every designation and order listed, every confirmation checked, every recipient's receipt verified where a portal or an office allows, and every expected credit award compared against the enrolled college's published table. Discrepancies found in that audit are repairable. A missing report can still be ordered; an under-applied credit can still be raised with the registrar while the term is young; a stray transmission, discovered in the log, can at least be understood rather than wondered about.

The audit also closes the learning loop. Most students send seriously only once, but younger siblings, future transfers, and the student's own later audiences all inherit whatever the family figured out. A one-page record that survives the season, destinations, dates, selections, confirmations, and outcomes, turns a completed process into institutional memory. It is the same instinct that makes strong students keep their study systems after an exam: the method outlasts the event, and the next event is always closer than it feels.

## How readers actually weigh a sent score

Inside an admissions reading, an AP score performs a corroborating role more often than a decisive one. The transcript carries the narrative of four years; the scores act as external verification that the grades mean what they claim. A strong result beside a strong grade confirms the story. A strong result beside a modest grade complicates it, sometimes productively, since it suggests the course was demanding or the teaching uneven rather than the student weak. A weak result beside a strong grade raises the opposite question, which is precisely why weak results are the ones students hold back during application season wherever the college's rules permit a choice.

Context governs magnitude. Readers weigh scores against the opportunities a school offered: a student presenting three strong results from a school offering four subjects reads differently from a student presenting the same three from a catalogue of twenty. Sending, accordingly, is not only about which numbers travel but about the company they keep. A compact set of strong results sent deliberately can read as focus; the identical results buried inside a long undifferentiated dump read as volume. The student cannot control the reader's context-setting, but selection shapes the raw material the reader contextualizes, and thoughtful selection is a form of respect for how files are actually read.

Standardized-test-optional policies have quietly raised the value of deliberate sending. Where an applicant submits no admission-test score, the AP record may be the only externally validated academic evidence in the file, and strong results submitted officially carry unusual weight as proof that classroom performance reflects genuine mastery. The calculus for borderline results sharpens in both directions under such policies: a solid score can anchor an otherwise unverified record, while a weak one can become the file's only hard data point. Applicants relying on AP evidence in place of admission tests should select with more care, not less, and should send officially where a college's process rewards certified documentation.

## Recruited athletes and other early timelines

Most sending advice assumes the standard calendar, but several populations live on earlier clocks, and their transmissions move accordingly. Recruited athletes assemble files for coaches and compliance processes well ahead of ordinary application deadlines, and a program evaluating whether a prospect can handle its academic environment may ask for evidence of college-level work, AP results among it, during recruitment rather than after admission. Where a coach or a compliance office requests official documentation, the request defines the send: destination, timing, and completeness follow the program's instruction, and the family's normal instinct to wait for application season gives way to the earlier audience that actually exists.

Early-commitment pathways of other kinds behave similarly. A student admitted through a binding early route in the autumn holds a settled destination months before the free designation deadline arrives, and for that student the spring designation becomes a formality with a guaranteed correct answer. Dual-admission and guaranteed-transfer arrangements, where a student begins at one institution under a structured path to another, create sending obligations at both ends of the path, each governed by the receiving institution's policy at the time of arrival. The general principle absorbs all of these cases without new machinery: wherever an institution has created a real file and stated a real requirement, sending follows that requirement's calendar rather than the generic one.

## When a score is disputed, sending proceeds on the record as it stands

A student who believes a result misrepresents the performance sometimes asks whether sending should wait for a review. The program offers limited review mechanisms for aspects of scoring, and those processes run on their own timetable through their own channels; they are not part of the sending system, and a report always transmits the record as it stands at the moment of the order. A pending question about a result is therefore a reason to sequence transmissions sensibly, holding the disputed score out of early reports where selection permits, rather than a reason to freeze the whole sending plan. If a review changes the record, a later report carries the corrected result, and a college that received the earlier version can be sent the updated one with a brief explanation to the receiving office.

The broader principle is that the record is a living document with a history of transmissions, and each transmission is a snapshot. Colleges understand snapshots; registrars reconcile updated reports routinely, and admissions offices replace superseded information as a matter of ordinary file hygiene. Fear of sending an imperfect snapshot should not delay a transmission whose deadline matters, because the system anticipates revision. What the system does not forgive is absence, the report that never went out because its sender was waiting for a perfection the calendar did not permit.

## Release week: a protocol for the busiest days

The days when scores become visible concentrate more bad sending decisions than the rest of the year combined, because information, emotion, and marketing all arrive simultaneously. A protocol imposes order. First, read the record completely and privately, with no orders placed on the day of first viewing. Second, compare each result against the policies gathered during the research season, college by college, marking each score as a send, a hold, or a per-destination judgment. Third, execute only the transmissions whose audiences exist and whose deadlines are real: the enrolled college's credit report above all, and any program file with a documented date. Fourth, record every transmission in the family log the day it happens. Fifth, schedule the confirmations for the interval the processing window requires, and actually perform them.

Resisting the first-day order deserves a word of its own. Nothing in the system rewards speed unconnected to a deadline, and results do not change while a family thinks. The student who waits a week loses nothing except anxiety, and gains the policy comparison that separates a plan from a reaction. Colleges built the release calendar expecting reports to arrive across the following weeks and months; the only transmissions genuinely harmed by a week's reflection are the ones already late for reasons that predated the release. Calm is a strategy in this system, and release week is where it pays.

## Why this article names no fees and no fixed deadline

A reader may wonder why a guide this detailed describes the free-send deadline and the report fees without printing them. The omission is deliberate and protective. Both figures are set cycle by cycle, and a printed number in a durable article would quietly become wrong while continuing to look authoritative, the exact failure mode that the research habit exists to prevent. The authoritative values live in two places that are always current by construction: the student's account, where the cycle's designation deadline and order charges appear in the act of using them, and the program's published fee and calendar information for that cycle. Every figure in this article that can age has been described structurally, per report, per cycle, with speed and archive handling as premiums, because the structure is permanent even when the numbers are not.

The verification habit generalizes. Before any season's first order, a family should re-read the account's sending screens as if for the first time, checking the designation deadline, the standard and rush charges, and any provision that differs from the family's memory of a prior cycle. Older siblings' experience is a particularly seductive source of stale figures, since the structure they describe will sound identical while the numbers underneath have moved. Treat every cycle as a new contract with familiar architecture, and the sending plan survives fee changes, deadline changes, and interface changes without absorbing any of them as errors.

## The counselor's office: sending at the scale of a whole school

Individual strategy plays out inside institutions that process hundreds of students at once, and the school's vantage explains several features of the season that puzzle families. Counselors push the free designation hard in senior spring not because every designation is wise, but because at scale the dominant error is omission: students who never learn the mechanism exists. A school that runs a designation drive accepts a few ill-aimed sends as the cost of preventing dozens of missed ones. A family with a genuinely unsettled destination should understand that tension rather than mistake the school's blanket encouragement for personal advice, and should make the per-student judgment this article describes while appreciating why the loudspeaker version sounds simpler.

Schools also maintain their own informal knowledge that no website publishes: which colleges on the local list actually file official reports during application season, which registrars are slow to post credit, which portals show receipts clearly and which require a phone call. That knowledge accumulates from cohorts, and a short conversation in the counseling office can replace hours of inference from ambiguous web pages. Students should bring specific questions rather than general anxiety: the list of destinations, the scores in hand, the deadlines in play. Specific questions harvest specific knowledge, and the office's memory of last year's identical case is often the most accurate document available anywhere.

Where a school is unhelpfully passive, the family record and the decision flow carry the full weight, and the sections of this article on reading college instructions and confirming arrival become the operating system. The variation among schools is itself a reason the sending skill belongs to the student: schools change staff, calendars slip, reminders fail, and the account remains under one person's control from the first exam to the last transmission.

## Summer programs, internships, and other off-calendar audiences

Between application seasons, scores acquire minor audiences that families rarely anticipate. Competitive summer programs held on college campuses sometimes request documentation of preparation, and a certified result can serve as evidence that a student is ready for the program's level. Research placements and selective institutes occasionally ask for the same. Dual-enrollment partners, the colleges through which a high school student takes courses for simultaneous credit, may read an AP record as placement evidence for their own course sequencing, a use entirely separate from credit at a future destination.

Each off-calendar request should be handled by the standing rule: read the actual requirement, send officially where an official report is demanded, share the lighter documentation where it is accepted, and log the transmission. The fees at stake are small and the audiences are genuine, so these sends are usually worth making promptly. Their strategic significance is larger than their size, though. They teach the student the account mechanics, the verification habit, and the logging habit in a low-stakes season, long before the transmissions that carry tuition-sized consequences. A student who has sent twice for summer purposes will not fumble the enrolled college's credit report under summer pressure a year or two later.

One caution travels with these minor sends. A program that asks a student to self-report scores or upload a score view is asking for exactly that, and upgrading the evidence voluntarily to a paid official report buys the program nothing it requested. Generosity with documentation is not a virtue in a system built on specified requirements. Match the evidence to the request, every time, in both directions.

## Self-reporting and the audit that follows it

Because many colleges read self-reported scores during application season, the relationship between the application and the eventual official report deserves exact treatment. The official report sent after enrollment functions as an audit of everything the application claimed. Registrars and admissions offices compare the certified record against the self-reported one, and material discrepancies, a score claimed higher than the record shows, a score claimed that does not exist, are treated as integrity matters rather than clerical ones. The defense is boring and total: report on applications exactly what the record shows, no rounding of memory, no optimistic reconstruction of a result half-remembered from release day.

The audit relationship also settles a recurring family debate about whether to self-report a borderline score that will later be sent for credit anyway. Since the enrolled college will eventually hold the certified record, concealment during application season has a defined and limited lifespan, which argues for deciding the question on admissions merits alone rather than on any fantasy of permanent invisibility. Where a college does not require the full record and the borderline result adds nothing to the application, holding it back remains legitimate and often wise; the point is only that the later credit transmission is a planned second act, not an exposure to be feared. Students who understand the two-act structure stop experiencing the post-enrollment send as a risk and start treating it as the routine accounting it is.

Colleges differ in how formally they reconcile the two versions, and most mismatches born of honest error, a transposed pair of subject results, a score attributed to the wrong year, are repaired by explanation and the certified report itself. The system punishes invention, not imperfection. Accuracy at the self-reporting stage, checked against the account rather than against memory, keeps the audit a formality from start to finish.

## The one-page sending brief

Everything this article recommends about administration condenses into a single document a family can maintain on one page. Its fields are few and each earns its place. For every transmission: the destination institution, the date of the order or designation, the exact set of scores sent, the order confirmation reference, the date receipt was verified and the channel that verified it, and the outcome, credit or placement awarded, where the destination is the enrolled college. A line per transmission, kept in the order the transmissions happened, produces a complete history that can answer any registrar, counselor, or program officer in one reading.

The brief's value compounds across the season. Before a new order, the page shows what has already gone to that destination, preventing duplicates. During a dispute about receipt, the page supplies the dates and references that turn a vague complaint into a solvable case. At the end of the process, the page is the audit instrument described earlier, walked line by line. Families who keep it describe sending as a solved problem; families who rely on memory describe it as a fog. The difference is one page, maintained in minutes, across a process worth thousands in credit when it runs correctly.

The page also outlives the student who inspired it. Younger siblings inherit not a set of stale figures but a working method and a realistic sense of the calendar, and the student keeps a template for the transfer, graduate, and professional transmissions that adult life may still require. Few documents in the college process repay their upkeep as reliably.

## The complete case for not sending

The negative decisions deserve a consolidated statement, because holding is the active skill this article most wants to teach. Do not send where no audience exists: a college with no application, no enrollment, and no stated requirement has no use for a report, and the report has no use for the college. Do not send where no policy value exists: a destination that awards neither credit nor placement for a result, and whose application process does not invite it, gains nothing from the transmission and neither does the sender. Do not send a duplicate of information a destination already holds. Do not send a weak score into an application file where selection is permitted and the score cannot help. Do not send under a disputed identity, a mismatched name, or a fractured duplicate account, because repairing the record first converts a doomed transmission into a routine one.

Each prohibition protects something concrete: money, privacy of the record, the coherence of a file, the credibility of a presentation. Together they describe the posture the whole system rewards. Transmission is a tool with a cost attached to every use, and mastery looks less like sending everything everywhere than like a short list of transmissions, each with a named audience, a known policy behind it, and a verified arrival at the end. A record can sit quietly in an account for years without losing value. A report, once sent, belongs to the file it joined. The asymmetry should make every sender a little reluctant, and a little deliberate, in exactly the way the decision flow formalizes.

## Paper files, electronic feeds, and why arrival times vary

Students experience sending as a single act, while colleges experience receiving as several different systems wearing one name. Large institutions ingest reports through electronic feeds that post to student records in batches, often overnight or on fixed processing days, so a report transmitted on one date may become visible in a portal several days later without anything having gone wrong. Smaller offices may receive material through a document channel that staff open, scan, and file by hand, a process whose speed depends on workload and season. A handful of recipients handle score material through services that aggregate deliveries from many sources. The sender cannot choose among these channels and cannot accelerate them; the variation explains why two reports ordered on the same morning surface in two portals a week apart.

Season multiplies the variation. The weeks after release are the receiving system's busiest period of the year, when every enrolled student's credit record is in motion at once, and ordinary processing stretches accordingly. A report ordered into that surge should be judged against the surge calendar, not against a quiet-season expectation. This is one more reason the post-enrollment transmission belongs early: an order placed at the front of the busy season rides the first processing batches, while an order placed at its peak joins the longest queue the year produces. None of this involves failure. It involves volume, and volume is predictable enough to plan around, which is all the decision flow ever asks.

Paper survives at the edges of the system, mainly where archive material is concerned and where an institution's intake remains document-based. An archive-route request may produce a report that travels more slowly and posts more slowly still, and families using that route for a deadline should build the generous lead time the route demands. For ordinary active-record orders, though, the governing expectation is electronic speed tempered by batching, measured in days for transmission and up to a couple of weeks for full visibility at the receiving end during peak volume.

## Communicating with the receiving office

When a transmission needs human help, the quality of the student's message decides how fast the help arrives. An effective inquiry to a registrar or admissions operations office contains the student's full legal name as it appears on the record, the birth date used for matching, the destination institution and campus, the date the order was placed, the set of scores the report carried, and one precise question. Messages written that way can be acted on by the first person who opens them. Messages that narrate anxiety and omit identifiers begin a correspondence instead of resolving a case, and every round trip costs days the deadline may not contain.

Channel choice matters as much as content. Portals with case systems keep the inquiry attached to the student's file and produce a reference the family log can hold; email to a general office address works nearly as well when the subject line carries the student's name and the topic. Telephone calls suit genuinely urgent deadline cases and leave no record, so a call should be followed by a written summary sent the same day, converting the conversation into documentation. Whichever channel carries the first contact, courtesy and brevity accelerate everything. Receiving offices process thousands of these cases in a season; the family that makes its case easy to solve gets it solved in the first pass.

The same communication discipline applies at the sending end when an account problem blocks an order. Duplicate accounts, identity corrections, and archive requests move through support channels that respond to complete information and stall on fragments. A student who assembles the identifiers, the exam years involved, and a plain statement of the problem before making contact shortens a process that otherwise sprawls across weeks of incremental discovery. Sending, it turns out, is partly a writing skill, and the students who write clearly to the two bureaucracies involved collect their credit with the least friction.

## Triage for the long list: ten colleges, finite fees

A long application list forces prioritization, and the sending plan for ten colleges should be built as tiers rather than as a single habit repeated ten times. The first tier is mandatory: colleges whose processes require official reports with the application. Those transmissions are conditions of complete files and are ordered on the application calendar without debate. The second tier is invitational: colleges that recommend or welcome official scores, where a strong record should accept the invitation and a mixed record should be weighed destination by destination. The third tier is silent: colleges content with self-reporting until enrollment. Nothing official travels to the third tier during application season at all, however anxious the season feels, because the audit after enrollment will ask exactly one member of the entire list for the certified record.

Triage also runs within the calendar. A student facing several first-tier deadlines in the same fortnight should order earliest for the college with the firmest date, verify receipts tier by tier rather than all at once in a panic at the end, and keep the family page updated after each order so the state of the whole campaign is visible in one glance. The failure mode triage prevents is the diffuse one: a family that half-sends everywhere, rushes twice, verifies nothing, and cannot afterward reconstruct what any college actually holds.

When money is tight, tiering becomes the budget. Mandatory sends are protected, invitational sends are chosen by the strength of the record at each destination, and silent-tier spending is eliminated rather than trimmed. The savings are real, and they arrive without any college receiving less than its process asks. A long list is expensive in application fees already; the sending layer should be the one part of the campaign whose cost a family controls almost completely, because so much of it is optional and so much of the value concentrates in a single final report.

## After the award: when the credit granted disappoints

Sending ends at receipt only in the simplest cases. A student who verifies the award and finds it smaller than the published policy suggested has a further move available, and it begins with information rather than complaint. Departments decide many credit questions, and a department asked to reconsider an award will want evidence about the course behind the score: the syllabus the student studied, the topics covered, sometimes the textbook used. A graduate of the course who kept the syllabus, or whose school can supply it, can support a review that a bare score cannot. The report opened the conversation; documentation continues it.

Expectations should stay calibrated while the conversation proceeds. A policy table is a promise of the standard award, and departures usually have discoverable reasons: the score fell below a departmental threshold that differs from the general one, the award was posted to a different requirement than the student assumed, or a cap on total credit trimmed the last subject in line. Each explanation points to a different remedy, which is why the first step is always to ask which mechanism produced the result. Registrars answer that question routinely, and students who ask it precisely often find the award was correct under a rule they had not seen, a discovery that still counts as value because it ends the uncertainty.

Where an award genuinely contradicts a published promise, a polite petition with the policy language quoted and the syllabus attached succeeds more often than families expect. Institutions prefer their own tables to be honored, and errors in applying them are corrected without drama when the evidence is assembled. None of this advocacy is possible, notice, on a record that was never sent, or on an award nobody verified. The post-send phase is the final argument for the habits this article has repeated from its first sections: transmit deliberately, verify from the recipient's side, and keep the page that proves both.

## Sending when the major is undecided

An unsettled field of study changes the value map of a record, because credit applies differently depending on whether a score lands inside a major's requirements or in the general education structure around it. A student certain of a direction can judge each result against that direction's needs. An undecided student faces a wider possibility space: a score in a subject the student may never pursue formally can still clear a distribution requirement, shorten a foreign language sequence, or satisfy a quantitative expectation, while a score in the eventual major may matter most as placement into the right starting course. Breadth, for the undecided, is not indecision; it is the accurate reflection of how many doors the record may be asked to open.

The sending consequence is simple and cheap. The undecided student's post-enrollment report should be complete across every subject with any plausible credit or placement function, rather than edited down to the subjects connected to a tentative interest. Registrars apply awards against the whole degree structure, and a result the student privately considers irrelevant may be the one that frees a term slot two years later when the major has declared itself and the schedule has tightened. Editing the enrolled college's report for taste is a false economy that costs nothing to avoid, since the complete set travels in the same single order for the same single charge.

Application season follows the opposite logic even for the undecided. Selectivity, not breadth, governs what readers should see, and an undeclared direction is no argument for flooding a file. The two audiences want opposite things from the same record: readers want the curated proof of strength, registrars want the full inventory of usable results. Holding both ideas at once, curate for judgment and complete for accounting, is the mature form of the sending skill, and the undecided student who masters it loses nothing to a premature choice of field in either direction.

## Privacy, ownership, and the filed report

A closing word belongs to ownership. Before transmission, a score record is the student's private information, held in an account the student controls and disclosed to exactly the destinations the student selects. Sending changes the record's custody without changing its sensitivity: once filed, a report becomes part of the student's education record at the receiving institution, handled under that institution's privacy practices and shared within it on a need-to-know basis among the offices that use it. The student cannot dictate the internal routing, which is one more reason transmissions should be deliberate, addressed only where a process justifies them.

Parents of older students meet the practical edge of this ownership at the end of high school. The account credentials, the order confirmations, and the verification conversations all run through the student, and colleges direct their answers about a filed record to the student whose record it is. Families function best when that reality is planned for rather than discovered mid-dispute: the student executes and communicates, the family supports with research, records, and reminders. The arrangement is also a fair rehearsal for the next four years, in which every registrar interaction will follow the same rule, and the student who has run a sending season has already learned how to deal with the office that will hold the degree record.

## The strategic verdict on score sending

Sending is the smallest task in the AP program and the one on which the program's payoff most directly depends. The exams take years. The reports take minutes. Yet an unread record earns nothing, a misdirected record earns nothing, and a record sent without selection earns less than it should. The students who collect the full value of their work treat transmission as the final strategic act of the enterprise: one free designation spent on a destination that deserves it, selections made college by college against published policy, official reports ordered where a process requires them and withheld where self-reporting serves, and the complete credit record delivered to the enrolled college early enough to shape a first schedule.

The habits generalize beyond any single cycle's fees or deadlines, which will shift as the program's per-cycle figures always shift. Destination before transmission. Selection before submission. Verification before and after the order. Confirmation from the recipient rather than comfort from the receipt. A family that internalizes those four habits will handle any version of the sending system correctly, and will never pay to publicize a weakness, never miss a free benefit through inattention, and never let a strong record sit unread in an account while a college waits for information it was owed. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you send AP scores to colleges through your account?

Sign in to the account where your scores live and open the score-sending function. Search the institution directory for the college, using its code where you know it, and read the confirmation line carefully, because similar college names in different states are the most common source of misdirected reports. Select the scores the report should carry, since selection is made fresh for every order and nothing defaults to the full record. Review the name and birth date the report will bear against your application details, then submit. Keep the confirmation and note the destination, date, and score set in your records, because the later verification step, checking that the college filed the report, depends on having those details at hand.

### Q: At what point in the application process should you send AP scores?

The governing rule is to follow each college's stated process rather than a universal moment. Where a college requires official scores with the application, send during the application window with enough lead time for processing and matching. Where a college reads self-reported scores during admission and asks for the official report only after enrollment, the official transmission belongs after admission, aimed at the one college you will attend. A smaller middle group, colleges with scholarship files or special programs carrying their own documentation dates, should be served on their own calendars. Sending everywhere at the first opportunity mostly buys duplicate fees, because reports sent to colleges that never enroll you do work for no one.

### Q: Is the first AP score report free?

One score send each cycle carries no charge, and it works by designation rather than by order. You name a single college inside your account before a deadline that falls after the May exams and before scores are released, and the report travels to that college when the results post. The defining condition is blindness: you commit the destination before you can see the scores the report will contain. That makes the free send ideal for a senior whose enrollment is settled, since the college named is the one that needs the record for credit evaluation, and risky for a student guessing at a destination, because a transmitted report cannot be recalled once the receiving college has filed it.

### Q: How much extra does an additional score report cost?

Each additional report is charged at a fee the program sets for that cycle, and durable guidance should describe the structure rather than a figure that will age. The charge applies per report, not per score, so one order carrying your whole selected set to one college costs the same as an order carrying a single result. That structure rewards batching: assemble every score a destination should receive and send them together, instead of paying repeatedly for fragments. The exact charge for a given season appears inside the ordering screens when you place the order, and checking it there, rather than relying on an older sibling's memory of a prior cycle, keeps the budget honest.

### Q: Can you choose which AP scores to send to a college?

Yes, and the choice is made separately for every college. A report carries the scores you select for that destination, so one institution can receive your complete record while another receives a single subject, and neither college is told what the other was sent. Selection is the central strategic tool in the whole sending system: strong and credit-bearing results travel, weak results stay in the account, and borderline results are decided destination by destination against each college's policy. One boundary matters. If a college explicitly requires the full record, that requirement governs, and selection stops being a strategy and becomes a compliance question. Read each destination's stated requirement before exercising choice.

### Q: Do colleges require you to send all of your AP scores?

Most do not. The default architecture of the system is selective sending, and the majority of colleges accept the set a student chooses to transmit, particularly where official reports are used mainly for credit evaluation after enrollment. A minority of institutions state a requirement for the complete record, usually as part of an application instruction about full disclosure of testing history, and those instructions bind the students who apply there. The practical habit is to check the requirement college by college rather than assuming either answer everywhere. Where no full-record rule is stated, withholding a score from a report breaches nothing; where one is stated, honoring it exactly is part of the application's integrity.

### Q: How do you rush an AP score report to a college?

Place the order through the same account process as a standard report and select the rush handling option, which adds a per-cycle premium charge to the order. Rush buys priority in preparation on the sending side. It does not change the receiving college's matching and filing timetable, and it cannot influence how a policy is applied once the report arrives. Reserve it for a documented deadline that the standard window would genuinely miss, such as a credit evaluation date or a program file closing within days. Before paying the premium, confirm that the college actually needs the official report by that date, because a surprising share of rush orders race to satisfy deadlines that turn out to be self-reporting deadlines instead.

### Q: Will a college see AP scores you chose not to send?

No. A college's view of your record is limited to the reports addressed to that college, containing the scores you selected for those reports. Scores withheld from a transmission remain in your account, visible to you and to no recipient. The college cannot browse the rest of the record, request the unselected portion through the sending system, or learn from another institution what that institution received. The single exception is a college that requires the full record as a condition of its process, where honesty about the requirement, not concealment of the gap, is the correct response. Outside such a requirement, an unsent score is simply private, and it stays private for as long as you leave it unsent.

### Q: Does sending one score reveal your other AP results?

It does not. A report is a defined set, assembled by your selection at the moment of the order, and the receiving college sees the contents of that report and nothing beyond it. Sending one subject does not attach a summary of the rest of the record, does not disclose how many other exams you sat, and does not hint at results you withheld. This containment is what makes per-college strategy possible: each destination can be given the presentation appropriate to its policy and its selectivity. The containment runs in both directions, too. A college also cannot see which other institutions received reports, so a sending history leaves no trail across your application list.

### Q: How long does it take a sent score to reach a college?

Plan in days for transmission and up to a couple of weeks for full visibility at the receiving end. The report must be prepared, delivered through the channel the college uses, and then matched to a file by the college's own systems, which often post in batches rather than continuously. Peak season stretches every stage, because the weeks after scores are released put the entire national cohort's credit records in motion at once. A confirmation from the ordering side proves the request was accepted, not that the college has filed the report, so any send tied to a real deadline deserves a verification check through the college's portal or registrar after a reasonable interval, early enough that a matching problem can still be repaired inside the window.

### Q: Should you send AP scores you are not proud of?

Judge the score against the receiving college's behavior, not against your feelings about it. A modest result that earns credit or clears a placement threshold at the destination should be sent by the time enrollment lets the registrar act on it, because value at that college is real money and schedule freedom. The same result sent into a competitive application file, where it earns nothing and can only dilute a strong presentation, should usually be held wherever the college permits choice. A genuinely weak result earns nothing anywhere and should stay in the account by default. Pride and embarrassment are both poor advisors in this system; policy tables and audience are the reliable ones.

### Q: How do you send AP scores to several colleges in one order?

You cannot, and recognizing that shapes the whole budget. Each report is addressed to exactly one institution, so reaching several colleges means placing a separate order for each destination, selecting the score set that college should receive and paying the per-report charge each time. The system offers no bulk envelope. That architecture is why indiscriminate sending gets expensive and why the disciplined sequence matters: official reports during application season only where a college's process requires them, reliance on self-reporting where the process allows it, and one complete report to the enrolled college after admission, which is the transmission that carries nearly all of the credit value. Several colleges are reached by several deliberate orders, never by one generous one.

### Q: Do you send AP scores during senior year or after admission?

Both moments occur, but they serve different audiences and the heavier value sits after admission. During senior year, official sending is required only where a college's application process demands official scores rather than self-reported ones, or where the free designation can be aimed at an enrolled destination in the spring. For most students the essential transmission happens once the enrollment decision is final: the complete credit-relevant record goes to the college you will attend, early enough for credit evaluation and advising to work with it. Senior-year sending to a long list of colleges mostly duplicates that single later report at several times the cost. Let each college's instructions, not the calendar alone, decide which moment applies.

### Q: What becomes of your AP scores if you never send them anywhere?

They remain in your record, intact and available, owned by you and visible in your account. Unsent scores do not lapse at graduation, and they can be transmitted years later to a transfer institution, a second-degree program, or any other audience that asks for certified results. After the active window, older results move to an archive, and ordering then follows an archive request route rather than the ordinary online order, with more handling involved but the same record at the end of it. The practical consequence is comforting: a decision not to send is almost never permanent in its effects. The record waits, and a score held back in one season loses none of its value for a later one.

### Q: Do AP score reports expire before you send them?

Scores do not carry an expiration date that prevents sending. Results remain orderable throughout their active life in your account and, after results are eventually archived, retrievable through the archive route for official transmission. What can change with time is reception rather than availability: a college's policy governs which scores it will honor for credit, and an institution may treat very old results under the policy in force when you enroll, occasionally with conditions about how the credit applies. None of that blocks the send itself. If a destination matters, the safest course is to transmit while the record is active and the ordering is simple, rather than relying on the archive process under a future deadline.

### Q: Can you send AP scores to a college before you have applied there?

Yes. The ordering system addresses reports to institutions rather than to applications, so a college can receive your scores with no file yet in existence, and it will hold the material pending a match. No obligation to apply is created, no signal is sent to any reader, and nothing about the early arrival harms a later application. The reasons to do it are narrow, though: a settled destination using the free designation, or a program that has explicitly requested official results ahead of an application. In most situations patience is better, because a report sent into a vacuum cannot be checked against a file, and the same transmission placed later delivers identical information with the matching risk removed.

### Q: How do you confirm that a college received your AP score report?

Confirmation comes from the receiving side, not from your order receipt. Check the college's applicant or student portal for a checklist item or a posted credit entry, since many institutions surface a filed report visibly there. After enrollment, a degree audit showing the awarded credit is the strongest confirmation of all. If the portal shows nothing after the ordinary processing window, contact the registrar or admissions operations office with your full name, birth date, the order date, and the set of scores sent, and ask whether the report has been matched to your file. Most silent cases turn out to be matching delays caused by small identity differences between the record and the application, which the office can reconcile once the case is in front of a person.

### Q: What should you do if you sent your scores to the wrong college?

Act on the parts you control. Place the correct order to the intended college immediately, especially if any deadline is involved, and tell the intended college what happened if its date is at risk. Then contact the institution that received the stray report and ask how it handles misdirected score material; in practice, an unrequested report for someone with no file there sits inert and creates neither an application nor a review. Understand the limit clearly: a transmitted report cannot be recalled from a system that has already filed it, so the remedy is correction rather than retrieval. The durable lesson is preventive. Reading the institution name and location on the confirmation screen before submitting would have caught the error at the only moment it was free to fix.

### Q: Do scores from all of your exam years arrive together in one cumulative report?

They can, because your record is a single cumulative file rather than a stack of separate year accounts. When you order a report, you select from the whole history visible in your account, so results from sophomore, junior, and senior administrations can travel together in one transmission to one college, which is exactly what a registrar evaluating credit wants to see. Cumulative does not mean automatic or compulsory: the report carries the years and subjects you select, and results you leave unselected stay behind. One caution applies to older histories. Results from the least recent years may sit in the archive rather than the active account, in which case those results are requested through the archive route and may arrive as a separate handling rather than inside the ordinary order.

### Q: Can you send AP scores to colleges outside the United States?

Yes. Universities in many countries recognize AP results for admission consideration, placement, or credit under their own published frameworks, and reports can be addressed to institutions abroad through the same ordering process used for domestic colleges. The transmission is the routine part; the interpretation is not. A foreign university may map your scores to subject-specific entry expectations, treat them as supporting evidence beside a national qualification, or apply a credit framework of its own, so research what the destination does with the record before ordering. Build in extra lead time as well, since matching and filing practices vary more across systems, and confirm receipt through the institution's own admissions channel rather than assuming the domestic timetable applies.
