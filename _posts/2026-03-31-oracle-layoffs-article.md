This page tracks every layoff round at Oracle Corporation from 2022 onward in one place, and it is updated each time the company runs another one. It records what each round cut, where, and under what stated reasoning, alongside the standing machinery that governs how Oracle separations work: how notice is given, how severance is structured in the United States and India, how the WARN Act applies, and what happens to stock, benefits, and visa status when a role is eliminated. Event coverage ages quickly; the record below is built to stay accurate as the history grows.

![Oracle Layoffs: Every Round, Severance and What Changed - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-28.webp)

The cumulative picture is five tracked rounds in four years. The largest, in March 2026, eliminated an estimated 20,000 to 30,000 roles worldwide — an analyst estimate, because the company has never issued an official total — against a global workforce of approximately 162,000. Before it came a WARN-documented round of about 3,000 in August and September 2025, the closure of the Oracle Advertising business in 2024, roughly 3,000 reductions at the Cerner health unit across 2023, and the advertising and customer experience cuts that opened the sequence in August 2022. Summed across the five rounds, the tracked total is a reported range of roughly 27,000 to 42,000 roles, and the width of that range is itself part of the record: Oracle almost never publishes its own headcounts, so this tracker labels every figure with the strength of its source, from SEC filings and WARN notices at the top to analyst estimates and aggregated reporting below them.

A note on how this page is kept. The ledger begins at 1 January 2022 by design: Oracle's earlier reductions, including the 2019 closure of its China research operation and the acquisition consolidations of the 2000s, are part of the company's history and are summarised in the snapshot, but they predate the documentary standard the ledger applies. A round enters the ledger only when a company announcement, an SEC disclosure, or a WARN filing establishes it, or when two independent outlets report the same reduction of at least 100 roles with a consistent scope. Rounds that are known but cannot be verified to that standard are named in the snapshot as reported-but-unverified rather than silently dropped, and they are promoted into the ledger if the evidence arrives. When a new round qualifies, it is added at the top of the ledger, the cumulative table is recomputed, and the date at the head of this page is updated; nothing already in the ledger is removed, and a corrected figure is amended in place with the correction visible in the round's own section.

## Company Snapshot

Oracle is one of the largest enterprise software companies in the world, founded in 1977 and headquartered in Austin, Texas. The company built its position on database software and enterprise applications, expanded by acquisition for two decades, and has spent the past several years repositioning itself as a cloud infrastructure provider competing with Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud. Two acquisitions frame the modern company's shape. NetSuite, bought in 2016 for $9.3 billion, anchors its cloud ERP business for smaller companies. Cerner, bought in 2022 for $28.3 billion and renamed Oracle Health, was the largest acquisition in the company's history and made Oracle a central supplier of electronic health record systems, including the United States Department of Veterans Affairs' troubled modernization programme. Both acquisitions also supplied two of the five layoff rounds tracked on this page: the Cerner integration produced the 2023 reductions, and NetSuite units appear in the 2025 and 2026 rounds.

The workforce trajectory tells the story in one line. Oracle reported approximately 143,000 full-time employees at the end of fiscal 2022, before the Cerner acquisition closed and added roughly 28,000 people. The combined company reported approximately 164,000 at the end of fiscal 2023, approximately 159,000 at the end of fiscal 2024, and approximately 162,000 at the end of fiscal 2025. After the March 2026 round, the fiscal 2026 annual filing recorded approximately 141,000 full-time employees — a net decline of 21,000 in a single year, slightly below where the company stood before Cerner. Net decline is not the same number as roles eliminated, because Oracle kept hiring throughout, particularly in data center construction and artificial intelligence roles; but the filings confirm the direction and the scale, and they give this tracker its anchor points for the share-of-workforce figures used below.

The structural pressure behind the recent rounds is the cost of the cloud repositioning. Oracle has committed itself to an artificial intelligence data center buildout of unusual scale, anchored by the Stargate project, a joint venture with OpenAI, SoftBank, and MGX announced in January 2025 with planned investment of up to $500 billion in United States AI infrastructure by 2029. Oracle's own agreement with OpenAI covers more than $300 billion of capacity. The company guided capital expenditures of approximately $50 billion for fiscal 2026, spent $55.7 billion in that year on the final tally, and guided toward a net $70 billion the year after. TD Cowen estimated the total capital requirement of the buildout at $156 billion. Free cash flow over the trailing twelve months reported before the March 2026 round was negative $24.7 billion, and total debt had risen above $100 billion, including $58 billion of new debt taken on in the two months before the round and an $18 billion bond issuance in September 2025 that later drew a bondholder class action alleging the coming borrowing had not been adequately disclosed. Moody's rates the company Baa2, two notches above junk status and below the ratings of its principal cloud competitors. The stock, which reached an all-time high of $345.72 in September 2025 — briefly making co-founder Larry Ellison the world's wealthiest person — had fallen to roughly $152 by March 2026, a decline of more than 55 percent.

The cuts therefore did not arrive from a position of revenue weakness. In the third quarter of fiscal 2026, reported weeks before the March 2026 round, Oracle's net income rose 95 percent to $6.13 billion, revenue grew 22 percent to $17.2 billion, cloud infrastructure revenue grew 84 percent to $4.9 billion, and remaining performance obligations — contracted revenue not yet recognized — reached $553 billion, up 325 percent year over year, with approximately 58 percent of that backlog tied to OpenAI alone. Management described the quarter as the company's best in 15 years. The workforce reductions are a capital allocation decision: salary cost converted into construction capacity. TD Cowen estimated the March 2026 round would free $8 billion to $10 billion in annual cash flow, a figure the company itself has not confirmed. The earlier rounds had different drivers — acquisition overlap in 2023, a collapsing advertising business in 2022 and 2024, cost discipline in 2025 — and the ledger below keeps those reasons separate rather than flattening them into one story. What unites the five rounds is method, not motive: in each case Oracle moved quickly, said little publicly, and let filings and statutory notices carry the documentary record.

Underneath the strategy sits a financial doctrine that long predates it, and the snapshot records it because it predicts the company's behaviour in every round. Oracle has for decades run one of the highest operating margin structures in enterprise software — margins in the region of forty percent in its mature years — defended by a cost discipline that treats headcount as the primary variable expense and acquired workforces as raw material to be repriced. The doctrine is why Oracle's acquisitions so reliably produce the pattern described above, why its rounds so rarely coincide with revenue declines, and why its executives describe cost programmes in the language of standards rather than emergencies: Catz's remark that Cerner was "not how we run a place" is the doctrine stated in one sentence. The AI buildout did not create the doctrine; it gave the doctrine its largest ever application, converting a margin-defence practice into a capital-formation strategy. Readers of this tracker should therefore expect the pattern to persist independently of any single round's stated reason: while the buildout's financing needs last, payroll is where Oracle has historically found money, and the restructuring plan mechanism described in the standing section is where the intention will be documented first.

Leadership forms part of the context. Safra Catz, chief executive for more than a decade, stepped down in September 2025, moving to Executive Vice Chair of the Board, and was succeeded by co-CEOs Clay Magouyrk, who built Oracle Cloud Infrastructure's second-generation architecture after joining from Amazon Web Services in 2014, and Mike Sicilia, who leads the industry applications business, including Oracle Health. Larry Ellison, co-founder, remains Chairman and Chief Technology Officer and owns approximately 42 percent of the company. Catz presided over the first four rounds tracked here and personally announced the advertising exit on an earnings call; the March 2026 round was the new leadership's first large workforce action, executed five months into their tenure and coinciding with the acceleration of the AI infrastructure strategy they were appointed to deliver. Magouyrk received stock options worth $250 million on appointment and Sicilia $100 million, figures that entered the public discussion of the 2026 round because they were set against the severance costs recorded in the same period's filings.

Oracle has reduced its workforce before 2022, and those earlier episodes belong to the snapshot rather than the ledger. In May 2019, the company closed its China research and development operation, eliminating a reported 900 or more engineering roles in a first wave, with the total reported higher as the closure completed — an early example of the whole-unit elimination the advertising closure would repeat in 2024, and of the company's preference for acting on a unit rather than trimming across all of them. In 1990, an internal audit that revealed overstated earnings led to layoffs of about 400 people, roughly 10 percent of the workforce at the time, in a crisis that nearly ended the company. The acquisitions of PeopleSoft in 2005 and Sun Microsystems in 2010 each produced consolidation rounds as overlapping roles were absorbed, in the pattern the Cerner acquisition would repeat a decade later. The company's institutional reputation — lean staffing, aggressive cost control after acquisitions, high margins defended as a point of doctrine — was formed in those episodes, and executives have occasionally said so in as many words: at an October 2022 investor event, Catz remarked of Cerner that "that's just not how we run a place" and that Oracle would "clean Cerner up," months before the health unit's reductions began.

The competitive frame matters because it explains why Oracle, alone among the large cloud providers, has funded its buildout so heavily from payroll. Oracle entered the cloud infrastructure contest late and from behind: Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud each hold larger shares of the market Oracle is spending to enter, and each funds its construction from a larger and more diversified revenue base. Oracle's wager is that the $553 billion contracted backlog — demand already signed, much of it from OpenAI — justifies borrowing and cutting ahead of the revenue, converting future contracted income into present capacity. The workforce consequence is structural rather than cyclical. A company building ahead of demand with borrowed money treats its salary base as its most adjustable liability, and every round in this ledger after 2023 can be read as an adjustment of that liability: the Cerner overlap first, then the advertising exit, then the cloud organisation itself, then a fifth of the company. The tracker takes no position on whether the wager succeeds. It records that the wager, not quarterly performance, is the stated and reported logic connecting the rounds.

The acquisition-integration pattern deserves separate statement, because it accounts for two of the five rounds and shadows a third. Oracle's historic method after a large acquisition is to hold the acquired workforce briefly, then remove the functions that duplicate the parent's — finance, legal, marketing, human resources, and eventually overlapping product and engineering roles — while repricing the remainder to Oracle's cost structure. PeopleSoft's absorption after 2005 and Sun Microsystems' after 2010 both followed that sequence, and Cerner's did too, on a compressed timetable: the acquisition closed in June 2022, campus closures were decided by October 2022, the chief executive had publicly promised to "clean Cerner up" the same month, and the headcount waves ran through the first half of 2023, removing roughly a tenth of the acquired workforce inside a year. NetSuite, acquired in 2016 and long since integrated, appears in the later rounds not as an acquisition story but as an ordinary division — its India Development Centre in 2026, its global business unit in 2025 — which is itself evidence of how completely the pattern finishes: a decade on, an acquired company's staff are simply Oracle staff, exposed to the same rounds as everyone else.

Geography is the last piece of standing context. At the end of fiscal 2026, Oracle's annual filing recorded roughly 49,000 employees in the United States and 92,000 internationally, with India the largest single offshore concentration at an estimated 30,000 before the March 2026 round. That distribution explains the shape of the documentary record throughout this tracker. United States rounds generate WARN filings, state-level counts, and statutory detail. India rounds — proportionally as large, and in 2026 the largest single-country component — generate no equivalent public filing, and reach the record through aggregated reporting of a lower evidentiary grade. A reader comparing the rounds should therefore expect the United States detail to be precise and the India detail to be approximate, not because one matters more, but because the two countries' disclosure regimes differ, and the tracker's sourcing labels are calibrated to that difference throughout.

Oracle's rounds also sit inside two industry waves, and the tracker records the context because the company's stated and reported reasons repeatedly invoke it. The first wave was the sector-wide correction of 2022 and 2023, when the large technology companies that had hired aggressively through the pandemic reversed course together: Meta eliminated 11,000 roles in November 2022 and more the following spring, Amazon ran reductions across 2022 and 2023 totalling well over 25,000, Google cut 12,000 in January 2023, Microsoft 10,000 in the same season, and Salesforce, Cisco, and others ran their own programmes in the same months as Oracle's August 2022 and Cerner rounds. Oracle's $1 billion savings programme was one instance of an industry pattern rather than an idiosyncratic event — and characteristically, it was among the least formally announced of them, since several of those peers published chief-executive letters explaining their rounds while Oracle said nothing on the record at all. The comparison is worth keeping in the snapshot because it isolates what is distinctive about Oracle's practice: not the fact of the cuts, which the whole sector shared, but the documentary minimalism with which they were carried out, which the peers' more communicative rounds throw into relief. The second wave, running from 2025 onward, is distinct in its stated logic: reductions justified not by overhiring or recession but by artificial intelligence — roles eliminated because the work is expected to be automated, and payroll redirected into the computing capacity that performs the automation. Oracle's 2025 and 2026 rounds belong to this second wave in their reported reasoning, and the company's annual filing adopted the wave's vocabulary directly, attributing workforce reductions to "the adoption and deployment of AI technologies across our operations." The distinction matters for reading the ledger: the 2022 and 2023 rounds have precedents and parallels across the industry in the same quarters, while the 2026 round's combination — record profits, record contracted backlog, and the largest reduction in company history, executed to fund construction — was, even within the second wave, an extreme case, which is why it drew coverage well beyond the technology press.

The financing structure underneath the buildout is the snapshot's last element, because it is the transmission mechanism between strategy and layoffs. Oracle funded the construction programme from three sources: operating cash flow, which the buildout itself drove negative — free cash flow was negative $24.7 billion over the twelve months before the March 2026 round; debt, which passed $100 billion in total, including $58 billion raised in the two months before the round; and payroll, converted through the rounds in this ledger. The debt leg developed its own complications. The $18 billion of bonds sold in September 2025, at the moment of maximum optimism — the same month the stock made Ellison briefly the world's wealthiest person — fell in price when Oracle returned to the market weeks later for $38 billion in loans, and bondholders sued in January 2026 alleging the scale of the coming borrowing had not been disclosed when the bonds were sold. Credit analysts placed the company's Baa2 rating under negative scrutiny, two notches above speculative grade, an uncomfortable position for a company whose enterprise customers buy stability as much as software. Capital expenditure tells the same story in the company's own guidance: approximately $50 billion guided for fiscal 2026, $55.7 billion actually spent, and a net $70 billion guided for the following year — a rising line against which the workforce line, 162,000 falling to 141,000, is the mirror image. When a company's construction budget grows by tens of billions a year and its revenue, though growing fast, cannot cover it, the difference is found somewhere. In Oracle's case, the filings and the rounds agree on where it was found.

The OpenAI relationship concentrates the risk in a way no earlier Oracle strategy had. Approximately 58 percent of the $553 billion backlog reported before the March 2026 round was tied to a single customer, itself dependent on continued capital raising to fund its commitments, and Oracle's own borrowing for the buildout — more than $100 billion of debt, the contested $18 billion September 2025 bond issue, a further $38 billion of loans weeks later — was undertaken against that concentration. Analysts who supported the strategy pointed to the backlog's scale; those who questioned it pointed to the circularity, in which Oracle borrows to build capacity for a customer whose ability to pay depends on the same AI investment cycle. For this tracker, the relevance is narrower and factual: the workforce became the flexible term in that equation. When the stock fell 55 percent from its high and bondholders sued, the adjustable cost was payroll, and the two largest rounds in the ledger — September 2025's and March 2026's — bracket exactly the period in which the financing pressure peaked.

Three reported items are recorded here as reported-but-unverified, because they are known to this tracker but do not meet its evidence standard for a ledger entry. First, a single low-quality outlet reported a round of roughly 1,200 jobs announced in March 2024, with detailed division breakdowns and severance terms; no reputable outlet, filing, or WARN record corroborates a distinct March 2024 round, and several of its details conflict with the documented record, so it is not carried in the ledger. Second, during the August–September 2025 round, individual insiders quoted by The Register put the global figure far above the roughly 3,000 carried in the ledger — one estimate ran to nearly 12,000 — but those figures rest on single anonymous sources against WARN-documented hundreds in the United States and consistent multi-outlet reporting at the lower level, so the ledger keeps the documented figure and records the claims here. Third, after the March 2026 round, reporting in late 2026 described a further reduction of undisclosed size, with one independent researcher's estimate of 5,000 to 8,000 roles; Oracle disclosed no number and no second outlet established the same scope, so that round awaits the evidence the ledger requires. Each of these items will move into the ledger if filings or corroborated reporting establish it, and the distinction between the two treatments — documented and reported — is the tracker's core discipline.

Read as a sequence, the five rounds describe an escalation in both scale and candour of purpose. The 2022 round was a division-scale cost programme of a familiar kind, executed inside a sector-wide correction. The 2023 round was an acquisition being repriced — the oldest story in Oracle's own history, repeated from PeopleSoft and Sun. The 2024 round was a business abolished outright, the ledger's only whole-unit elimination. The 2025 round was the first whose reported purpose was the AI buildout, and the first to be documented primarily by the state rather than by the press. The 2026 round combined all of the precedents at ten times the scale: acquisition units cut deepest, a strategic buildout as the stated destination of the money, statutory filings trailing a same-day global execution. The documentary quality of the ledger rises and falls across the sequence in a pattern worth naming — the rounds with filings (2025, and 2026 in part) are known to the day and the site; the rounds known by reporting (2022, 2023) are known to the month and the approximate thousand; and the round known by announcement (2024) is known by its rationale but not its number. That gradient is not a defect of this tracker. It is a property of how Oracle discloses, and the table's labels exist to keep the gradient visible rather than average it away.

**Reconciling the table with the filings.** Readers comparing this table with Oracle's reported headcounts will notice that the two do not move together in any simple way, and the reconciliation is worth stating once, here, rather than leaving it to inference. The company's reported workforce rose from about 143,000 to about 164,000 between fiscal 2022 and fiscal 2023 — not because Oracle hired 21,000 people in a year, but because the Cerner acquisition added roughly 28,000 while the 2022 round and ordinary attrition subtracted. It fell to about 159,000 by fiscal 2024, a net decline of 5,000 in a year whose only ledger round was the advertising closure at roughly 900, because hiring slowed and attrition continued underneath. It recovered to about 162,000 by fiscal 2025 despite the Cerner reductions and the 2025 round, because cloud and data center hiring ran ahead of the cuts. And it fell to about 141,000 by fiscal 2026, a net decline of 21,000 in the year of the largest round, because that round's 20,000 to 30,000 eliminations were partly offset by continued hiring in the buildout organisations. The ledger and the filings are therefore measuring different things: the ledger counts roles eliminated in identified rounds, the filings count the net of everything — rounds, hiring, attrition, acquisitions, and divestitures — and neither can be derived from the other. Where the two appear to conflict, as when the fiscal 2026 net decline of 21,000 sits below the TD Cowen estimate's midpoint for the March round alone, the filings are not evidence against the estimate; they are evidence that hiring continued while it executed, which the reporting on spared and growing buildout teams independently confirms. Both measures are therefore carried in this tracker, each for what it proves, and neither is ever substituted for the other.

Two further figures frame the table, because Oracle's restructuring charges are the closest thing to an official ledger the company keeps. The fiscal 2023 restructuring plan, disclosed in the company's regulatory filings as the Cerner reductions proceeded, was expected to cost approximately $927 million. The fiscal 2026 plan, disclosed in the 10-Q for the quarter ending February 28, 2026, carries an estimated total cost of $2.1 billion, of which $982 million had been recorded in the first nine months of fiscal 2026, with the filing attributing most of the cost to employee severance; the fiscal 2026 annual filing later recorded approximately $1.84 billion of restructuring costs for the year as a whole. Neither figure is a headcount, and this tracker does not present it as one. ## March 2026: The Termination-Email Round

**Announcement and effective dates.** Bloomberg reported on March 5, 2026 that Oracle was planning cuts in the thousands across multiple divisions, including roles the company expected artificial intelligence to make redundant. The round was executed on March 31, 2026, when termination emails from "Oracle Leadership" reached affected employees at approximately 6 a.m. local time in each geography, telling them their roles were eliminated effective that day and that access to company systems would be cut immediately. Some employees recorded April 3, 2026 as their formal last working day, with a garden leave period of about one month to follow; the sequencing varied by division and region, and some United States employees reported later formal dates, including June 1.

**Roles cut.** Oracle has never confirmed a total. The figure used throughout this tracker is the TD Cowen estimate of 20,000 to 30,000 employees, published in the bank's research coverage of the round and widely cited in reporting. An Oracle spokesperson declined to comment when contacted by CNBC, The Washington Times, and The Next Web, and the company did not address the round on its third-quarter fiscal 2026 earnings call earlier in March. No figure in this section is a company-published headcount. The strongest retrospective corroboration came from the company's own annual filing three months later: Oracle ended fiscal 2026 with approximately 141,000 full-time employees, down 21,000 from 162,000 a year earlier, and recorded approximately $1.84 billion in restructuring costs for the year. The 21,000 is a net figure — hiring continued in data center and AI roles throughout the year — so it neither confirms the top of the TD Cowen range nor contradicts it; it establishes that a reduction of at least that net scale occurred across the year containing the round.

**Share of workforce.** Against approximately 162,000 full-time employees in the company's fiscal 2025 annual filing, the estimate range represents roughly 12 to 19 percent of the global workforce — about 18 percent at the top of the range. Employee reports described reductions of at least 30 percent in the hardest-hit units, well above the company-wide average, which is the arithmetic signature of a round aimed at particular organisations rather than spread evenly.

**Divisions and geographies.** Oracle released no official breakdown. Employee reporting on Reddit's r/employeesOfOracle community, Blind, and LinkedIn, together with analyst coverage, establishes the following picture. The Revenue and Health Sciences division (RHS), the healthcare technology business built on the Cerner acquisition, reportedly lost at least 30 percent of its workforce, as did the SaaS and Virtual Operations Services group (SVOS), which runs operational support for Oracle's cloud applications. Many of the United States health cuts fell in Kansas City, Missouri, the former Cerner headquarters, where affected employees included staff with ten to twenty years of service. NetSuite's India Development Centre took significant reductions across project management and engineering roles at multiple levels. Cuts touched cloud infrastructure, communications and marketing, engineering, sales, operations, and customer success as well, but reporting consistently distinguished the teams attached to the Oracle Cloud Infrastructure buildout itself — data center deployment, networking hardware provisioning, GPU cluster management — which saw no cuts or substantially lighter ones; the wider cloud division was also reported to be under a hiring freeze. Geographically the round was global and rolled outward by time zone: employees in India, Canada, Mexico, and Uruguay reported notices before the United States wave. India was among the largest single geographies affected; aggregated reporting put the India figure at approximately 12,000 of an estimated 30,000 India headcount, a reported estimate rather than a company figure. In Canada and Mexico, some employees were notified in meetings rather than by email, a procedural difference from the email-only approach used in the United States and India.

**How the notice read, and its variant.** The email's core sentence told the employee that the company had decided to "eliminate your role as part of a broader organizational change," that the day of the email was the last working day, and that a severance package would follow signing termination paperwork delivered through DocuSign. A subset of employees who had not yet received the email were instead added to short-notice calendar invitations titled "Project Update," where a human resources representative read a prepared statement; system access was cut within minutes of those calls as well. Employee reporting also described advance internal preparation that never reached individuals: SaaS business unit executives were reportedly told in early March to prepare for cuts of ten to twelve percent of their teams, a budget directive passed down without personal warning to the employees concerned. The absence of manager involvement was the round's most remarked-upon feature: there were no HR calls and no video meetings for most recipients, and many managers learned of their team members' terminations from the employees themselves.

**The company's stated framing.** Oracle made no public announcement of the round. The only company wording most affected employees received was the termination email itself, which said that following a review of the company's current business needs, a decision had been made to eliminate their roles as part of a broader organizational change. The strategic rationale — freeing cash flow for the AI data center buildout and removing roles expected to become redundant — comes from analyst analysis and the company's financial disclosures, not from a company statement about the round, and this tracker attributes it accordingly. The company's annual filing later supplied the closest thing to an official explanation, stating that the adoption and deployment of AI technologies across its operations "have resulted, and may continue to result, in reductions to our workforce," and that it could initiate new restructuring plans in the future. The market's immediate verdict was favourable: Oracle's stock rose close to 4 percent on the day the round was executed, even as it remained more than 55 percent below its September 2025 high.

**WARN Act posture.** The federal WARN Act requires employers with 100 or more employees to give 60 calendar days of advance notice before a plant closing or a mass layoff affecting 50 or more employees at a single site, or 500 or more employees overall. Because Oracle's notice to most employees arrived on the day the cuts took effect, the round's WARN position turned on how the separations were structured: the reported garden leave and pay arrangements are the mechanism by which a company in this position can satisfy the notice period without keeping employees in their roles, and whether Oracle's arrangements met the requirement in each state was an open legal question in discussion among affected employees. Oracle did file WARN notices in multiple states; in Washington, its filing disclosed 491 cuts effective June 1, 2026, a notice pattern consistent with separations structured around the 60-day window.

**Severance terms.** Oracle has not published its severance terms. What is established: severance pay was offered contingent on the employee signing separation paperwork containing a general release of claims, and the restructuring plan in the company's SEC filing attributes most of its $2.1 billion cost to employee severance. Reported terms, aggregated from affected employees' accounts and labelled here as reports rather than company-confirmed terms, are set out in the standing section below: a United States formula of approximately two weeks of pay per year of service, and an India structure reported as N plus 2 months of base salary. Employees with vested stock were told they would retain access to those shares through Fidelity.

**The India dimension.** India requires separate treatment in this round because it combined the largest reported national share with the thinnest official documentation. Aggregated reporting put the India figure at approximately 12,000 of an estimated 30,000 India headcount — roughly 40 percent of the country's workforce, double the company-wide rate — concentrated in Hyderabad, Bengaluru, and Pune. The units named in reporting were the NetSuite India Development Centre, where cuts crossed project management and engineering at multiple levels, and the health and SaaS support organisations. India's separation mechanics differed from the United States pattern in ways the standing section sets out: a reported severance structure of N plus 2 months of base salary, statutory gratuity for employees past five years of service, and a formal last working day of April 3 followed by garden leave, which for H-1B holders and for India's own notice conventions moved several legal clocks to later dates than the March 31 email suggested. The scale of the India cuts also made the round a national employment story there in a way the United States coverage was not: India's technology sector employs a significant share of its urban professional class in exactly the support, operations, and non-AI product roles this round targeted, and the Oracle round landed in the same season as AI-attributed reductions across the Indian IT services industry.

**The execution record.** The mechanics of March 31 were documented by recipients in unusual detail, and the consistent elements belong in the record. The email arrived at approximately 6 a.m. local time in each time zone, so the round rolled westward around the world over a single day, beginning in Asia-Pacific and ending in the Americas. It was signed "Oracle Leadership," named no manager, and offered no meeting. System access — email, Slack, code repositories, cloud tooling — was revoked within minutes for most recipients, in some cases before the DocuSign separation paperwork that the email directed them to sign had been completed from a work device, a sequencing failure reported across multiple geographies. Managers were not briefed in advance as a class; the reported pattern is of managers discovering their teams' status from the team members themselves, and in a number of cases receiving their own termination email in the same hour. The "Project Update" meeting variant applied to a minority, reported more often where larger groups were cut together. Five senior executives had departed in the weeks before the round, a detail reporting connected to the reorganisation the round belonged to. None of this execution detail is incidental to the tracker's purpose: the notice pattern is one of the standing facts about how Oracle conducts reductions, and the 2026 pattern — same-day, email-only, access revoked simultaneously — is the reference case against which the next round's execution will be compared.

**The aftermath in the record.** The week after execution produced the documents by which the round will be remembered alongside the emails. Health-sector coverage, including TelecareAware's running account, consolidated the division picture — RHS and SVOS at thirty percent or more, Kansas City's tenured Cerner population at the centre of the United States impact — and recorded the detail that no WARN or OWBPA filings had yet been identified at the federal level in the first days, a gap the later state filings partly filled. CIO's coverage framed the round for the customer base as a support and roadmap risk, the framing through which most enterprises outside the technology industry encountered it. The analyst debate settled into the two positions the snapshot describes: TD Cowen's cash-flow case, in which the round funds the buildout and pays for itself, against the concentration case, in which a fifth of the workforce was exchanged for capacity committed to a single customer's demand. The company's own contribution to the week's record was silence, maintained without exception — no statement, no earnings-call discussion, no confirmation of any figure — which is why this section, like every round section in the ledger, closes by naming the sources it rests on rather than the company it describes.

**Sources.** This section rests on Oracle's 10-Q filing for the quarter ending February 28, 2026 (restructuring plan and charges) and its fiscal 2026 annual filing (year-end headcount of 141,000 and $1.84 billion of restructuring costs); TD Cowen research estimates (headcount range and cash flow savings); Bloomberg's March 5, 2026 report of the planned cuts; reporting by CNBC, CIO, The Washington Times, and The Next Web; People Matters and LinkedIn reporting on India terms; and aggregated employee accounts on Reddit, Blind, and TheLayoff.com, used only for execution detail and always labelled as reports above.

**The customer dimension.** A reduction of this size at a company whose products run other companies' finance, supply chain, and health record systems is also an event for its customers, and the coverage record reflects that. CIO-oriented reporting led with the risk to enterprise support and product roadmaps: the organisations cut deepest — health sciences, SaaS operations and support, customer success — are precisely the organisations enterprise customers interact with, and the round removed, among others, the support engineers and product staff those customers knew. Oracle's licensing model gives the concern a specific edge: customers are typically locked into multi-year agreements and proprietary systems whose migration costs are measured in years, so a support degradation cannot be answered by switching vendors on any short timetable. Oracle made no public statement addressing support continuity. The tracker records the customer dimension as part of the round's documented significance — it is the channel through which a workforce decision inside one company becomes an operational fact for thousands of others — and notes that the measurable evidence, in support outcomes and renewal behaviour, accumulates after the round rather than during it, which is why this dimension is revisited in updates rather than settled here.

**The health business in this round.** Oracle Health's place in the March 2026 round closes an arc that began in 2023, and the round section records it because the two rounds are one story told twice. The Revenue and Health Sciences division — the organisation containing the former Cerner — reported reductions of at least 30 percent, among the deepest of any unit, and the United States cuts fell heavily on Kansas City, where Cerner's workforce had already absorbed the 2023 waves, the campus closures, and three years of attrition under the new owner. Affected employees there included staff with ten to twenty years of service, hired by Cerner to build and run the health record systems Oracle bought the company to obtain. The trajectory — acquire at $28.3 billion in 2022, remove a tenth of the workforce in 2023, remove a further third of the remaining division in 2026 — is the acquisition-integration pattern of the snapshot carried to its conclusion, and it is why this tracker treats the Cerner acquisition as the single most consequential event in Oracle's layoff history: no other decision the company made in the period put as many people into the ledger, across as many years, as the decision to buy Cerner and then run it at Oracle's cost structure.

**The NetSuite dimension.** NetSuite, the cloud ERP business Oracle bought in 2016, appears in this round with a distinct profile worth recording separately. Its India Development Centre, in Hyderabad, took reductions across project management and engineering at multiple seniority levels simultaneously — individual contributors and their managers in the same action — while the unit's global business organisation had already been cut in the 2025 round. NetSuite's position inside Oracle made it a useful index of the round's logic: a mature, profitable, non-AI product line, growing steadily rather than explosively, whose payroll the company treated as available for conversion into infrastructure funding. Reporting on the round noted that NetSuite customers are predominantly small and mid-sized companies dependent on the platform for core finance operations, and trade coverage of the round raised the support and roadmap questions that CIO-oriented reporting put at the centre of its analysis — whether a reduction of this size in support, services, and product organisations degrades the enterprise support that Oracle's licence base pays for. Oracle did not address those questions publicly. The tracker records the question as part of the round's documented reception, and the answer — visible in renewal and support outcomes — as part of what future updates will be able to measure.

**The execution's external record.** Two features of the round were documented outside employee reporting and belong in the permanent record. First, the market reaction: Oracle's shares rose close to 4 percent on March 31, 2026, continuing the pattern, visible in the 2022 round as well, in which investors initially price large Oracle reductions as margin improvement; the same shares remained more than half below their September 2025 peak, which is the longer verdict on the strategy the round served. Second, the round's scale made it a reference event for the industry within days — the largest single reduction of the 2026 technology layoff wave to that point, cited in subsequent coverage of other companies' AI-attributed cuts — and it was executed under a restructuring plan whose remaining unrecorded balance, roughly $1.1 billion at the February filing, told any careful reader of the 10-Q in advance that separation spending on a large scale was still to come in the fiscal year. The plan's expansion from $1.6 billion to $2.1 billion, disclosed in the same filing, was the filing-grade announcement of this round, published weeks before the emails: a worked example of the paper-trail principle the standing section describes.

A companion analysis of the strategy, geopolitics, and governance questions behind this round is published separately as [Oracle's 30,000 Job Cuts Decoded](https://insightcrunch.com/2026/04/01/oracle-layoffs-strategic-analysis/) and is not part of this tracker's ledger.

## August–September 2025: The WARN-Documented Round

**Announcement and effective dates.** This round had no announcement at all; it surfaced through statutory filings and reporting. Bloomberg reported cuts in Oracle Cloud Infrastructure on August 13, 2025. Oracle filed WARN notices in Washington in August 2025 covering 161 Seattle employees, who were notified by August 13 and separated between October 13 and 15, and a supplemental Washington notice on September 2, 2025 covering a further 101 Seattle employees, notified that day and separated between November 3 and 5. California WARN notices followed on September 3, 2025, covering 254 positions across three campuses, also effective November 3 to 5. Reporting through September described the round as continuing, with further cuts expected in October. The separations therefore ran on the statute's own timetable: notice in August and September, effect in October and November.

**Roles cut.** The widely reported global figure is more than 3,000 employees. That figure rests on multiple independent outlets and on the pattern of the filings, and it is consistent with the description later given by RBC Capital Markets' Rishi Jaluria, who characterised the round as affecting more than 3,000 employees across the United States, Canada, and India. The WARN-documented United States portion is smaller and exact: 262 in Seattle across the two Washington notices, and 254 in California — 187 at the Redwood Shores campus, 36 at the Pleasanton campus (which had already taken cuts in August), and 31 at Santa Clara — for 516 documented positions at named sites. The gap between 516 and 3,000 is the round's geography: WARN reaches only United States sites above its thresholds, and the balance of the round fell in India, the Philippines, Canada, and parts of Europe, where no equivalent public filing exists. Individual insiders quoted during the round claimed far higher global figures, up to nearly 12,000; those claims are recorded in the snapshot as reported-but-unverified and are not carried here.

**Share of workforce.** Against approximately 162,000 full-time employees in the fiscal 2025 annual filing, about 3,000 roles represent roughly 2 percent of the global workforce — a mid-sized round by Oracle's standards, and one whose significance came from where it landed rather than how large it was.

**Divisions and geographies.** Reporting and the WARN filings' own job titles establish a round aimed principally at the cloud organisation. Oracle Cloud Infrastructure was the first and hardest-hit division, with the Seattle cuts concentrated in cloud roles — software developers, site reliability engineers, product managers, and programme managers dominate the Washington filing's title list. Oracle Health, the former Cerner business, took cuts including at its Kansas City operations; the Advanced Customer Services organisation and the NetSuite Global Business Unit were also affected, as were corporate and support functions. Within the round, the source record includes approximately 70 people at the MySQL division, among them long-serving engineers, a detail that drew a rare public reaction from MySQL co-creator Monty Widenius about the open source project's future. Geographically the round spanned the United States, India, the Philippines, Canada, and parts of Europe.

**The company's stated framing.** Oracle issued no public statement about this round and did not respond to press requests for confirmation. The only company wording on record is the statutory language of its WARN letters, which describe the separations as arising from "a reduction in force and other terminations," state that the affected sites are not closing, and confirm that no bumping rights exist and the layoffs are expected to be permanent. The strategic explanation — that the cuts redirect cost toward AI infrastructure spending — comes from reporting and analyst commentary, not from the company, and is attributed accordingly wherever this tracker uses it.

**WARN Act posture.** This is the round in which Oracle's WARN practice is fully documented, and it is the opposite of the March 2026 pattern: notices filed roughly 60 days before separations took effect, site by site, with affected employees individually notified on the filing timetable. Washington's filings were made under the state's Securing Timely Notification and Benefits for Laid-Off Employees Act, and the supplemental September notice is itself a compliance artefact — it exists because a second group of employees was notified later than the first, and the statute required a second filing. The filings also show the threshold structure of the law in practice: Seattle's 3,900-strong local workforce (per the Puget Sound Business Journal) lost 262 people across two notices without any site closing, each notice covering its own notification cohort.

**The round's wider reporting.** Beyond the WARN states, the round's reporting fills in a national and international picture that the filings only sample. ITPro's coverage described cuts continuing into September across the United States, Canada, and India, with the health division's Missouri operations — the Kansas City organisation — among those affected, and roles spanning cloud operations managers, analysts, user experience staff, and software engineers. Employee discussion tracked by the same coverage put the running total well beyond the WARN-documented figures within weeks, which is consistent with the multi-outlet global figure of more than 3,000 and with the later analyst characterisation of the round. The Register reported in September that a further round was expected in October; whether October's separations are read as this round's tail or a separate action, the documentary record does not distinguish them, and this tracker treats the August-to-autumn 2025 reductions as one round with a rolling execution — the same treatment the 2023 Cerner waves receive, and for the same reason: the reporting that establishes the scale treats them cumulatively. The round also coincided, within weeks, with the chief executive transition announced on September 22, 2025, and it was the last workforce action of Safra Catz's tenure — a sequence the snapshot records because the new leadership's first action, five months later, was the largest round in the company's history.

**The cloud repricing.** Reporting on this round returned repeatedly to one irony that belongs in the record because it explains the round's location inside the company. Oracle Cloud Infrastructure had been built, from the late 2010s, by recruiting aggressively from the rival hyperscalers — Amazon and Microsoft above all — and paying premium salaries to do it, a strategy centred on Seattle precisely because that is where the experienced cloud workforce lives. The 2025 cuts fell disproportionately on exactly that population: the Washington filing's job titles are a catalogue of the roles OCI had paid up to hire — software developers at every grade, site reliability engineers, product and programme managers — and the stated context in reporting was the cost of the AI buildout those same engineers' infrastructure supported. The round is therefore best understood as a repricing of the cloud workforce hired in the expensive years, executed in the city where the premium had been paid, at the moment the company's capital priorities moved from hiring capacity to building it. The same repricing logic, extended from one division to the whole company, is what the March 2026 round then applied at scale.

**The Seattle footprint.** The Washington filings document a local contraction that had been running longer than the round itself. Oracle was one of the Seattle area's largest technology employers, with about 3,900 local employees before the 2025 notices, drawn heavily from its cloud organisation's recruiting from rival hyperscalers at premium salaries. The company had already surrendered a substantial share of its downtown Seattle office space in 2023. The two 2025 notices — 161 separations in the August filing, 101 in the September supplemental — removed 262 Seattle positions in one season, and the supplemental notice is unusually informative about the round's mechanics: it covers employees who work remotely in Washington as well as those at the two named Seattle sites, it lists the affected job families (software development at every level from Applications Developer 1 to Software Development Director, site reliability, product management, programme management, database administration, support, and individual finance and marketing roles), and it records that the August cohort had been individually notified by August 13 for separations in mid-October while the September cohort was notified on the filing date for separations in early November. A reader can therefore reconstruct this round's United States timetable to the day from public records, which is true of no other round in this ledger — and the reason is not the round's size but its structure, since it proceeded site by site under two state notification regimes rather than globally at once.

**Severance terms.** The reporting on this round does not disclose severance terms, and Oracle published none. This section therefore records the position the template requires: terms were not disclosed in Section 3-grade sources for the 2025 round. The documented facts are the notice periods themselves — roughly 60 days between notification and separation in the WARN-covered United States sites, which is paid employment time rather than severance.

**Sources.** This section rests on Oracle's Washington WARN notices of August and September 2, 2025 and its California WARN notices of September 3, 2025 (site counts and effective dates); Bloomberg's August 13, 2025 report of the cloud cuts; The Register's September 4, 2025 reporting on the Washington and California filings; the Puget Sound Business Journal's Seattle workforce figure via TechXplore's coverage; and multi-outlet reporting of the global total above 3,000, including the later RBC characterisation of the round.

## June 2024: The Advertising Business Closure

**Announcement and effective dates.** This round is the only one in the ledger that Oracle announced itself, and it announced it to investors before it told most of the staff. On the fiscal 2024 fourth-quarter earnings call on June 11, 2024, chief executive Safra Catz told analysts: "In Q4, we decided to exit the advertising business, which had declined to about $300 million in revenue in fiscal year '24." The closure took effect over the following quarter: Oracle stopped entering new advertising contracts on June 17, 2024, and the advertising products — Moat, Grapeshot, BlueKai, the data management platform, and the rest of the former Oracle Data Cloud — reached end of life on September 30, 2024. Layoffs began in the week after the call, and reporting described most affected staff learning of the closure from the public announcement itself.

**Roles cut.** Digiday's reporting put the figure at about 900 executives laid off across the division's closure, describing several hundred layoffs in the initial wave with a skeleton crew retained briefly to wind the business down. Oracle never published a number. The division's listed size at the time — between 1,001 and 5,000 employees on its own LinkedIn summary — brackets the figure without confirming it, and this tracker carries the reported 900 with its source attached. Competitors' hiring gives the figure indirect support: Integral Ad Science alone reported hiring more than 20 former Oracle Advertising staff within weeks, and ad technology firms openly recruited the displaced teams at the Cannes Lions festival in June 2024.

**Share of workforce.** Against approximately 159,000 full-time employees in the fiscal 2024 annual filing, about 900 roles represent under 1 percent of the company — the smallest round in this ledger by share, and the only one that eliminated an entire business rather than reducing several.

**Divisions and geographies.** The round was confined to a single organisation: Oracle Advertising, the business assembled from a decade of acquisitions beginning in 2012, including Datalogix, BlueKai, Moat, and Grapeshot. Its products covered advertising data, audience targeting, contextual intelligence, and measurement. Unlike the other rounds in this ledger, there was no wider division list and no reported spill into engineering, sales, or cloud organisations; the cut was the business. Oracle also announced the sunset of related consumer marketing products, including Oracle Maxymiser, over the same period. The company chose closure over sale — reporting indicated would-be buyers circled the assets, and Oracle declined to sell, a decision analysts attributed in part to the privacy liabilities attached to the division's data assets.

**What the business had been.** The closure ended a twelve-year experiment. Oracle assembled its advertising business from acquisitions beginning in 2012 — Datalogix for offline purchase data, BlueKai for the data management platform, Moat for measurement, Grapeshot for contextual targeting — at a cumulative cost well into the billions, and as late as fiscal 2022 the assembled business produced a reported $2 billion in revenue. Its decline was driven by forces largely outside the division's control: European privacy regulation ended its third-party data targeting in Europe in 2020, the wider industry lost the tracking signals its products depended on, and the business's growth had already slowed to about 2 percent a year by 2022, when the first round in this ledger cut deeply into the teams supporting it. The 2024 closure is therefore best read as the second act of the August 2022 round rather than an unrelated event: 2022 reduced the cost base of a weakening business, and 2024, with revenue down to $300 million, abolished the business. Reporting on the closure also recorded the human mechanics in unusual detail — the announcement was made to investors first, the division's staff learned of their employer's exit from the same public call, competitors began recruiting the displaced teams within days, and Oracle declined expressions of interest in buying the assets, preferring a clean shutdown with its data liabilities extinguished to a sale.

**The company's stated framing.** The company's framing is on the earnings call transcript: the business had declined to about $300 million in revenue in fiscal 2024, from approximately $2 billion in fiscal 2022, and Oracle decided to exit. The longer arc was visible in advance. European third-party data targeting had been withdrawn in 2020 under GDPR pressure, the AddThis service had been shut down the year before the closure, and the division's revenue had fallen by roughly $1.7 billion in two years as data deprecation — the industry-wide loss of third-party tracking signals — undermined its model. Catz's sentence is the complete public rationale, and this tracker records it as such.

**The wider sunset.** The advertising closure was the centre of a broader withdrawal from consumer marketing technology that the same announcement set in motion. Forrester's analysis of the exit noted that Oracle also planned to sunset related business-to-consumer marketing products, including Oracle Maxymiser, over the following months — the testing and personalisation tooling that had served consumer brands rather than advertisers. The pattern is consistent with the doctrine described in the snapshot: a cluster of products serving a market Oracle had entered by acquisition, growing slowly, and facing regulatory and signal-loss headwinds was not trimmed but vacated, products, contracts, and people together, on a published timetable. For the tracker, the sunset matters because it marks the boundary of the round: the roles counted here are the advertising division's, and the adjacent product sunsets, which affected smaller teams inside the wider marketing organisation, are recorded as part of the same exit rather than as a separate round, there being no reporting that separates their staffing effect from the division's closure.

**WARN Act posture.** No WARN filing for the advertising closure figures in the reporting on this round. The separations were distributed across a division rather than concentrated at a single site above the federal thresholds in the coverage available, and the wind-down structure — contracts honoured through September 30, 2024 — spread effective dates across a quarter. The absence is recorded here because the contrast with 2025 is instructive: Oracle's WARN footprint depends on how a round is distributed, not only on how large it is.

**The wind-down in practice.** The closure's mechanics were published in unusual detail because the customers, not only the employees, needed a timetable. Oracle stopped entering new advertising contracts on June 17, 2024, six days after the announcement; existing campaigns and contracts were honoured through September 30, 2024, when the products reached end of life together — Moat Analytics, the contextual intelligence service, the data management platform, the data append and enrichment services, and the digital audiences business among them. A skeleton crew of the division's own staff was retained to run that wind-down, on the roughly two weeks' notice Digiday reported for the final group. The labour market's response was equally public: Integral Ad Science published an open invitation to affected clients and staff on June 13, within two days of the announcement, and reported more than twenty hires from the division within weeks; DoubleVerify competed for the same people and clients; Experian's marketing services arm hired former Oracle Advertising staff into revenue roles. The episode is the cleanest illustration in this ledger of the diaspora mechanism described in the standing section — a workforce whose skills had a liquid external market was reabsorbed at a speed that said more about the division's remaining value than the closure decision did, and the contrast with the 2026 round, whose displaced population was an order of magnitude larger and far more specialised to one company's systems, is part of the record.

**Severance terms.** Oracle published no severance terms for this round. The one concrete term in Section 3-grade reporting is notice rather than severance: Digiday reported that the skeleton crew retained to close the business was given about two weeks' notice before their own roles ended. Anything beyond that was not disclosed, and this tracker does not estimate it.

**Sources.** This section rests on Oracle's fiscal 2024 fourth-quarter earnings call of June 11, 2024 (the exit announcement and the $300 million revenue figure); Digiday's June 2024 reporting on the closure and the roughly 900 layoffs; The Register's and Adweek's coverage of the announcement; Forrester's analysis of the exit; and the company's published end-of-life schedule for the advertising products (new contracts ending June 17, 2024; end of life September 30, 2024).

## March–June 2023: The Cerner Reductions

**Announcement and effective dates.** The Cerner reductions arrived in waves rather than on a single day, and this ledger records them as one round because the reporting that establishes their scale treats them cumulatively. Bloomberg reported in March 2023 that notifications had begun the week of March 13, with cuts in Kansas City, Missouri — where Cerner was headquartered — likely numbering under 500 in that wave. On March 24, 2023, staff were told not to expect raises or promotions in the coming fiscal year. In mid-May 2023, Becker's Hospital Review reported, on the accounts of current and former employees, that approximately 3,000 Cerner employees had been laid off since the acquisition. On June 15, 2023, a further wave followed: Insider reported that Oracle had laid off hundreds more employees in the health unit that day, rescinded outstanding job offers, and eliminated open positions.

**Roles cut.** The figure carried in the cumulative table is approximately 3,000, the reported cumulative total for the Cerner reductions across 2023. Becker's reported the 3,000 figure in May, attributed to employees; Healthcare Dive, covering the June wave, described Oracle as having fired almost 3,000 Cerner employees since the acquisition, across marketing, engineering, accounting, legal, and product teams. The June wave itself was reported as hundreds, and the rescinded job offers and eliminated open positions in that wave are recorded here as what they are — offers withdrawn and roles left unfilled — and are not counted in the 3,000, which counts people laid off. Oracle disclosed no figure of its own; what it disclosed was money: a restructuring plan in its regulatory filings for the period, expected to cost approximately $927 million, which is the filing-grade anchor for the round's scale.

**Share of workforce.** Against approximately 164,000 full-time employees in the fiscal 2023 annual filing, about 3,000 roles represent roughly 2 percent of the company. Inside the affected unit the share was far higher: Cerner brought approximately 28,000 employees into Oracle in June 2022, so the reductions removed on the order of a tenth of the acquired workforce within its first year.

**Divisions and geographies.** The round was concentrated in one organisation — Cerner, by then renamed Oracle Health — and within it, in the functions an acquirer typically overlaps: marketing, engineering, accounting, legal, and product teams were all named in reporting. Kansas City, Cerner's home city, was the centre of gravity; Oracle had already decided in October 2022 to close two of Cerner's Kansas City-area campuses, a facilities decision that preceded the headcount waves. The datacenter operations staff were among those cut in March, including employees with more than fifteen years of service. Reporting at the time noted the cuts appeared calibrated below the numbers that would trigger a WARN Act filing — a structural feature of this round that the machinery section below returns to.

**The company's stated framing.** Oracle's public framing was made before the cuts, not during them. At an October 2022 investor event, chief executive Safra Catz said of Cerner that "that's just not how we run a place," adding that Oracle would "clean Cerner up" — a reference to the unit's cost structure that reporting on the layoffs consistently quoted back. The proximate cause established by reporting was the Department of Veterans Affairs electronic health record programme: Cerner's system, deployed at VA facilities under a contract worth around $10 billion, had suffered patient-safety findings — a Senate committee hearing heard evidence the system had caused harm to nearly 150 patients at a Washington hospital — and in May 2023 the VA renegotiated the contract, pausing the rollout work that much of the unit's staffing depended on. Insider's sources attributed the June wave largely to that stalled contract. Oracle declined to comment on the individual waves.

**The Veterans Affairs background.** The contract at the centre of this round deserves its own paragraph, because it is the rare case in this ledger where a single customer's programme drove a round. Cerner had won the Department of Veterans Affairs' electronic health record modernisation contract — a programme valued at around $10 billion over its life — to replace the VA's homegrown system with Cerner's platform across the entire veterans' health system. The deployment went badly: go-lives were paused, a Senate committee heard evidence that the system had contributed to harm to nearly 150 patients at a Washington facility, and in May 2023 the VA renegotiated the contract and halted further rollouts while problems were addressed. Work that thousands of Cerner staff had been hired and retained to perform was therefore suspended by the customer, and Oracle's response ran through the unit's cost base: the March notifications, the freeze on raises and promotions for the remainder of 2023, the benefit changes for hourly Cerner staff that had begun the previous October, and the June wave. Oracle Health's later history — including its place among the hardest-hit organisations in the March 2026 round, with the Revenue and Health Sciences division reporting reductions of at least 30 percent — begins here: the 2023 reductions were the first demonstration that the acquired health workforce would be managed to Oracle's margin doctrine rather than to the contract's original staffing plan.

**Kansas City.** The geography of this round carried a weight the others did not, because Cerner was not merely located in Kansas City — it was one of the city's defining employers, headquartered there since its founding, with a workforce and campus footprint built over four decades and a civic identity closer to a hometown institution than to a division of an Austin software company. The campus closure decisions of October 2022, the benefit changes for hourly staff, and then the 2023 waves were therefore received locally as the dismantling of an institution by an acquirer that had promised, at the deal's announcement, to keep and grow the health business's home. Local coverage and employee accounts from the period record the texture of that reception: long-tenured staff describing a culture built under Cerner's founders being replaced, within a year, by Oracle's cost structure and vocabulary. The tracker records this because it is part of what the round was — and because Kansas City's story did not end in 2023. The same workforce, reduced and repriced, was still Oracle Health's centre of gravity when the March 2026 round cut the division by a reported thirty percent, and the two rounds together describe the full arc of the acquisition for the city that hosted it.

**What happened inside Cerner.** The detail of the unit's first year under Oracle explains why the reductions took the shape they did. Cerner entered the acquisition with roughly 28,000 employees, a Kansas City campus footprint built for a workforce that worked on site, and a cost structure Oracle's leadership regarded as bloated by the standards of its own famously lean operations. The sequence of changes reported through the year ran: the October 2022 decision to close two Kansas City-area campuses; changes the same month to paid time off benefits for hourly Cerner staff; the rebranding of the unit as Oracle Health; the March 2023 notifications and the accompanying freeze on raises and promotions for the rest of 2023; and the May and June waves. Reporting from inside the unit described the cultural dimension of the same process — a workforce that had understood itself as a healthcare company being repriced as a software division — and the specific exposure of the Veterans Affairs programme team, whose work was suspended by the customer while its staffing costs continued. Cerner's products did not disappear: the electronic health record business contributed $1.5 billion of revenue in the third quarter of fiscal 2023 alone, and Oracle Health remains one of the company's named strategic businesses. The reductions were therefore not an exit but a repricing — the same business, expected to run at Oracle's margin structure with roughly a tenth fewer people — and the unit's subsequent appearance at the centre of the March 2026 round, three years later, shows the repricing was a direction rather than an event.

**WARN Act posture.** No WARN filing anchors this round in the reporting; The Register noted in March 2023 that the cuts were understood to be structured in numbers below WARN thresholds. Whether that reflects site distribution or wave sizing, the documentary consequence is the same: the 2023 round is established by multi-outlet reporting and the company's restructuring disclosure, not by statutory notices, and its figure is labelled reported throughout this tracker.

**Severance terms.** This round carries the most precisely reported severance terms in the ledger, because Insider obtained them from sources inside the process. Employees laid off in the June 2023 wave were reported to receive four weeks of severance pay as a base, plus one additional week for every year of service, plus a payout of accrued vacation days. Oracle declined to comment on the terms. The formula is recorded here as reported terms for the June 2023 Cerner wave; it should not be read as a company-wide schedule, and the machinery section compares it with the different formula reported in 2026.

**Sources.** This section rests on Bloomberg's March 2023 reporting of the first notifications and the raise and promotion freeze; Insider's reporting of May and June 2023 (the cumulative figure's basis and the June wave, including severance terms), as carried by Becker's Hospital Review, Healthcare Dive, Computerworld, and The Register; Oracle's regulatory disclosure of the restructuring plan expected to cost approximately $927 million; and The Register's March and June 2023 coverage of the Kansas City cuts and the Veterans Affairs contract.

## August 2022: The Advertising and Customer Experience Cuts

**Announcement and effective dates.** The sequence opened with reporting, not an announcement. In July 2022, weeks after the Cerner acquisition closed, Bloomberg and The Information reported that Oracle was considering eliminating thousands of jobs to save up to $1 billion in costs. The cuts began on August 1, 2022, when employees — concentrated in the advertising and customer experience organisation — began receiving notifications, and they continued through the following weeks across the United States, Canada, India, and parts of Europe. A California WARN filing later in the programme, covering 201 employees at Redwood City and Belmont effective in October 2022, shows the round still executing three months after it began.

**Roles cut.** This is the least precisely documented round in the ledger, and the table's range is the honest expression of that. The Wall Street Journal, in the first days, reported that Oracle had laid off hundreds of employees. The programme those cuts belonged to was larger: The Information's reporting described a plan to cut thousands of jobs toward $1 billion in savings, and Futurum Group, summarising the reporting at the time, put the total jobs lost in the round as high as 5,000. The Register recorded looser speculation running toward 10,000, which this tracker treats as speculation and excludes from the table. The range carried in the cumulative record — 200 to 5,000 — therefore runs from the floor implied by the Journal's "hundreds" to the largest sourced estimate, and the round section, not the table, is where the texture belongs: what is certain is that hundreds were cut in the first week, that entire teams were eliminated, and that the programme was designed in the thousands.

**Share of workforce.** Against approximately 143,000 full-time employees in the fiscal 2022 annual filing — the last count before Cerner's staff joined — the reported range represents roughly 0.1 to 3.5 percent of the company. Even at its high end, this was a division-scale event, not a company-scale one; its importance in this ledger is that it established the pattern the later rounds repeated.

**Divisions and geographies.** The cuts principally hit Oracle's advertising and customer experience group — the organisation selling customer data analysis and advertising targeting services — and marketing. Insider's sources described the Advertising and Customer Experience department as "obliterated," with some teams cut by 30 to 50 percent and individual teams eliminated entirely; roles lost spanned CX presales engineering, marketing, analyst relations, talent acquisition, CRM, and development, and affected employees ranged from recent hires to staff with more than twenty years of service. Some Cerner employees were also reported affected in the same weeks, an early overlap between the cost programme and the acquisition that had just closed. The documented geography began in the San Francisco Bay Area and extended, in the reporting's own sequence, to Canada, India, and parts of Europe.

**The company's stated framing.** Oracle did not announce the round. The framing established by the Journal's reporting, attributed to people familiar with the company's actions, was prioritisation: the cuts hit advertising and customer experience as the company prioritised its healthcare IT services and cloud businesses. The financial logic reported alongside was the $1 billion savings target. Employees told Insider the cuts contradicted management assurances that their jobs were safe, and described the process as chaotic; the company did not comment on the record. The framing matters to this tracker because it is the first appearance of the rationale structure that recurs in 2026 — an underperforming or non-core cost base eliminated to fund the two strategic priorities — with healthcare, the beneficiary named in 2022, becoming a source of cuts itself within a year.

**WARN Act posture.** The October 2022 California WARN filing — 201 employees at the former headquarters campus in Redwood City and neighbouring Belmont, including data scientists, developers, and marketing specialists — is the round's statutory trace. It demonstrates the lag built into WARN as a documentary source: the filing records the tail of a programme that reporting had established two months earlier, at the one site large enough to require it.

**The earnings context.** The round's timing relative to Oracle's reporting calendar became a documented feature in its own right. The cuts began on August 1, 2022, six weeks before the company's first-quarter fiscal 2023 earnings, and when those results arrived in September they showed a company growing, not shrinking: revenue rose strongly on the first contribution from Cerner, even as operating income fell under the acquisition's costs and the cost programme's charges. The juxtaposition — hundreds of roles eliminated in the same quarter as record-setting revenue — was noted at the time as characteristic, and it is the earliest appearance in this ledger of the configuration that defines the March 2026 round: reductions executed from revenue strength, in service of margin structure and a strategic priority, rather than in response to a downturn. A reader who understands the 2022 round's context will not be surprised by 2026's; the scale changed, the logic did not.

**The programme behind the round.** The August cuts were the execution of a decision reporting had tracked since July. Oracle had closed the $28.3 billion Cerner acquisition on June 8, 2022, absorbing roughly 28,000 employees and a cost base its leadership considered undisciplined, in the same quarter that inflation and recession fears were compressing technology valuations across the sector. The plan attributed to the company by The Information and Bloomberg — thousands of roles, up to $1 billion of annual savings, concentrated in the United States and Europe — was the first statement of the doctrine that runs through this entire ledger: when Oracle needs money for a priority, it takes the money from payroll, and it takes it from the organisations furthest from the priority. In August 2022 the priorities named were healthcare IT and cloud; the distance was measured in the advertising and customer experience group's weak growth. Employees described the execution as chaotic, with teams learning their fate from social media posts by colleagues, entire groups dropped at once, and cuts reaching from new hires to staff with more than twenty years of service — accounts that, allowing for the different scale, match the execution record of the rounds that followed and suggest the method was set here. The round also landed weeks before Oracle's first-quarter fiscal 2023 earnings, a timing relationship between reductions and reporting dates that recurs in the ledger.

**Severance terms.** Reporting on the 2022 round does not disclose severance terms in Section 3-grade sources, and Oracle published none. As with the 2025 round, this section records that the terms were not disclosed rather than reconstructing them from later rounds' practice; Oracle's severance formulae have demonstrably differed between rounds, as the machinery section shows, so borrowing one would invent a fact.

**Sources.** This section rests on The Information's and Bloomberg's July 2022 reporting of the planned programme and its $1 billion savings target; the Wall Street Journal's August 2022 report of the first cuts, as carried in subsequent coverage; The Register's August 2, 2022 reporting from affected employees; CRN's August 1, 2022 report of the first notifications; Insider's reporting via WebProNews and HIStalk; Futurum Group's summary of the round's scale; and the California WARN record for the Redwood City and Belmont separations of October 2022.

## How Layoffs Work at Oracle

The sections above record what happened in specific rounds. This section records the machinery that any Oracle round runs on, drawn from how the five tracked rounds were executed and reported. It changes only when a later round changes the pattern, and the clearest way to read it is comparatively: Oracle has run the same underlying process five times with materially different settings, and the settings are the content.

**How notice is given.** Oracle has used three distinct notice patterns. In the 2025 round, notice was statutory and advance: WARN filings preceded separations by roughly 60 days, and affected employees were individually notified on the filing timetable — notification and separation were different events, two months apart. In the March 2026 round, notification and separation collapsed into one event: a single email from "Oracle Leadership," sent at approximately 6 a.m. local time, with no advance communication from human resources and no prior conversation with the employee's direct manager; many managers learned of their own team members' terminations at the same time as the employees, and access to company systems, including Slack, was deactivated simultaneously with the email, so the notification and the lockout were the same moment. A subset of 2026 employees received a variant — a short-notice calendar invitation titled "Project Update," where an HR representative read a prepared statement — and in parts of Canada and Mexico, notification was delivered in meetings instead of by email. The 2024 advertising closure used a third pattern: notification by earnings call, with most of the division learning of its elimination from a public investor announcement, and the layoffs following in the subsequent weeks. Across all three patterns, one constant holds: Oracle does not accompany rounds with a company-wide announcement or press statement. The 2026 round was not discussed on the earnings call that preceded it; the 2025 round was never publicly confirmed at all.

**Severance practice, compared across rounds.** Oracle does not publish a severance schedule, and the tracked rounds prove that no single schedule exists. The most precisely reported terms in the ledger belong to the June 2023 Cerner wave: four weeks of severance pay as a base, plus one additional week for every year of service, plus a payout of accrued vacation days — terms Insider obtained from people familiar with the process. The March 2026 round's United States terms, aggregated from affected employees' accounts, were described as approximately two weeks of base pay per year of service, with a minimum floor and a maximum ceiling that vary by seniority, together with health coverage continuation through COBRA and access to outplacement services and an employee assistance programme. Those two formulae are structured differently — a flat base plus a service increment in 2023, a pure service multiple in 2026 — and produce different outcomes at different tenures, which is why this tracker records each round's terms inside its own round section and treats this section's account as reported practice, never as an entitlement a future round must follow. For the 2022 and 2025 rounds, no terms were disclosed in reliable reporting, and the tracker says so in those sections rather than filling the gap. Severance, where it is paid, is classified as supplemental income for United States federal withholding, at the flat supplemental rate of 22 percent on amounts up to $1 million, with state tax applied on top. Payment of unused paid time off depends on state law and company policy: in California, unused PTO is treated as earned wages and must be paid at termination; in most other states, payout follows the employer's written policy — the 2023 Cerner vacation payout was a term of that round, not a universal rule.

**What the two formulae are worth.** Because the 2023 and 2026 formulae are structured differently, neither is uniformly more generous, and the comparison is worth doing in numbers. Take the reported terms at face value. Under the June 2023 Cerner formula — four weeks base plus one week per year of service — an employee with two years of service receives six weeks of severance; with ten years, fourteen weeks; with twenty years, twenty-four weeks. Under the reported March 2026 United States formula — approximately two weeks per year of service, subject to a floor and ceiling that were not published — the same employees receive roughly four weeks, twenty weeks, and forty weeks respectively. The 2023 structure therefore favours short-service employees, whom a pure service multiple nearly excludes, while the 2026 structure rewards tenure at twice the rate and makes long-serving employees — a large population at Oracle, where the 2026 round cut staff with ten to twenty years of service in significant numbers — considerably more expensive to separate. That arithmetic is one reason restructuring charges cannot be converted into headcounts by simple division: the same round costs more per person in a tenured workforce than in a young one, and Oracle's charges per eliminated role have risen across the tracked plans accordingly. India's reported 2026 structure, N plus 2 months of base salary, sits between the two in shape — a service multiple with a floor built in — before the statutory gratuity entitlement is added for employees past five years.

**The release, and the time built around it.** Severance at Oracle, as at most large United States employers, is contingent on signing a separation agreement with a general release of claims, which typically covers claims arising from the termination itself. Federal law builds deliberation time into that signature for older workers: under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act, employees over 40 who are part of a group layoff must receive at least 45 days to consider a waiver of age discrimination claims, and 7 days after signing to revoke. For individual agreements outside a group programme, the standard consideration period described in the March 2026 reporting was 21 days. The release is delivered electronically — in the March 2026 round, through DocuSign — and severance payment follows the signed, unrevoked release.

**Garden leave and the effective date.** Some March 2026 employees were separated on a two-step timeline: a formal last working day days after the email — April 3 was commonly reported in India — followed by a garden leave or pay-in-lieu period of about one month during which employment formally continued. The effective termination date in the separation paperwork, not the date of the notification email, is the date from which most downstream clocks run, including the visa grace period described below and the WARN analysis in the round section. The 2025 round shows the alternative structure: no garden leave was needed, because the WARN timetable itself supplied the interval between notice and separation as ordinary paid employment.

**Equity and benefits.** Employees in the March 2026 round were told that vested stock remained theirs and accessible through Fidelity, the plan administrator. Unvested awards are the exposed side of an Oracle separation: reporting from the 2026 round included employees whose scheduled June vesting fell days after their termination, and unvested restricted stock units do not survive the end of employment under standard plan terms. Health coverage continues through COBRA continuation, under which the former employee carries the premium cost. The equity dimension has a particular weight at Oracle because the company's senior leadership compensation is heavily equity-based — the co-CEOs' appointment grants of $250 million and $100 million in options were made in the same period in which the 2026 round's severance charges were accruing — but the mechanism for departing employees is the standard one: vested value is kept, unvested value is forfeited, and the termination date relative to the vesting calendar decides how much that distinction is worth.

**WARN Act position.** The federal WARN Act's 60-day notice requirement applies to Oracle at the scale of its larger rounds by headcount alone, and state mini-WARN laws add their own thresholds and notice periods in the states that have them. Oracle's practice across the tracked rounds covers the full range of relationships an employer can have with the statute. In 2025 it complied in the ordinary way, filing in Washington under the state's own notification act and in California, roughly 60 days ahead of separations, with supplemental filings when a second cohort was notified later. In 2022 the statute captured only the programme's tail — 201 people at two California sites. In 2023, reporting described the Cerner waves as structured in numbers below WARN thresholds, and no filing anchors that round. In 2026, the employee-facing notification was same-day while WARN notices were filed in multiple states — the Washington filing disclosed 491 cuts effective June 1, 2026 — with the gap between the two bridged by the garden leave and pay structure described above. A reader of this tracker should therefore treat WARN filings as a floor, not a measure: they document the sites and cohorts the statute reaches, and the rounds differ mainly in how much of their true size that happens to be.

**The European difference.** Oracle's tracked rounds touched Europe in 2022 and 2025, and the mechanism there differs enough from the United States pattern to belong in this section. Collective redundancy law across the European Union and the United Kingdom requires consultation with employee representatives before large reductions take effect, on timetables that cannot be compressed into a single morning: proposed cuts must be notified to public authorities, representatives must be given the reasons and the numbers, and a consultation period runs before notices can be issued. The practical consequences show in the record's shape. European components of Oracle rounds surface later, execute slower, and are documented through consultation reporting rather than filings, which is one reason the 2022 programme's European phase was reported as following its American phase "in coming days or weeks" that stretched considerably longer. The same legal architecture is why a global Oracle round can never be perfectly simultaneous: the March 2026 round's time-zone roll-out was a single day in the Americas and Asia, but any European component of a round that size runs on consultation clocks measured in weeks, and the tracker's round sections date European effects accordingly where the reporting establishes them.

**Remote workers and the site problem.** WARN and its state counterparts were written around sites — physical workplaces with countable employees — and Oracle's recent rounds show the statute adapting to a distributed workforce in real time. The September 2025 Washington supplemental notice expressly covers employees who work remotely in Washington alongside those at the two named Seattle facilities, attributing remote workers to the state for notice purposes; the California notices, by contrast, are organised campus by campus, Pleasanton, Redwood Shores, and Santa Clara each carrying its own count. The practical consequence for anyone reading Oracle's filings is that a round's WARN total is an artefact of how its workforce is distributed across notification regimes: a round concentrated in offices produces clean site filings, a round spread across remote workers produces supplemental notices and attributions, and a round concentrated outside the United States produces almost nothing. The 2023 Cerner round illustrates the third case from the other side — a round at physical campuses that reporting described as sized below filing thresholds — and the 2022 round the first, with its single California filing trailing a global programme. The statute is a lens with a fixed focal length; this tracker uses it for what it resolves sharply, site-level United States separations, and uses reporting for everything beyond it.

**India machinery.** India's separation terms follow a different statutory frame. Reporting on the March 2026 round described a severance structure of N plus 2 — N being years of completed service — paid in months of base salary, together with notice pay of one month's base salary and encashment of accumulated unused leave. Gratuity is statutory rather than discretionary: under the Payment of Gratuity Act, employees with at least five years of service are entitled to it. The Industrial Disputes Act adds notice and compensation requirements for employees classified as "workmen," a category that generally excludes senior professional and managerial staff. India appears in four of the five tracked rounds — as part of the 2022 programme's geography, in the 2025 round, and at the largest scale in 2026, when aggregated reporting put the India figure at approximately 12,000 of an estimated 30,000 India headcount — and the largest India cuts in 2026 were reported in Hyderabad, Bengaluru, and Pune, Oracle's principal India development and operations centers.

**How selection was made, as reported.** The consistent account across the March 2026 reporting is that selection was decided above the team level and was not performance-based in any way employees could detect: workers with strong ratings, recent promotions, and decades of tenure were cut alongside everyone else, and the round reached senior levels, including directors and vice presidents. Frontline and mid-level managers were not consulted and in many cases were not informed in advance; some received their own termination emails minutes after their reports. The 2022 round produced a similar account from the other direction — employees told Insider the cuts contradicted explicit management assurances of safety — and the 2023 Cerner waves fell on functions rather than named individuals, in the acquirer's pattern. This tracker records these accounts as the reported pattern of each round; Oracle has not described its selection criteria publicly for any of them, and in the 2026 round employees and reporting found no indication that selection varied by immigration status.

**How information about a round reaches the record.** The five tracked rounds also teach a standing lesson about sources, which this section records because the tracker's labels depend on it. Information about an Oracle round surfaces in a characteristic order. First, the filings: a restructuring plan estimate moves in a quarterly filing, sometimes weeks before any person is notified, because accounting rules require the commitment to be disclosed once made. Second, the statutory notices: WARN filings appear at the sixty-day horizon where a round's structure generates them, naming sites and counts that no press report can match for precision. Third, the reporting: major outlets establish the round's existence, scale, and reported reasoning, usually from sources inside the process, and characteristically within days of the first notifications. Fourth, the employee accounts: Reddit, Blind, LinkedIn, and TheLayoff.com supply the execution detail — the wording of emails, the timing of access revocation, the experience of the "Project Update" meeting — that no other source documents, at the price of being unverifiable individually. This tracker weights the four layers in that order, uses each for what it can prove, and labels anything that rests on the fourth layer as reported. A claim that appears only in the fourth layer, however often repeated there, does not enter the ledger; the insider estimates of the 2025 round's global size are the standing example, recorded in the snapshot and excluded from the table for exactly that reason.

**Visa consequences, as mechanism.** An H-1B visa is employer-sponsored, so an Oracle termination starts a fixed clock: under USCIS rules the holder has a 60-day grace period, running from the date employment terminates, to have a new employer file a transfer petition, change status, or depart the United States. Two features of the mechanism matter for reading any Oracle round. First, severance pay does not extend the grace period; the clock is tied to the termination date, not to the last paycheck. Second, a garden leave period that keeps the employee formally on payroll moves the termination date, and therefore the start of the clock, to that later date — which made the 2026 round's two-step timeline materially different from a same-day termination for visa holders, even though the notification felt identical. Under H-1B portability rules (AC21), a holder may begin working for a new employer as soon as that employer files the petition, without waiting for approval, provided the filing happens inside the grace period. Employees on OPT or STEM OPT sit under a different allowance: STEM OPT carries a 90-day unemployment allowance that starts running at termination and must be reported through the employee's designated school official.

**How restructuring charges work.** The paper trail deserves one more level of detail, because the charges are routinely misread as headcounts in coverage of Oracle's rounds. A restructuring charge is an accounting recognition, not a count: when a company commits to a plan and communicates severance terms, it accrues the expected cost — predominantly severance and related benefits, with facilities and contract costs behind them — and the filing discloses the plan's estimated total and the amount recorded to date. Three consequences follow for readers of this tracker. First, charges lag decisions: the fiscal 2026 plan's $982 million recorded by February 2026 measured rounds already executed or committed, while the plan's $2.1 billion ceiling anticipated the March round before any employee was notified — the filing was, in effect, the round's first public document. Second, charges per person vary: the comparison of the 2023 and 2026 severance formulae above shows why, since tenure-weighted formulae in an ageing workforce cost more per separation, and facilities and contract-exit costs ride in the same line. Dividing a charge by an assumed average severance produces a headcount with no reliability, and this tracker never does it. Third, the annual reconciliation is the check: fiscal 2026 closed with approximately $1.84 billion of recorded restructuring costs and a year-end headcount of 141,000, figures that bound the year's total activity — rounds, hiring, and attrition combined — without resolving it into a single round's count. The tracker's figures come from the sources named in each round section; the charges are used as corroboration and scale, which is what they are competent to prove.

**The paper trail: restructuring plans.** The standing documentary record of Oracle's rounds is not its press output but its SEC filings, and learning to read them is part of how this tracker works. Oracle discloses restructuring plans in its quarterly and annual filings, with an estimated total cost and the amount recorded to date, most of it attributed to employee severance. The fiscal 2023 plan was expected to cost approximately $927 million as the Cerner reductions proceeded. The fiscal 2026 plan was first disclosed at $1.6 billion, revised to $2.1 billion in the February 2026 10-Q — of which $982 million had been recorded in the first nine months of the fiscal year — and closed the year at approximately $1.84 billion of recorded restructuring costs alongside the annual filing's statement that AI adoption has resulted, and may continue to result, in workforce reductions. A rising plan estimate in a filing is therefore the earliest filing-grade signal that a round is larger, or longer, than the one before it, and it is the source this tracker checks first when a new round is reported. For professionals working through those filings directly, the [ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org) tools offer data utilities and career planning resources designed for a technology landscape that is shifting at this speed.

**Where the people go: the diaspora mechanism.** A standing feature of Oracle rounds, documented in every tracked case, is the speed with which competitors absorb the displaced workforce — a mechanism that shapes how rounds are reported and, plausibly, how the company itself calculates their cost. In 2024 the pattern was visible in its purest form: rival advertising technology firms recruited Oracle Advertising's teams publicly and within days, with Integral Ad Science reporting more than twenty hires from the division in the first weeks and competitors working the Cannes Lions festival as a hiring venue. In the technology rounds, the Q&A record of the March 2026 round lists the destinations employees and recruiters identified: SAP, Salesforce, Workday, and ServiceNow hiring Oracle applications staff; the hyperscalers' India organisations recruiting from Oracle's cloud infrastructure population; product companies hiring Oracle-competitive and Oracle-integration skills simultaneously. The mechanism matters to the tracker for two reasons. First, it is part of the factual aftermath of each round — the workforce does not vanish, it redistributes, and the redistribution is one measure of how the market valued the people the round priced as surplus. Second, it explains a recurring feature of Oracle's method: a company whose alumni are hired this readily can run repeated rounds without exhausting the external labour market's willingness to supply it with future hires, which lowers the long-term cost of the practice relative to a company whose departures are permanent losses of scarce skill.

**Open source stewardship.** Oracle is the corporate steward of widely used open source and formerly open technologies — MySQL, acquired with Sun Microsystems, and the Java platform among them — and layoff rounds that reach those teams produce an effect no other category of cut produces: public concern from communities that depend on the software but have no relationship with the company. The September 2025 round's inclusion of roughly 70 MySQL staff, among them long-serving engineers, drew a public reaction from MySQL co-creator Monty Widenius about the project's long-term health, and the March 2026 round renewed the same discussion at larger scale. The mechanism is worth recording because it is a real constraint, if a soft one, on how Oracle staffs these teams: the credibility of a stewardship commitment is part of the product's value, and rounds that cut into steward teams are read by their communities as signals about the product's future, in a way that cuts to internal functions are not.

**Reading a round before it lands.** The tracked rounds were each preceded by signals that were visible in public or reported sources, and the standing list of them is part of this section's machinery, because it is how this tracker — and any careful observer — detects a round before execution. A restructuring plan estimate revised upward in a quarterly filing preceded the March 2026 round by weeks and remains the most reliable signal, because it is a legal disclosure rather than a rumour. Pre-reporting by a major outlet preceded both the 2022 programme (Bloomberg and The Information, July 2022) and the 2026 round (Bloomberg, March 5, 2026). Internal budget directives preceded execution in 2026, when SaaS executives were reportedly told in early March to prepare for cuts of ten to twelve percent. Executive departures clustered before the 2026 round, with five senior executives leaving in the preceding weeks. Hiring freezes and compensation freezes accompanied the 2023 and 2026 rounds. And WARN filings, where a round's structure generates them, convert reporting into documentation at roughly sixty days' distance from separations. No single signal is proof; the pattern of the ledger is that at least two of them have appeared before every tracked round, and the absence of a company denial — Oracle's consistent practice is no comment — has never, in the tracked period, meant a reported round was not real.

**The legal exposure that follows rounds.** Large reductions create a standing set of legal questions, and the tracked rounds show Oracle managing them through structure rather than through advance communication. The WARN questions are covered above. Behind them sit the release mechanics: the OWBPA's 45-day consideration and 7-day revocation periods for workers over 40 in group programmes exist because age discrimination claims are the most common legal challenge to selection patterns in large rounds, and the group disclosure requirements that accompany those waivers — the ages and titles of those selected and not selected — are the one place where a round's selection pattern becomes a document employees can inspect. The 2026 round's reported pattern, in which senior and long-tenured employees were cut at visibly high rates, is exactly the configuration those disclosures are designed for. Securities exposure runs in parallel rather than in the same cases: the January 2026 bondholder class action concerned what the company disclosed about its financing, not about its workforce, but it belongs to the same record, because it alleges the company understated a plan whose costs the workforce rounds were simultaneously funding. This tracker records the existence and subject of such proceedings as part of a round's aftermath; it does not assess their merits, and none of the proceedings referenced here has produced a finding against the company on the matters described.

**Adjacent tools: freezes, rescinded offers, and campus closures.** Not every workforce reduction at Oracle is a layoff, and this tracker keeps the categories separate because the cumulative table counts only people whose roles were eliminated. Three adjacent mechanisms recur. Hiring freezes: the wider cloud division was reported under a hiring freeze during the March 2026 round, and Cerner employees were told in March 2023 not to expect raises or promotions through that year — a compensation freeze that reporting treated as part of the same cost programme. Rescinded offers and eliminated openings: the June 2023 Cerner wave withdrew outstanding job offers and cut open positions alongside the layoffs, and those numbers are recorded in the round section but excluded from its roles figure. Facilities: Oracle closed two Cerner Kansas City campuses by decision of October 2022, and the 2025 WARN letters took care to state that the Seattle sites were not closing — a distinction the statute cares about, because a site closure triggers a different notice category than a reduction in force. Each mechanism reduces the workforce or its cost; only one of them is a layoff round, and the ledger's discipline is to keep them unmerged.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many people has Oracle laid off in total?

Across the five rounds this tracker records from 2022 onward, the total is a reported range of roughly 27,100 to 41,900 roles. The range is wide because its largest component is an estimate: the March 2026 round accounts for 20,000 to 30,000 of the total on TD Cowen's estimate, and Oracle has never published an official headcount for that round or any other. The remaining rounds contribute about 3,000 each for the 2023 Cerner reductions and the August–September 2025 round, about 900 for the 2024 advertising closure, and a reported range of 200 to 5,000 for August 2022. The company's own filings bound the picture without resolving it: Oracle's headcount fell by a net 21,000 in fiscal 2026 alone, to 141,000, with hiring continuing throughout.

### Q: Why does Oracle lay off employees while posting record profits?

Because the recent rounds are a capital allocation decision, not a response to losses. In the quarter reported just before the March 2026 round, Oracle's net income rose 95 percent to $6.13 billion and revenue grew 22 percent to $17.2 billion, with contracted backlog reaching $553 billion. At the same time, the company's AI data center buildout required capital expenditure guided at approximately $50 billion for fiscal 2026 and $70 billion the year after, free cash flow was negative $24.7 billion over the trailing twelve months, and debt had passed $100 billion. Eliminating roles converts salary cost into construction funding; TD Cowen estimated the March 2026 round would free $8 billion to $10 billion a year. The earlier rounds had different stated drivers — acquisition overlap, a collapsing advertising business — but shared the same structure: cuts made from revenue strength, in service of margin and strategy.

### Q: How are Oracle employees notified when their role is eliminated?

It depends on the round, and Oracle has used three distinct patterns. In March 2026, notification was a single email from "Oracle Leadership" sent at approximately 6 a.m. local time, stating that the role was eliminated effective that day, with system access cut simultaneously and no prior conversation with the employee's manager. In the 2025 round, notification followed the WARN timetable: employees were individually notified roughly 60 days before their separation dates, in step with the company's state filings. In the 2024 advertising closure, most of the division learned of its elimination from the company's public earnings call, with layoffs following over the next weeks. A minority pattern in 2026 was a short-notice meeting under a "Project Update" invitation, and in parts of Canada and Mexico notification was by meeting rather than email.

### Q: Does Oracle give advance notice before a layoff takes effect?

In the 2025 round, yes — the WARN-documented separations ran on roughly 60 days' notice, with employees individually notified on the filing timetable. In the March 2026 round, no: notification and termination arrived together for most employees, and the notice obligation was addressed structurally instead, through WARN filings made with state agencies and garden leave or pay arrangements of about one month after the formal last working day for some employees. Whether that structure satisfies the 60-day federal notice requirement in each state is a legal question that depends on how each separation was documented. The 2022 and 2023 rounds produced no comparable notice record in reporting, and the 2023 Cerner waves were described as structured below WARN thresholds.

### Q: What severance does Oracle offer in the United States?

Oracle does not publish its severance terms, and the tracked rounds show that no single schedule exists. The most precisely reported terms belong to the June 2023 Cerner wave: four weeks of severance pay as a base, plus one additional week for every year of service, plus a payout of accrued vacation days, as reported by Insider from people familiar with the process. The March 2026 round's United States terms, aggregated from affected employees' accounts, were described as approximately two weeks of base pay per year of service, subject to a minimum floor and maximum ceiling that vary by seniority, with COBRA continuation and outplacement access. For the 2022 and 2025 rounds, no terms were disclosed in reliable reporting. Payment in each case was contingent on signing a separation agreement, and the terms recorded here are reported practice for specific rounds, not a company-confirmed schedule.

### Q: How is severance calculated for Oracle employees in India?

Reporting on the March 2026 round described a structure of N plus 2 — N being years of completed service — paid in months of base salary, together with notice pay of one month's base salary and encashment of accumulated unused leave. Gratuity is a separate statutory entitlement under the Payment of Gratuity Act for employees with at least five years of service, payable regardless of the company's severance structure. The Industrial Disputes Act adds notice and compensation requirements for employees classified as "workmen," a category that generally excludes senior professional and managerial staff. Some reports of the 2026 round mentioned an additional two-month top-up; accounts differed on whether it was automatic or conditional, and Oracle has not confirmed the formula publicly. India terms for the earlier rounds in this tracker were not disclosed in reliable reporting.

### Q: Do laid-off Oracle employees have to sign a release to receive severance?

Yes, in the pattern documented across the tracked rounds, severance is contingent on signing separation paperwork — delivered electronically, through DocuSign in the March 2026 round — containing a general release of claims, which typically covers claims related to the termination itself, including discrimination claims. The release is the standard exchange in United States corporate severance: the payment is made in return for the waiver, and severance follows the signed release once any revocation period has passed. The termination email in March 2026 made the sequence explicit, telling employees they would be eligible for a severance package after signing their termination paperwork. Employees who decline to sign decline the severance with it; the statutory entitlements that do not depend on a release, such as India's gratuity, are unaffected.

### Q: How long do employees over 40 have to consider an Oracle severance agreement?

Federal law sets the floor, and it is the same at Oracle as at any large United States employer. Under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act, employees over 40 included in a group layoff must be given at least 45 days to consider a waiver of age discrimination claims, and 7 days after signing to revoke it. For individual agreements outside a group programme, the consideration period described in reporting on the March 2026 round was 21 days. These periods are statutory minimums designed into the process; they are not set by Oracle's discretion, and they run from delivery of the agreement, which in the 2026 round arrived through DocuSign after the termination email itself — one reason the formal separation paperwork, rather than the email, is the document from which most legal clocks in a round are measured.

### Q: Does the WARN Act apply to Oracle layoffs?

Yes, at Oracle's scale — but how much of a round it documents varies enormously, and this tracker's rounds display the full range. The federal WARN Act requires employers with 100 or more employees to provide 60 calendar days of notice before mass layoffs affecting 50 or more employees at a single site, or 500 or more overall, and state mini-WARN laws add their own rules. In 2025 Oracle filed in the ordinary way, in Washington under the state's own notification act and in California, documenting 516 positions at named sites roughly 60 days ahead of separations. In 2022 a single California filing captured 201 positions at the tail of a global programme. In 2023 the Cerner waves were reported as structured below filing thresholds. In 2026 Oracle filed in multiple states — Washington's filing disclosed 491 cuts effective June 1, 2026 — while notifying most employees on the day itself, bridging the gap with garden leave and pay arrangements.

### Q: What happens to vested Oracle stock when an employee is laid off?

Vested shares remain the employee's property. In the March 2026 round, affected employees were told they would retain access to their vested stock through Fidelity, the plan administrator. Termination ends the employment relationship; it does not forfeit shares that have already vested, and the same principle applies across the rounds in this tracker. The practical questions for a departing employee are administrative — how long account access and any trading windows remain open — rather than questions of ownership. This is one of the few elements of an Oracle separation that does not vary by round, country formula, or seniority: the vesting line, once crossed, is not revisited by the separation.

### Q: What happens to unvested Oracle stock and RSUs at termination?

Unvested awards do not survive the end of employment under standard plan terms. Reporting from the March 2026 round included employees whose next scheduled vesting fell in June, days after their termination date, and who therefore lost the unvested portion entirely. The gap between the termination date and the next vesting date is one of the largest single variables in what a long-serving employee actually loses in a round — larger, for senior staff with substantial annual grants, than the severance itself. It is also one of the mechanisms by which the formal termination date, rather than the notification date, carries financial weight: where a garden leave arrangement moves the employment end date past a vesting date, the awards on that date vest; where it does not, they lapse. Oracle's plans follow the standard structure here, and no tracked round reported an exception programme for unvested awards.

### Q: Does severance pay extend an H-1B holder's 60-day grace period?

No. The grace period under USCIS rules runs from the date employment terminates, and it is unaffected by how long severance payments continue afterward. One qualification matters: where a garden leave arrangement keeps the employee formally employed to a later end date — as in parts of the March 2026 round, where a formal last working day of April 3 and a garden leave period followed the March 31 email — the termination date in the paperwork is that later date, and the grace period runs from it. The controlling date is the employment end date in the separation documents, not the last pay date and not the notification date. That distinction made the 2026 round's two-step timeline materially different for visa holders from a pure same-day termination, even where the notification experience was identical.

### Q: Can an H-1B holder move to a new employer after an Oracle layoff?

The mechanism is H-1B portability under AC21. A new employer files a transfer petition on the holder's behalf; once filed within the 60-day grace period, the holder may begin working for the new employer immediately, without waiting for the petition to be approved. The constraint is the clock, not the approval timeline: the petition must be filed before the grace period expires. The demand side of that mechanism was visible in the tracked rounds — the Q&A record of March 2026 lists SAP, Salesforce, Workday, ServiceNow, and the hyperscalers' India organisations among the employers recruiting Oracle staff — because a holder with a filed petition is employable at once, while a holder whose grace period lapses must leave and re-enter through a new process. Nothing in the tracked reporting indicated that Oracle's rounds treated visa holders differently in selection; the exposure is created by the immigration framework, not by the company's criteria.

### Q: What happens to Oracle employees on OPT or STEM OPT when they are laid off?

STEM OPT carries a built-in unemployment allowance of 90 days, and the allowance starts consuming at termination. The unemployment period must be reported through the employee's designated school official at their university. Unlike the H-1B grace period, which is about changing employer or status, the STEM OPT allowance is a fixed total of permitted unemployment days across the OPT period, so days used in any earlier unemployment count against the same allowance. For junior employees — the population most likely to be on OPT — a layoff round therefore runs on the shortest clock of any immigration category in the workforce, and the tracked rounds' junior populations, including recent hires cut in both 2022 and 2026, included employees in exactly that position. The mechanism is statutory and identical across employers; what varies is only how much of the allowance an individual has already used.

### Q: Which Oracle divisions have been hit hardest by layoffs?

Different divisions in different rounds, which is itself the pattern. In March 2026, employee reporting described reductions of at least 30 percent in the Revenue and Health Sciences division — the healthcare business built on the Cerner acquisition — and in the SaaS and Virtual Operations Services group, with NetSuite's India Development Centre also taking deep cuts, while the Oracle Cloud Infrastructure buildout teams were largely spared. In 2025 the cloud organisation itself was the target, with Oracle Cloud Infrastructure first and hardest hit. In 2023 the Cerner workforce absorbed the entire round, losing roughly a tenth of its people. In 2024 the advertising division was eliminated whole, as the advertising and customer experience group had been gutted in 2022. Across the ledger, the exposed populations share one trait: they sat furthest from the company's stated priority of the moment — until, in 2025, the priority organisation itself was repriced.

### Q: Which countries are affected by Oracle layoff rounds?

Every tracked round was multi-country, and the March 2026 round was fully global. Employees were affected in the United States, which saw the largest absolute numbers in most rounds and is the only country whose cuts are documented site by site through WARN filings, and in India, Canada, Mexico, Uruguay, the Philippines, and parts of Europe across the various rounds. Aggregated reporting put the India figure in March 2026 at approximately 12,000 of an estimated 30,000 India headcount — a reported estimate, not a company-published figure — making India the largest single geography in that round. Notices in 2026 rolled outward by time zone, with some non-US employees notified before the United States wave. The documentary imbalance is structural: United States rounds generate statutory records, while India, the Philippines, and Europe generate reporting of a lower grade, and this tracker's sourcing labels reflect that difference rather than concealing it.

### Q: How much has Oracle budgeted for restructuring and severance?

The filings give two plan figures for the tracked period. The fiscal 2023 restructuring plan, disclosed as the Cerner reductions proceeded, was expected to cost approximately $927 million. The fiscal 2026 plan was first disclosed at $1.6 billion, revised to $2.1 billion in the 10-Q for the quarter ending February 28, 2026 — with $982 million recorded in the first nine months of fiscal 2026, most of it attributed to employee severance — and the fiscal 2026 annual filing recorded approximately $1.84 billion of restructuring costs for the year as a whole. The roughly $1.1 billion of the 2026 plan not yet recorded at the February filing was the clearest public measure, in advance, of the separation spending the company still expected to incur. These charges are the closest thing to an official account of the rounds that Oracle publishes; they measure cost, not people, and this tracker never converts them into headcounts.

### Q: Who leads Oracle, and did the leadership change play a role in the layoffs?

Safra Catz stepped down as chief executive in September 2025 after more than a decade, becoming Executive Vice Chair of the Board. She was succeeded by co-CEOs Clay Magouyrk, the architect of Oracle Cloud Infrastructure's second-generation platform, and Mike Sicilia, who leads industry applications including Oracle Health. Larry Ellison, the co-founder, remains Chairman and Chief Technology Officer and owns approximately 42 percent of the company. Catz presided over the first four tracked rounds and personally announced the advertising exit; the August–September 2025 round was the last of her tenure. The March 2026 round was the new leadership's first large workforce action and coincided with the acceleration of the AI infrastructure strategy they were appointed to execute — Magouyrk's own organisation, cloud infrastructure, having been the 2025 round's target and the 2026 round's relative shelter. The leadership transition is recorded in this tracker as context for the rounds' timing and direction, not as their sole explanation: the cost doctrine behind them predates both leaderships.

### Q: Has Oracle laid off workers before the March 2026 round?

Yes — repeatedly, and this tracker exists to record it. Four qualifying rounds precede March 2026 in the ledger: the advertising and customer experience cuts of August 2022, the Cerner reductions of 2023, the advertising business closure of 2024, and the WARN-documented round of August–September 2025, together accounting for a reported range of roughly 7,100 to 11,900 roles. Before the tracked period, the pattern extends further back: the closure of the China research operation in 2019, consolidation rounds after the PeopleSoft acquisition in 2005 and the Sun Microsystems acquisition in 2010, and the 1990 reduction of about 400 people — roughly 10 percent of the workforce at the time — after an audit revealed overstated earnings. What distinguishes March 2026 is scale, not novelty: it is the largest round in the company's history and the first driven primarily by redirecting payroll into infrastructure construction, but the method — speed, silence, filings over announcements — was established across the earlier rounds.

### Q: Will there be more Oracle layoff rounds?

This tracker records rounds when they are announced or filed; it does not forecast them. The public record contains three relevant facts. First, Oracle's fiscal 2026 annual filing states that the adoption and deployment of AI technologies "have resulted, and may continue to result, in reductions to our workforce," and that the company could initiate new restructuring plans in the future — the standing language of an employer that expects the question to recur. Second, reporting in late 2026 described a further reduction of undisclosed size, with one researcher's estimate of 5,000 to 8,000 roles; it is recorded in this tracker's snapshot as reported-but-unverified, because Oracle disclosed no figure and no second outlet established the same scope. Third, the signals that preceded earlier rounds — a restructuring plan estimate revised upward, a hiring freeze, pre-reporting by a major outlet — are the indicators this tracker watches. Any future round that meets the tracker's sourcing rules will be added to the ledger and the cumulative table above.
