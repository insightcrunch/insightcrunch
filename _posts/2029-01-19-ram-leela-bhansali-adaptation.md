Ram-Leela, released in full as Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela, is the strongest mainstream Indian answer to a stubborn critical habit: the habit of treating Verona as the natural home of Romeo and Juliet and every other setting as decoration. Sanjay Leela Bhansali's 2013 Hindi film moves the tragedy to Ranjaar, a fictional Gujarati town where two clans manufacture and sell firearms in the open, and the move is not decoration. The guns change the feud's physics, the festivals change the plot's machinery, the matriarch who runs the Capulet house changes the tragedy's balance of power, and the soundtrack changes how desire itself is staged. The verdict defended across this study is direct: Ram-Leela keeps the tragic core of the play intact, and spectacle in the film functions less as an escape from that core than as the vernacular language in which the core is spoken. Where the film falters, in a stretched second half and in one ornamental song placed for star value, the failures are failures of proportion rather than of nerve. The double death survives translation, restaged as a mutual shooting agreed by both lovers, and the peace that follows arrives too late in the precise Shakespearean manner. A tragedy that ends with two households cremating their children together has not traded its core for color. The color is how the core reaches its audience.

The play's own opening supplies the standard against which the adaptation should be measured. The Prologue announces "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1) and an "ancient grudge" breaking "to new mutiny" (Prologue, line 3), then promises that "a pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, line 6). Three commitments follow from those lines: parity between the houses, a feud older than any living grievance, and a foreknown ending in which the lovers' deaths carry the moral weight. Ram-Leela honors all three commitments with unusual literalness. The Rajadi and Sanera clans hold matched power in Ranjaar, matched arsenals, matched codes of honor. The feud between them is dated by the film's own narration at five hundred years, an antiquity no character can explain and none can escape. The ending is never in doubt for any viewer who knows the source, and Bhansali builds the final movement as a countdown toward a death the lovers choose with open eyes. Fidelity of that order is rare in popular adaptation, and it frames every liberty the film takes elsewhere. The liberties are extensive: a heroine promoted to clan chief, a villain with no counterpart in the play, a mother rebuilt as a gang sovereign, songs that pause the action for communal dance. Each liberty is examined in turn against the three commitments, and each survives the examination except where proportion fails.

## What Is Ram-Leela? The Film, the Credit, and the Claim

Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela opened on 15 November 2013, written and directed by Sanjay Leela Bhansali, who also composed the songs, and produced by Bhansali Productions with Eros International and its head Kishore Lulla. The screenplay credit is shared between Bhansali and the writing team Siddharth-Garima, who also supplied the lyrics. Ranveer Singh plays Ram Rajadi, the younger son of the Rajadi leadership, and Deepika Padukone plays Leela Sanera, daughter of the Sanera matriarch. The supporting cast carries the adaptation's boldest remappings: Supriya Pathak Kapur as Dhankor Baa, the Sanera chief; Richa Chadda as Rasila, Leela's sister-in-law and confidante; Sharad Kelkar as Kanji, Leela's brother; Abhimanyu Singh as Meghji, Ram's elder brother; Gulshan Devaiah as Bhavani, Leela's cousin and the plot's concealed engine of violence; Barkha Bisht Sengupta as Kesar, Meghji's wife; and Raza Murad as the Sarpanch, the village authority standing in the Prince's place. Priyanka Chopra appears in a special appearance in the song Ram Chahe Leela. Ravi Varman shot the film, and the production design is by Wasiq Khan. Every one of those credits matters to the adaptation argument, because the film distributes Shakespearean functions across an ensemble rather than concentrating them in the lovers, and the distribution is where the reading of the play happens.

### Does the film name Shakespeare in its own credits?

The film names Shakespeare plainly. Its opening titles declare the basis in Romeo and Juliet, and Bhansali stated the debt in public during the title litigation of November 2013, clarifying that the picture was inspired and based on Shakespeare's work and was neither a dramatization of the Ramayana performance tradition nor a Krishna pageant.

Attribution of that explicitness is uncommon in Hindi popular cinema, where Shakespearean borrowings have often gone unacknowledged, and the openness shapes the film's reception: reviewers in India and abroad measured the picture against the play from the first notices onward, rather than discovering the parallel afterward.

The explicit credit also disciplines the adaptation's freedom. A film that announces its source invites scene-by-scene comparison, and Bhansali accepts the invitation at the structural level while refusing it at the verbal level. Almost no Shakespearean dialogue survives in translation; the Hindi screenplay speaks in a heightened, aphoristic register of its own, full of boasts, taunts, and endearments shaped for the Gujarat setting. What survives is architecture: the meeting at a festival gathering in the enemy house, the swift mutual recognition, the kin-killings that convert private desire into public blood debt, the elopement, the forced separation, the peace negotiated one movement too late. Critics who charged the film with looseness were measuring verbal fidelity, a standard the picture never claimed. Critics who praised its fidelity were measuring structure, the standard the credit sequence actually invokes. The adjudication matters for everything that follows in this study: Ram-Leela is a structural adaptation of unusual rigor wearing the clothes of a free one, and many of the disagreements in its reception reduce to a quarrel over which fidelity counts.

The title itself, once the lawyers had finished with it, encodes the whole method. Goliyon Ki Raasleela translates roughly as a dance, or play, of bullets; raas names the circular folk dance of Gujarat, most associated with the dandiya raas of Navratri, and leela names divine play in the Krishna tradition as well as the heroine. Ram names the hero and echoes the god. Bhansali's public clarification insisted that Ram the character depicted Lord Rama in no manner at all, and the film supports the disclaimer: its Ram is a cheerful libertine who runs a video parlor stocking adult films, deals in stolen cars, and courts women with a water pistol. Yet the names do cultural work regardless of disclaimers, as the scholar Paulomi Sharma argues in a reading of the film's postcolonial politics: the mythic resonance loads the lovers with a sanctity the plot then desecrates and mourns, so that a Gujarati audience watches not merely two clan children but a desecrated iconography fall. The dance of bullets is therefore also a dance of meanings, and the film's Indian identity is not a backdrop laid behind a European story. The story's names, dances, festivals, and weapons all belong to the place, and the place changes what the story can say.

One further production fact belongs in this opening account because later sections return to it. The film was shot on sets and locations split between Gujarat and Rajasthan, with sequences filmed at Udaipur, including the palace and Gangaur Ghat, standing in for parts of Ranjaar. The town is a built world, deliberately heightened: streets arranged for processions, courtyards scaled for dance, a haveli architecture of painted plaster and carved wood that no single Gujarati town assembles in one frame. Reviewers divided over the artifice, some reading the sets as a storybook unreality and others as the correct register for a tale that begins with a chorus announcing its own theatricality. The play, after all, opens by asking its audience to accept a stage as Verona. Bhansali asks a cinema audience to accept a studio Gujarat as Ranjaar, and the request is the same in kind.


## Ranjaar: A Gun-Running Gujarat Replaces Verona

Verona in the play is a city where private violence keeps breaking into public space: servants brawl in the opening scene, the Prince threatens death for a third disturbance, and the tomb becomes the final public square. Ranjaar translates that civic condition into an economy. The town's open trade is arms and ammunition, manufactured and sold without inhibition, displayed, as one widely repeated description of the film's world puts it, with the casualness of vegetables at a market stall. The simile matters. Where Verona's feud is a habit of honor enforced by swords, Ranjaar's feud is an industry. Guns are livelihood, inheritance, dowry, and rhetoric at once; a clan that laid down its weapons would lay down its income and its identity in the same gesture. Bhansali thereby answers the oldest question audiences put to the play, the question of why the feud cannot simply stop, with a materialist answer the play itself only implies. The Capulet and Montague households fight because fighting is what their world is organized to produce. For the background logic of that organization, [the feud at the heart of the Capulets and Montagues](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) remains the necessary companion reading, since the play leaves its own grudge unexplained and every adaptation must invent an explanation or expose the blank.

The five-hundred-year dating performs similar work. Shakespeare's grudge is "ancient" without a number; the film supplies a number so large that no living character's memory can reach an origin, and the effect is to make the feud a condition of nature rather than a dispute. Nobody in Ranjaar litigates a first cause. The clans keep ledgers of corpses instead, each killing entered as payment against an older killing, and the arithmetic guarantees continuation because every settlement creates a fresh debt. Ram's elder brother states the code in the film's early movements, and Ram's refusal of the code defines his character: he is the one Rajadi who speaks of peace as something other than a pause for reloading. His pacifism is not saintliness. The film gives him a libertine's motives, pleasure and boredom and appetite, and the mixture is truer to Romeo than a reformer's purity would be. Romeo in the play is no peace activist; he is a young man whose desire happens to cross the feud's line, and whose refusal to fight Tybalt in 3.1 springs from a secret marriage rather than a philosophy. Ram likewise refuses the gun from inclination before he refuses it from love, and the film is careful to show the refusal costing him standing in his own house long before Leela gives it a reason.

Geography sharpens the translation. Ranjaar is landlocked, dusty, and enclosed, a town of courtyards and terraces where sightlines are short and every errand crosses enemy ground. The lovers' meetings exploit the enclosure: a Holi crowd as cover, a terrace within shouting distance, a fair, a procession. Verona's orchard wall, scaled in 2.2 while Juliet speaks above, becomes a series of thresholds guarded by armed kin, and the balcony scene's vertical poetry is redistributed into horizontal daring, Ram walking into the Sanera stronghold in daylight festivities rather than climbing by night. The change of axis is a small masterstroke of adaptation. Shakespeare's lovers meet across a height, desire looking up and down; Bhansali's meet across a street, desire looking through gun sights, and every subsequent meeting carries the risk calculus of a border crossing. The town is the tragedy's third antagonist, after the feud and time, and it is wholly invented. Nothing in the play corresponds to Ranjaar's arms bazaars. Everything in the play corresponds to its function.

The arms economy also explains the film's most distinctive visual signature, the fusion of festivity and firepower. In Ranjaar, celebration and combat share a choreography: guns are fired into the air at dances, processions carry weapons as ornaments, and the Garba circle turns with rifles slung like jewelry. A celebrated sequence sets a traditional Garba in motion with gunshots marking the beat. The fusion is not a director's conceit pasted onto the story. In a town where the gun is the universal tool, the gun enters the dance the way the sword enters the Capulet ball, as the period's ordinary equipment carried into its pleasures. Shakespeare's ball is shadowed by Tybalt's recognition of an enemy voice and his instant reach for a duel; Bhansali's festivals are shadowed by the certainty that any gathering of both clans, however festive, is one insult from a firefight. The adaptation thereby keeps the play's deepest rhythm, joy and violence alternating on a hair trigger, while changing every surface through which the rhythm is felt.

Read as an argument, the map shows a consistent preference: functions are preserved, machinery is replaced. The potion plot vanishes because its function, the fatal failure of a message, is reassigned to Bhavani's forgeries and manipulations, which perform the same office in a gun culture's idiom. The Friar vanishes because a clan society has no neutral churchman with a cell and a pharmacopoeia; counsel and concealment move inside the families, where Rasila and the lovers' friends carry messages at mortal risk. The Prince shrinks to a Sarpanch because Ranjaar's clans outgun any authority the village can field, a bleaker civic diagnosis than Shakespeare's, where Escalus at least commands the stage at the end and pronounces the moral. In Ranjaar no authority pronounces anything. The mothers and elders negotiate the peace themselves, over the bodies, which is either a more democratic ending or a more desolate one, and the film leaves the choice to the audience while tilting the imagery toward desolation: the joint cremation is shot as exhaustion, not triumph.

The map also exposes the adaptation's boldest structural wager, the conversion of the friend-and-cousin killings into brother-killings. In 3.1 of the play, Mercutio dies under Romeo's arm, cursing both houses with "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), and Romeo then kills Tybalt, his wife's cousin, in revenge. The deaths bind Romeo by guilt and by marriage. Bhansali tightens both bonds into blood. When Kanji kills Meghji, the dead man is Ram's elder brother, and when Ram kills Kanji, the dead man is Leela's brother. Each lover has buried a sibling by the other's house before the elopement's aftermath tears them apart, so the feud's price is no longer paid by proxies at one remove. The lovers themselves are the bereaved. That single change powers the film's second half, in which grief, not parental command, becomes the separating force, and it justifies the ending's logic: two people who have each lost a brother to the feud can read its arithmetic to the end and refuse to keep paying.


## Ram Rajadi: A Romeo Who Refuses the Gun

Shakespeare's Romeo enters the play in a posture of performed melancholy, in love with Rosaline and with the Petrarchan vocabulary of love itself, until the Capulet ball replaces performance with experience. Ram enters in the opposite posture: extroverted, theatrical, delighted with himself, introduced by the film's most exuberant song, Tattad Tattad, sung by Aditya Narayan as a hero's fanfare in which the town itself seems to applaud his arrival on a motorcycle. The inversion is pointed. Where Romeo must be educated out of artifice into feeling, Ram must be educated out of feeling's ease into consequence. His early scenes define him by refusals: he breaks up a clan altercation rather than joining it, saves the Sanera man Bhavani from his own brother's vengeance in the opening movement, and preaches peace to armed men who tolerate the preaching as an eccentricity of the boss's son. The film thereby front-loads the quality the play reveals gradually, Romeo's instinctive recoil from the feud's code, the recoil that makes him plead with Tybalt that "the reason that I have to love thee doth much excuse the appertaining rage" (3.1). Ram's whole first hour is that plea expanded into a character.

Ranveer Singh's performance carries the conception with a physicality that reviewers across the spectrum praised even when they faulted the film around him. Rajeev Masand, in a notice that admired the picture's passion while protesting its second-half bloat, singled out the firecracker energy of the lead pair as the element separating the film from Bhansali's heavier earlier work. Trade critic Taran Adarsh, awarding the film four and a half stars, treated the hero's charm as the commercial engine of the whole enterprise. The performance's key is volatility inside the charm: Ram jokes while armed men argue, flirts during a festival intrusion that could get him shot, and grieves with a rawness that strips the charm away in the brother-killing's aftermath. When he kills Kanji, the film refuses heroic framing. The act is a spasm of grief and rage, committed by the peace-preacher against his own doctrine within hours of his brother's death, and Singh plays the aftermath as horror at his own hand. Romeo's "I am fortune's fool" (3.1) finds its counterpart in that horror, the discovery that the feud can operate a man's body against his convictions.

The role also tests the adaptation's gender politics from the male side. Ram's desire is frank and reciprocal from the first meeting; the film's erotic charge, remarked by nearly every reviewer, depends on Leela's answering desire rather than on pursuit and surrender. His signature prop in the early courtship is a water pistol, a toy weapon flourished in a town of real ones, and the joke is a thesis: the hero's masculinity is defined against the clan's armament, not through it. As the plot darkens, the props fall away. He cuts his own finger in solidarity when Dhankor Baa mutilates Leela's, answering her punishment with a matching wound, in a scene that converts the play's language of shared suffering into a blood rite between equals. By the final movement he is a clan leader in spite of himself, commanding Rajadi men in a war he tried to prevent, and the performance's arc from fanfare to command measures the tragedy's thesis about inheritance: the feud conscripts even its refusers, and the only exit it leaves is the one the ending takes.

A comparison with the play's Romeo also clarifies what Bhansali discarded. Rosaline vanishes entire; Ram has no prior attachment, only a general reputation with women, so the film loses Shakespeare's point about the speed with which one devotion replaces another, the point Friar Laurence presses when he marvels that "young men's love then lies not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3). The loss is real but affordable, because the film relocates the inconstancy theme into Ram's reputation: a man known for casual desire must prove, to Leela's family and to the audience, that the Holi meeting is a different order of event. His proof is conduct rather than verse, the refusal of revenge after revenge becomes available, the marriage sought in a temple rather than a liaison accepted in hiding. The adaptation trades the play's verbal irony for behavioral evidence, a fair exchange in a medium that trusts faces over soliloquies.

## Leela Sanera: A Juliet With a Gun and a Clan

Juliet enters Shakespeare's play at thirteen, nearly silent in her mother's presence, and grows across five acts into the tragedy's most decisive intelligence: she proposes the marriage, designs the potion plot's acceptance, and drinks first in every sense that matters. Leela enters Bhansali's film already grown into decisiveness. From her first scenes she argues with her mother, rides, shoots, taunts her armed guards, and treats the Sanera code as an inheritance she respects without obeying. Deepika Padukone's performance, honored with the Filmfare Award for Best Actress, builds the heroine on appetite and command in equal measure: Leela desires Ram at sight and says as much with her eyes and her wit before any touch, and when the feud crushes the elopement she does not retreat into a chamber. She succeeds her wounded mother as Sanera chief, negotiates with Ram as an equal sovereign, signs orders, and carries the clan's war on her own shoulders. No major screen Juliet before her had been handed the state as well as the love plot.

The scholar Rosa Maria Garcia Periago, writing on the film's localization and female agency, identifies this promotion as the adaptation's central political act. In Garcia Periago's reading, the film provincializes the play by rooting it in Gujarat's festivals and myths, and the heroine's enlarged agency belongs to the same movement: Leela is at once oppressed by the clan order and resistant to it, and her seizure of power as the next don exposes the order's rules by forcing a woman to operate them. The reading persuades because the film insists on the doubleness. Leela's chieftaincy is genuine power, exercised in council and in command, and it is also a trap: Bhavani exploits her seal and her signature to authorize a massacre of Rajadis that reignites the war at the very moment the two chiefs, who are also the two lovers, are negotiating its end. Agency inside a corrupt machine becomes another instrument of the machine. The play's Juliet faces a simpler cage, a father's will and a forced marriage; the film's Leela faces the modern cage of responsibility without clean hands, and her tragedy is correspondingly adult.

The finger-mutilation scene marks the adaptation's darkest revision of the heroine. When the elopement fails and Leela is dragged home, Dhankor Baa cuts off her daughter's ring finger, punishing the secret marriage at the digit that wears its sign. Shakespeare has no counterpart scene; Capulet's violence against Juliet is verbal, the famous tirade of 3.5 in which "hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" (3.5) pours over a daughter who kneels. Bhansali translates verbal violence into the clan world's bodily idiom, and the translation is defensible on the tragedy's own terms: the play's households maim by word because words are their weapons, while Ranjaar's households maim by hand because the hand is theirs. The scene also rebalances the lovers' suffering. Ram's answering self-wound makes the injury shared, a grim parody of the exchanged ring, and the film shoots the pair's bandaged hands thereafter as the marriage's true certificate, more binding than the temple rite the clans refuse to recognize.

Leela's final choices complete the argument. She does not die by a lover's error, a misdelivered letter, or a potion's timing; the film discards every mechanism by which Shakespeare's Juliet becomes a victim of information. Leela dies by agreement, facing Ram, gun in hand, in full knowledge of the war their survival would restart, one heartbeat before the peace she herself had been negotiating arrives to make the sacrifice unnecessary. The irony is Shakespearean in structure, salvation arriving after the fatal decision, and anti-Shakespearean in agency, since the decision is fully informed except for that single fact. Critics divided over whether the mutual shooting ennobles or melodramatizes the ending, a disagreement taken up in the spectacle discussion below. The position defended in this study is that the scene earns its scale: a heroine established across three hours as the clan system's most capable product can only exit that system by a sovereign act, and the film has spent its length making the sovereignty credible.

Padukone's casting carries its own documented history, reserved for the casting discussion below, but one performance consequence belongs here. The role demands a heroine who can hold a frame against Bhansali's sets, costumes, and crowds, and the notices agreed that Padukone holds it: the trade and broadsheet reviews alike treated the year 2013 as her year, with Ram-Leela as the fourth of four major successes, and Ram-Leela as the performance among them that joined glamour to command. Leela's ghagra cholis, designed by Anju Modi, became the film's signature images, and the spectacle discussion must reckon with the fact that the costume is also characterization: a Sanera princess dressed as a festival banner, whose splendor is her house's propaganda worn on a body the house will later mutilate.


## Dhankor Baa: The Capulet Household Remade as a Matriarchy

Lady Capulet occupies a handful of scenes in the play, delivers the Paris suit, and vanishes from the tragedy's machinery; her husband carries the household's will. Bhansali fuses the parents into a single figure and genders the fusion female: Dhankor Baa, widow, sovereign of the Saneras, feared by both clans, played by Supriya Pathak Kapur in a performance that won the Filmfare Award for Best Supporting Actress. The change rearranges the tragedy's power circuit. Juliet's antagonist at home is a father whose authority is patriarchal and domestic; Leela's antagonist is a mother whose authority is political and military, exercised over gunmen, territory, and a marriage market in which her daughter is the chief asset. When Dhankor Baa arranges Leela's match with a simple-minded NRI bridegroom, the transaction is statecraft: an alliance sealed with a bride, the oldest instrument in the clan repertoire, and her fury at the elopement is the fury of a ruler whose treaty has been stolen, not merely a parent disobeyed.

### How does Dhankor Baa change the Capulet side of the story?

Dhankor Baa converts the Capulet household from a family with a temper into a state with an army. Lady Capulet pleads and reports; Dhankor Baa commands, punishes, negotiates, and finally orders the peace, ruling gunmen, territory, and her daughter's marriage as instruments of one government.

The fusion gives the feud a second sovereign mind equal to the conflict's scale, makes the heroine's rebellion an act of treason as well as disobedience, and lets the ending's reconciliation pass through a mother's recognition rather than a prince's decree, which deepens the tragedy's domestic wound even as its scale turns public.

Pathak's performance builds the sovereign from stillness rather than rant. Dhankor Baa chews betel nut, measures men with a level stare, and delivers sentences of death in the tone of household instruction; the betel-nut crusher in her hand became the film's most quoted prop, a domestic tool carried like a scepter and, in one famous beat, deployed as an instrument of execution. The menace is maternal in texture, which is the point: the film's Gujarat runs its violence through kinship offices, mothers and brothers and sisters-in-law, so that every act of war is also an act of family. Garcia Periago's reading notes the alignment of Dhankor Baa with Capulet's function as much as with Lady Capulet's identity, and the alignment explains the character's doubleness, her tenderness and her mutilation of her own daughter issuing from the same office. A father in the play disowns; a sovereign mother in the film amputates the treason from the body of the house.

The character also carries the ending. When Bhavani's treachery is exposed, the wounded Dhankor Baa kills him herself and orders peace with the Rajadis, a recognition scene with no equivalent in the play, where the reconciliation is spoken over the bodies by fathers who have lost less onstage. Bhansali moves the recognition earlier and gives it to the tragedy's most formidable will, so that the peace is not a stunned reaction to the deaths but a decision that races them and loses by minutes. The lovers shoot each other in ignorance of the order their own diplomacy had finally extracted. Structurally the device mirrors the play's failed letter: a saving message exists, travels, and arrives late. Morally it sharpens the blame, because the message's lateness is measured against a mother's change of heart that the audience has watched happen. The feud's last victim is its own repentance, arriving out of breath.

Around Dhankor Baa the film builds a female court with no Shakespearean precedent: Rasila, the sister-in-law who serves as Leela's confidante and messenger, and Kesar, Meghji's wife on the Rajadi side, whose widowhood after the brother-killing gives the feud a female casualty ledger. Garcia Periago remarks the widows as an addition without counterparts in the play, women whose grief is the feud's interest payment made visible. Rasila performs the Nurse's office, carrying messages, arranging meetings, shielding the lovers, while holding a kinswoman's rank that the servant-Nurse never holds; her risk is therefore dynastic as well as personal, and her later grief, widowed by the same arithmetic that widows Kesar, binds the two houses in a symmetry of bereaved women that the ending's joint cremation merely formalizes. The adaptation's feminism is of this structural kind: not speeches about women, but a redistribution of offices, sovereign, confidante, widow, chief, until the feud's whole administration is female and its costs are female-counted, while the men fire the guns and fall in the earlier reels.

## Bhavani, Kanji, Meghji: The Feud's New Machinery

Every adaptation must solve the play's middle, the machinery by which a secret marriage becomes a public catastrophe. Shakespeare's machinery is temperamental: Mercutio's wit curdles into a duel, Tybalt's honor demands one, and chance, a hot day, a plague on both houses, does the rest. Bhansali keeps the temperaments and adds a conspirator. Bhavani, Leela's cousin, played by Gulshan Devaiah, wants command of the Sanera clan and manufactures the war that will clear his path: he provokes clashes, shoots his own chief Dhankor Baa during the Navratri movement and frames the Rajadis for the shot, and later tricks the newly installed Leela into signing an order that massacres Rajadi men. No figure in the play combines these functions. Bhavani is part Paris, the insider with a claim; part Tybalt, the feud's arsonist; and part a figure Shakespeare distributes elsewhere, the manipulator whose forged or intercepted communications wreck the lovers' plans, kin to the tradition that runs from this play's failed letter to later tragedies' planted evidence.

Purists may object that a villain dilutes the play's tragic mechanism, in which no one needs to conspire because the feud conspires through everyone. The objection deserves a direct answer, and the answer is that Bhansali's film does not in fact rest its catastrophe on Bhavani. The brother-killings precede his largest treacheries and spring from the old arithmetic, Kanji's shot and Ram's answer, with no conspirator required. Bhavani governs the second half's failure of peace, the movement in which Shakespeare relies on the potion's secrecy and the letter's miscarriage, mechanisms at least as contrived as a forger. The difference is one of temperature: Shakespeare's accidents feel like fate because their agents are impersonal, plague and timing, while Bhavani gives the miscarriage a face and a motive. A popular cinema that personifies fate in a villain is making a legible trade, depth of metaphysics for clarity of blame, and the trade is honestly signaled by the character's construction: Devaiah plays him smiling, reasonable, and indispensable, the courtier every clan keeps.

Kanji and Meghji, the exchanged brothers, carry the first half's catastrophe, and their redesign deserves separate emphasis because it alters the tragedy's emotional bookkeeping. Mercutio is the play's beloved outsider, kin to the Prince, bound to the feud by friendship alone, and his death speech curses both houses from a position outside their blood. Meghji cannot curse from outside; he is the Rajadi heir, Ram's elder brother, a husband and a clan commander, and his death at Kanji's hand is a dynastic wound. Kanji likewise is no hired kinsman but Leela's brother, so Ram's revenge killing makes him his wife's brother's killer, a pollution no temple marriage can wash. The play's Romeo kills his cousin-in-law and is banished by law; the film's Ram kills his brother-in-law and is exiled by arithmetic, since every Rajadi who shelters him and every Sanera who spares him must price a brother's corpse into the shelter. The forced separation that follows needs no prince's sentence. The town itself enforces it, courtyard by courtyard.

### What work does Bhavani do that no character in the play performs?

Bhavani manufactures the failure of the peace. Shakespeare wrecks his lovers' plans through accident, a quarantined friar and an undelivered letter, while Bhansali personifies the miscarriage in a cousin who shoots his own chief, frames the enemy clan, and forges the heroine's authority into a massacre order.

The substitution gives the tragedy's second half a legible agent of blame, trades metaphysical fate for conspiracy, and concentrates in one smiling courtier the functions the play scatters across Paris, Tybalt, and chance itself.

The supporting design extends to the Sarpanch, Raza Murad's village authority, whose weakness is the film's civic verdict. Escalus opens the play with a death sentence for further brawling and closes it with judgment; the Sarpanch opens with appeals and closes with absence. Ranjaar's clans settle their own war because no institution exists that could settle it for them, and the lovers' deaths are therefore also a constitutional event, the price of order in a place where order has no other purchaser. Against that bleakness the film sets the joint cremation, a rite performed by the two houses together, as the first act of a state founded on grief. The play ends with promised statues of gold, monuments commissioned by fathers; the film ends with a funeral performed by mothers, widows, and surviving kin, monuments replaced by labor. The substitution is the adaptation in miniature: splendor converted into work, Verona's gilded memorials refused in favor of ash.


## Holi, Navratri, Dussehra: Festivals as Plot Machinery

Shakespeare builds his plot on a calendar of feasts: the Capulet ball, the marriage day converted to a funeral, the "ancient feast" where a masked enemy is recognized. Bhansali rebuilds the calendar from Gujarat's festival year, and the festivals are not interludes between plot movements; they are the plot movements, each one advancing the tragedy a full act. Holi carries the meeting. Ram enters the Sanera house inside the Holi crowd, where colored powder licenses touch, anonymity, and trespass, and the meeting's choreography, pursuit through courtyards amid thrown color, restages the ball's masked recognition with the disguise made communal: at Holi everyone is disguised, so the enemy's son can walk in painted like everyone else. The song of the sequence, Lahu Munh Lag Gaya, sung by Shail Hada, binds color to blood in its governing image, the taste of blood on the lips, so that the festival of color announces the film's thesis in its first celebration: in Ranjaar, pigment and gore are neighboring substances, and the lovers meet inside the announcement.

Navratri carries the middle catastrophe. The nine nights of the goddess bring the Garba, the circular dance around the lamp, and the film's signature number, Nagada Sang Dhol, sung by Shreya Ghoshal and Osman Mir, in which Leela dances at the center of her clan's devotion with a command that doubles as a public coronation. Around the festival the plot's treacheries gather: Ram comes seeking peace, Bhavani shoots Dhankor Baa and frames the Rajadis, and Leela rises to the chieftaincy in her mother's place. The dance's circle is the tragedy's emblem for this movement, a community turning in devotion around a center that violence is about to empty. Dussehra carries the ending. The festival of the victory of good over evil, with its effigies of Ravana raised for burning, supplies the stage for the final confrontation, and the irony is exact and bitter: a festival dramatizing evil's defeat presides over the lovers' mutual killing, while the peace order that would have made the day a genuine victory travels minutes behind the shots. Jonathan Gil Harris, in the reading cited by Garcia Periago, treats the film's festival violence as a pretext structure in which communal celebration and communal killing share one choreography; the observation holds for the whole calendar, and the film's defenders can accept it without surrendering the tragedy, since the play's feasts behave identically, a ball that produces a death sentence and a wedding day that produces a corpse.

Garcia Periago's account stresses the mythologizing pressure the festivals exert: the lovers are framed against Ram and Krishna iconography, Leela's dance aligning her with the goddess's devotees, so that the hypotext, the play beneath the film, is not merely translated but sacralized. Paulomi Sharma's postcolonial reading draws the political corollary: a tragedy rooted in Gujarat's festival calendar and mythic names makes its claim on the audience through belonging, and the claim cuts two ways, dignifying the regional culture as a vessel for the classic while binding the classic to a majoritarian iconography that the title litigation then exposed. Both readings agree on the fact, whatever their valuations: the festivals carry identity as well as plot. A skeptical position, represented among reviewers who found the picture's Gujarat ersatz and studio-bound, holds that the calendar is décor after all, painted on sets that no real town matches. The adjudication favors the scholars on the evidence of structure: remove Holi and the meeting has no cover; remove Navratri and the chieftaincy has no occasion; remove Dussehra and the ending has no stage. Décor can be removed without collapsing a plot. Machinery cannot, and the festivals pass the removal test as machinery three times over.

One festival element deserves separate notice because it folds the title litigation inside the film. During the Dussehra movement, a performance of the Ramlila itself, the traditional enactment of Rama's story, is staged within the fiction, so that Bhansali's lovers move through a fairground where another pair of exalted lovers is being enacted by other hands. Harris's reading, as carried in Garcia Periago's essay, treats that inner performance as a pretext structure for communal violence: the festival that should dramatize the victory of good over evil becomes the ground on which the clans' war resumes, the sacred play and the massacre sharing a stage. The layering is the film's most self-conscious gesture. A picture accused in court of borrowing the Ramlila's name without the Ramlila's content answers, inside its own plot, by staging the Ramlila and showing the feud desecrating it, an argument in images that the sacred frame and the violent story were the film's subject all along rather than its marketing accident. Few adaptations build their legal defense into their third act, and the gesture typifies the production's habit of converting its controversies into material.

The festival structure also solves the play's notorious time problem. Shakespeare compresses his action into some four days, a haste that strains credulity and powers the tragedy's theme of precipitance. Bhansali stretches the action across a festival season, Holi to Navratri to Dussehra, months of screen time in which a courtship can deepen, a separation can harden into rival chieftaincies, and a peace process can plausibly rise and fail. The stretch costs the play's feverish speed and buys social credibility in exchange: the lovers of Ranjaar are not children destroyed by a weekend but adults ground down by a season of governance, grief, and failed negotiation. Critics who missed the play's breathlessness were registering the trade accurately; the defense is that the film's subject has shifted from haste to inheritance, and inheritance takes time to dramatize. The festival calendar is the clock that makes the new subject visible, each feast a dial on which the feud's seasons turn.

## The Soundtrack: Bhansali as Composer, Song as Argument

Bhansali composed the film's songs himself, his most complete exercise of the composer's role on a major production, with lyrics by Siddharth-Garima across a ten-track album. The dual authorship matters to the adaptation question because song in Hindi popular cinema performs the office that soliloquy performs in the play: it suspends the action to expose an interior, and the exposure is public, sung to the community and the audience at once. Juliet's potion speech and Romeo's banishment outcry have no spoken equivalents in Ranjaar; their equivalents are numbers. Laal Ishq, sung by Arijit Singh, carries the lovers' consecration in a classical register, its refrain joining the two names into a single phrase, while Ang Laga De, sung by Aditi Paul and Shail Hada, scores the wedding night with a sensuality the reviews noted as unusually frank for the director. Dhoop, sung by Shreya Ghoshal, gives Leela a solo of waiting and exposure, the heroine alone in sunlight with her desire stated as plainly as any soliloquy states it.

Two numbers anchor the album's fame and the film's argument. Nagada Sang Dhol is the Garba of the Navratri movement, dhol and nagada drums under Ghoshal's and Mir's voices, choreographed as a communal circle with Leela at its devotional center; music reviewers treated it as the album's peak, one leading review rating it the finest of the ten tracks and praising its pure Indian instrumentation against an era of borrowed beats. Lahu Munh Lag Gaya is the Holi number, Shail Hada's voice over a rustic Gujarati arrangement, and its image of blood tasted like color fuses the film's twin substances at the moment of first desire. Around the anchors the album ranges widely: Tattad Tattad as the hero's fanfare; Mor Bani Thanghat Kare, sung by Osman Mir and Aditi Paul, as a folk-styled duet; Ishqyaun Dhishqyaun as a playful courtship number; Poore Chand as a moonlit romantic piece; and Ram Chahe Leela, sung by Bhoomi Trivedi in a folk-rock fusion and filmed on Priyanka Chopra in a special appearance, as the album's deliberate commercial insertion.

### Which numbers do the tragedy's work, and which do the market's?

Nagada Sang Dhol and Lahu Munh Lag Gaya do the tragedy's work: the Garba stages Leela's public sovereignty inside her clan's devotion on the eve of its violation, and the Holi song fuses color with blood at the lovers' first meeting, announcing the film's whole thesis in a refrain.

Laal Ishq and Ang Laga De carry the marriage's interior. Ram Chahe Leela does the market's work, a star item number whose defenders call it clan propaganda sung inside the fiction and whose detractors, among them the Screen Anarchy notice, treat its interruption as the film's most cynical jolt.

The music's critical reception mirrored the film's, admiration for craft braided with suspicion of excess. Reviewers of the album praised the Garba and the classical tracks while rating several numbers as niche or middling, one notice calling the soundtrack a mixed bag whose quality ran high without achieving universal appeal. The mixed verdict is itself evidence for the spectacle adjudication below: even sympathetic ears heard an album serving two masters, the tragedy's emotional architecture and the album market's demand for a dance-floor single and an item number. What distinguishes Ram-Leela's score from a merely commercial compilation is the placement of its peaks. The two numbers every account agrees in praising occur at the plot's two hinges, the meeting and the chieftaincy, so that the soundtrack's best music and the tragedy's essential movements coincide. A score that decorates would scatter its peaks at random; a score that argues concentrates them at the joints, and Bhansali's concentration is exact.

The album reached its audience ahead of the film, in circulation and under review by October 2013, so that the Garba and the Holi song entered the festival season's public life before the picture opened, an unusual case of an adaptation's music preceding its narrative into the culture. The sequencing mattered for reception: audiences arrived at the Navratri sequence already knowing its number as a Garba standard of that season, and the scene plays, in part, as the fulfillment of a song the hall had been dancing to for weeks. Shakespeare's company enjoyed no such instrument; the closest parallel is the play's own ballad echoes, tunes the audience carried in from the street. Bhansali's soundtrack strategy turns the parallel into a system, seeding the tragedy's hinges in the audience's ears before the hinges turn.

Instrumentation reinforces the argument. The album leans on dhol, nagada, shehnai-like reeds, folk percussion, and Gujarati melodic turns, with the fusion elements confined largely to the item number and the hero's fanfare. The soundscape therefore localizes the tragedy at the level of the ear: a viewer who closed the eyes would still know the story had moved to Gujarat, because the drums of Navratri and the folk refrains do the same work as the costumes and the sets. Global Shakespeare debates often ask whether an adaptation's local identity survives translation into a world medium; in Ram-Leela the identity is carried by the least translatable element of all, a regional festival music that resists export while the images travel. The film's international notices, even the hostile ones, remark the music's force, which suggests the resistance is survivable: rhythm crosses borders that lyrics cannot.


## The Built World: Design, Camera, and Costume in Ram-Leela

Ranjaar is a constructed place, and its construction is an argument. Production designer Wasiq Khan built a town of painted havelis, carved balconies, temple steps, dye-works, and gun stalls arranged for processions, and cinematographer Ravi Varman, in his first collaboration with the director, photographed the construction in saturated reds, oranges, and indigos that reviewers across the spectrum treated as the film's signature even when they resisted everything else. Masand's notice, for all its reservations, praised the indelible moving images, from the song sequences to a chase and a religious procession's burst of color. The Screen Anarchy reviewer, hostile to the whole enterprise, still conceded the most stupefyingly gorgeous Shakespeare adaptation on a cinema screen. When a film's detractors and champions agree on the look, the look is a fact, and the critical task becomes explaining what the look does rather than whether it exists.

What the look does is translate the play's heraldry. Shakespeare's households announce themselves through liveries, masks, and the semiotics of the ball; a stage audience reads allegiance at sight. Bhansali's clans announce themselves through palette and textile: the Sanera and Rajadi worlds are color-coded in costume and décor, so that any crowd shot reads as a map of the feud, and the lovers' meetings register visually as incursions of one palette into another. Costume designer Anju Modi's work for Padukone became the film's most reproduced imagery, the mirror-worked ghagra cholis of the Garba and the festival sequences, and the imagery is characterization at the level of cloth. Leela is dressed as her house's banner, splendid because the Saneras display their wealth and defiance on their women, and the mutilation of her finger lands with extra force against the memory of the hands that danced. Ram's dress runs the opposite argument: the hero's whites and open shirts set him against both clans' heraldic weight, a man dressed for leisure in a town dressed for war, until the second half darkens his palette as command settles on him. Costume plots the tragedy before dialogue explains it.

The camera's treatment of violence completes the design's argument. Gunfire in Ranjaar is choreographed with the same musicality as the dances, volleys cut on rhythm, celebrations punctuated by shots fired into the air, so that the audience's body learns the film's thesis somatically: in this town the dance and the firefight are one gesture with two intentions. Critics who found the violence decorative were responding to that choreography, and the response is understandable; choreographed violence risks pleasure where the story needs cost. The film's answer is bookkeeping. Every choreographed volley is entered in the corpse ledger that the plot keeps with unusual care, brothers counted, widows named, funerals shown, until the final movement's plain staging collects the debt the choreography incurred. The design thereby implicates the audience in the spectacle quarrel that the next section adjudicates: pleasure taken in the film's beauty is pleasure taken in the feud's own self-display, the pageantry by which two clans persuade themselves that five hundred years of killing is a heritage rather than a crime. The lovers are the only figures who see the pageantry from outside, and their education in seeing it is the plot.

A word belongs to the film's dialogue, since design in Bhansali includes the verbal surface. The screenplay by the director with Siddharth-Garima writes in a heightened aphoristic Hindi, proverb-shaped taunts, boasts, and declarations that trade realism of speech for memorability, line by quotable line. Reviewers divided over the register as they divided over the sets, some hearing poetry and others hearing filmi declamation, and the division replays the realism quarrel in miniature. The play licenses the heightening: its Veronese speak in sonnets, conceits, and set speeches, a vernacular nobody in Italy ever spoke, and the tragedy's register has always been ceremonial. Ranjaar's aphorisms are the local equivalent of Verona's conceits, speech burnished for public occasions in a culture where honor is transacted in public phrases. When the registers drop, at the brother's corpse and at the final meeting, the plainness registers as shock, which is how ceremonial worlds mark sincerity.

## Spectacle Versus the Tragic Core: The Complication Adjudicated

The brief for this study names the complication directly: spectacle against the tragic core. The film's reception staged the quarrel in its purest form, with verdicts spanning the full scale. Meena Iyer in the Times of India awarded five stars and praised the director's impudence in resetting the tale in Gujarat with the moment's most bankable pair. Taran Adarsh awarded four and a half stars and called the picture Bhansali's most commercial film, a compliment inside trade logic. Rajeev Masand awarded three and a half, praising the passion, the leads' chemistry, and the genuine feeling, while protesting that the second half bloats and bears down on patience. Shubhra Gupta in the Indian Express admired the mounted spectacle and the love story's build, then faulted the director's old failing, an insistence on perfect choreography that will not let a messy story have messy emotions, will not let the heroine's tears streak her cheeks. Raja Sen at Rediff awarded a single star and dismissed the picture as an over-plotted bloody mess, in the summary carried by the review roundups. Sudhish Kamath in The Hindu occupied the interesting middle, arguing that the film's borrowings from the play are loose while its form is diegetically Indian, a judgment that grants the adaptation its method while withholding full surrender. Abroad, the Screen Anarchy notice called the picture perhaps the most stupefyingly gorgeous Shakespeare adaptation on a cinema screen and then charged it with forfeiting narrative flesh for visual flash, wasting its opportunities in loud music and caricature.

A spread from one star to five is not noise; it is a disagreement about what a film of this kind is for. The one-star and five-star verdicts apply incompatible standards. Sen's standard is narrative economy: a plot known in advance must justify its retelling by momentum and surprise, and by that standard a three-hour festival pageant with a foreknown ending is indefensible. Iyer's and Adarsh's standard is experiential amplitude: the retelling justifies itself by the intensity of what it makes an audience feel inside a known frame, and by that standard the pageant is the point. Masand's and Gupta's middle verdicts accept amplitude and tax its excesses, the stretched second half, the choreography that polishes grief. The adjudication defended here sides with the middle against both poles, for reasons grounded in the play itself. Shakespeare's tragedy is also a spectacle that retells a known story: the Prologue gives away the ending in fourteen lines, the source tale was famous, and the play's pleasures are amplifications inside a foreknown frame, a ball, a balcony, a tomb staged for maximum feeling. A standard that condemns Ram-Leela for amplitude would condemn its source on the same count. Yet the middle's taxes are also just, and the film pays them in this study's accounting: the second half repeats confrontations the first half had already priced, and Ram Chahe Leela interrupts the tragedy's tightening noose for a star turn whose in-fiction defense, a song about Ram's desire performed inside a clan celebration, persuades only in part.

The deeper question is whether the spectacle ever displaces the core, and the test is the ending. A film intoxicated by its surfaces would soften the close, rescue the lovers, or drown the deaths in choreography. Ram-Leela does none of these. The mutual shooting is staged plainly against the Dussehra machinery, the lovers in each other's arms in the water as the festival burns behind them, and the peace order's lateness is given in dialogue rather than pageant. The joint cremation that follows is the film's most restrained sequence, communal labor instead of display. Set against that restraint, the earlier splendor reads retrospectively as the tragedy's stakes made visible: the audience has been shown, in color and drum and dance, exactly the world whose continuation the lovers' deaths purchase and whose beauty the feud was consuming. Verona's gold statues are the play's version of the same transaction, splendor spent on the dead. Bhansali spends the splendor on the living world first, so that its loss can be measured. That is not spectacle against the core. That is spectacle as the core's evidence, with lapses of proportion taxed where the record shows them.

One more strand of the quarrel concerns realism. Several notices, Gupta's among them, observed that the film's Gujarat is a designed elsewhere, the Rann and the haveli sets arranged with not a thread out of place, and treated the artifice as a failure of grounding. The charge assumes that a folk tragedy should look like a location documentary, an assumption the play's own stage never honored: Shakespeare's Verona was a London stage with Italian names, and the tragedy's power has never depended on municipal accuracy. Bhansali's sets declare their theatricality the way a stage set does, and the declaration licenses the film's heightened register, its aphoristic dialogue, its festival geometry, its lovers who speak and move at the pitch of opera. Judged as realism the film is ersatz; judged as staged tragedy the artifice is the medium. The international reviewer who called the picture gorgeous and hollow and the Indian reviewer who called it choreographed to a fault are both, in the end, describing the same surface from opposed premises about what the surface is for. The premise defended here is the play's own: a chorus walks out first and announces that the story is a performance, and everything after is judged by whether the performance makes the foreknown deaths hurt. On that test the film passes, and the hurt is the verdict that matters.

Duration deserves a sentence of its own in the accounting. At roughly two and a half hours the picture asks more patience than the play's two hours of stage traffic, and the bloat Masand and others taxed concentrates in the separation movement, where parallel chieftaincies generate councils and confrontations that restate the deadlock. The defense available, that governance must be shown to be believed, covers some of the length; the remainder is the director's known indulgence, the same indulgence the Express notice diagnosed in the un-streaked tears. This study's verdict therefore carries a qualification rather than a pardon: the film's greatness is real and its excess is real, and the excess is located in duration and in one ornamental number, not in the conception. Conception is where adaptations live or die, and the conception, Verona rebuilt as an arms-trading festival town ruled in part by women, is the boldest mainstream Indian reading of the play on record.


## The Title Fight: Ram-Leela Becomes Goliyon Ki Raasleela

The film was announced and long known simply as Ram-Leela, the lovers' names hyphenated into a single word that any Indian ear also hears as Ramlila, the traditional enactment of the life of Lord Rama performed across North India during the festival season. Religious groups and a Ram Leela committee carried the resemblance to court, arguing that a film of guns, desire, and clan violence wearing that title misled the public and offended religious sentiment. The Delhi High Court stayed the release on 12 November 2013, three days before the scheduled opening. Bhansali answered with a public clarification on 13 November, stating that the title named his characters, that the film was inspired and based on Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet, that it related neither to the Ramlila folk tradition nor to the Raasleela of Krishna, and that his Ram depicted Lord Rama in no manner. The title was then expanded to Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela, a dance of bullets prefixed to the names, and the stay was lifted in time for the film to open on its fixed date, 15 November. The Lucknow bench of the Allahabad High Court nevertheless banned the film in Uttar Pradesh on 21 November, a week into its run, and protests flickered in several cities even after the renaming.

The episode belongs in an adaptation study for two reasons beyond its color. First, the litigation forced the film's Shakespearean basis into a legal record: the director's clarification is among the most formal statements of source any Bollywood adaptation has made, entered not in a press kit but in answer to a court, and it corroborates the opening titles' plain credit. The film's debt to the play is thus documented at three levels, the credits, the publicity, and the sworn public clarification, a chain of attribution with few parallels in popular cinema anywhere. Second, the controversy rehearsed, in the streets and the courts, the exact tension the scholars describe inside the film: the names Ram and Leela carry devotional resonance whether the director disclaims it or not, and an audience's claim on its icons collided with a filmmaker's claim on his characters. Paulomi Sharma's postcolonial reading gains a documentary footnote here, since the politics of reimagining the play in a postcolonial nation turned out to include a courtroom chapter in which the nation argued back.

A parallel dispute adjusted the film's ethnography. The Kshatriya community objected to the use of real community names for the clans, and the names carried in early production, Jadeja and Rabari, were replaced by the invented Sanera and Rajadi. The substitution is a small fact with a large implication for the adaptation's method: Bhansali's Gujarat is deliberately denatured at the points where real communities could claim injury, while remaining specific in festival, dress, music, and speech. Ranjaar is therefore a composite, a town built from Gujarat's materials and severed from Gujarat's map, which is why the realism charge discussed earlier misfires. The film never promised a documentary Gujarat; its legal history shows a production negotiating, name by name, the boundary between a regional imagination and a regional claim, and settling the boundary on the imagination's side by inventing its clans and its town outright.

The Uttar Pradesh ban, imposed a week into the run by the Lucknow bench, deserves its footnote in the commercial record as well: the film lost a major territory's screens during its opening fortnight and still crossed its thresholds, which trade commentators noted at the time as evidence of the picture's hold in its remaining circuits. The episode completes the title fight's paradox. Litigation meant to shrink the film's name and reach instead certified its source in a public record, advertised its defiance across the press, and left its marquee more descriptive than the one the producers had chosen. The courts became, against their petitioners' intentions, the adaptation's first scholarly apparatus, forcing the questions of source and naming that the essays later took up at leisure.

The prefixed title, adopted under duress, arguably improved the film's self-description. Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela announces method before names: a dance, of bullets, then the lovers. The Shakespearean parallel is exact. The play's early audiences knew it by a title that advertised its matter, the excellent conceited tragedy of two lovers, and the film's final title performs the same advertising in Hindi, genre first, lovers second. Few legal defeats in cinema history have produced a more accurate marquee.

## The Casting Change: Kareena Kapoor Out, Deepika Padukone In

The Leela who reached the screen was not the Leela first cast. Kareena Kapoor was set to play the role opposite Ranveer Singh, with sets built and the start of filming days away, when she exited the production. Accounts of the exit agree on the timing, roughly a week to ten days before the cameras rolled, and differ in coloring: Kapoor stated in a 2013 interview that she changed her mind and chose another film, Gori Tere Pyaar Mein, instead, saying plainly that the decision was hers and that she had missed her chance with Bhansali. Singh, recounting the episode a decade later in a televised conversation, said the set stood ready and that Kapoor had to exit for reasons he left unspecified, whereupon he urged Bhansali and the assistants to consider Deepika Padukone, whose performance in Cocktail had just drawn his attention, and Padukone was cast. The record supports the sequence and the vacancy's suddenness; beyond the principals' own statements, motive belongs to gossip and is excluded here.

The counterfactual is irresistible and should be resisted only after its terms are stated. Kapoor in 2012 was the bigger star, a decade into marquee standing, and her Leela would have carried a different charge: an established sovereign of the industry opposite a leading man four films into his career, with an age and authority gradient between them that the film would have had to absorb. Padukone, twenty-seven during filming and Singh's near contemporary, made the lovers a matched pair of near-equals in age and in ascent, and the film's erotic and political symmetry, two heirs, two sovereigns in waiting, depends on the match. Her casting also completed the year's arc that the trade press chronicled, a sequence of four major releases in 2013 with Ram-Leela as the dramatic peak, and it began the Singh-Padukone screen partnership that Bhansali would extend in later productions. Garcia Periago's agency reading is inseparable from the performance that embodies it: Leela's command reads as command because Padukone plays authority without apology, and the notices that treated the role as a career summit for its performer were ratifying the substitution the production had improvised under deadline pressure.

Singh's own casting carried no such drama, and his Ram had been designed around his volatility from the start. The supporting cast assembled a repertory of strong character players, Pathak and Chadda and Kelkar and Devaiah and Abhimanyu Singh, whose ensemble weight gives the clans their density; a feud between two families needs faces the audience can track across councils and funerals, and the film's middle sections hold because the bench is deep. Chopra's single-song appearance, announced as a special appearance, belongs in the spectacle ledger as taxed earlier. The casting history's final lesson is about contingency in adaptation: the most consequential interpretive choice in the Indian Romeo and Juliet of its decade, the heroine who makes its feminism legible, arrived by emergency substitution a week before the shoot. Adaptations are argued in essays as if they were theorems. Their records keep showing deadlines, vacancies, and a leading man's hunch after a rival film's screening, and the theorem got proved anyway.


## The Elopement and the Two Chieftaincies: The Separation Movement

Between the brother-killings and the ending, the film builds the movement with no counterpart in the play, and its invention explains both the picture's length and its difference in kind from a filmed Romeo and Juliet. The lovers elope and marry in a temple, are tracked through Ram's own friends, who betray the hiding place after drink loosens their loyalty, and are dragged apart by Leela's kin. Dhankor Baa's punishment, the severed ring finger, and Ram's answering self-wound follow, and then the film performs its boldest structural act: it separates the lovers not by exile across a border, as Mantua separates Romeo, but by promotion inside one town. Ram takes command of the Rajadis in his dead brother's place; Leela, after Bhavani's bullet fells her mother, takes command of the Saneras. The final act's antagonists are therefore the lovers themselves, each governing a war machine, each negotiating with the other across a council table where every concession is priced against a brother's corpse and a clan's watching honor.

The invention converts the play's private separation into a public one and changes the tragedy's political physics. Shakespeare's separated lovers are powerless: Romeo waits on news, Juliet waits on a potion, and their tragedy is that the world's machinery operates without them. Bhansali's separated lovers hold the machinery's levers and discover that holding them changes nothing, because the machines run on constituencies: Ram cannot concede without his clan reading surrender, Leela cannot concede without hers reading the same, and Bhavani farms both readings for his own ascent. The film thus relocates the tragedy's fatalism from fortune to politics. Nothing supernatural or accidental prevents the peace; the peace is prevented by audiences, by the clans' rank and file whose honor both chiefs must carry, a democratic fatalism for a democratic century's popular cinema. Garcia Periago's agency reading reaches its sharpest point in this movement: Leela's power is genuine, and its genuineness is the trap, since the signature Bhavani steals is powerful precisely because her authority is real.

The movement also gives the film its most Shakespearean irony, one the play achieves by different means. In 4.1 Juliet kneels to her father in feigned obedience while planning the potion, a double agent inside her own house. Leela plays the inverse figure, a sincere sovereign whose sincerity is exploited, signing in good faith the order her cousin has poisoned. Ram across the table signs his own escalations in the same good faith. The lovers' tragedy in this movement is not deception practiced or suffered but competence itself: two able rulers, honestly negotiating, produce a war neither wants, because the system they govern prices peace as treason and both are too dutiful to default. When the ending releases them from office by the only resignation the system accepts, the logic has been fully prepared. The play's lovers die because a letter fails; the film's die because governance succeeds at everything except mercy, and the substitution is the adaptation's deepest claim about its own society, argued in councils and signatures rather than in speeches.

## The Ending: A Double Death Restaged, a Peace Arriving Late

Shakespeare's ending is a machinery of error: a plague-quarantined friar, a letter undelivered, a potion timed to the hour, a husband arriving minutes early at a tomb. Bhansali discards the entire machinery and rebuilds the catastrophe from the film's own materials, the feud's arithmetic and the lovers' sovereignty. Ram and Leela, each having buried a brother, each commanding a clan armed for a Dussehra war the peace talks have failed to prevent, meet and choose the only act that can stop the mobilization their own survival would trigger: they shoot each other, together, by agreement, and fall into the water as the festival fires rise. Neither knows that Dhankor Baa, having killed the traitor Bhavani with her own hand, has ordered the peace the lovers had been negotiating. The clans then do together the one act left to them, cremating the pair in a joint rite, the feud's first cooperative enterprise in five hundred years.

The correspondences with 5.3 are structural and exact. The play's lovers also die in ignorance of a fact that would have saved them, Juliet's living sleep; the film's lovers die in ignorance of a fact that would have saved them, the peace order. In both, the audience holds the saving fact while the lovers act without it, and the dramatic irony, not the mechanics, is the tragedy's engine. Bhansali's substitution of an informed mutual act for Shakespeare's sequence of error and discovery raises the lovers from victims of information to authors of the ending, at the price of the tomb scene's terrible misunderstandings. Paromita Chakravarti, reviewing the Indian Romeo and Juliet films together in Shakespeare Bulletin, describes the death as an act of consummation, the lovers taking their last minutes for embrace and desire before the shots, and the description captures the scene's operatic logic: the Liebestod pattern, love completed in a shared death chosen as union, which the wider tradition of [love and death entwined in the Liebestod](/2024/01/05/love-and-death-liebestod-analysis/) traces from the Tristan legend through the operatic stage. Ram-Leela's ending belongs knowingly to that lineage. Its lovers do not stumble into death; they consummate with it, and the film's frank eroticism, so often taxed as indulgence, reveals itself at the close as preparation: a pair whose desire has been public, mutual, and unashamed from the Holi meeting can plausibly choose a death in the same register.

Fidelity questions usually ask whether an adaptation keeps the ending. The sharper question is whether the adaptation keeps the ending's cause. Shakespeare's cause is haste compounded by secrecy: every disaster in the play is a message delayed, a plan half-shared, a truth arriving after its hour. Bhansali's cause is inheritance compounded by honor: every disaster is a debt collected, a signature honored, a brother answered for. Both plays of cause produce the same final configuration, two young bodies and a room, or a ground, full of elders doing arithmetic over them, and the film's elders speak the play's closing note in a different idiom, labor instead of statues, cremation instead of monuments, grief performed with the hands. The Prince's verdict that "all are punish'd" (5.3) hangs over Ranjaar without a prince to say it, and the omission is the film's coldest judgment on its world: a town that must punish itself because no authority survived to do the punishing.

The lovers' last exchange of looks across the Dussehra ground also completes the film's visual argument about gazes. Ram and Leela meet through a crowd's sightlines at Holi, negotiate across council tables as rival chiefs, and die facing each other with no intermediary left alive who could have carried a message between them. The trajectory from crowd to council to solitude is the feud's biography in three framings, and the final framing, two figures and water and fire, strips the spectacle to its minimum at the moment of maximum consequence. Directors of spectacle are often accused of being unable to stop; the accusation fails at this film's close, where the stopping is the meaning.

## Critics and Scholars: The Disagreement Adjudicated

Three kinds of authority have judged the film, and the brief for this study asks for one of each: a scholar of Indian cinema, a critic of global Shakespeare, and an editor of the play. Their positions, set side by side, produce the disagreement this section adjudicates. Tejaswini Ganti, the anthropologist of the Mumbai film industry whose account of Bollywoodization names the industry's standard operations, inclusion of songs, addition of emotion, and expansion of the narrative, supplies the framework Garcia Periago applies to the film: by Ganti's measures Ram-Leela is a complete Bollywoodization of its source, the play reworked through every operation the industry performs. Paulomi Sharma, reading the film beside Ek Duuje Ke Liye in Literature/Film Quarterly, argues that the reimagining's politics are postcolonial: the Shakespearean story is indigenized, its location torn away from the metropolis and planted in a regional Gujarat, and the appropriation demonstrates both the playwright's global cultural capital and Hindi commercial cinema's assimilative power, while the mythic naming loads the lovers with a Hindu iconography that gives the tragedy a majoritarian frame. Garcia Periago, the global Shakespeare critic of the three, concurs on localization and adds the female-agency axis: the film provincializes the play, mythologizes its source through festival and divinity parallels, and redistributes power to its women until the adaptation becomes a reading of modern India's own tensions, with the heroine's rise balanced against the widows' grief and the item number's objectified dancer, agency and oppression held in one frame.

The editor's bench contributes the source-side standard. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition of the play, the standard modern scholarly text, establishes the feud's textual thinness: the play gives its grudge no origin, its Prince no name beyond office, its households no interior economy, blanks that every production and adaptation must fill or expose. Measured against the edited text, Bhansali's fillings, an arms economy, a five-hundred-year ledger, a sovereign mother, are not decorations on a complete design but completions of an incomplete one, licensed by the text's own silences. Where the editor's standard bites is at the potion plot: the edited text's machinery of feigned death and failed message is intricate and deliberate, and an adaptation that discards it entire owes a replacement of equal structural quality. Bhavani's forgeries and the peace order's lateness pay that debt in this study's judgment, as argued in the machinery discussion, though the payment is made in a coarser currency, conspiracy rather than contingency.

The disagreement to adjudicate is therefore triangular. Ganti's framework, neutral in itself, lets the film's champions call its operations mastery and its detractors call them formula. Sharma's politics warn that the indigenization carries a communal frame, the festivals and divine names privileging one community's iconography in a story sold as universal. Garcia Periago's reading answers that the frame is interrogated from inside, by the heroine's agency and the widows' presence, even as it concedes the item number as an unreconciled remainder. The verdict reached here accepts Sharma's warning as the heaviest cost on the film's ledger and Garcia Periago's answer as sufficient but not total: the iconography is majoritarian, the interrogation is real, and the remainder, one ornamental number and a heroine's splendor packaged for the market, stands unreconciled in the manner of most popular art that argues with its own conditions. Against the hostile reviewers, the scholarly record converges on a point the one-star notices missed: three independent scholarly treatments, in Literature/Film Quarterly, in Adaptation, and in Shakespeare Bulletin's review pages, found the film worth sustained argument within a few years of release, a distinction no merely decorative adaptation earns. Scholarship does not grade on stars, but it votes with attention, and its vote is in.


## Box Office and Awards: The Trade Record

The trade record settles the commercial question in the film's favor, with the usual caution that Indian box-office figures are trade estimates compiled from distributor and exhibitor reporting rather than audited accounts. Bollywood Hungama's lifetime tables give the picture an India net collection of Rs 116.33 crore against a reported budget of Rs 88 crore, with an India gross of Rs 161.57 crore, an overseas gross of Rs 45.76 crore, and a worldwide gross of Rs 207.33 crore, and the site's verdict line reads HIT. Other trade tallies place the worldwide total in a band around that figure: the compilation carried by Koimoi lists Rs 218.07 crore worldwide, while the figure of Rs 201.4 crore circulates in reference summaries drawing on Box Office India reporting. The spread is typical of the era's accounting and should be reported as a spread: a domestic net comfortably past the Rs 100 crore mark that the industry treated as its headline threshold, an opening day of Rs 16 crore and a first week of Rs 82.15 crore in Bollywood Hungama's day-wise tables, and a worldwide gross somewhere between roughly Rs 200 crore and Rs 218 crore. On any tally inside the band the film was a clear commercial success, the year's fifth-highest-grossing Indian production in the common rankings, and the director's largest hit to that point in his career, achieved with a tragedy whose ending the entire audience knew in advance.

The commercial fact carries an interpretive consequence often missed in the spectacle quarrel. The hostile notices treated the film's amplitude as a director's self-indulgence that the market would punish; the market instead rewarded the amplitude at blockbuster scale, in a year crowded with star vehicles, for a picture with no rescuable ending and a heroine who spends its second half governing a clan. Popularity does not prove quality, but it does disprove the specific charge that the spectacle was a private language. The audience that made the film a hit was the audience the festivals and the Garba and the Gujarati folk instrumentation addressed, and the receipts record that the address landed. An adaptation's Indian identity is sometimes discussed as a scholarly attribution; here it is also a box-office datum, the regional frame functioning as the film's broadest appeal rather than its niche.

The awards record is narrower and more pointed. At the Filmfare Awards the film earned eight nominations, including Best Film and Best Director, and won three: Best Actress for Padukone, Best Supporting Actress for Pathak, and the choreography award for the dance direction. The pattern of the wins ratifies this study's reading with unusual precision. The academy's voters passed over the director and the picture while honoring the two performances that carry the adaptation's boldest revisions, the heroine promoted to sovereign and the mother promoted to chief, and the craft award went to the choreography, the festival machinery itself. Prizes are not arguments, but when a prize list independently selects the same elements a critical reading selects, the convergence is worth recording: the industry's own jury located the film's distinction exactly where the scholars located it, in the women and the dances.

Supriya Pathak's supporting win deserves its clause. Dhankor Baa is a character class Hindi cinema usually reserves for male stars, the clan don whose word disposes of lives, and the award registered the performance's occupation of that class without softening. Padukone's lead win, in a year when her four releases could have split any vote, registered Leela as the year's defining heroine. Between them the two trophies describe the film's center of gravity more honestly than its poster, which sold a romance. The romance is real; the film is a power tragedy wearing one, and its honors knew it.

## Verdict: Ram-Leela and the Indian Romeo and Juliet

The series thesis running through these adaptation studies holds that the cliché keeps Verona Italian, as if the tragedy's meaning were stored in its geography. Ram-Leela is the thesis's strongest mainstream exhibit, because the film demonstrates that the geography was never the meaning. Move the feud to a Gujarati arms town, give the Capulet house a mother for a sovereign, promote the heroine to chief, replace the potion with a forged signature and the letter with a peace order minutes late, score the whole with Garba drums and a Holi song about the taste of blood, and the tragedy not only survives but sharpens: its account of inheritance, of debts collected from children for quarrels they did not start, reads in Ranjaar with a documentary force that period Verona cannot supply to a modern audience. The film's final image of two houses performing one funeral is the play's closing reconciliation translated into labor, and it lands harder than gold statues because the audience has watched the hands that carry the biers spend the whole film loading guns.

Placement inside the Indian tradition clarifies the achievement's scale. The same years produced leaner regional and metropolitan versions of the feud story, the Banaras-set Issaq and the small-town Ishaqzaade among them, each translating the lovers into a local economy of violence at a fraction of the budget; [the U.P. versions in Issaq and Ishaqzaade](/2029/01/26/issaq-ishaqzaade-bollywood/) repay study as the tradition's gritty wing. Ram-Leela is the tradition's maximalist wing, and the fuller survey of [the Bollywood Romeo and Juliet tradition](/2029/02/02/bollywood-romeo-and-juliet-tradition/) sets the film at that tradition's peak, the point where budget, stardom, scholarship, and box office converge on a single title. Maximalism has its taxed costs, recorded in this study without flinching: a second half that repeats its deadlock, an item number that serves the market before the story, a majoritarian iconography that the film interrogates without escaping. None of the costs reaches the conception. The conception is that Romeo and Juliet was always, among other things, a story about an arms race between households, a marriage market administered by parents, and a peace purchased with children's bodies, and that Gujarat's festival calendar and clan politics could say all three in a native grammar. That grammar is the film's gift back to the play: productions and readers return to Verona after Ranjaar hearing the swords differently, as weapons in an economy, and hearing Lady Capulet's few lines as the silence of a woman the story should have feared.

The film's afterlife confirms the placement. Bhansali reunited his two leads in Bajirao Mastani in 2015 and Padmaavat in 2018, building an unofficial trilogy on the pairing that Ram-Leela improvised under deadline pressure, and the later pictures' scale and reception rest on the foundation the feud film laid: a director, two stars, and an audience taught to read their meetings as events. Within the adaptation's own afterlife, the scholarly file has kept growing, the Literature/Film Quarterly and Adaptation essays joined by review coverage in Shakespeare Bulletin and by a steady stream of comparative studies in Indian journals, a dossier no other Hindi Romeo and Juliet has accumulated. The film has become the reference point against which the tradition's other members, the U.P. versions included, are measured, which is the practical definition of the peak the survey article assigns it.

Bhansali's film therefore stands as the verdict this series keeps approaching from different angles: fidelity is a property of structure and consequence, not of costume or tongue. The picture changes nearly every surface of its source and keeps nearly every load-bearing wall, and where it changes a wall, the brothers for the friend and cousin, the mother for the father, the chosen death for the botched one, the change argues with the play rather than drifting from it. An adaptation that argues is the only kind worth a sixteen-thousand-word study, and among the screen Romeos and Juliets of its decade, in any country, Ram-Leela is the one that argues hardest and, on the evidence assembled here, argues best.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela based on?

The film is based on Romeo and Juliet by William Shakespeare, and it says so itself. The opening titles declare the source, the publicity repeated the declaration, and director Sanjay Leela Bhansali confirmed the basis in a public statement issued during the title litigation of November 2013, clarifying that the picture was inspired and based on Shakespeare's work. The adaptation keeps the play's architecture, two feuding households, a meeting at a festival gathering, kin-killings, an elopement, a forced separation, and a double death followed by reconciliation, while replacing the setting, the language, and much of the machinery with Gujarati counterparts.

### Q: Who directed Ram-Leela and who plays Ram and Leela?

Sanjay Leela Bhansali directed the film, shared the screenplay credit with the writing team Siddharth-Garima, and composed the songs. Ranveer Singh plays Ram Rajadi, the younger son of the Rajadi clan's leadership, and Deepika Padukone plays Leela Sanera, daughter of the Sanera matriarch Dhankor Baa. Supriya Pathak Kapur plays Dhankor Baa, Richa Chadda plays Rasila, Leela's sister-in-law and confidante, Sharad Kelkar plays Leela's brother Kanji, Abhimanyu Singh plays Ram's elder brother Meghji, and Gulshan Devaiah plays Bhavani, the cousin whose treachery drives the second half. Priyanka Chopra appears in a special appearance in one song.

### Q: Where is Ram-Leela set and what do the Rajadi and Sanera clans trade in?

The film is set in Ranjaar, a fictional town in Gujarat invented for the story, and the clans trade in arms and ammunition. Ranjaar's open economy is the manufacture and sale of guns, displayed and handled with everyday casualness, so the feud between the Rajadi and Sanera houses is an industry as well as an honor code. The setting replaces Shakespeare's Verona, where the feud is a habit of brawling households, with a town whose livelihood depends on the weapons the feud consumes, giving the violence a material basis that explains why neither clan can simply stop fighting.

### Q: How long have the Rajadi and Sanera clans been feuding in Ram-Leela?

The film dates the feud at five hundred years. No character can name its origin, and no living memory reaches its first cause; the clans keep ledgers of killings instead, each death entered as payment against an earlier death. The number enlarges Shakespeare's "ancient grudge" from the Prologue into a condition of nature, older than the town's institutions and immune to negotiation for most of the story. The antiquity also frames the ending: when the two houses finally cooperate, in cremating the lovers, the act closes a half-millennium of arithmetic in a single rite.

### Q: Why was the film's title changed before release?

The film was first titled Ram-Leela, the lovers' names, but religious groups and a Ram Leela committee argued in court that the title misled audiences by echoing Ramlila, the traditional enactment of Lord Rama's life, and that a film of guns and desire offended religious sentiment under that name. The Delhi High Court stayed the release on 12 November 2013. Bhansali clarified publicly that the title named his characters and that the film derived from Shakespeare, and the title was expanded to Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela, a dance of bullets, after which the stay lifted and the film opened on schedule on 15 November.

### Q: Who was cast as Leela before Deepika Padukone?

Kareena Kapoor was cast as Leela and exited the production roughly a week to ten days before filming began, with sets already built. Kapoor stated in a 2013 interview that she changed her mind and chose the film Gori Tere Pyaar Mein instead. Ranveer Singh later recounted that, after the exit, he proposed Deepika Padukone to Bhansali and the assistants, having admired her performance in Cocktail, and Padukone was cast. The substitution shaped the finished film: Padukone and Singh, near contemporaries, play the lovers as matched heirs, and her performance won the Filmfare Award for Best Actress.

### Q: How do Ram and Leela meet in the film?

Ram and Leela meet during Holi at the Sanera house. Ram enters the enemy stronghold inside the festival crowd, where thrown color licenses trespass and disguise, and the pair recognize each other across the celebration unfolding in the courtyards. The meeting restages the Capulet ball of the play, at which Romeo enters masked among enemies and is recognized by voice, with the masquerade made communal: at Holi everyone is painted, so an enemy's son can walk in openly. The sequence is scored by Lahu Munh Lag Gaya, a song whose image of blood on the lips fuses the festival's colors with the feud's violence from the first encounter.

### Q: What happens to Meghji and Kanji in Ram-Leela?

Each lover loses a brother to the feud. Kanji, Leela's brother, kills Meghji, Ram's elder brother and the Rajadi heir, and Ram then kills Kanji in revenge, becoming his wife's brother's killer. The exchange tightens Shakespeare's machinery: in the play, Romeo loses his friend Mercutio and kills Juliet's cousin Tybalt, while Bhansali converts both deaths into brother-killings, so the bereavement falls inside both families and the blood debt binds the lovers themselves. The killings end the elopement's brief peace, force the lovers' separation, and load every later negotiation with a corpse on each side of the table.

### Q: Who is Bhavani and what does he do to keep the feud alive?

Bhavani, played by Gulshan Devaiah, is Leela's cousin, a Sanera insider who wants command of the clan and manufactures the war that would clear his path. He provokes clashes between the clans, shoots his own chief Dhankor Baa during the Navratri movement and frames the Rajadis for the shooting, and later tricks the newly installed chief Leela into signing an order that massacres Rajadi men, reigniting the war at the moment peace was near. He has no single counterpart in the play; his functions gather the play's scattered wrecking devices, the duel's provocation and the failed message, into one conspirator, whom Dhankor Baa finally kills herself.

### Q: How does Ram-Leela end?

Ram and Leela shoot each other, by mutual agreement, on the eve of the Dussehra war, and fall together into the water as the festival fires rise. Each commands a clan mobilized for battle, each has buried a brother, and both judge that their survival would restart a war their peace talks failed to stop. Neither knows that Dhankor Baa has killed the traitor Bhavani and ordered peace between the clans, so the saving decision arrives minutes after the fatal one, in the play's own pattern of rescue outrun by death. The film closes with both houses cremating the lovers together, the feud's first joint act in five hundred years.


### Q: What does Dhankor Baa do after Bhavani's treachery is exposed?

Dhankor Baa kills Bhavani herself and orders peace with the Rajadi clan. The Sanera matriarch, wounded earlier by Bhavani's own shot and framed as the Rajadis' victim, learns that her cousin engineered the shooting and the later massacre carried out under Leela's forged authority. Her execution of the traitor is the film's recognition scene, placed in the sovereign's hands rather than in any court, and her peace order races the lovers' final decision and loses by minutes. The reconciliation of the houses therefore begins as her act of government before it becomes the clans' act of mourning at the joint cremation.

### Q: Who composed the music for Ram-Leela and who wrote the lyrics?

Sanjay Leela Bhansali composed the film's songs himself, and the writing team Siddharth-Garima, who shared the screenplay credit with him, wrote the lyrics. The album runs to ten tracks and leans on Gujarati folk instrumentation, dhol and nagada drums, folk percussion, and regional melodic turns, with fusion elements confined mainly to the hero's fanfare and the item number. Bhansali had composed for earlier productions, but Ram-Leela stands as his most complete exercise of the composer's role on a major film, and the score's placement of its strongest numbers at the plot's hinges makes the soundtrack part of the adaptation's argument rather than an accompaniment to it.

### Q: What is Nagada Sang Dhol in Ram-Leela?

Nagada Sang Dhol is the Garba number of the film's Navratri movement, sung by Shreya Ghoshal and Osman Mir over dhol and nagada drums. Leela dances at the center of her clan's circular Garba, and the sequence doubles as a public coronation: the heroine's command of the festival floor establishes the authority she will exercise when she succeeds her wounded mother as Sanera chief shortly afterward in the plot. Music reviewers treated the track as the album's peak, praising its traditional instrumentation and Ghoshal's rendition, and the choreography of the film's dance numbers received the Filmfare choreography award, with this sequence as its showpiece.

### Q: What is Lahu Munh Lag Gaya about in Ram-Leela?

Lahu Munh Lag Gaya is the Holi song of the film, sung by Shail Hada over a rustic Gujarati arrangement, and its governing image is the taste of blood on the lips. The number scores Ram and Leela's first meeting at the Sanera house's Holi celebration, where thrown color covers the courtyards and the enemy's son moves through the festival painted like everyone else. By binding blood to pigment at the moment desire begins, the song states the film's thesis in miniature: in Ranjaar, celebration and violence are neighboring substances, and the lovers meet inside a festival whose colors already prophesy the feud's price.

### Q: How much did Ram-Leela earn at the box office?

Trade sources record a clear hit, with figures stated as estimates in the manner of Indian box-office reporting. Bollywood Hungama's lifetime tables give an India net collection of Rs 116.33 crore, comfortably past the Rs 100 crore mark the industry treated as its headline threshold, against a reported budget of Rs 88 crore; an India gross of Rs 161.57 crore; an overseas gross of Rs 45.76 crore; and a worldwide gross of Rs 207.33 crore. Other trade tallies place the worldwide total between roughly Rs 200 crore and Rs 218 crore. The film ranked among the five highest-grossing Indian productions of its year.

### Q: Which Filmfare Awards did Ram-Leela win?

The film won three Filmfare Awards from eight nominations, the nominations including Best Film and Best Director for Bhansali. Deepika Padukone won Best Actress for Leela, Supriya Pathak Kapur won Best Supporting Actress for Dhankor Baa, and the film's dance direction won the choreography award. The pattern is pointed: the voters honored the two performances carrying the adaptation's boldest revisions, the heroine promoted to clan chief and the mother promoted to sovereign, together with the festival choreography that forms the film's machinery, while passing over the picture and its director, a distribution of honors that matches the critical reading of where the film's distinction lies.

### Q: How did Indian critics disagree about Ram-Leela?

The notices spanned the full scale. Meena Iyer in the Times of India awarded five stars and Taran Adarsh four and a half, praising the spectacle, the soundtrack, and the lead pair's chemistry. Rajeev Masand awarded three and a half, admiring the passion while protesting the second half's bloat. Shubhra Gupta in the Indian Express admired the mounted spectacle and faulted the choreography's perfection for refusing messy emotion. Raja Sen at Rediff awarded one star and dismissed the picture as an over-plotted bloody mess. Sudhish Kamath in The Hindu judged the Shakespeare borrowings loose but the form diegetically Indian. The spread records a genuine quarrel over whether amplitude serves or smothers a known tragedy.

### Q: What do scholars of Indian cinema and global Shakespeare say about Ram-Leela?

Scholarly treatments appeared within a few years of release in Literature/Film Quarterly, Adaptation, and Shakespeare Bulletin. Paulomi Sharma reads the film as a postcolonial reimagining that tears the story away from the metropolis into a regional Gujarat, loading the lovers with Hindu mythic resonance. Rosa Maria Garcia Periago reads its localization together with female agency, Leela's rise to clan chief set against the widows the adaptation invents and the item number's objectification. Both apply Tejaswini Ganti's account of Bollywoodization, songs, added emotion, and expanded narrative. Paromita Chakravarti, reviewing the Indian Romeo and Juliet films, describes the lovers' death as an act of consummation.

### Q: How does Leela's authority differ from Juliet's situation in the play?

Leela rules; Juliet petitions. Shakespeare's heroine acts inside a household governed by her father, and her instruments are secrecy, a feigned death, and a dagger; her power is real but exercised in hiding and at the price of isolation. Leela begins with public standing as the Sanera chief's daughter, argues openly, carries a gun, and midway through the film succeeds her wounded mother as clan chief, negotiating peace with Ram as an equal sovereign and signing orders in her own authority. The enlargement is the adaptation's central political act, and it darkens the tragedy: Leela's signature, tricked from her by Bhavani, authorizes a massacre, so her agency itself becomes an instrument of the feud.

### Q: Is Ram-Leela faithful to the ending of Romeo and Juliet?

The film is faithful to the ending's structure and consequence while replacing its machinery entire. Shakespeare's lovers die through error, an undelivered letter and a potion's timing, each acting on false information; Bhansali's lovers die by mutual agreement, fully informed except for one fact, the peace order Dhankor Baa has just issued, which arrives minutes too late in the play's own pattern. Both endings join the houses over the bodies: Verona's fathers promise statues of gold, while Ranjaar's clans cremate the pair together in a shared rite. The double death, the late rescue, and the purchased reconciliation all survive; only the potion, the tomb, and the letter are gone.
