The guerrilla road and the electoral road were not temperaments. They were theories, argued in print by named writers who gave reasons, predicted consequences, and accepted wagers about how history in their region actually moved. One held that a small armed nucleus in the countryside could create the conditions for revolution instead of waiting for those conditions to ripen. The other held that a coalition could win office through elections and legislate a transformation of property relations under the shelter of legality. Between the early nineteen sixties and the end of the nineteen seventies, Latin America tested both theories about as thoroughly as any region has ever tested a strategic dispute, and it tested them under conditions close enough to make the comparison mean something: the same decades, the same world economy pressing on the same kinds of states, the same adversary in Washington, and in several countries both roads attempted by rival organisations of the same left within a few years of each other.

![A mountain road and a ballot box on a table before a map of Latin America, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-32.webp)

That closeness of conditions is what gives this article its method, and the method should be stated before any case is narrated. Strategic arguments are usually judged by partisans, each side exhibiting its own near successes and the other side's catastrophes, and the exhibition settles nothing because the cases are chosen for the verdict. A comparison conducted across a whole region and a whole period cannot choose its cases so freely. It must take the armed road's full record, including the countries where the attempt was brief and the organisation small, and it must take the electoral road's full record, including the one case where that road was followed to its end. When the records are taken whole, a pattern appears that neither side's partisans emphasised, because it flatters neither. The two roads failed against the same obstacle. The armed road was destroyed by the coercive apparatus of the state, the soldiers, police, intelligence services and courts through which a state enforces its monopoly of violence. The electoral road was removed by the same apparatus, which it had won office without displacing and had governed while leaving intact. Neither strategy, as its theorists formulated it, contained an answer to that apparatus. This article names that finding the common failure, and it treats the finding as more useful than any verdict for one road against the other, because a verdict would teach a reader which banner to prefer, while the finding teaches what question any future strategy in the region would first have to answer.

Three cautions govern everything that follows, and they are stated here so that the comparison is not misread as something it is not. The first is that the failure of a strategy is a finding about a strategy, not a verdict on the doctrine in whose name it was attempted. Marxism in Latin America contained much besides these two roads: an analysis of land and dependence, a historiography, a labour movement, a theology, a literature. The roads were wagers about the seizure and holding of power, and their records grade the wagers. A reader who finishes this article believing that the region's Marxism stands refuted by the fate of its guerrillas has confused a part with the whole, and the article will return to that confusion explicitly, because correcting it is one of its purposes. The second caution concerns violence. Both roads ran through violence, the violence of insurgents who ambushed, kidnapped and executed, and the violence of states that tortured, disappeared and massacred on a scale the insurgents never approached in capacity, whatever their intentions. This article reports that violence factually and without detail, and it declines to weight either side's violence rhetorically, because the comparison is about strategic reasoning and its fate, and because the moral accounting of the region's violence belongs to the commissions and courts that were later charged with it. The third caution concerns numbers. No casualty figures are given anywhere in this article, for any insurgency or any repression. The counts are contested, the contest is itself political, and the comparison does not need them: what the record shows about strategy is visible in outcomes, in which organisations survived and which did not, in which governments finished their terms and which were removed, and those facts are not in dispute.

## Two theories stated before any verdict

Begin with the definitions, because the polemic between the roads was often conducted by men who defined the opposing position carelessly, and a comparison that inherits careless definitions will grade caricatures. The armed road, in its classical Latin American form, proposed that revolution need not wait for objective conditions to mature. A small nucleus of committed fighters, inserted into favourable rural terrain, would begin armed actions; the actions would demonstrate that the regime could be fought; peasants would rally as the nucleus survived and grew; and the guerrilla army that resulted would eventually defeat or dissolve the state's forces and take power. The sequence inverted the older insurrectionary model, in which a party first built a mass movement over years and only at the end, in a moment of crisis, called the rising. Here the armed nucleus came first and was expected to manufacture the crisis, the movement and eventually the party itself. The electoral road proposed the opposite sequence. A broad coalition of the left, allied where possible with reformist forces beyond it, would contest elections, win executive office, and use the legal powers of government to transfer property: nationalising commanding industries, breaking up great estates, extending the franchise of economic citizenship downward. Its wager was that legality was not merely a costume but a real constraint on its enemies, that a government installed by votes and governing by law could make its removal costlier than its toleration, and that the state's own institutions, including its armed forces, would obey the constitution that had seated the government.

Each theory had a reasoning about the state folded inside it, and the reasoning is what the record eventually tested. The armed road reasoned that the state's apparatus was the regime's property, staffed by its class, and therefore could not be inherited, only smashed or outlasted; elections held under such an apparatus were, on this view, a mechanism by which the apparatus chose its civilian managers. The electoral road reasoned that the apparatus was an institution with its own legal self-understanding, that officers and judges were formed by constitutions as well as by class, and that a government which kept scrupulously to legality could hold the apparatus to its professed norms. Both reasonings were coherent. Both were held by serious people who had read the same founding texts and drawn opposite conclusions about the institution those texts had taught them to analyse. The general form of their quarrel was old: it is the argument Rosa Luxemburg conducted against Eduard Bernstein at the turn of the century, over whether the existing state could be used as the instrument of its own transformation, and that argument's Latin American career is traced in the article on [the reform or revolution debate](/2013/02/01/reform-or-revolution-debate/). What the region added to the old quarrel was not a new theory but a new evidentiary situation: both answers were tried, repeatedly, within living memory of each other, by movements that could observe each other's experiments in something close to real time.

### What does a controlled comparison require?

It requires that the cases compared share enough conditions for their different outcomes to be informative: the same period, the same kind of state, the same external pressures, and strategies that differ in the variable being tested. Latin America between the nineteen sixties and the nineteen eighties supplies that structure more completely than most historical disputes ever receive.

The comparison also requires honesty about what varies besides strategy. Countries differed in the strength of their parties, the depth of their electoral traditions, the professionalism of their armies and the weight of the United States in their affairs. No honest comparison can hold all of that constant, and this article does not pretend to. What it claims is narrower and stronger: that when the same obstacle terminates both strategies across many different national settings, the obstacle, and not the national settings, is where the explanation should be sought. A finding that survives changes of country, decade and doctrine is the closest thing strategic history offers to a result.

### Why were the two roads debated as theories rather than chosen as moods?

Because each was published, argued and defended against objections before it was attempted at scale. The armed road had manuals and a codifier; the electoral road had programmes, congress resolutions and a worked example of gradual transformation to point to. Men and women chose between documents, not between impulses, and many changed sides as the documents were tested.

That theoretical character matters for how the record should be read. A mood cannot be refuted, but a theory can be, because a theory specifies the mechanism by which it expects to succeed. The armed road specified a mechanism: survival of the nucleus, peasant incorporation, growth into an army, collapse or defeat of the state's forces. The electoral road specified a mechanism too: electoral victory, legal transformation, rising costs of removal, consolidation. When a specified mechanism fails at the same joint in case after case, the failure locates itself precisely, and the location is the knowledge the comparison yields. This article is organised to follow the mechanisms rather than the banners: first the armed theory and its record, then the electoral theory and what befell it, then the joint at which both mechanisms broke.

## The strategies assessed

The table below is this article's findable artifact, and it is placed early because the argument keeps returning to it. The strategies assessed are five, not two. The polemic remembered a duel, but the region's record contains the rural nucleus of the foco, the urban armed strategy that succeeded it in several countries, the broad insurrectional alliance that actually took power in the armed cases, the electoral road, and a territorial autonomy strategy that refused the duel's very terms. Setting the five side by side is already an analytical act, because it prevents the commonest error in the literature, which is crediting or blaming the foco for outcomes produced by the alliance form, a different organisation with a different theory folded inside it.

Read the table by its last column, because the last column is where the comparison lives. The first three rows describe armed strategies, and their reasons differ instructively: the rural nucleus and the urban strategy failed against the apparatus, while the alliance succeeded where the apparatus was already rotting and the regime's support had narrowed to a palace. The fourth row describes the electoral strategy failing against the same apparatus that defeated the first two rows, approached from the opposite direction. The fifth row records a strategy that declined the encounter. The common failure named by this article is the pattern the last column makes visible: where the state's coercive apparatus was intact and willing, no strategy that sought state power prevailed against it, whether it came with rifles or with ballots; where power was taken, the apparatus had ceased to be intact before the taking. That pattern, and not a preference between rifles and ballots, is the region's strategic bequest.

### How does the table arrange the five strategies?

It orders the comparison by refusing the duel. By placing five strategies in one frame, with the outcome and the reason set beside the theory in each row, it forces the reader to grade mechanisms rather than banners, and it makes the shared obstacle visible as a pattern across rows rather than an excuse within one.

A table of this kind also disciplines the prose that surrounds it. Every claim made later in this article can be checked against a row: if a section praises the alliance form, the table has already recorded the condition that made it work, the prior disintegration of the regime's support; if a section criticises the electoral road, the table has recorded that the same apparatus defeated the armed rows too. Readers who keep the table in view will notice when an argument drifts toward partisanship, because the drift will contradict a row.

## The guerrilla road in its own terms

The armed road deserves to be stated as its theorists stated it, because it was not a romance but an argument, and arguments can only be graded in their strong form. Its founding experience was Cuba, where a small landing force, reduced by disaster to a handful of survivors in the Sierra Maestra, had grown within two years into a movement before which a dictatorship's army disintegrated. The lesson drawn by Ernesto Guevara, whose writings gave the road its theory and whose career is treated in the article on [Che Guevara's theory and practice](/2018/11/15/che-guevara-marxist-theory/), was that the Cuban sequence could be generalised. In his manual on guerrilla warfare, published in nineteen sixty, and in his subsequent essays, Guevara argued that it was not necessary to wait for all the conditions of revolution to exist before beginning; the insurrectional nucleus could itself create a portion of those conditions. The countryside of the continent, he held, was the natural terrain: there the state's presence was thinnest, the peasantry's grievances over land were sharpest, and a mobile force could survive against a conventional army that would crush any rising in the cities. The guerrilla fighter was to be a social reformer in arms, conducting agrarian reform in liberated zones, so that the peasantry would experience the revolution's benefits before the revolution had won.

The codification came from Régis Debray, a French philosopher who had travelled with Guevara and who published in nineteen sixty seven the book that turned the Cuban experience into a portable doctrine. Debray's argument was organisational as much as military. The traditional communist parties of the continent, he wrote, had subordinated the armed struggle to a political apparatus seated in the cities, and the apparatus, cautious, legalistic and committed to its own survival, would always postpone the struggle until it evaporated. The remedy was to reverse the subordination: the guerrilla foco was to be the party in embryo, the political leadership growing out of the military nucleus rather than directing it from outside. Debray described the foco as a small motor whose action would set in motion the large motor of the masses. The formulation was vivid, teachable and, its critics later observed, a mechanism asserted rather than demonstrated: the small motor's connection to the large one was exactly what the subsequent record would test.

### What exactly did the foco claim?

It claimed that a small armed nucleus, inserted into rural terrain, could create the subjective conditions for revolution by fighting: that its survival would prove the regime beatable, its reforms in liberated zones would win the peasantry, and its growth would proceed until the state's army collapsed or was defeated in open war.

The claim had a negative corollary that gave the road much of its polemical force. If conditions could be created by action, then waiting for conditions was not prudence but abdication, and the parties that counselled waiting, building unions, contesting elections, accumulating forces, were objectively conserving the order they claimed to oppose. The foco theory thus arrived with an accusation built into it, aimed at the existing left. That accusation explains both the speed of the theory's spread among the young and the bitterness of the argument it provoked, because to accept the theory was to accept a judgement on one's own organisation's history. Strategic debates inside movements are rarely only about the enemy, and this one was also a generational and organisational reckoning conducted in the vocabulary of military science.

### What separates a foco from a general insurrection?

A foco begins with the armed nucleus and expects the rising to follow from its example; a general insurrection begins with a mass movement already in motion and arms it at the moment of crisis. The foco manufactures its precondition. The insurrection rides a precondition it did not manufacture and could not have scheduled.

The distinction is not pedantry, because the region's two armed successes belong to the second category wearing the clothing of the first, and much confusion in the literature flows from grading them as foco victories. An insurrection draws on grievances already generalised across classes, on a regime already isolated, on strikes, defections and crowds; the armed element is one instrument among several, and often not the decisive one. A foco, by contrast, stakes everything on the proposition that armed example can substitute for that prior generalisation. When the proposition is stated this plainly, the test the record applied to it can be stated plainly too: in case after case, did the example generalise, or did the nucleus fight and die inside a society that watched, sympathised at most, and did not rise?

Guevara himself attached conditions to the doctrine, and fairness requires recording them, because the later debate often graded the doctrine by attempts that violated its own stated conditions. He held that where a government had come to power through some form of popular vote and maintained at least the appearance of constitutional legality, the guerrilla outbreak could not be promoted, since the possibilities of peaceful struggle had not been exhausted. He held that the struggle required terrain where the fighters could manoeuvre and a peasantry whose land hunger the movement could answer. And he held that the first duty of the nucleus was survival, the patient construction of a base, rather than spectacular action. These conditions matter to the assessment in two directions. They show the theory was not, in its author's hands, a licence for any dozen people with rifles to declare themselves a foco in any country at any moment. And they show the theory's vulnerability, because in practice the conditions were elastic enough to be discovered wherever a determined group wished to find them, and the organisations that attempted the road through the nineteen sixties repeatedly found, in the event, that the terrain was less favourable, the peasantry less ready and the state less feeble than the doctrine's reading of their country had promised.

The theory also carried a sociology of the enemy that deserves attention, since it is the premise the record damaged most. The armies of the region were read, in the foco literature, as the praetorian guards of oligarchies: conscript forces officered by a caste, formidable against unarmed crowds but brittle against a determined insurgency, because their soldiers were peasants in uniform who would not sustain a long war against their own class. There was evidence for the reading. Batista's army had indeed dissolved in Cuba, more by desertion and collapse of will than by defeat in battle. But the reading generalised a disintegrating conscript force, commanded by a dictator whose regime had lost its external patron and its internal coalition, into a continental law about armies. The armies the focos actually met were increasingly professionalised counterinsurgent forces, trained and supplied within a hemispheric security system organised after the Cuban revolution precisely to prevent its repetition, equipped with intelligence services that penetrated the movements' urban support networks, and officered by men for whom anti-communism was a doctrine of institutional identity rather than an employer's instruction. The theory had described the enemy of nineteen fifty eight. The enemy of nineteen sixty five had studied the same war and drawn his own conclusions.

### Why did the theory spread faster than the parties could answer it?

Because it converted a humiliation into a programme. The legal left had promised that history was moving its way and had decades of defeats, coups and proscriptions to show; the foco offered the young a mechanism by which their own generation could act within its own lifetime, and it arrived carrying the prestige of the one victory everyone had witnessed.

There was also an institutional reason. The theory's organisational corollary, that the armed nucleus should not subordinate itself to a party apparatus, freed new organisations from the discipline of old ones, and the continent in the nineteen sixties was full of young militants for whom that freedom was the point. Splits multiplied: parties divided between their leaderships and their youth, and the youth frequently departed to found the foco themselves. The strategic argument was therefore never conducted between two unified camps. It was conducted inside nearly every organisation of the left, and its conduct weakened several of the organisations that would later be blamed for the attempts' failure, a complication the record sections below must keep in view.

## The urban variant and the argument it provoked

The rural doctrine met an obvious objection in the more urbanised countries of the southern cone and Brazil: a strategy addressed to the peasantry spoke past societies whose populations, industries and politics were concentrated in great cities. The urban variant answered by relocating the armed initiative. Its manual appeared in nineteen sixty nine, written by the Brazilian Carlos Marighella, a veteran communist who had broken with his party over its refusal of armed struggle. His minimanual of the urban guerrilla proposed small, compartmented firing groups conducting expropriations, kidnappings and attacks in the cities, where the press would amplify every action and where the regime, forced to militarise daily life to respond, would expose its repressive character to the urban population it claimed to protect. The strategy's logic was provocation as pedagogy: the state's answer to urban action would itself radicalise the spectators.

The urban strategy was attempted, in distinct national forms, in Brazil by Marighella's organisation and others, in Uruguay by the Tupamaros, whose elaborate operations made them for a time the most famous urban guerrillas in the world, and in Argentina by organisations that grew large enough to contest territory and mount major operations. Each case ended in the organisation's dismantling. Marighella was killed by police in an ambush in nineteen sixty nine, the year his manual appeared. The Tupamaros were defeated by the armed forces in nineteen seventy two, after the military was given charge of the anti-subversive war, and the Uruguayan democracy whose space they had used was itself abolished by a coup the following year. The Argentine organisations were destroyed during the military government that seized power in nineteen seventy six and conducted a campaign of disappearance against the left as a whole.

### What did the urban manual change?

It changed the terrain and the tempo. Where the rural doctrine asked for patience, remote geography and a peasantry to organise, the urban variant asked for small groups, immediate action and the publicity of the city, trading concealment for amplification and exchanging the slow construction of a base for the rapid manufacture of a crisis.

### Did the urban strategy's critics inside the left prove right?

In their central contention, substantially yes. Critics within the left argued that urban armed action would provoke repression that the wider movement, legal parties, unions and publications, would pay for without having chosen the wager, and that the action's publicity would substitute for the base it claimed to build. The sequence in country after country followed the critics' script.

The critics' argument deserves precise statement, because it is the point at which the armed road's internal debate anticipated this article's finding. The critics did not deny the state's violence; they reasoned about its triggers and its incidence. A legal movement accumulates slowly: unions recognised, papers published, deputies elected, neighbourhoods organised. That accumulation is hostage to the state's tolerance, and the tolerance is withdrawn when the state can present the whole left as an armed conspiracy. Urban action, the critics held, handed the state that presentation at the moment of the state's choosing, and the resulting repression fell not on the firing groups, which were built to absorb it, but on the accumulated movement, which was not. The armed organisations answered that the tolerance was a fiction, that the state repressed whenever it judged the movement dangerous, armed or not, and that legality merely disarmed the victims in advance. Both arguments were coherent; both had cases. What the record added was an asymmetry: the states of the region proved able to destroy urban organisations quickly once they committed their intelligence apparatus to the task, and the destruction regularly extended, as the critics predicted, to organisations that had opposed the armed strategy all along.

## The armed road's record

The record can be summarised with a bluntness that the case by case literature sometimes obscures: in republic after republic during the sixties and seventies, the rural foco was attempted repeatedly and succeeded nowhere on its own terms. In Venezuela, guerrilla fronts sustained for years in the mountains were ground down by a constitutional government's army while that government combined repression with an electoral opening that drained the insurgency's urban support. In Guatemala, the insurgency that began with a revolt of nationalist officers in nineteen sixty was contained and then, in successive campaigns, largely destroyed in its rural zones, while the conflict's centre of gravity shifted to a capital where state terror operated against the movement's civilian periphery. In Peru, the focos of nineteen sixty five, launched in the expectation that the Andean peasantry would rise, were located and eliminated by the army within months, their expected peasant mobilisation failing to materialise on the timetable the theory required. In Bolivia, Guevara's own column, the theory's most authoritative attempt, was isolated in a region where the peasantry had already received land from an earlier revolution, where the local communist party withheld support, and where the army, advised and supplied from abroad, encircled and destroyed it in nineteen sixty seven. In Colombia, organisations founded in the same years survived at low intensity for decades without approaching power, an outcome that belongs neither to victory nor to the quick destruction of the other cases but to a stalemate the theory had not envisaged: the foco as permanent marginality. Later insurgencies in Central America grew far larger than the classical focos, fielding armies and holding territory, and even they ended, in El Salvador and Guatemala, not in the seizure of power but in negotiated settlements after military stalemates, settlements whose terms registered that the state's apparatus could not be defeated outright by the forces the armed road could assemble.

The pattern inside the defeats is more instructive than the defeats themselves, because the theory failed at specific joints of its mechanism, and the joints recur. The first joint was intelligence. The foco's survival depended on concealment, and concealment depended on the peasantry's silence; but the state's intelligence services, working through informers, captured documents and the movements' own urban supply networks, repeatedly located columns whose whole strategy presupposed they could not be located until they chose to fight. The second joint was the peasantry itself, whose behaviour the theory had read off from its grievances. Land hunger was real, but peasants also feared the army, distrusted outsiders, calculated the odds of the column's survival against the certainty of reprisal, and in several regions had received, or hoped to receive, land through reforms the state deployed precisely as counterinsurgency. The third joint was the urban rear. No foco lived off the countryside alone; all depended on cities for money, medicine, recruits and communications, and the cities were where the state's apparatus was densest, so that the rural column's fate was repeatedly decided by arrests in apartments and safe houses hundreds of miles from the mountains. The fourth joint was time. The theory imagined a war of growth, in which each year found the guerrilla stronger; the states imposed a war of attrition in which each year found the guerrilla's founding generation dead, captured or exhausted, and its replacement pool thinned by the very repression the struggle had summoned.

### Why did the guerrilla strategy fail so often?

because it assumed an insurrectional nucleus could create the conditions for revolution, and the state's coercive apparatus proved far stronger than the theory allowed. Attempts across more than a dozen countries were destroyed, frequently taking the legal left organisations blamed for them down as well.

That sentence is the brief's summary and this article's, and the sections around it exist to earn it. Notice what the sentence does not say. It does not say the fighters lacked courage; the record is full of a courage that shames the summariser. It does not say the grievances were invented; the land regimes and dictatorships the focos opposed were real, and in several countries murderous. It does not say armed struggle as such never succeeds anywhere; the next section examines the cases where power was taken by arms and asks what made them different. The sentence says that a specified mechanism, nucleus creates conditions, was tested against a specified obstacle, the intact coercive apparatus of states that had organised themselves after Cuba to prevent exactly this, and the mechanism failed at the obstacle. Grading the theory at the joint where it broke is the only form of respect a strategic theory can be paid, since its authors asked to be judged by results and despised the consolation of pure intentions.

### What happened to the legal left when the focos were destroyed?

It was repressed as an accomplice. States presented the legal parties, unions and publications as the insurgency's political wing, and emergency legislation, proscription, imprisonment and exile fell on organisations that had in many cases opposed the armed strategy from its first day and had split with its founders over precisely that question.

This collateral destruction is a material fact of the comparison, not a footnote to it, because it altered what the other road could attempt afterwards. The electoral road presupposed a legal left capable of accumulating organisation across decades: unions that could not be dissolved at will, parties with presses and deputies, a public presence that made a coalition plausible. The armed road's defeats repeatedly consumed that presupposition, as states used the insurgency to justify dismantling the legal infrastructure of the entire left, including its anti-guerrilla majority. The two roads were therefore not independent experiments. Each attempt at one road changed the conditions under which the other would later be tried, and the changes ran downhill: the armed attempts left the legal left weaker, and the electoral attempts, as Chile would show, left their survivors to face a dictatorship that proscribed politics itself. A comparison that scored the roads separately, as two runners on parallel tracks, would miss the region's bleakest dynamic, in which the roads shared not only an obstacle but a graveyard.

## The two apparent exceptions

Against the record just summarised, partisans of the armed road exhibited two victories, and the comparison stands or falls on grading them honestly. The first is Cuba itself, the theory's birthplace. The second is Nicaragua in nineteen seventy nine, where the Sandinista Front, after a long armed struggle, entered Managua as the Somoza dynasty collapsed. If either case were what the foco theory predicted, a nucleus creating its conditions and growing into victory, the theory's continental defeats could be written off as failures of execution. Neither case is that, and examining why they are not is the most instructive exercise in this article, because the exceptions reveal the conditions under which armed movements actually did take power in the region, conditions the theory had not specified and in part had denied.

Cuba first, with the detail assigned to the article on [the Cuban revolution's Marxist reading](/2018/11/01/cuban-revolution-marxist-analysis/). The movement that landed from the Granma in nineteen fifty six and fought in the Sierra Maestra was not a communist organisation and did not present itself as one. The July Twenty Sixth Movement was a nationalist, anti-dictatorial coalition in arms, gathering students, professionals, Orthodox party dissidents and workers, committed publicly to restoring the constitution of nineteen forty and holding elections. The island's actual communist party, the Popular Socialist Party, had condemned the Moncada attack of nineteen fifty three as putschist, maintained an uneasy distance from the Sierra for most of the war, and joined the winning coalition late. The regime the movement faced was a dictatorship whose social base had narrowed to the palace, the casino economy and the officer corps' upper reaches; whose patron in Washington had imposed an arms embargo on it in its final year; and whose army, a conscript force rotted by corruption, disintegrated through desertion and negotiated surrenders as much as through battle. The revolution's socialist character was declared after power was taken, in nineteen sixty one, not proclaimed as the war's aim. Every element of that summary cuts against the foco theory that was later built on the case: the movement was broader than a nucleus, its ideology was not the doctrine the theory presupposed, its enemy's apparatus collapsed from internal rot and external abandonment rather than being defeated at full strength, and the theory itself was formulated afterwards, by codifiers generalising a victory whose actual mechanics they simplified. Cuba does not vindicate the foco. Cuba is the case from which the foco was misderived.

### Why does Cuba not vindicate the foco?

Because the movement that won was a broad anti-dictatorial coalition rather than a communist nucleus, because the regime it faced had lost its patron and its social base before the end, and because the island's socialist turn was declared after the victory, so the sequence the theory claims, nucleus first and conditions created by arms, runs backwards in the founding case.

Nicaragua requires the same care, because it is the stronger exhibit: here a self-consciously Marxist organisation did lead an armed struggle to power. The Sandinista National Liberation Front was founded in nineteen sixty one, in the foco's first hour, and for most of its history its experience confirmed the continental pattern: rural columns in the northern mountains were contained and nearly destroyed, and the organisation survived the nineteen sixties as a small, hunted sect. What changed its fate was not a refinement of the foco but a series of departures from it. The Front split into tendencies in the nineteen seventies, and the tendency that prevailed, the insurrectional or tercerista current, argued for exactly what the foco denied: a broad alliance across classes, including sectors of the business community and the church, urban insurrection in the cities rather than a purely rural war, and the construction of a civic opposition front alongside the armed one. The dictatorship, meanwhile, was performing the other half of the Cuban sequence on itself. The Somoza family's theft of earthquake relief after the destruction of Managua in nineteen seventy two, the assassination of the newspaper editor Pedro Joaquín Chamorro in nineteen seventy eight, and the dynasty's open conversion of the state into a family estate drove its own former social base, business, church hierarchy, much of the middle class, into opposition. When the end came in nineteen seventy nine it came as a general insurrection: neighbourhoods rose, the National Guard fought a population rather than a column, neighbouring governments and eventually Washington withdrew support from the dynasty, and the Guard disintegrated as its commander fled. The Front's armed forces were indispensable to that outcome, and their courage and organisation across two decades are not in question here. But the mechanism that delivered power was the insurrectional alliance riding a regime's general collapse, which is the older model the foco had claimed to supersede.

### What made Nicaragua different from the foco cases?

The alliance's breadth and the regime's isolation. The Sandinistas won as the armed core of an opposition that included business associations, church figures and civic groups, against a dynasty that had alienated its own class and lost its external support, in cities that rose in general insurrection at the end.

The two exceptions therefore teach one lesson from opposite ends. Armed movements took power in the region when the opposing apparatus was already disintegrating and when the movement's form was the broad alliance rather than the nucleus; they failed when the apparatus was intact and the form was the nucleus. The variable that separates victory from defeat in the armed record is not the fighters' doctrine, discipline or bravery, all of which were present in defeats as fully as in victories. It is the prior condition of the state's coercive apparatus. Hold that variable in view, because the electoral road's record is about to exhibit it again, approached from the opposite direction: an electoral victory that left the apparatus intact, and an apparatus that then decided how much transformation it would permit.

## The electoral road in its own terms

The electoral road also deserves its strong form, because its partisans were not naive parliamentarians who had failed to notice that states possess armies. Their theory began from the armed road's emerging record and reasoned about a different asset: the legitimacy that only votes confer, and the inhibitions that legitimacy imposes on those who would overturn a government by force. A coalition elected on a programme of transformation, governing through congress and courts, multiplying its support by delivering land, wages and nationalisations to constituencies who would then defend the process as their own achievement, would raise the price of a coup beyond what the officer corps and its civilian allies could pay. The armed forces, on this theory, were not a monolith of class reaction but an institution with a professional self-image, formed in countries like Chile by a tradition of constitutional obedience, and an institution can be held to its self-image by a government that never gives it the pretext of illegality. The transformation, meanwhile, would change the balance of forces as it proceeded: each nationalised industry and redistributed estate would shrink the social base of reaction and enlarge the government's, so that time, which attrited the guerrilla, would compound for the elected government. The theory had a name in its fullest case, the Chilean road, and its proponents presented it explicitly as a third possibility between the social democratic administration of capitalism and the insurrectionary seizure of power: a revolution conducted, uniquely, with the constitution open on the table.

The theory's reasoning about the state was the mirror image of the foco's. Where Debray's codification treated the apparatus as the regime's property, to be smashed because it could never be inherited, the electoral theorists treated the apparatus as a terrain: staffed by men with institutional loyalties and legal training, penetrable by promotion policies, bound by traditions, and in the last resort dependent on a civilian consensus that a coup would have to manufacture before it could act. The general theory of the state within the Marxist tradition, its distinction between the machinery of coercion and the wider apparatuses through which consent is organised, is set out in the article on [the Marxist theory of the state](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/), and the electoral road can be read as a wager placed on one side of that theory: that the consent organising institutions, parties, press, church, congress, were strong enough in a country like Chile to discipline the coercive one. It was not a stupid wager. Chile had the continent's most durable electoral tradition, parties of the left with roots generations deep, a labour movement of real weight, and an officer corps that had not governed for four decades. If the electoral road could work anywhere in the region, it could work there, which is why its failure there carries the evidentiary weight it does.

### What did the electoral road promise that the armed road could not?

It promised transformation without the war: property transferred by statute rather than by conquest, a government's legitimacy renewed at elections rather than asserted by victory, and the survival of the movement's accumulated organisation, its unions, parties and presses, which the armed road's defeats kept consuming as collateral damage in countries where it was tried.

### Why did the Chilean coalition believe legality could hold?

Because Chile's institutions had held for generations. Its parties were old, its congress was real, its courts functioned, its officers professed a doctrine of constitutional obedience, and the coalition's leaders, formed inside that system across whole careers, read those facts as a structure of protection rather than as a fair weather arrangement.

The electoral road had also been argued, in a preliminary and partial way, by the record of the region's legal left before Chile. Communist and socialist parties had won municipalities, ministries in coalition governments, and large congressional blocs in several countries across the middle of the century, and had used office to deliver reforms their supporters remembered. Against that stood the counter record, which the armed road's theorists recited: elected or legal left advances had repeatedly been terminated by force, in Brazil in nineteen sixty four, where a reformist government's fall to a military coup taught a generation that legality protected nothing, and in the proscriptions and interventions that punctuated the century. The electoral theorists did not lack an answer. The terminated governments, they argued, had been reformist administrations without a programme of structural transformation or a mobilised majority behind one; the Chilean coalition would differ in having both, and in governing a country whose democratic institutions were the strongest on the continent. The answer was, once again, coherent. Its test required a case in which a government with a transformative programme and mass support met an intact apparatus, and Chile supplied the case with terrible completeness.

## Chile: the electoral road's fullest test

The Chilean case is treated in its own article, on [Allende's Chile and the democratic road](/2018/12/01/allende-chile-democratic-road/), and only its bearing on the comparison belongs here. In September nineteen seventy the Popular Unity coalition's candidate, Salvador Allende, won a plurality of the presidential vote, and after negotiation over a statute of democratic guarantees, the congress confirmed him, as the constitution directed, between the two leading candidates. The government that followed proceeded by law. It nationalised the great copper mines by a constitutional amendment that the opposition congress passed unanimously, a fact worth pausing on, because it shows the transformation at its outset commanding the assent of the very institution that would later be cast as its jailer. It accelerated an agrarian reform enacted under the previous government, raised wages, extended price controls, and brought banks and industries into public ownership through legal mechanisms, some of them antique statutes repurposed with ingenuity. It also governed without a congressional majority, against an opposition that controlled the legislature, the courts and most of the press, in an economy subjected to external credit denial and internal dislocation, while its own coalition argued publicly over the pace of the process and movements to its left pressed occupations of farms and factories beyond the government's programme.

The end is known. In September nineteen seventy three the armed forces, acting together and under a junta, overthrew the government, bombed the presidential palace, and installed a dictatorship that dissolved congress, proscribed the parties of the left, and conducted a repression whose scale and character were later documented by the official commissions of inquiry established after the dictatorship ended, to whose reports the reader is directed for the accounting this article does not attempt. The government had held office for just under three years. It had not disarmed, purged, divided or otherwise neutralised the institution that removed it; indeed its strategy forbade the attempt, since the strategy's premise was that scrupulous legality would bind the institution to its constitutional profession. The premise failed at the moment it was needed. When the officer corps concluded that the transformation in progress exceeded what the existing order could absorb, the constitutional profession yielded to what the institution took to be its deeper duty, and the government's legality, so carefully preserved, provided it with no instrument of self defence beyond appeals to a constitution its interpreters in uniform had set aside.

### What did the unanimous copper vote show?

It showed that the transformation's opening measures could command the whole political system's assent, including the opposition's, when they were framed as national rather than class measures. The same congress that voted copper into public ownership without dissent later became the government's institutional adversary, which locates the conflict in the process's later reach rather than its legality.

### What did the Chilean test establish about the electoral road?

It proved that a coalition could take office, govern and transform property relations by law for three years in the region's strongest democracy, and that none of that settled the question of the coercive apparatus, which retained throughout the capacity and, at the end, the will to terminate the process the voters had authorised.

Both halves of that answer must be held together, because the polemical literature keeps tearing them apart. Partisans of the armed road exhibit the second half as the whole finding: elections deliver governments that armies remove. Defenders of the electoral road exhibit the first half: the road was working, its difficulties were economic and political rather than constitutional, and the coup was a crime committed against a viable process, not the exposure of an impossible one. The comparison this article is conducting can accept both exhibits, because they agree on the fact that matters to it. A viable process was terminated by the apparatus it had left intact. Whether one calls that the road's refutation or its martyrdom depends on whether one believes a strategy is responsible for the obstacles it declines to address, and on that question the armed road's theorists and the electoral road's survivors have argued ever since, in an argument the next sections take up directly. What cannot be argued is that the obstacle was absent, or incidental, or unique to Chile. It is the same institution that destroyed the focos, wearing its other face: against the nucleus it acted as hunter, against the elected government as judge.

## The common failure

The finding can now be stated in its full form. Both strategies were, at bottom, theories about how to deal with the state's coercive apparatus, and both theories were wrong about it in opposite ways. The armed road proposed to defeat the apparatus in the field, beginning from nothing, and discovered that a modern state, even a poor and dependent one, disposed of resources for rural war, intelligence, logistics, air mobility, external supply and sheer replacement of losses, that no nucleus could match within the time its own survival allowed. The electoral road proposed to bind the apparatus by legality, governing so constitutionally that the institution would hold to its professed norms, and discovered that the institution's norms were conditional: they held while the government governed within the order the apparatus existed to defend, and they were reinterpreted, by the apparatus itself, when the government's programme reached the order's foundations. The armed road overestimated what violence from below could do to the apparatus. The electoral road overestimated what legality from above could do to it. Between the two overestimations lies the strategic history of the Latin American left in the period, and the common failure is the name this article gives to their intersection: neither side answered the question of the coercive apparatus, the armed road being destroyed by it and the electoral road being removed by it.

It is worth being exact about what an answer would have had to be, because the finding is sometimes misread as a counsel of despair, as though the article were reporting that nothing could have worked. An answer to the apparatus question could take several conceivable forms, and the region's debates named most of them at one time or another. A movement might split the apparatus, detaching part of the armed forces to its side, as happens when junior officers or conscript masses refuse orders or fraternise; several theorists of insurrection placed this at the centre of their model, and it is in fact how many successful revolutions elsewhere proceeded, the old army dissolving politically before it was defeated militarily. A movement might neutralise the apparatus by raising the external cost of its intervention, through international opinion, foreign pressure or the threat of wider war. A movement might build a counter apparatus of its own capable of deterring a coup, the armed militia solution, which the Chilean left debated in its final period and which its government declined, fearing, not without reason, that the attempt would precipitate the coup it was meant to deter. Or a movement might transform so gradually, with such broad consent, that the apparatus never found the moment or the pretext at which intervention became thinkable to its officers and their civilian allies. The point is not that these answers were available and were foolishly refused. Several were attempted in fragments, and each carried costs and risks its critics correctly identified. The point is that neither road's theory, as formulated by its founders, placed any of them at the centre. The foco theory assumed the apparatus would collapse under pressure it proved able to sustain. The electoral theory assumed the apparatus would abstain under a legality it proved able to reinterpret. The question neither theory asked was the question the record kept answering.

### What is the coercive apparatus, concretely?

It is the set of institutions a state uses to impose its decisions on resisters: the army and its intelligence services, the police and their political branches, the prisons, and the courts insofar as they process the state's enemies. Governments change by election. The apparatus persists across governments, with its own personnel, doctrine and memory.

### Why could the armed road not answer the apparatus?

Because its mechanism required years of growth in the countryside while the apparatus, supplied from abroad and informed by penetration of the movement's urban networks, could concentrate force against a nucleus that had to survive every single engagement. The theory needed time and concealment. The apparatus denied it both, repeatedly and in country after country.

### Why could the electoral road not answer it either?

Because answering it would have required the government to purge, divide or arm against the institution, and each of those measures contradicted the strategy's founding premise, that legality itself was the government's protection. The road's strength and its exposure were the same commitment, which is why its ablest defenders could describe the danger precisely and still not escape it.

The apparatus question also explains a feature of the period that purely national histories treat as a series of coincidences: the similarity of the endings across dissimilar countries. Governments of the left, armed movements of the left, and in several cases the entire legal political space of the left were terminated in the same few years across the southern cone and beyond, by institutions that had studied one another's methods and in some cases trained together within a hemispheric security system. The coups and counterinsurgencies of the period were not copies of one another, and their local causes differed; but they expressed a continental fact, that the region's armed forces had, by the nineteen seventies, developed a shared doctrine of internal war in which the enemy was defined ideologically and the whole of society was the theatre. Against an apparatus that had reconceived itself in this way, the foco's expectation of collapse and the coalition's expectation of abstention were both anachronisms, survivals from an earlier period in which armies were smaller, less professionalised, less doctrinally unified and less externally sustained. Both strategies were, in this precise sense, fighting the previous war.

The external dimension of the apparatus deserves its own paragraph of the analysis, because both strategies underweighted it in the same way. The coercive institutions of the region did not face the left alone. After the Cuban revolution, the United States reorganised its military assistance to the hemisphere around internal defence: training programmes for officers, counterinsurgency doctrine, intelligence cooperation, and material supply on a scale that altered what any national apparatus could do. The School of the Americas and its predecessor institutions trained generations of the region's officers in a common curriculum. This did not make the national armies puppets, and the article does not argue that local officers lacked their own convictions or interests; the doctrine of national security, elaborated most systematically in Brazil and Argentina, was an authentic product of the region's military intellectuals, who taught that underdevelopment was a security problem, that the enemy was internal and ideological, and that the armed forces were the nation's permanent institution, entitled to intervene whenever the political system failed the nation's essence as they defined it. But external support multiplied the apparatus's capabilities at exactly the joints where the foco was weakest: communications intelligence, air mobility, interrogation, the patient bureaucratic work of mapping a movement's networks. The foco theory had been formed against Batista's army, an institution its patron was abandoning. The electoral theory had been formed against the memory of Chilean officers of an earlier generation. Both theories faced, in the event, an apparatus that had been rebuilt, by its own intellectuals and its external sponsor together, for the precise purpose of defeating them. A strategy debate that treated the apparatus as a constant, while the apparatus was consciously transforming itself into the debate's referee and executioner, was a debate conducted one war behind.

There is a further, quieter implication of the common failure that the literature on both sides resisted, because it reflects on the quality of the left's own institutions rather than on the enemy's strength. Neither road built organisations good at learning from the other's defeats. The armed organisations read Chile, when it came, as confirmation of what they had always said, and drew from it recruitment rather than reflection; the survivors of the Chilean process read the guerrilla defeats as confirmation of their own road's necessity, and many carried that reading into exile and beyond. The parties and movements of the region were structured, by their internal lives, to convert evidence into vindication. Congresses ratified lines; dissenters split; the split organisations competed for the same constituencies by sharpening the very claims the record was damaging. The result was that the strategic argument was never resolved by the record, as this article's brief puts it, not only because the obstacle was shared but because the arguers' institutions processed shared evidence into opposite certainties. Identifying the common failure is therefore also an organisational criticism: a movement that cannot grade its own strategies cannot correct them, and the region's left paid for its grading failure across two decades, in the currency the commissions later counted.

### Does the common failure grade Marxism itself?

No, and the distinction is load bearing for this whole article. The common failure grades two strategies for taking and holding state power, wagers made by particular organisations in particular decades. The doctrine those organisations invoked also contained analyses of land, dependence, class and culture whose fortunes were separate, and several of which the period strengthened rather than damaged.

Consider what would follow if the distinction were refused. The dependency analysis that Latin American Marxists built in the same decades entered the world's universities and has never left them; the historiography transformed how the region's past is written; the labour and peasant movements won concrete goods in countries where no road to power succeeded. None of that is graded by the fate of the focos or of the Chilean coalition, any more than liberalism stands graded by every government that invoked it and fell. Strategies are instruments proposed in a doctrine's name, and their failure is evidence about the instruments, the conditions, and the organisations that wielded them. It becomes evidence about the doctrine only through an argument that the doctrine entailed the instruments, and that argument, in this case, is weak: the same canon supplied the electoral road and the armed road, the gradualists and the insurrectionists, each quoting the founders against the other with texts in hand. A doctrine that underdetermines strategy cannot be refuted by a strategy's record. What the record refutes is more specific and more useful: two mechanisms, and the theories of the state folded inside them.

## The objection to this comparison

The strongest objection to everything argued so far is not a defence of either road. It is an objection to the comparison itself, and it deserves to be stated at full strength, because if it stands, this article's central finding is an artefact of its method. The objection runs as follows. Strategies are not items on a menu from which movements choose in abstraction. They are answers to situations, and the situations differed in the very respects that decided the outcomes. The foco was attempted in countries where elections were fraudulent, parties proscribed and peaceful roads demonstrably closed; its theorists could reply that they never proposed the nucleus for a functioning democracy. The electoral road was attempted where a real electoral tradition existed; its theorists could reply that they never proposed ballots for a dictatorship's subjects. To line the records up side by side, the objection continues, and to announce a common failure is to commit a category error: it grades each strategy in theatres it never claimed, manufactures a symmetry out of incommensurable cases, and discovers, at the end, only the tautology that strategies fail where their presuppositions are absent. Worse, the comparison flatters the historian's hindsight. The actors chose under uncertainty, with the information of their moment, against enemies who concealed their intentions; the comparison grades them with the record complete, a privilege no strategist has ever enjoyed.

The objection is partly right, and the part in which it is right disciplines the finding rather than dissolving it. What the comparison can legitimately show is not that one road was better, nor that the actors were fools for choosing as they did. It can show the conditions under which each road's presuppositions held, and how often those conditions obtained. The foco presupposed a state whose rural presence was thin, whose army was brittle, and whose peasantry was ready to be organised by example; the comparison shows those conditions approximated, in the period, almost nowhere except where a regime was already collapsing for other reasons. The electoral road presupposed an apparatus that would accept a transformative government as the constitution's product; the comparison shows that presupposition failing in its strongest case, where the tradition of legality was deepest. And the comparison can show the shared term: in case after case, on both roads, the variable that decided the outcome was the condition and disposition of the coercive apparatus, a variable neither theory had placed at its centre. That is a finding about the world the strategies operated in, and about the theories' shared blind spot within it. It is not a finding that the actors should have seen the future.

### Is the comparison itself fair?

Only in a restricted sense, and the restriction is the answer to the objection. The comparison is not fair as a grading of choosers, who faced different situations under uncertainty. It is fair as an audit of presuppositions: each theory claimed its mechanism would work where its conditions were named, and the record shows how the conditions and the mechanism actually behaved when they met.

The concession the objection extracts should be made without varnish. The common failure is a finding about situations as much as about strategies. It says that in the Latin America of those decades, the situations in which either road's presuppositions held were rare and shrinking, because the apparatuses were being rebuilt to ensure they would not hold. A different region or decade, with weaker or divided apparatuses, might have graded the same strategies differently, as indeed the region's own earlier history sometimes had. Nothing in this article should be read as a law of strategy for all times and places. It is a report on a controlled comparison that one region, in one period, happened to run at terrible cost, and its exportable content is the method, find the obstacle both roads share, more than the verdict, which belongs to its time.

## The armed road's objection, and what survives of it

Within the comparison, the polemical objection each road addressed to the other also requires grading, and the armed road's objection to the electoral road was the sharper of the two. Stated at full strength, in the terms its theorists used, it ran: a government that does not disarm its enemies will be disarmed by them. The electoral road delivers its militants, its unions, its ministers and its president into a state whose armed core remains in the hands of the class being expropriated, and it forbids them, by its own premise, to touch that core. The coup is therefore not an accident that befalls the road from outside; it is the road's destination, written into its first concession. Chile, the theorists said, demonstrated the syllogism in full: three years of scrupulous legality, a unanimous congress voting the government's copper amendment, a president who refused every proposal to arm his supporters or purge the officer corps, and at the end the palace bombed by the air force of the state he headed, the militants delivered to the stadiums and the ships with legal formalities attached. A strategy whose refutation is enacted upon its own adherents' bodies has, the objection concluded, no standing to call the alternative irresponsible.

The answer available to the electoral road's defenders was real, and this article records it at the same strength. The armed road's own record was worse by the very measure the objection proposed. The focos did not protect their militants either; they led them, in country after country, into encirclements from which few returned, and their defeats delivered not only their own adherents but the unarmed left around them to the repression that followed. If the electoral road ended in the stadium, the armed road ended in the mountains and the safe houses, and it ended there more often, in more countries, with the legal movement destroyed as a by product. Nor did the armed theorists' own prescription escape the syllogism they aimed at Chile: to disarm the enemy's apparatus in advance is precisely what the foco could not do, since the apparatus destroyed the attempt in its infancy. Both roads, pressed by the other's objection, arrived at the same confession: neither had a demonstrated method for the interval between challenging the old order and surviving its armed reply. The Chilean survivors who conceded that their government had no answer to the question of the armed forces, and the guerrilla survivors who conceded that their columns had no answer to the question of the state's strength, were conceding the same thing from opposite ends, and the convergence of the concessions is the human form of this article's finding.

### In what way are the two roads' objections mirror images?

Each objection accused the other road of delivering its adherents to the apparatus, the armed critics pointing to the coup that ends an unarmed government, the electoral critics to the encirclement that ends an exposed column and the repression that then falls on everyone. The objections are mirror images, and their symmetry is evidence for the common failure.

What survives of the armed objection, after the answer has been given, is its negative clause, and it is not nothing. It survives as a standing question that any electoral strategy must answer in advance and in detail, not with assurances about constitutional tradition: what, concretely, prevents the apparatus from terminating the process at the moment of its choosing, and what does the strategy do in the weeks when the termination is being prepared? The Chilean experience made that question unignorable for every subsequent electoral project in the region, and later projects can be read, in part, as attempts to answer it by moderation, by breadth of alliance, by the deliberate limitation of the transformation's pace and scope. Whether those answers concede the objection or evade it is a live argument, and it is conducted, knowingly or not, in the shadow of the comparison this article has drawn.

## The third road: refusing the question

The comparison between the two roads presupposes that the object is state power, seized by arms or won by ballots. In January nineteen ninety four, a movement in the southern Mexican state of Chiapas announced itself in arms and proceeded to reject that presupposition, and the rejection is the third road this article must describe. The Zapatista Army of National Liberation had been founded a decade earlier, in nineteen eighty three, in the Lacandon jungle, by a small group that included survivors of earlier Mexican guerrilla organisations, and it had grown quietly among the indigenous communities of the highlands and the jungle, communities whose grievances over land, contempt and abandonment long predated the organisation's arrival. When it emerged publicly, on the day the North American free trade agreement entered into force, it occupied towns, issued a declaration of war against the federal government, and then, after a brief period of combat, accepted a ceasefire and settled into a posture it has defined ever since in its founding documents: it would not march on the capital, would not seek to govern Mexico, and would not lay down its arms in exchange for office. Its stated object was not the state but autonomy, the right of indigenous peoples to govern themselves in their own territories under their own norms.

The movement's stated principles, as set out in its declarations from the Lacandon jungle and the agreements it signed with the government at San Andrés in nineteen ninety six, form a coherent position that should be given in its own terms before it is graded. Power, the position holds, corrupts those who take it and reproduces in the victors the structures they fought; the revolutionary subject is not a vanguard that knows but communities that deliberate; authority within the movement's territory is exercised under the principle its Spanish formula names mandar obedeciendo, governing by obeying, in which office holders are mandated, supervised and recallable by the assemblies they serve. The movement proposed, for the country beyond its territory, not a programme to be administered from a palace it declined to occupy but a politics of encounter among the excluded, and for the world the formula of a world in which many worlds fit. Its organisational forms matched the doctrine: autonomous municipalities, later grouped under councils of good government, administering justice, health and education in the movement's zones, with rotating and unpaid office, and a strict refusal of government aid programmes the movement judged to be instruments of counterinsurgent purchase.

### What does the third road refuse?

It refuses the object the other two roads competed for. It does not seek to take state power, by arms or by election, and it does not accept that a movement's success is measured by its occupancy of the national government. Its arms are held defensively, as the guarantee of a territory in which another form of government is practised.

### Where did the third road come from?

From the defeat of the roads before it, studied at close range. Its founders included veterans of the Mexican guerrilla organisations destroyed in the nineteen seventies, and its doctrine reads as a point by point negation of the foco: no march on power, no vanguard substituting itself for the communities, no timetable of victory, no party being born from a gun.

Whether the third road is an alternative or an evasion is disputed, and the dispute should be reported rather than settled. Its defenders argue that it alone among the region's strategies correctly diagnosed the common failure: if the apparatus cannot be defeated and cannot be trusted, then a politics that requires neither its defeat nor its trust, that builds a governed territory at the margin of the state and defends it, escapes the trap that closed on both roads. They point to the movement's survival across decades in which every foco perished, and to the concrete institutions of its zones, schools, clinics, courts and assemblies, as evidence that the refusal of power was a construction and not merely a renunciation. Its critics answer that the autonomy exists on the state's sufferance, that the apparatus the movement declined to fight retains the capacity to end the experiment whenever a government calculates the cost acceptable, and that a strategy whose survival depends on the enemy's restraint has not answered the apparatus question but outsourced it. Critics add that the model does not travel: it presupposes a territory, a communal indigenous social fabric and a state willing to tolerate an enclave, conditions absent for the urban majorities of the continent, whose problems, wages, housing, public services, are problems of the state and cannot be seceded from. The article records the dispute in this balanced form deliberately. The third road's founding position and stated principles are historical facts, set out above. Its present activity is not this article's subject, and the movement, which continues to exist, is described here only as its founding documents describe it, because a strategy that refuses the comparison's terms can be introduced fairly only in its own.

One further observation belongs to the comparison rather than to the dispute. The third road is the only one of the five in the table whose theory was written after, and in full knowledge of, the common failure. The foco's theorists wrote before the continental record existed; the Chilean coalition theorised with Brazil's coup behind it but its own test ahead. The Zapatista doctrine was composed by survivors of the earlier defeats, in a region littered with the graves of both roads' adherents, and its central move, the renunciation of state power as the objective, is best read as a conclusion drawn from the comparison itself: if every road to the palace ends at the apparatus, stop walking to the palace. Whether that conclusion is wisdom or surrender is the argument the region's left continues to have with the movement, and the argument's persistence, decades on, is itself evidence that neither of the older roads produced a successor doctrine capable of commanding the field.

## The argument as it was conducted

The strategic argument was not conducted only in books. It was conducted in party congresses, in splits, in international conferences and in the pages of journals, and its institutional conduct shaped what the strategies became in practice. The Cuban leadership, after nineteen fifty nine, used its prestige to press the armed road on the continent's parties, most formally at the Tricontinental Conference in Havana in nineteen sixty six and at the founding conference of the Latin American Solidarity Organisation in nineteen sixty seven, which endorsed armed struggle as the region's fundamental path and pointedly diminished the parties that counselled otherwise. The Soviet aligned communist parties resisted, defending the peaceful road in the vocabulary of their own doctrine, and the quarrel between Havana and the parties ran through the decade as a public argument about which capital's experience generalised. Venezuela became the quarrel's sharpest case: its communist party, after participating in the armed struggle of the early sixties, withdrew toward legality as the insurgency waned and the government offered an electoral opening, and Havana's denunciation of the withdrawal, and the party's defiant reply, marked the moment when the armed road's continental sponsorship and the parties' national calculations openly parted.

Inside individual parties the argument produced a recognisable anatomy. A leadership formed in legality, holding unions, newspapers and parliamentary seats, confronted a youth wing for whom those assets were evidence of corruption by the system; the youth demanded the foco; the leadership warned of annihilation; the youth split, or were expelled, and founded the armed organisation; and the state then repressed both, citing each as proof of the other's nature. Chile's Socialist Party supplied the anatomy in an unusual form, since its nineteen sixty seven congress at Chillán adopted a resolution declaring revolutionary violence inevitable and legitimate, while the party simultaneously contested elections and prepared the coalition that would win in nineteen seventy; the Chilean left thus carried both roads inside single organisations, argued between them at the level of families and friendships, and in the end travelled the electoral one with a significant minority of its own cadres unconvinced that legality would be permitted to finish. The comparison's abstraction, two roads, should not be allowed to hide this lived texture: the roads were positions in arguments that divided organisations, generations and households, and the bitterness of the later reckonings came from the fact that the arguers had known each other.

### Why did the argument split organisations rather than settle between them?

Because each road indicted the other's custodians. To adopt the foco was to declare the party apparatus an obstacle; to defend legality was to declare the foco's founders adventurers spending other people's lives. Positions that accuse their holders cannot be compromised at a congress, so the argument reproduced itself by fission, each split founding a purer organisation.

The journals carried the argument's theoretical register. The debates were conducted principally in Spanish and Portuguese, in party newspapers, in the theoretical reviews of the movements and in the exile press, and a later section of this article addresses what that linguistic fact means for a reader confined to English. What matters here is the argument's tempo. The roads were not formulated once and then applied; they were revised under fire, sometimes literally, as each new defeat or near success arrived by radio and newspaper. The Venezuelan withdrawal altered the Chilean debate; the Bolivian disaster of nineteen sixty seven, in which the theory's author died with his column, altered everything, and the armed organisations that persisted into the nineteen seventies were already revisions, urbanised, mass oriented or alliance seeking, of the doctrine of nineteen sixty seven. A comparison that froze the strategies at their founding formulations would grade doctrines nobody still held by the time of the later attempts. This article has therefore graded the mechanisms rather than the manifestos, since the mechanisms, nucleus creating conditions, legality binding the apparatus, survived the revisions intact, and it was the mechanisms that the record tested.

## The record in closer grain

A summary verdict on a dozen countries invites the suspicion that the summariser has flattened the cases, and the suspicion is best answered by slowing down over several of them, so that the reader can watch the mechanism fail at its joints in different terrains. Venezuela's insurgency of the nineteen sixties was among the most serious: it drew in the communist party and a split from the governing party's left, it operated in both mountains and cities, and it faced not a dictatorship but an elected government that combined military pressure with a political offer. The government of Rómulo Betancourt, itself the target of attempts from the right as well as the left, held elections on schedule, and the insurgency's urban constituency, offered a ballot that functioned, drained away; the armed fronts in the hills, deprived of their city's oxygen, were reduced by the army over years. The case is the comparison in miniature inside one country: the electoral mechanism, operated by a reformist government against the left, accomplished what the apparatus alone had not, and the guerrillas' own historians have treated the population's preference for the functioning ballot as the decisive fact. Strategy is graded by the enemy's options as well as by its own, and in Venezuela the state had a political option it used skilfully.

Guatemala's case runs darker and longer. The insurgency that followed the nineteen fifty four overthrow of an elected reformist government, a precursor examined below, began in nineteen sixty with a revolt of junior officers against a corrupt regime, officers whose nationalism the young left then joined and gradually led. The state answered with a counterinsurgency built with external assistance, and the war's geography shifted over two decades between the eastern hills, the capital and the indigenous highlands, while the state's violence expanded from the insurgents to the entire ecology of the legal left, the universities and the unions, an expansion documented afterwards by the historical clarification commission established under the peace accords, to whose findings the reader is again directed. The Guatemalan insurgency was never destroyed in a single campaign as the Peruvian focos were; it was contained, fragmented and outlasted, and the war's formal end in nineteen ninety six registered a balance in which the insurgency could not win and the state could not legitimise its victory. For the comparison, Guatemala is the case that most damages any comforting reading of the armed road's failure as a series of avoidable errors: here the movement corrected errors across thirty years, built alliances, rooted itself in indigenous communities, and still could not defeat or split the apparatus, which in the war's worst years turned its violence on the civilian population of whole regions.

Bolivia concentrates the theory's refutation into a single campaign, because the campaign was the theory's own, led by its author, in a country chosen for its centrality and its revolutionary tradition. Every condition the doctrine required was misread in a way the doctrine itself invited. The peasantry of the region where the column operated had received land in the revolution of nineteen fifty two and its aftermath, and defended its parcels with a suspicion of outsiders that the column's mostly foreign fighters could not dissolve in the time allowed. The miners, the country's storied proletariat, were being simultaneously repressed by the government and were far from the zone of operations. The communist party's leadership, after a meeting with Guevara, withheld the support the plan assumed. The army, forewarned, supplied from abroad and operating with intelligence drawn from the column's own captured documents and deserters, closed the encirclement methodically. The lesson the continent drew was not simply that this foco failed. It was that the doctrine's diagnostic instruments, its way of reading a country's readiness from its grievances, had failed at the hands of their inventor, which suggested the flaw was in the instruments rather than the reading.

Peru's nineteen sixty five focos add a further joint to the anatomy, the question of timing against a reforming state. The organisations that launched them, the Revolutionary Left Movement and the Army of National Liberation, faced a government that was itself enacting a hesitant agrarian reform and that answered the insurgency with a combination of military encirclement and accelerated land distribution in the affected zones. The peasantry of the central sierra, whose rising the focos expected, in several places informed on the columns or stood aside, calculating, as peasants under fire have always calculated, the relative dangers of the army that would remain and the guerrillas who might not. Within months the columns were destroyed and their leaders dead. The Peruvian case also seeded a later and distinct phenomenon, the Shining Path insurgency launched in nineteen eighty from a provincial university, which explicitly rejected the foco in favour of a protracted people's war on Maoist lines; that insurgency's rise and defeat belong to a different strategic family and a later period, and it is noted here only to mark the boundary of this article's subject, and to record that its fate, defeat by an intelligence led state campaign in the nineteen nineties, extended rather than disturbed the pattern the comparison identifies.

Colombia presents the limiting case, the foco neither victorious nor destroyed but absorbed into permanence. Organisations founded in the nineteen sixties, among them the National Liberation Army, founded by students and workers inspired directly by Cuba, and the Revolutionary Armed Forces, which grew from peasant self defence communities of an earlier period of violence, survived in the country's peripheries for decades, taxing, administering and fighting at low intensity, while the Colombian state, never as centralised or as doctrinally unified as the southern cone dictatorships, contained them without eliminating them. For the comparison the Colombian outcome is a distinct cell in the table that polemic on both sides preferred to ignore: the armed road as a durable sub state presence, neither revolution nor defeat, whose costs were paid by the rural population among whom it was embedded and whose existence across generations became a feature of the political landscape rather than a challenge to it. When peace negotiations eventually converted the largest of these organisations into a political party, in the second decade of the following century, the conversion itself restaged the old argument in reverse, an armed organisation travelling the road toward ballots after fifty years, and finding the apparatus question waiting for it in the new form of the security of its demobilised members.

Argentina's experience binds the urban variant to the electoral question in the tightest knot the region offers. The great armed organisations of the nineteen seventies there grew inside and alongside a mass Peronist movement whose leader returned from exile and was elected in nineteen seventy three, so that for a brief period an electoral victory of a movement containing a revolutionary left and an armed strategy pursued by that left's organisations coexisted within one political family. The coexistence collapsed into violence within the movement, the elected leader's death, a successor government besieged by the armed organisations it could neither absorb nor defeat, and then the coup of nineteen seventy six, after which the military government conducted the period's most systematic programme of disappearance directed against the left and its periphery, a campaign later documented by the national commission on the disappeared, whose report stands as the region's foundational document of its kind. Argentine historians continue to argue over the weight the armed organisations' campaign bears in explaining the coup's ferocity and social licence. For this article's narrower purpose, the case shows the two roads not as alternatives but as interacting poisons: the armed campaign supplied the coup with its public rationale, and the coup destroyed the electoral space in which any road, including the armed organisations' own political futures, might have been travelled.

Mexico's armed movements of the nineteen sixties and seventies, in the mountains of Guerrero under leaders like Lucio Cabañas, a rural schoolteacher turned guerrilla, were defeated by the army in campaigns that remained largely invisible to the country's public, within a political system whose dominant party absorbed opposition electorally rather than confronting it in the open. The Mexican case matters to the comparison for its sequel: it was from the survivors and students of that defeated generation, and from the indigenous communities of the southeast, that the third road described above was eventually composed, so that Mexico holds both the armed road's defeat and the doctrine that renounced the objective for which it had been fought.

## The second wave in Central America and the negotiated endings

The armed road's largest wars came after the classical foco period, in Central America in the nineteen eighties, and their scale obliges the comparison to test itself against them, since a critic might argue that the focos failed merely for want of size. In El Salvador, the Farabundo Martí National Liberation Front, a coalition of five organisations, fielded forces that held territory, mounted a major offensive against the capital in nineteen eighty nine, and fought the state's army, rebuilt and supplied on a vast scale from abroad, to a standstill. In Guatemala, the insurgent organisations united in a single command and rooted themselves in the highlands before the army's campaigns of the early eighties devastated the communities among whom they operated. These were not focos in the classical sense. They were protracted wars waged by mass organisations with political programmes, closer in form to the insurrectional alliance stretched across a decade. And their endings are the comparison's point. Both wars ended in negotiated settlements, in El Salvador in nineteen ninety two and in Guatemala in nineteen ninety six, settlements mediated internationally, in which the insurgencies demobilised, became legal political parties, and accepted the electoral road they had spent a decade fighting to transcend, while the states accepted reforms, truth commissions and the formal subordination of their armies that the settlements prescribed. Neither side had won the war for the state. The apparatus had not been defeated; it had been bargained with, from a stalemate, under external sponsorship, at a cost in civilian life that the truth commissions of both countries later documented and that this article, by its protocol, does not enumerate.

### What did the negotiated endings prove?

They proved that even the armed road at its maximum scale, with mass support, territory and a decade of war, arrived at the electoral road's starting point, the ballot and the legislature, by negotiation rather than victory, and that the apparatus survived every war waged against it in the period, to be reformed, where it was reformed, by politics.

The Salvadoran sequel contains the period's most quietly devastating footnote for both roads. The former insurgency, converted into a party, eventually won the presidency by election, in two thousand and nine, seventeen years after the settlement, and governed within the constitution the war had amended. The electoral road, in other words, delivered to the armed road's veterans the office that arms had not, at the price of the moderation the settlement had imposed and the transformation's postponement beyond recognition. Partisans of each road read the footnote as vindication: the electoralists, that ballots outlast bullets; the veterans, that only the war had made the ballot real. The comparison reads it as the two roads converging, late and at ruinous cost, on the discovery that the state in the region could be entered by election and pressured by arms but that its coercive core yielded, in the whole period, to neither, only to bargains struck when the apparatus itself, or its external sponsors, calculated that bargaining was cheaper than war.

## Earlier lessons the theorists had in front of them

The comparison has a prehistory, and recovering it protects the finding from the charge that the obstacle was invisible until it struck. In nineteen fifty two, Bolivia's Nationalist Revolutionary Movement, having won an election whose result the ruling junta annulled, rose in arms; the army was defeated in three days of fighting by miners' and workers' militias and police who sided with the rising, and the victorious government dissolved the old army, enfranchised the indigenous majority, nationalised the tin mines and decreed agrarian reform. Here, uniquely in the region's century, the apparatus question received a practical answer: the coercive institution was destroyed as a corporate body, by a broad insurrection in which its own ranks split, and replaced. The theorists of the nineteen sixties knew the Bolivian case intimately. It stood behind the foco's hope that armies could disintegrate, and behind the electoralists' fear of what followed when they did, since Bolivia's revolution was contained within a decade by the reconstitution of its army under external patronage, and the reconstituted army was the one that destroyed Guevara's column in nineteen sixty seven, a circle the continent's strategists contemplated with awe.

Guatemala supplied the prehistory's darker lesson. Between nineteen forty four and nineteen fifty four the country travelled an electoral road avant la lettre: elected reformist governments, the second under Jacobo Árbenz, enacted an agrarian reform that reached the great estates, including those of the United Fruit Company, and in nineteen fifty four the government was overthrown by a small invading force whose victory was delivered less by its arms than by the Guatemalan army's refusal to defend the government and by the external sponsorship that organised the operation. Árbenz, who had declined to arm the peasant and worker organisations that might have resisted, resigned when the officer corps withdrew its obedience. The Chilean coalition studied this case explicitly; it was the standing exhibit in the argument over whether to distribute arms to supporters, and its lesson was read in both directions, as proof that unarmed governments fall and as proof that arming supporters merely hastens the confrontation. Either way, by nineteen seventy the electoral road's central danger had been demonstrated in the region, in a case with an elected reformist government, a land reform and an army that stood aside for its removal, nineteen years before the Chilean test began.

The Dominican Republic in nineteen sixty five added the prehistory's final term, the external one. A constitutionalist revolt restored an elected president who had been overthrown two years earlier, and the armed forces split, with a substantial portion of the officer corps and enlisted ranks joining the constitutionalists, who held much of the capital. The apparatus had done what neither road's theory expected: it had divided against itself on a question of constitutional legitimacy. The division was then overridden from outside, by an intervention of the United States, landed to prevent a second Cuba, which imposed a settlement and an electoral outcome acceptable to Washington. For the comparison, the Dominican case completes the prehistory's syllabus. The apparatus could be defeated where it disintegrated, as in Bolivia; it could remove an elected government by abstention, as in Guatemala; and where it split, the hemisphere's dominant power reserved the right to decide the split. The strategists of the nineteen sixties theorised in the presence of all three lessons. That they nevertheless produced theories in which the apparatus figured as brittle or as bindable is the intellectual puzzle at the centre of the period, and it is explained, in part, by hope's arithmetic: each movement read the prehistory for the lesson its own road required, the foco theorists dwelling on Bolivia's three days, the electoralists on the decades of Chilean legality, and each filing the other lessons under the heading of the exceptional.

## Where the two theories came from

Neither road was invented in the region out of nothing, and their genealogies explain some of their blind spots, since each imported assumptions from the experience that had formed it. The electoral road descended, in part, from the doctrine of the peaceful road proclaimed to the world communist movement in nineteen fifty six, when the Soviet leadership allowed that transitions to socialism might in some countries proceed through parliamentary forms, and from the Italian party's elaboration of a national road suited to a country with strong democratic institutions. Latin American parties that adopted the line were not merely obeying a signal from abroad; the line ratified what their own histories had taught them, decades of legal accumulation punctuated by proscriptions they had survived by patience. But the imported doctrine carried an assumption formed in Europe: that the state in question was a settled national state whose armed forces were integrated into a constitutional order older and stronger than any government. Transplanted to countries where armies had founded and dissolved governments within living memory, the assumption travelled badly, and the parties that held it were slow to re-examine it because it was woven into their organisational identity, their archives of martyrs to legality and their suspicion of the young.

The armed road's genealogy ran through the anti-colonial wars of the postwar decades as much as through Cuba. Its theorists read Algeria, Vietnam and China as proofs that determined insurgencies defeated modern armies, and they read the founders' Russian texts, the insurrectionary chapters, with the partisan wars of Europe in mind. The genealogy carried its own misfit. The victorious insurgencies of Asia and North Africa fought colonial or client regimes whose metropolitan sponsor could be exhausted politically, at a distance, by a population that was the overwhelming majority and a countryside the state barely administered. The Latin American states of the nineteen sixties were none of those things: they were sovereign republics a century and a half old, with national armies rooted in their own societies, capitals that administered the countryside through parties, churches, markets and schools, and a sponsor whose commitment to the hemisphere was not a colonial embarrassment but a declared first principle of its world position. The foco's theorists were not ignorant of these differences; Debray and Guevara both discussed the continent's specificity at length. But the emotional template of the age, the guerrilla as the figure of the century's hopes, did the argumentative work that analysis declined to do, and organisations were founded on the template.

Between the two genealogies stood the mediating figures whom the polemic tended to erase, and a fair comparison should restore them for a paragraph. Marta Harnecker, a Chilean sociologist formed in Paris, wrote the era's most widely read Marxist primers, texts that taught a generation the vocabulary of historical materialism, and then devoted herself to documenting and theorising the Chilean process from inside its coalition, and after its destruction to interviewing the survivors of both roads across the continent, guerrilla commanders and electoral ministers alike, in a decades long project of collective stocktaking. Her career embodies the possibility the period's organisations mostly refused: that the two roads were hypotheses held by comrades, to be graded together when the evidence arrived. That the grading was left largely to an individual scholar's interview project, rather than performed by the movements' own institutions, is the organisational failure noted earlier in this article, visible here in biographical form.

### Why does the genealogy of a strategy matter to its grade?

Because a strategy imports the experience that formed it as a set of silent presuppositions, and it fails, when it fails, at the presuppositions rather than at its explicit claims. The electoral road presupposed a European constitutional state; the armed road presupposed a colonial war. The region supplied neither, and the imports were graded accordingly.

The genealogies also explain the argument's strange immunity to the prehistory set out above. Bolivia nineteen fifty two and Guatemala nineteen fifty four were not unknown; they were unusable, because each genealogy had already classified them. For the peaceful road's doctrine, Guatemala was an imperialist aggression, a crime from outside that proved nothing about the road itself; for the foco's doctrine, Bolivia was a bourgeois revolution that proved the masses could defeat an army, while its sequel, the army's reconstruction and return, was filed as a separate story. A theory that classifies its counterexamples in advance has made itself comfortable at the price of becoming untestable, and the strategic literature of the period, read whole, is full of this comfort on both sides. The comparison this article conducts is possible only because the region ran the experiment anyway, outside the theories' filing systems, and the results were recorded by the institutions, the commissions and courts of a later period, that the theories had not foreseen either.

## Who filled the columns and the coalitions

A comparison of strategies can become bloodless if it forgets that strategies are carried by people whose social composition shaped what the strategies could do. The focos were disproportionately organisations of the young and the educated: university students, young professionals, seminarians and teachers, joined in the countryside by peasants in numbers that varied by country and were everywhere smaller than the theory's diagrams required. The composition had consequences the theorists acknowledged too late. Students made brave fighters and poor quartermasters; the movements' logistics, medicine and intelligence, the unglamorous systems on which survival depended, were chronically weaker than their combat morale. The urban support networks, on which the columns depended for everything from boots to couriers, were staffed heavily by women whose names the later literature long omitted, and the repression's records, and the commissions that later read them, restored a picture of movements sustained by a hidden infrastructure of safe houses, false documents and prison visits that the heroic literature had rendered invisible. When the networks were penetrated, as they repeatedly were, the columns starved in the hills within weeks, a sequence so regular across countries that it deserves to be named a law of the armed road's operation: the foco died in the city before it died in the mountains.

The electoral coalitions had the opposite composition and the opposite pathologies. They were built from parties with decades of accumulated membership, unions of industrial workers, miners and transport workers whose strikes were the coalition's muscle, teachers and public employees, and a penumbra of the professional middle class. Their strength was exactly the accumulation the foco despised: they could deliver votes, crowds and stoppages on a scale no insurgency approached, and their weakness was the accumulation's price, bureaucracies with interests in survival, leaders with careers inside the institutions, and a consequent tendency, visible throughout the Chilean process, to fear the movement's own uncontrollable energies almost as much as the enemy's. The base organisations of the Chilean years, the neighbourhood supply committees and the industrial cordons that linked factories across districts, were the coalition's attempt to grow an organ that was neither party nor union, and their fate condenses the electoral road's dilemma: the government alternately encouraged them as the revolution's popular power and restrained them as a threat to its legality, and in the final crisis they were neither armed enough to defend the process nor dissolved enough to escape its enemies' attention.

### What did the two movements' compositions predict about their failures?

The armed organisations were built for the sprint of the example and against the marathon of supply, so they failed logistically before they failed politically. The electoral coalitions were built for accumulation and against rupture, so they failed at the moment that required rupture's instruments. Each movement's sociology was its strategy's fate written in advance.

Peasants, workers and students also moved between the roads in ways the organisational histories understate. The same Chilean student who distributed the coalition's leaflets in nineteen seventy was, in the dictatorship's prisons or in exile, re-arguing the strategy question with the same Argentine or Uruguayan counterpart who had chosen the other road, and the exile communities of the later nineteen seventies became the comparison's first real seminar, conducted in the apartment blocks of Mexico City, Caracas, Havana, Madrid and Stockholm by people who had paid for their hypotheses. Much of the reassessment described in the next section was drafted in those rooms, and its tone, grief stricken, unsparing and notably free of the old polemic's satisfactions, reflects the seminar's membership. The strategies had been theories; in exile they became biographies, and the grading changed accordingly.

## How the survivors reassessed

The strategic argument did not end with the defeats; it changed its venue and its tone. Through the later nineteen seventies and the nineteen eighties, as the dictatorships themselves began to exhaust their projects and transitions to electoral rule opened across the region, the survivors of both roads conducted a reassessment whose documents are among the most honest the century's left produced anywhere. The Chilean case generated the largest literature. A current that came to be called the renovation, formed largely in exile, argued that the coalition's failure had not been caused by its legality but by its conception of politics itself: a vanguardist certainty that history had assigned the left the leading role, an underestimation of democracy as a value rather than a terrain, and a failure to build the broad social and political majority that alone could have defended the process. The renovation's critics, including veterans of the armed organisations and of the coalition's left wing, answered that the reassessment dissolved the specific question, the apparatus, into a general sermon about moderation, and that a process so defended would have transformed so little that its survival would not have been worth the name. The argument was never resolved, and it structured the Chilean left's reconstruction under the transition, in which the renovated parties governed for two decades in coalition, administering a market economy whose foundations the dictatorship had laid, while their critics founded new organisations to their left. Both heirs of the reassessment can be seen, from the comparison's vantage point, as answers to the common failure that decline to name it: the renovators answered by lowering the transformation until the apparatus could tolerate it, the critics by insisting the question remained open.

The armed road's survivors conducted a parallel reassessment with less institutional sequel. Former commanders and theorists, in interviews, memoirs and the interview projects mentioned above, conceded with remarkable frankness the foco's diagnostic failures: the peasantry misread, the state underestimated, the party question evaded. What many would not concede was the inference their critics drew, that the armed road as such had been refuted, since the Central American wars had shown, in their reading, that a corrected armed strategy, mass based, politically led, alliance seeking, could fight the region's armies to a standstill and negotiate from strength. The electoral veterans answered that a standstill purchased at that cost, ending in the acceptance of elections, was the electoral road's vindication written in the armed road's blood. The reader will recognise the shape: the argument of the nineteen sixties, restated by its survivors over its own results, each side exhibiting the same record as its trophy. It is the strongest evidence available for this article's claim that the argument was never resolved by the record, and it is why the article has insisted on grading mechanisms rather than exhibiting outcomes. The mechanisms converge where the exhibitions diverge. Nucleus creating conditions: unconfirmed in every trial. Legality binding the apparatus: unconfirmed in its strongest trial. Apparatus as the decisive variable: confirmed in all.

### What conditions would each road have needed?

The armed road would require an apparatus already disintegrating, a population already in motion and an alliance far broader than a nucleus, which is to say it would require the insurrection's conditions it claimed to be able to replace. The electoral road would require an apparatus genuinely subordinated, or a majority so broad and mobilised that termination costs more than toleration.

Those requirements, stated together, are the comparison's practical residue. They do not amount to a third strategy, and the article does not pretend otherwise. They amount to a checklist of conditions, any one of which a future strategist in the region would have to verify rather than assume, and whose verification the founders of both roads replaced with theory. The foco assumed the apparatus's brittleness; the coalition assumed its subordination; the assumptions were the strategies' true foundations, and the record's service is to have converted them from assumptions into questions. A movement that begins by asking, of its own country and decade, how the coercive institution is composed, what it believes, who supplies it, what would split it and what it would cost it to act, has learned the period's lesson in the only form in which lessons survive their period, as an interrogative discipline rather than a doctrine.

The counterfactuals that haunt the literature deserve a brief, disciplined visit before the article closes its analytical sections, because they mark the boundary between the comparison's findings and speculation. Would the Chilean process have survived had it armed its supporters or purged the officer corps in its first year? The comparison cannot say; it can say that such measures would have abandoned the strategy being tested and begun a different one, whose own record, in the region, is the civil wars of Central America, and that the men who declined the measures did so knowing the Guatemalan precedent and judging, on grounds their critics have never fully answered, that a confrontation in nineteen seventy one would have been lost faster and bloodier than the one that came in nineteen seventy three. Would the focos have fared better with the patience their founder prescribed, slower accumulation, deeper peasant work? The comparison can say that the organisations which approximated that patience, in Colombia and Central America, purchased duration rather than victory, and that duration's price was the transformation of the struggle into a permanent feature of the landscape its founders had hoped to change. Counterfactuals of this kind are not idle; they are how the survivors thought, and how the next generation will. But they are graded by the comparison's discipline or they are graded by loyalty, and the article has tried to keep them in the first court.

## The founders' texts and the use made of them

Both roads claimed the founders, and the claims repay a short audit, because the textual situation explains why the argument could not be settled by authority and had to be settled, if at all, by experiment. The armed road's central text was the lesson drawn from the Paris Commune and codified by Lenin in nineteen seventeen: that the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready made state machinery and wield it for its own purposes, that the bureaucratic and military machine must be broken up. Read as the foco read it, the passage licensed the conclusion that the apparatus was an enemy object, to be destroyed from outside by a force built for the purpose. The electoral road's central texts were the founders' own qualifications: Marx's observation, in a speech at Amsterdam in eighteen seventy two, that in countries like America and England the workers might attain their goal by peaceful means, and Engels's late introduction acknowledging that the old style rising had become obsolete while legal agitation had carried the German party to strength. Read as the coalition read them, the passages licensed the conclusion that the founders themselves had treated the road as a question of national conditions, not of dogma.

The audit's finding is uncomfortable for both custodians. The founders' corpus genuinely contains both lines, because the founders were arguing across fifty years of changing states, and the Latin American parties that quoted one line against the other were engaged in proof texting of a kind the founders' own practice, concrete analysis of concrete conditions, forbade. Lenin's state text was written about a collapsing empire in wartime; Marx's Amsterdam remark was made about two countries without large standing armies or entrenched bureaucracies, a description no Latin American republic of the nineteen sixties fitted comfortably. The honest inheritance from the founders was not an answer but a method, and the period's tragedy includes the fact that both roads, while invoking the method's name, substituted a quotation for its exercise. When this article grades the strategies by their presuppositions against the record, it is applying, belatedly and from outside, the discipline the arguers claimed as their birthright.

### Are arms the operative variable in taking power?

The period's evidence answers in a form neither road wanted. Power was taken without arms where the apparatus disintegrated or abstained, and was not taken with arms where the apparatus held, so the operative variable was never the presence of arms in the movement's hands but the condition of the arms in the state's.

That formulation should be handled carefully, because it can be misread as a counsel that movements need only wait for apparatuses to rot. Apparatuses rot for reasons, among them the pressure of movements themselves, and the insurrectional cases show pressure and rot interacting. What the formulation excludes is the two simpler morals the polemic preferred: that arms are the answer, refuted by a dozen destroyed columns; and that arms are the obstacle, refuted by the elected governments removed at gunpoint. The comparison leaves the question of means open and closes the question of the obstacle, which is the most a historical audit should promise.

## The external sponsor in both calculations

Each road also carried a theory of the United States, and the two theories failed in mirror image, which is worth a section because the failure is part of the common failure's structure. The foco theory held that imperialism was overextended, fighting in Asia, contested everywhere, and that a continent of focos would multiply its fronts beyond its capacity to respond, while each intervention it mounted would advertise the insurgency's importance and recruit for it. The calculation inverted the actual relationship. The hemisphere was the one region where the sponsor's commitment was unconditional and its logistics short; interventions and assistance programmes there were cheap relative to its Asian war, and they were administered by institutions built for the purpose over decades. The electoral theory held the opposite hope: that a scrupulously legal, elected government, respectful of forms and of foreign property's compensated transfer, would raise the political cost of intervention beyond what Washington would pay, in an era when the sponsor publicly preached democratic development for the hemisphere. The documentary record assembled afterwards by the United States Senate's own inquiry into covert action in Chile showed the calculation's error in documentary form: the decision to make the Chilean economy scream, in the phrase the record preserves, the funding of opposition media and organisations, and the encouragement of the military's interventionist wing were undertaken against a government whose legality was not in question, because the transformation's direction, not its legality, was the offence. The reader is directed to that published record for the detail; its bearing here is that both roads mispriced the sponsor, the armed road by assuming his distraction, the electoral road by assuming his scruples, and the apparatus each road faced was, in consequence, stronger and freer than either theory had budgeted for.

## The apparatus's own answer: the national security state

The comparison has treated the apparatus as an obstacle, but in the period's second half the apparatus became an author, and its authorship is the outcome against which both roads' failures should finally be measured. Across the southern cone and Brazil, the armed forces did not merely terminate left governments and destroy insurgencies; they took power themselves and attempted a refoundation of their societies on the doctrine of national security, restructuring economies, rewriting constitutions, and reorganising the state as a permanent counterinsurgency. The coordination among several of these regimes, later documented under the name of Operation Condor, extended the apparatus across borders, so that exiles discovered the obstacle had become regional in the same years the left's strategies had failed to. The dictatorships' own ends belong to other articles, but their existence reframes the comparison's stakes. The question the two roads contested was who would refound the region's states. The answer the period gave was: the apparatus, in the countries where the contest was sharpest, refounded them against both roads, and the transitions that followed were negotiated with the apparatus as a surviving party, not conducted over its grave. Even the period's ending, therefore, confirms the finding. When the dictatorships withdrew, they withdrew on terms, with amnesties, reserved domains and tutelary constitutions, the apparatus bargaining from strength it had never lost. A strategy debate that had begun by asking how the left might take the state ended by discovering that the state, in its coercive core, had taken the period.

### Why does the dictatorships' durability matter to the comparison?

Because it excludes the last consolation available to both roads, that the apparatus won battles but lost the period. It won the period in the countries where it governed, set the terms of its own withdrawal, and survived into the democracies that followed as an institution with prerogatives intact, which is the measure of what the strategies were up against.

The intelligence dimension of that durability deserves separate emphasis, since it was the apparatus's decisive arm against both roads and the least theorised by either. Against the foco, the work was penetration: informers in the support networks, agents inside the exile communities, documents captured and exploited faster than the movements could change their ciphers and safe houses. Against the electoral road, the work was preparation: the mapping of the government's supporters, the lists that the commissions later found ready at the moment of the coups, the cultivation of civilian allies who would request intervention and staff it. Neither road's theory had a chapter on the enemy's intelligence service as an institution with its own memory, files and patience, longer than any campaign and any government. The foco treated secrecy as a technique its fighters could practise; the coalition treated legality as a publicity that protected it. The files outlasted both, and in several countries the transition's bargains left the files, and their keepers, substantially undisturbed, a continuity the region's democracies have lived with since.

## Land, reform and the moving countryside

Underneath the strategic argument lay a rural transformation that kept altering the ground on which both roads were planned, and a section on it is owed to the reader because it explains several defeats that pure strategy talk attributes to error. The foco presupposed a peasantry of tenants, labourers and squatters on great estates, land hungry and available for organisation by whoever arrived with a programme and protection. That peasantry existed, but the states of the region, instructed by the Cuban example and financed through the Alliance for Progress, set out to change it before the guerrillas could organise it. Land reform became the era's universal counterinsurgent language: enacted radically in some places, fraudulently in others, but everywhere proclaimed, with titles, credits and colonisation schemes that gave the state a rural presence and a rural clientele it had previously lacked. Peru's military government after nineteen sixty eight carried out one of the continent's most sweeping agrarian reforms, expropriating the coastal sugar estates and the highland haciendas that the insurgents of nineteen sixty five had expected to rise against, and thereby abolished, by decree from a barracks, the social formation on which an entire strategy had been predicated. The spectacle of the apparatus as reformer, delivering the revolution's agrarian programme in uniform, was the period's deepest irony, and neither road's theory had a category for it: the foco had expected the state to defend the latifundio to the death, and the electoralists had expected land reform to be their own gift to bestow.

The countryside was also changing from below in ways that dissolved the classical subject of both strategies. Migration to the cities, already the region's dominant demographic fact, converted the rural question into an urban one of settlements, informal work and municipal services, terrains on which the foco's geography was useless and on which the electoral road's union based parties were only partly at home. The new urban poor were organised, where they were organised, by churches, by neighbourhood associations and later by movements of the landless and the homeless that owed little to either road's organisations. A strategist reading the region in nineteen eighty faced a society whose working population was increasingly informal, whose peasantry was increasingly a minority, and whose largest cities held populations the size of the nations the founders had theorised about. The strategies' failure against the apparatus is this article's finding; but even had the apparatus yielded, the strategies would have met, behind it, a society that had been quietly ceasing to match their diagrams. The complete audit of the period records both, and assigns the first the decisive role in the outcomes, since it was the apparatus, not demography, that killed the columns and removed the governments.

### How did land reform function as a strategy against both roads?

It competed for the constituency each road needed. Against the foco it drained the countryside's readiness to shelter a column, by giving the state a reforming presence among the peasants the column came to organise. Against the electoral left it occupied the programme's centre, letting governments of the centre and even of the barracks claim the transformation's most popular measure.

The religious field intersected the same competition. The base communities and the theology of liberation, treated elsewhere in this cluster, built an organised presence among the urban and rural poor that neither road's parties controlled, and the dictatorships' repression fell on catechists and priests as on the left generally, a fact the commissions documented. For the strategic comparison, the church's importance is that it constituted a third infrastructure of popular organisation, older than both roads and more durable than either, and its existence qualifies any account in which the left's two strategies exhaust the period's popular politics. The roads competed for the allegiance of populations who also prayed, organised and endured in institutions neither road founded, and the populations' own judgements, delivered in their choices between the column, the coalition, the parish and simple survival, are the comparison's final electorate.

## The economics each road carried

Strategies are also economic programmes in waiting, and the comparison is incomplete until the programmes are set beside the mechanisms, because in the electoral case the programme's progress was itself one of the causes alleged for the strategy's end. The foco's economics were the economics of the liberated zone: agrarian reform decreed and enacted in the territory the column held, rents abolished, the landlord's and the company's authority replaced by the movement's, with production continuing under new management. In the Sierra Maestra the rebel administration issued an agrarian reform law for its territory in nineteen fifty eight, and the gesture's importance was proleptic: it showed the peasantry the future in miniature. The foco's weakness as economics was the obverse of its weakness as war: the zones were small, poor and besieged, their reforms unenforceable beyond the column's range, and no foco ever held territory long enough or rich enough for its economic administration to be tested as a system. The electoral road's economics, by contrast, were tested at national scale and at speed, and their testing generated the period's most contested causal argument.

In Chile the government redistributed income sharply upward in its first year by wage policy, expanded public employment and consumption, nationalised copper and much of banking and industry, and drove the agrarian reform through the countryside. By its second and third years the economy was in severe disorder: shortages, a flourishing black market, runaway price rises and a fiscal deficit financed by emission, a disorder its enemies attributed to the transformation's inherent incoherence and its defenders to a siege economy, credit denied abroad, investment struck at home, and a truck owners' stoppage, financed in part from outside as the documentary record later showed, that paralysed distribution in a country strung along a single highway. This article takes no side in the attribution, because its protocol excludes the figures by which the argument is usually conducted and because the attribution is not needed for the comparison. What the comparison needs is the interaction between the economic disorder and the apparatus question, and the interaction is this: the disorder supplied the coup with its civilian constituency and its public rationale, broadening the coalition that requested or welcomed intervention from the expropriated to the much larger public that experienced the process as chaos. The electoral road's theory had assumed that delivering benefits would multiply its defenders faster than the transformation multiplied its enemies. The Chilean sequence suggested a refinement its theorists had not priced: the transformation's costs arrived universally and immediately, in queues and shortages, while its benefits arrived particularly and were soon absorbed into expectation, so that by the third year the government's social base was being asked to defend, with sacrifices, gains it had ceased to notice. A strategy that must survive its own economics long enough for legality to bind the apparatus needs an economics that does not spend its legitimacy faster than the legality accrues, and stating that requirement is easy only in retrospect.

### Did Chile fall because of its economics or because of the apparatus?

The comparison treats the question as badly posed when either answer is offered alone. The economics determined how wide the coalition for termination became and how much of the government's own base could be mobilised in its defence; the apparatus determined that termination was available as an act, at a moment of its choosing, against a government without the means to resist it.

The foco's theorists, watching Chile, drew from its economics the conclusion that gradualism was the fatal error, that a revolution must strike the enemy's property and apparatus together, in days, before the siege could organise. The conclusion had the period's characteristic shape: it converted the other road's defeat into the home road's vindication, by assuming away the home road's own unsolved problem, which was surviving the first month in the hills. The Chilean survivors, watching the focos' graves, drew the reciprocal conclusion with equal confidence. The comparison's colder finding is that the two defeats were not alternatives between which history was offering a choice of the lesser, but two faces of one configuration: a region whose states had organised, with external help, to make transformation by any road cost more than any movement of the period could pay, and whose movements, divided by the argument this article has traced, paid the cost in instalments, country by country, each instalment financing the apparatus's education for the next.

## Three national arguments revisited

Brazil condenses the whole comparison into one national tragedy of sequence. Its legal left and labour movement, in the early nineteen sixties, supported a reformist president whose programme of basic reforms, land, education, the vote for the illiterate and the enlisted ranks, alarmed the officer corps and the propertied classes into the coup of nineteen sixty four, a termination of the electoral road before the Chilean test had even been set, executed with external encouragement and followed by two decades of military rule. The armed organisations that arose in answer, Marighella's among them, were destroyed by the early nineteen seventies, their survivors imprisoned, exiled or dead, while the dictatorship, having eliminated both roads, proceeded to an economic refoundation under generals. Brazil then supplied the period's most ironic sequel: the transition back to electoral rule was led in part by the labour movement rebuilt under the dictatorship, and the party founded from that movement, the Workers' Party, eventually won the presidency by election in the following century on a programme of pronounced moderation, the electoral road travelled by the grandchildren of both defeats, at a pace and with limits that the common failure's checklist would have predicted. The Brazilian sequence is the comparison's argument in national form: first the legal road terminated, then the armed answer destroyed, then the apparatus governing alone, then legality returning on the apparatus's terms.

Uruguay tests the comparison against the region's most civic political culture, a small welfare state with old parties, an educated electorate and a tradition of compromise that led its citizens to call it the Switzerland of South America. The Tupamaros, the urban guerrillas whose operations were for years a marvel of clandestine craft, arose inside that culture and reasoned, in the urban strategy's terms, that spectacular action would expose the state's repressive essence beneath its civic surface. The state answered by giving the armed forces charge of the anti subversive war in nineteen seventy two; the Tupamaros were dismantled within the year; and the democracy whose exposure had been the strategy's object was abolished by the military in nineteen seventy three, with the political class, including much of the left, imprisoned or exiled. Uruguay is the urban critics' argument vindicated in its purest laboratory: a country where the legal space was widest, the movement's accumulation deepest and the provocation's cost, the loss of the democracy itself, most total. It is also the case that most damaged the urban strategy's retrospective defence, since the Tupamaros' own survivors, in the democracy restored a decade later, entered electoral politics and won office through the ballot, travelling in their own biographies the road their strategy had helped to close.

Cuba's continental policy after its victory forms the third panel, because the sponsoring state of the armed road was also, in its own conduct, a student of the common failure before the failure had a name. Havana supported insurgencies across the region through the nineteen sixties, trained and supplied them, and quarrelled with the parties that refused the road; but the support was always calibrated by the Cuban state's own survival, withdrawn or moderated when it threatened the island's position, and after the defeats of the decade and the island's deepening integration into the Soviet alliance, the sponsorship cooled into a diplomacy of state relations, including with governments fighting insurgencies Havana had once blessed. The revolution that had been the foco's exhibit thus behaved, as a state, exactly as the comparison would predict: it treated the apparatus question, its own and others', as the decisive variable, husbanded its forces, and declined to stake its existence on the mechanism its theorists had exported. The gap between the doctrine Cuba preached to the continent's youth and the prudence Cuba practised at home was noted bitterly by the survivors of the columns it had encouraged, and it belongs in this article as evidence that the common failure was understood, at the level of practice, even by the road's own sponsors, years before it could be said aloud in the movements' literature.

## The war of interpretation

No sooner were the events complete than their meaning became a battlefield, and the comparison owes the reader a section on the interpreters, because the strategies' afterlives in argument were governed by who got to narrate them. The dictatorships narrated first and with the state's megaphone: the insurgencies as foreign directed criminality, the Chilean government as a chaos from which the nation had been rescued, the repression as a war the other side had started. Those narrations were installed in official histories and school texts, and their purpose was the apparatus's own legitimation, the conversion of its violence into the period's common sense. Against them the exile and solidarity movements built a counter literature of testimony, denunciation and memoir whose moral force was immense and whose analytical habits were formed under the pressure of denunciation's needs: the dead were honoured, the enemy's crimes catalogued, and the strategic questions, what the movements themselves had misjudged, deferred to a later that solidarity's discipline kept postponing. The commissions of the transition period changed the evidentiary basis for everyone, establishing, with the authority of official inquiry, the factual record of the repression in country after country, and their reports remain the reference this article's protocol directs readers to for the scale of what was done. But the commissions were charged with the repression's facts, not with the strategies' grading, and the grading was left to the historians and the survivors, whose seminar, described above, proceeded for decades in the shadow of the two official narrations, the dictatorship's lie and solidarity's loyalty, each of which had reasons to avoid the comparison this article conducts.

The academic literature that eventually performed the grading arrived with its own divisions, and they map instructively onto the old argument. One school, writing the insurgencies' history from their archives and survivors, emphasised the movements' rationality within their situations and the state's violence as the independent variable, a literature that tends, in this article's terms, to restate the objection to the comparison at scholarly length. Another school, writing from the transitions, emphasised the left's own contributions to the catastrophe, its maximalism, its contempt for the democratic centre, its romance with violence, a literature that tends to grade the actors rather than the presuppositions and to find the actors wanting. The comparison offered here declines both emphases while using both literatures, on the principle stated at the outset: grade the mechanisms against the obstacle, record the situations' differences as the objection requires, and refuse to convert the dead into exhibits for a verdict about the doctrine they invoked. The reader who wants the founders and the tradition whole will find them in the article on [Marxism in Latin America as a whole](/2018/10/01/marxism-latin-america-guide/), within which this strategic argument occupied two decades of a much longer history.

### What grade does the common failure give each road?

Evenly, and that is its claim on the reader. It grades the armed road's mechanism as unconfirmed wherever the apparatus was intact, and the electoral road's mechanism as unconfirmed in the case where its conditions were most favourable, and it locates both grades in the same variable, which neither road's theory had placed at the centre of its calculations.

A word should also be said about the comparison's limits as the article approaches its close, because a method that cannot state its limits will be suspected of hiding them. The comparison grades strategies by outcomes across a region and a period; it cannot weigh the roads' indirect effects with the same confidence. The armed road's defeats educated the apparatus, but the armed road's existence also pressed reforms from frightened elites in countries it never threatened directly, and the electoral road's Chilean defeat educated a generation of the world's left in the apparatus question more thoroughly than any text. Effects of that kind are real and unquantifiable, and the article records them as a reservation rather than an argument. It likewise cannot grade the roads as moral choices for the individuals who made them, young people deciding, under dictatorships and proscriptions, what their lives were for; the comparison is an audit of mechanisms, and a mechanism's failure does not make its adherents' courage counterfeit or their reasons contemptible. The dead of both roads are owed that sentence, and the article pays it without qualification.

## The forgotten third actor: the reforming centre

The comparison has been conducted between two roads of the left, but the period's outcomes were co authored by a third actor whose choices both strategies persistently misread: the reforming centre, the Christian democratic and social democratic and populist governments that occupied the electoral space the left hoped to win and the state the guerrillas hoped to fight. In Chile, the Christian Democracy of Eduardo Frei governed from nineteen sixty four with its own programme, a revolution in liberty, enacting a partial agrarian reform, the Chileanisation of copper and a vast expansion of popular organisation through state sponsored unions and neighbourhood bodies. The centre's six years built much of the scaffolding the left's coalition later used, mobilised constituencies the left then had to win from a rival that spoke a language of reform, and demonstrated to the officer corps and the propertied that change could be administered without expropriation, a demonstration whose effect was to raise the threshold at which those classes would tolerate the left's deeper version. In Venezuela, the Democratic Action governments performed the same function across a longer arc, defeating the insurgency politically by holding elections and distributing oil revenue, and thereby teaching the continent's strategists a lesson neither road absorbed: that the state's most effective weapon against both roads was often not its army but its capacity to deliver, through the existing order, a portion of what the roads promised.

The centre's fate is part of the audit. In Chile the centre split under the pressure of the left's government, its left wing departing toward the coalition, its majority moving into opposition and finally into the civilian front of the coup, so that the apparatus's termination of the electoral road was executed with the centre's substantial collaboration. In the southern cone generally, the centre parties were proscribed by the dictatorships alongside the left, their leaders exiled, their organisations dissolved, a fate that retrospectively discredits the centre's own wager, that the apparatus could be used against the left and then returned to its barracks. The three actors of the period thus form a single system of misreadings: the left's two roads each misread the apparatus, and the centre misread it too, imagining it an instrument that could be borrowed. The common failure, stated at its widest, belongs to the period's entire political class, and the left's share in it is distinguished only by the fact that the left, alone among the actors, theorised the apparatus as its central problem and still misjudged it.

### Why did the centre matter to both roads' calculations?

Because it held the constituencies and the legitimacy both roads needed. The foco had to explain why peasants and workers should follow a column when a reforming government offered land and wages by decree; the coalition had to win voters the centre had organised; and the apparatus watched the centre's behaviour for its signal of when intervention would be welcomed.

## The world movement's quarrels imported

The regional argument was also a province of the world movement's quarrels, and the imports distorted it in ways a complete account should register. The Sino Soviet split divided Latin American parties in the early nineteen sixties, founding Maoist organisations that denounced both the Soviet aligned parties' peaceful road and, often, the Cuban foco, in favour of protracted people's war on the Chinese model, a third armed doctrine whose principal regional fruit, Peru's Shining Path, has been noted at this article's boundary. The split's effect on the strategic debate was to multiply its terms while thinning its conduct: organisations now competed for two external patrons' recognition as well as for their own publics, and doctrinal labels became badges in a market of affiliations. Meanwhile the European left's parallel argument, between the Italian party's historic compromise and the armed groups of its own far left, ran on a different continent under a genuinely subordinated military, and the Latin American readers of that argument sometimes imported its conclusions without its conditions, as the genealogies section above described. The comparison's discipline, presuppositions against conditions, is the antidote to the whole traffic in imports, and the period's literature, reread under that discipline, sorts itself with surprising neatness into arguments that verified their conditions and arguments that merely inherited them.

## Exile as the comparison's laboratory

The reassessment described earlier had a geography, and the geography mattered to its conclusions. The exiles of the southern cone washed up in Mexico City above all, where the Mexican state, maintaining its own revolutionary legitimacy, admitted them by the thousand and where the great publishing houses and universities gave them work; in Caracas during its oil rich and democratic decade; in Havana, where the Cuban state housed the armed road's survivors and the electoral road's ministers in the same neighbourhoods and the argument continued over kitchen tables; and in the European capitals whose parties and foundations funded the journals in which the renovation was drafted. Exile performed, involuntarily, the controlled comparison's human version: the survivors of the Chilean coalition, of the Argentine organisations, of the Uruguayan and Brazilian defeats, lived side by side, read one another's balance sheets, and discovered the shape of their shared obstacle in the biographies around them. The literature of the exile is full of the discovery's signature sentence, in its many national variants: we thought our case was special. The Chilean had blamed the coalition's errors, the Argentine the organisation's militarism, the Uruguayan the provocation, the Brazilian the party's timidity; in exile each learned that the neighbouring case, with every error corrected, had ended in the same place. That learning is the emotional archive beneath this article's analytical claim, and it is why the claim, when the survivors' generation finally wrote it down, was received not as a polemic but as a recognition.

Exile also imposed the comparison's discipline of time. The dictatorships lasted longer than any exile's expectation, and the strategies' debate was conducted, in the second decade, by people whose children were growing up in the host countries and whose return depended on negotiations with the apparatuses they had theorised about. The renovation's moderation and the armed veterans' intransigence can both be read as products of that waiting: moderation as the strategy of those who calculated what return would cost, intransigence as the refusal of those who calculated what return on those terms would mean. When the transitions came, they came as the apparatus's bargains, amnesties and reserved domains included, and the returned exiles governed, where they governed, inside constitutions their enemies had written. The comparison's last lesson is therefore biographical as well as analytical. The generation that had posed the question of the state more sharply than any in the region's history spent its maturity administering the state's survival, and its writings, read whole, are the record of an intelligence the equal of its obstacle and a tragedy not reducible to its errors.

## Misreadings this comparison corrects

The article closes its analytical work by naming the misreadings it was written against, since a comparison's value is measured by the errors it retires. The first misreading holds that Cuba vindicates the foco; the founding case was a broad coalition against a disintegrating regime, and the theory was codified afterwards from a simplified memory of it. The second holds that Nicaragua was a foco success; the victory belonged to the insurrectional alliance form, adopted in explicit revision of the foco, against a dynasty that had isolated itself from its own class. The third holds that the electoral road was never really tried; it was tried in the region's strongest democracy, by its most experienced left, for three years, and its termination is a datum, not an excuse. The fourth holds that the two roads exhausted the period's options; the territorial autonomy strategy refused the shared objective, and the centre's reformism, the church's base organisations and the apparatus's own refoundation were all, in their ways, answers to the same question. The fifth holds that the failures prove the region's left was strategically naive; both strategies were elaborated theories, defended in print against sophisticated objections, and their failure against a rebuilt and externally sustained apparatus is a finding about the balance of forces in a period, not about the intelligence of the defeated. A reader who carries those five corrections away has the article's substance, and a reader who wants the substance's evidence has the sections above and the cluster around them.

## The long shadow behind the argument

The two roads argued as though the region's states were a recent problem, but both inherited a century and a half in which the questions they posed had been answered repeatedly in other vocabularies. The republics of the region were founded by armies, and the nineteenth century's politics ran between the pronunciamiento, the barracks declaration that changed governments, and the oligarchic election, in which a restricted suffrage and a managed count seated the landowners' candidates. The left of the twentieth century defined itself against both inheritances: against the coup, by its constitutionalism, and against the managed election, by its demand for the real suffrage and the secret ballot. Its strategies therefore carried a historical pathos that a purely theoretical account misses. The electoral road was not an abstract preference for ballots; it was the claim that the suffrage, won across a century of struggle and extended to women and the illiterate within living memory, could be made to mean what it said. The armed road was not an abstract preference for violence; it was the claim that the barracks, having arbitrated the republics since their founding, would never be argued out of the arbitration, only defeated in it. Both claims had the century behind them, and the century, characteristically, supported both: it offered elections that had seated reformers and coups that had unseated them, armies that had founded nations and armies that had massacred their citizens. The strategists of the nineteen sixties were the heirs of an unsettled estate, and their argument's ferocity owed something to the fact that each side could cite the family history against the other without ever running out of true examples.

That inheritance also explains the argument's characteristic blind spot from a new angle. Both roads thought of the apparatus historically, as the heir of the caudillo's escort and the oligarchy's guard, a force whose political weight came from tradition and class composition. What neither fully registered was the apparatus's modernisation in their own lifetimes: its conversion, through the military academies, the war colleges and the hemispheric training system, from a praetorian tradition into a self conscious profession with a doctrine of society, an intelligence bureaucracy and a corporate project. The generals who governed Brazil after nineteen sixty four and Chile after nineteen seventy three were not caudillos; they were graduates, planners, readers of the same development literature as the civilians they displaced, and their regimes' technocratic character, the economists in the cabinet and the constitutions drafted like engineering specifications, marked the distance between the apparatus the strategies had imagined and the one that answered them. A theory of the enemy formed in one epoch and applied in the next is the definition of strategic anachronism, and the comparison's verdict can be restated in those terms: both roads were, in their different idioms, punctual, argued, courageous anachronisms, and the apparatus was the only actor of the period that was fully contemporary with itself.

## The soldier as a political subject

One further presupposition united the roads beneath their quarrel, and its failure deserves a section because it points beyond the period. Both strategies, at the moments when they imagined victory, imagined the soldier changing sides: the foco's diagrams ended with the army's dissolution as conscripts deserted to the revolution, and the coalition's hopes ended with the officer corps holding to the constitution while the people mobilised. The soldier, private and officer, was the period's most courted and least known political subject. The left's knowledge of the barracks was thin by structure: its organisations recruited in universities, unions and shantytowns, not in regiments, and its picture of military life came from memoirs, doctrine manuals and the behaviour of armies in crises, which is to say from the institution's surface at the moment of its decisions. What the surface concealed was the institution's internal sociology, the academies' formation of a caste consciousness across a cadet's adolescence, the intelligence services' role as the officer corps' political education, the material web of housing, clubs, pensions and arms contracts that bound the institution to the existing order more intimately than any ideology. The cases where soldiers did move, the constitutionalist officers of the Dominican revolt, the junior officers whose nineteen sixty revolt opened Guatemala's insurgency, the Peruvian officers who carried out a reforming coup in nineteen sixty eight and governed as nationalists, show the subject's reality: soldiers acted politically throughout the period, in every direction, as the institution's factions and doctrines disposed them, and the left's two roads, each of which needed a specific faction to prevail at a specific moment, had built no instrument for knowing, let alone influencing, which faction that would be. The apparatus question, pursued to its root, is a knowledge question, and the comparison's most transferable lesson may be that a strategy is only as good as its picture of the institution it must survive, drawn from inside that institution's life rather than from its parade ground behaviour.

## The party question underneath

Underneath the argument about roads ran an argument about organisations, and the comparison should surface it, because the roads implied different parties and the parties behaved as the roads predicted in ways that compounded the outcomes. The electoral road implied the mass party of the old type: dues paying, union rooted, electorally disciplined, with a press, a youth wing and a memory, an organisation built to accumulate across decades and to survive defeats by retreating into its municipalities and unions. Such parties existed, and their virtues were real; but the same structure that made them durable made them cautious, since an organisation with much to lose will discount any strategy that risks the loss, and the armed critics' charge of conservatism had this organisational truth beneath its rhetoric. The armed road implied the opposite organisation: small, clandestine, militarily disciplined, with its memory carried in persons rather than archives, built to survive illegality and to act without waiting for majorities. Its virtues were also real, initiative, commitment, the capacity to begin; and its pathologies mirrored its structure, since an organisation built for secrecy cannot deliberate in public, cannot correct its line by open argument, and substitutes the commander's judgement for the movement's, a substitution the foco theory had raised into a principle when it made the column the party in embryo. When the defeats came, each organisation processed them as its structure dictated: the mass parties by congresses that ratified the leadership's account and expelled the critics, the clandestine organisations by splits and by the elevation of the fallen into martyrs whose example forbade the audit. The common failure thus has an organisational floor. The obstacle was shared, and so was the incapacity to learn from it in common, because the instruments of learning, the open debate, the published balance sheet, the congress that can actually reverse a line, were precisely what both organisational forms had sacrificed, one to survival in legality, the other to survival in war.

### Why could the movements not grade themselves?

Because grading requires an institution that can survive its own verdict. A leadership that has sent a generation into the mountains, or into a coalition it promised would hold, cannot easily pronounce the mechanism mistaken without dissolving its authority to lead the survivors, so the verdict was outsourced to time, to exile and to the historians.

The dependency generation's place in the argument belongs here as well, because the same universities and exile circuits that produced the region's great analysis of its economic subordination also housed the strategic debate, and the two bodies of work judged each other. The dependency writers, several of whom had passed through the parties and some through the governments of the period, supplied the strategies with their picture of the enemy's structure, the dependent bourgeoisie, the associated development, the state as the committee of a class that was itself a junior partner. The strategists returned the favour with a standing reproach: that the analysis explained everything except what was to be done on Monday. The comparison can record, with the period closed, that the analysis has outlived the strategies in the world's seminar rooms, and that this survival is itself a datum for the distinction insisted on throughout, between a doctrine's analyses and its wagers. The wagers were graded by the apparatus. The analyses were graded by the slower court of scholarship, and several of them are still being cited in it, which is what a verdict on Marxism generally, as opposed to a verdict on two of its strategies, would actually have to engage.

## The geography of the two roads

Terrain decided more of the argument than the arguers' texts admit, and a section on the region's geography closes the analytical account by grounding the mechanisms in the ground itself. The foco's map was drawn from the Sierra Maestra and generalised to a continent whose cordilleras, jungles and plains it treated as interchangeable. They were not. The Andes offered altitude, indigenous communities with their own authorities and languages, and distances that exhausted pursuer and pursued alike; the column that survived there did so by becoming a part of a social world its mostly coastal and foreign fighters entered as guests. The Southern Cone's pampas and the Brazilian northeast offered no such shelter, and the attempts made in open country were located and destroyed with an ease that reads, in the campaign histories, like a refutation by landscape. The jungle theatres, in Central America and the Amazon's fringe, offered concealment at the price of disease, isolation and supply lines of heroic length. Against every terrain stood the period's transforming technology of counterinsurgency: the helicopter, which abolished the protective value of altitude and distance that mountain war had relied on since antiquity, the radio network, and the intelligence file. The foco's founders had theorised a war in which geography was the weak state's confession; the states answered by purchasing the means to make geography testify for them instead.

The electoral road had a geography too, less often mapped: the geography of the vote, of mining camps and nitrate fields where the left's unions were states within the state, of industrial belts around the great capitals, of ports and railways whose workers could stop a country's circulation, and of a countryside where the vote was delivered by landlords through their tenants until the secret ballot and the reform laws broke the mechanism. The coalition's strategists knew this map intimately, and their confidence rested on it: a left that held the mines, the ports and the industrial districts held, they reasoned, the country's economic throat, and a coup against its government would face a general strike of indefinite length. The reasoning underestimated the map's other side. The same concentration that made the left's strongholds formidable made them locatable, and the dictatorships' repression proceeded by the map, district by district and union by union, while the economy's throat, in the age of the truck and the imported spare part, proved easier to cut from the government's side than to squeeze from the strikers'. Geography, like the founders' texts and the century's history, supplied both roads with their confidence and both with their refutation, and the comparison's respect for terrain is a form of its general respect for the concrete, the quality whose absence in the theories, beneath their sophistication, the record kept exposing.

## The untranslated debate

A reader confined to English should be told plainly what portion of this argument is available to be read, because the asymmetry has shaped the Anglophone literature's grades. The principal manuals crossed languages: Guevara's writings were translated everywhere, Debray's codification appeared in English within the year, and Marighella's minimanual circulated in samizdat and official translation alike, since governments also studied it. The electoral road's classics crossed too, in part: the Chilean process generated an international literature in real time, and its protagonists wrote for the world's journals as the events proceeded. But the debate as the region conducted it, the party newspapers' polemics, the internal documents, the congress resolutions argued clause by clause, the exile journals' balance sheets, the memoirs published by small houses in Mexico City, Buenos Aires, Madrid and Havana, was conducted principally in Spanish and Portuguese, and most of it has never been translated. The Anglophone reader therefore meets the argument through its manifestos and its enemies, through the texts written for export and the reports written by the embassies and the commissions, and should discount accordingly: the foco looks more purely military, and the electoral road more purely parliamentary, in English than either looked in the languages in which their adherents argued about land, wages, the party and the mass work between the manifestos. The strategies assessed in this article have been graded on the record of outcomes, which translation does not alter; but the reader who proceeds to the sources should know that the record's texture, and much of its self criticism, waits in the two languages of the region itself.

## The Chilean argument conducted in real time

The electoral road's test was also an argument conducted at full speed inside the government itself, and the comparison should exhibit it, because the internal debate rehearsed every position in this article while the events graded them. The coalition contained, from its first day, two strategies wearing one programme. The Communist Party, the coalition's most disciplined component, argued for consolidation: the process must widen its majority, reassure the centre's voters, isolate the coup minded right, and postpone measures that would unite the government's enemies, since a process that survived could deepen later and a process destroyed could deepen never. The Socialist Party's dominant current and the smaller parties of the coalition's left argued the opposite: that the process's survival depended on its momentum, that a revolution which pauses teaches its enemies to organise, and that the popular organisations, the factory cordons and neighbourhood committees, should be developed as a power alongside the state until they could succeed it. Outside the coalition stood the Revolutionary Left Movement, founded by the generation of nineteen sixty five, which supported the government against the right while denouncing its legality as a trap and pressing occupations and armed preparation from the left. The president steered among the positions with the constitution as his compass, refusing both the pause that his communist allies counselled and the rupture his socialist allies demanded, and the steering itself became the strategy's essence and its exposure: a government arguing in public about whether its own road existed.

The argument's conduct refutes, in passing, the retrospective simplification that the coalition sleepwalked to its end. Every danger named in this article was named at the time, in the coalition's press and congresses, by participants who understood the apparatus question in their own vocabularies: the communists' insistence that the armed forces' constitutionalist faction must be cultivated and not provoked, the left's insistence that the same faction was a fiction the officers maintained until they needed it no longer. Both were describing the institution they faced; the institution resolved the description by acting, in September nineteen seventy three, as the left had predicted and the communists had hoped it would not, and the survivors of both positions carried the argument into exile unresolved, where it joined the continental seminar described above. For the comparison, the episode's value is methodological. It shows that the common failure was not a failure of information or of intelligence. The information was available, the intelligence was of the first order, and the argument between the readings was conducted openly and at strength. What failed was the prior question: which reading of the apparatus to stake the process on, when the evidence supported each reading in part and the choice could not be hedged. Strategy, the Chilean argument teaches, is the discipline of staking on a reading of the enemy under uncertainty, and the grading of strategies must leave room, as this article has tried to do, for the fact that the losing reading was not a foolish one.

## The mothers and the form nobody foresaw

The period produced one political form that neither road's theory contained and that altered, in the end, the apparatus's own position more durably than either road had managed: the organised relatives of the disappeared, above all the mothers who began, in nineteen seventy seven, to walk in circles around the pyramid in Buenos Aires's central square, wearing the names of their taken children. The form's power lay in its refusal of both roads' premises. It did not seek state power and did not contest elections; it did not take up arms and did not accept legality's terms, since the legality it faced had abolished the courts' protection; it simply insisted, in public, weekly, for years, on a fact the apparatus had organised its violence to conceal, that the disappeared had existed and had been taken by the state. Against an apparatus whose doctrine required the enemy to be an army or a conspiracy, the mothers were illegible: they could not be negotiated with, infiltrated to any effect, or answered by the exposure of a programme, and their repression, attempted and then restrained by the regime's own calculation of costs, advertised the fact they carried. The human rights movement of which they were the emblem became, across the region, the idiom in which the transitions were negotiated and the commissions written, and its method, the public insistence on documented fact, is the closest the period came to an answer to the apparatus that the apparatus could not shoot. The comparison records the form here not as a fourth road to power, since it refused power's question as completely as the third road did, but as evidence about the obstacle: the apparatus that destroyed columns and removed governments proved vulnerable, across decades, to an enemy it could not classify, armed only with names and photographs and the refusal to disperse. Later movements across the region learned the lesson in their own idioms, and the lesson's presence in this article is its author's judgement that the period's strategic bequest includes it.

## The balance sheet by decade

Walk the period once by its decades and the comparison's shape sharpens into a chronology of the obstacle's education. In the nineteen sixties the armed road was attempted in its pure form and destroyed in its pure form, while the apparatus, instructed by the Cuban shock, rebuilt itself with external assistance into a counterinsurgent profession; the decade ended with the theory's author dead in Bolivia, the urban variant's author dead in São Paulo, and the electoral road not yet tested at full strength, its parties accumulating in the shadow of Brazil's coup, which had already supplied the test's preliminary result. In the nineteen seventies the electoral road received its fullest trial and its termination, the urban and rural armed organisations of the southern cone were destroyed, and the apparatus graduated from obstacle to author, governing the region's major states directly and coordinating its repression across borders; the decade's single armed victory, Nicaragua, arrived in its final year in the insurrectional form the foco had disdained, against a dynasty in collapse. In the nineteen eighties the armed road fought its largest wars in Central America to negotiated stalemates, the dictatorships exhausted themselves against the human rights movement and their own economic failures, and the transitions restored elections on the apparatus's terms; the decade ended with the world's socialist states themselves entering the crisis that would dissolve the external framework within which the whole argument had been conducted. In the nineteen nineties the argument's survivors governed municipalities and then republics by election, the Central American settlements converted insurgents into parties, the commissions published the repression's record, and in a southern Mexican jungle the third road announced that the question itself had been wrongly put. Read as one chronology, the period is the biography of an obstacle: named by neither theory at the start, it educated itself faster than its challengers, survived every strategy addressed to it, dictated the terms on which the period closed, and bequeathed to the region's democracies the unfinished business of its own subordination, which remains, in several republics, the quiet centre of their politics.

### Why is a strategy's record not a verdict on the whole tradition?

The student should separate the wager from the library. A strategy is a wager placed by an organisation at a date, on a reading of its country's apparatus and society; the library is the whole body of analysis the tradition produced, on land, dependence, class, culture and the state, whose volumes are graded by other courts and over longer periods.

That separation, practised consistently, also protects the strategies' own history from a subtler distortion: the temptation to read the period's Marxism as nothing but its roads, and its roads as nothing but their endings. The tradition in the region, taken whole in the guide article this cluster builds on, includes the Peruvian founder's analysis of his country's land question, the dependency school's reconstruction of the world economy's history, the liberation theologians' rereading of their church's obligations, the historians who rewrote the national pasts and the novelists who carried the period's experience into the world's literature. The roads were the tradition's most consequential wagers and its most catastrophic, and this article has graded them without mercy because they asked to be graded by results. But a balance sheet of the tradition that entered only the wagers would be as false as one that entered only the library, and the reader who has followed the comparison to this point is equipped to keep both ledgers, which is the habit the series exists to teach.

## What the comparison leaves open

The article has argued a finding and refused several verdicts, and its closing obligation is to say exactly where the argument stops. It stops at the threshold of prescription. Nothing in the comparison instructs a movement of another period what to do, because the comparison's own discipline, the objection it entertained at full strength, forbids the export of a period's grades into another period's situations. What it exports is a set of questions, ordered by the record's insistence. Before the choice between arms and ballots, which the period debated to exhaustion, stands the question neither road asked first: what is the coercive apparatus of this state, in this decade, composed of, what does it believe about itself, who trains and supplies it, what would divide it, what would unite it, and what, concretely, does the proposed strategy do about the answers? A movement that asks that question first may still choose either road, or the refusal of both, or a form its predecessors did not imagine; the period cannot choose for it. But it will choose with the obstacle in view, which is the one advantage the region's dead, of both roads, purchased for their successors and the one their organisations, in their lifetimes, declined to take delivery of.

There is a last bequest to name, quieter than the checklist, and it concerns the manner of holding a strategy rather than its content. The period's arguers held their roads as identities, so that to revise the road was to betray the dead who had marched on it, and the revision, when it came, had to wait for exile, prison or old age to loosen the identification. A later strategist, reading this record, might resolve to hold the next road as a hypothesis instead: published with its presuppositions attached, graded against dates agreed in advance, and surrendered without dishonour when the grading fails, since the honour was never in the road but in the purpose the road was meant to serve. The dead of both roads are not honoured by the repetition of their wagers. They are honoured by the accuracy of the audit, and by successors who can say, in time and in public, what the survivors could say only in the seminar rooms of their exile: we thought our case was special, and it was not, and the obstacle had a name.

The comparison also leaves open, deliberately, the question of the tradition's worth, because it has argued throughout that the question is not this article's to close. The strategies were the tradition's wagers about power, and they failed against a common obstacle, for reasons that reflect on the wagers, on the organisations that placed them and on the balance of forces in a period, more than on the analyses that accompanied them. The tradition's other products, its readings of land and dependence, its historiography, its theology, its literature, continue to be read because they continue to explain, and the reader who wants the whole account will find it in the cluster this article completes, from the Peruvian founder's essays to the exam guide that follows. What the strategic argument contributes to that whole is a discipline the tradition's other branches can share: state the mechanism, name the obstacle, grade by the record, and concede what the record shows, including, above all, the finding that flatters nobody. The common failure is such a finding. It belongs to no faction, it acquits no leadership, and it is, for those reasons, the most useful sentence the period's terrible seminar produced.

## The scholars of the barracks

While the movements argued, a scholarly literature on the region's military grew up alongside them, and the comparison should acknowledge it, because the scholars named the obstacle with a precision the strategists rarely matched and because their marginality to the debate is part of the period's explanation. The Argentine sociologist José Nun, writing in the middle nineteen sixties, proposed that the Latin American military had become the political party of the middle classes, intervening not as the oligarchy's servant of the old textbooks but as the armed representative of a stratum that feared both the oligarchy above and the organised working class below. The thesis rearranged the strategic map at a stroke: if the officer corps acted for a class project of its own constituency rather than as a hired instrument, then neither the foco's expectation of its collapse nor the coalition's expectation of its neutrality had grasped the institution's motives, since an actor with its own project does not disintegrate when pressured nor abstain when its project is threatened. Other scholars documented the new professionalism, the doctrine formation in the war colleges, the counterinsurgency's intellectual architecture, and the Brazilian school of geopolitics in which the national security doctrine was elaborated as a theory of the state's permanent war against internal subversion. The movements read some of this literature and filed most of it as academic, a category error with a body count, since the scholars were describing, in real time, the institution that was preparing the period's endings. A strategist's library is part of his strategy, and the comparison records that the period's best map of the obstacle was drawn outside the movements that most needed it, in journals they disdained, by writers several of whom the repression later exiled or silenced as thoroughly as it silenced the strategists.

### Why did the movements underrate the scholarship on their enemy?

Because the scholarship arrived without a strategy attached, and movements in a hurry read for instructions. A literature that explained the officer corps' project in detail but prescribed no mechanism for defeating it seemed, to organisations choosing between a column and a coalition, like a commentary on their debate rather than a contribution to it, when it was in fact a correction of both sides.

The scholars' afterlife completes the irony. When the transitions came, it was the literature on the military, on civil military relations and on the reserved domains the departing regimes had entrenched, that supplied the new democracies with their working concepts, and the scholars of the barracks became the advisers of the very electoral politics the movements had theorised. The apparatus question outlived the strategies and became a field of ordinary democratic statecraft, concerned with budgets, promotions, intelligence oversight and the slow subordination of the institution to elected authority, pursued across decades with results that vary by republic. That long, unglamorous labour is, from the comparison's vantage point, the period's actual answer to the common failure, arrived at by exhaustion rather than by theory: neither defeating the apparatus nor trusting it, but reducing it, law by law and budget by budget, in the only temporality the institution could not answer with a coup, the temporality of the ordinary.

## The Caribbean mirrors

The comparison has been drawn on the mainland's large states, and two Caribbean cases test its edges instructively, because they vary the scale while holding the structure. In Jamaica, an elected government of democratic socialist orientation under Michael Manley won office in nineteen seventy two and again in nineteen seventy six, declared a programme of economic nationalism and social reform, and was ground between an external credit squeeze, capital flight, an opposition campaign of destabilising violence in the election season of nineteen eighty, and the electorate's exhaustion with shortages; it lost power at the ballot box, peacefully, to its conservative rival. The Jamaican case is the electoral road's mildest ending and therefore one of its most instructive: no coup was needed, because the apparatus of termination available to a small dependent economy includes the credit line and the import bill, and a government can be removed by its creditors' and suppliers' decisions as thoroughly as by its generals, leaving the constitution intact and the transformation reversed. The comparison's apparatus concept should accordingly be read widely, to include the external economic levers that small states cannot answer, a widening the Chilean case, with its credit denial, already suggested.

In Grenada, the region's smallest stage produced the period's most compressed tragedy. The New Jewel Movement, a revolutionary organisation formed from the island's opposition currents, took power in nineteen seventy nine not by election and not by a foco war but by a nearly bloodless seizure at the moment the discredited ruler was abroad, a coup of the left against a regime whose apparatus barely existed. The revolutionary government that followed built, in four years, an airport with Cuban assistance, a militia, and a programme of social transformation on an island whose entire population would fill a mainland provincial town. It was destroyed from within and without in nineteen eighty three: its leadership split, its deputy prime minister's faction arrested and killed the prime minister and others, and within days the United States invaded, citing the rescue of its medical students, and installed a government of its choosing. Grenada compresses the comparison into a parable. The apparatus question, which on the mainland concerned the movement's own state's army, appears here in its limiting form: a state too small to possess an apparatus capable of resisting its sponsor's displeasure, whose revolution's survival therefore depended entirely on calculations made in another capital. Neither road's theory had a chapter for the micro state, and the island's fate is recorded here as the comparison's outer boundary marker, the point at which the region's strategic categories dissolve into the geopolitics of size.

## The Chilean echo in Europe

The comparison's influence ran outward as well as inward, and its most consequential export was the Chilean coup's effect on the European left's strategy, an effect worth recording because it shows the apparatus question being learned, at one remove, by movements that had not paid the period's tuition. Enrico Berlinguer, the Italian Communist Party's leader, published in the weeks after the coup a series of reflections that drew the lesson explicitly: a government of the left resting on a bare majority, in a country polarised by its programme, invited its own overthrow, and the Italian party must therefore seek a historic compromise with the forces of the centre, however distasteful, broad enough to make the state's apparatus and the country's majority coincide in defending the democratic order. The Chilean road's failure thus became a founding document of Eurocommunism, the attempt by the Western European parties to define a democratic road to socialism distinct from the Soviet model, and the argument between that attempt's heirs and its critics, who charged that breadth purchased by moderation was the transformation's abandonment, restaged the Latin American argument in the opera houses of a continent whose armies were genuinely subordinated. The comparison's discipline applies to the echo as to the original: the Italian presupposition, that breadth could neutralise the apparatus, was formed in a state where the apparatus was not the deciding term, and its export back to Latin America in the renovation literature of the following decade carried the same misfit in reverse. Ideas about strategy travel badly across different conditions of the obstacle, which is, in the end, only the objection to this comparison restated as a law of intellectual trade.

## The period in the region's imagination

The strategies also had a cultural career, and the comparison owes it a closing acknowledgement, because for most of the region's people the argument arrived not as doctrine but as story, song and image, and the forms in which it was imagined shaped what the next generation believed had been at stake. The guerrilla became, through the photography of the Sierra and the poster art that carried a single famous portrait around the world, the period's universal emblem of youthful refusal, an emblem whose career the companion article on Guevara follows and whose effect on the strategic debate was double edged: it recruited magnificently and it taught disastrously, since the emblem showed the fighter and never the supply line, the intelligence file or the third year in the hills. The electoral road produced no equivalent emblem, its iconography being the queue, the rally and the presidential sash, and its cultural monument is instead a literature: the novels and testimonies that processed the Chilean years and the dictatorships, the songs of the new song movement that carried the period's hopes into exile and back, the films made in the host countries by directors who had begun in the process's own cinema. Testimonial literature, the recorded voices of survivors, prisoners, mothers and combatants, became the period's characteristic genre, and its evidentiary ethic, the named witness and the verifiable fact, passed into the commissions' method and from there into the region's historiography. A comparison conducted, as this one is, on the discipline of documented outcomes is itself a late product of that cultural settlement: the period taught its survivors to distrust the emblem and to keep the record, and the strategies assessed table with which this article began is written in the survivors' idiom, not in the idiom of the posters under which the columns marched.

## The argument about violence itself

Beneath the strategic argument ran an ethical one that the comparison should not leave implicit, because for many participants it was the argument that decided their allegiance when the strategic evidence was ambiguous. The armed road's theorists held that violence was not a choice the movement made but a condition the order imposed: the latifundio was violence administered slowly, the dictatorship violence administered openly, and the column merely returned, in concentrated and purposeful form, a fraction of what the poor received dispersed and purposeless. On that accounting, the refusal of arms was not innocence but a preference for the existing distribution of suffering, and the electoral road's clean hands were an illusion maintained by other people's dirty ones. The electoral road's theorists answered in kind. Violence, they held, was the enemy's home terrain, the one contest the apparatus was guaranteed to win and the one language in which it could always outbid the movement; worse, the practice of clandestine violence deformed the organisation that practised it, replacing deliberation with command and the citizen with the combatant, so that the armed road's victories, where they came, carried the germ of the barracks inside the revolution itself. Both arguments were moral before they were military, and both contained a prophecy the period fulfilled: the armed critics foresaw the elected government's helplessness before the coup, and the electoral critics foresaw the armed organisations' internal authoritarianism and the repression their campaigns licensed. The comparison, which has graded the mechanisms, records the ethical argument as the period's most evenly matched, since each side's prophecy about the other came true, and each side's prophecy about itself did not.

## The years the world seemed to move

The strategies were also wagers about a moment, and the moment's atmosphere is part of their explanation, because between nineteen sixty seven and nineteen sixty nine the region, like the world, appeared to its young to be entering a general crisis of the old order. In Mexico in nineteen sixty eight, a student movement that began over police brutality grew into a challenge to the ruling party's whole legitimacy and was answered, days before the Olympic Games opened in the capital, by the army's fire on a rally in the Tlatelolco housing district, an event whose accounting belongs to Mexico's own later investigations and whose political effect was immediate: a generation concluded that the region's most institutionalised civilian regime rested, at the limit, on the same foundation as the dictatorships. In Argentina in nineteen sixty nine, the Cordobazo, an uprising of workers and students in the industrial city of Córdoba, seized the city centre for days against the police and was put down by the army, and the insurrectionary left read it as the foco's urban vindication, proof that the masses would rise when the example was set, while its cooler readers noticed that the rising had no second day and no instrument for converting the streets into power. The two events, read together, are the period in miniature: the state's violence demonstrating the armed road's premise, the rising's evaporation demonstrating its problem, in the same twelve months. Strategists are not responsible for the weather of their age, but they are responsible for their reading of it, and the generation of nineteen sixty eight read a squall as a climate, a misreading the comparison should record with sympathy, since the squall was real, the grievances beneath it were just, and the climate they inferred from it was the one their hopes required.

## The world economy turns

The period's last act was set by a change in the world economy that neither road's theory had budgeted for, and a complete audit must register it, because it altered the value of every asset the strategies held. The oil shocks of the nineteen seventies first enriched the region's petroleum states and flooded its governments with borrowed money, letting dictatorships and democracies alike postpone their reckonings; Venezuela's boom underwrote its democratic stability and its parties' patronage through the decade in which its neighbours fell to coups. Then, in nineteen eighty two, the debt crisis broke over the whole region at once, as interest rates rose and the loans came due, and the dictatorships discovered that the economic refoundation on which they had staked their legitimacy had been financed by the credit that was now withdrawn. The transitions to electoral rule that followed were negotiated in the shadow of the debt, and the insurgencies of Central America fought their last years in economies collapsing under them, while the incoming democracies inherited treasuries whose emptiness limited every programme the returning left proposed. The strategies had been theories of a world of expanding states and commodity booms; the world that graded them last was one of austerity, in which the question of power was increasingly accompanied by the question of what power, over an economy disciplined from abroad, was worth. Later movements inherited that accompaniment as their starting condition, and the distance between their programmes and the programmes of nineteen seventy measures how completely the period's world had ended.

## The prisons as seminar rooms

One institution of the period deserves record as the comparison's strangest venue: the political prison, in which the dictatorships inadvertently assembled the strategic argument's most concentrated readership. Across the region, the prisons and camps held, in enforced proximity and with enforced leisure, the cadres of every organisation and both roads, and the debates that the movements' disciplines had suppressed outside were conducted inside with a freedom born of having nothing left to lose but the argument. Survivors' accounts from the Chilean camps, the Uruguayan military prisons and the Argentine detention system describe the same curriculum: the balance sheet of the process, the reading of the enemy, the quarrel between the roads restaged by men and women who had each paid for one of them, and, in the Uruguayan case, a prison leadership that maintained the organisation's discipline and study across a decade of incarceration designed to dissolve both. The prisons did not resolve the argument; no venue did. But they changed its carriers, since the generation that emerged from them at the transitions had conducted the comparison at the level of cell blocks before it conducted it in print, and the renovation literature's distinctive tone, its refusal of the old certainties, was formed in that curriculum. The apparatus had built the seminar rooms in which its challengers finally compared their defeats honestly, an irony the comparison records as the period's most complete, and as evidence that the obstacle's victory, however total in the field, never extended to the interpretation of the field, which remained contested ground the files could not close.

## Bolivia across the century

No single country contains the whole argument as Bolivia does, and the article returns to it for its closing national panel, because the Bolivian sequence runs through every cell of the table in order. In nineteen fifty two a broad insurrection, miners' militias at its head, defeated the army in open fighting and the victorious movement dissolved the old coercive institution, nationalised the mines and enfranchised the indigenous majority: the apparatus question answered by destruction, in the insurrectional form. Within a decade the revolution's own government had rebuilt an army under external patronage, and that rebuilt army, in nineteen sixty four, took power for itself, and in nineteen sixty seven destroyed the foco that the continent's most famous theorist had led into the country's southeast, in a region whose peasants, enfranchised and landed by the earlier revolution, declined to rise for the later one. In the decades after, the miners' federation that had been the insurrection's spearhead was broken by successive regimes, and the country's indigenous and peasant movements rebuilt their strength by the slowest of the roads this article has surveyed, organising unions, parties and eventually an electoral instrument that carried them to national office in the following century on a programme of resource nationalism, a victory of accumulation over both the column and the coup. Bolivia's century thus rehearses the comparison entire: the apparatus destroyed and rebuilt, the foco refuted on the very ground of a prior revolution, the electoral road travelled at last by the social forces the foco had sought in the hills. A student who masters the Bolivian sequence has the article's argument in one country's biography, and the article leaves the sequence standing as its final exhibit, unadorned by any moral except the one the chronology supplies.

## The Venezuelan exception

One mainland country travelled the period with its electoral order intact, and the comparison owes the exception a section, because an exception that can be explained strengthens a finding more than a clean sweep does. Venezuela emerged from dictatorship in nineteen fifty eight under a pact among its major parties, the Punto Fijo agreement, which distributed ministries, respected electoral outcomes in advance and, crucially, gave the armed forces a guaranteed place and budget inside the settlement rather than a grievance outside it. When the insurgency of the nineteen sixties came, sponsored from Havana and staffed by the communist party and a dissident left, the elected governments fought it with a combination the comparison has met before, military pressure in the hills and political absorption in the cities, and the combination worked because the pact held: no significant civilian actor invited a coup, the opposition that lost elections expected to win later ones, and oil revenue let the state deliver patronage and wages broadly enough to keep the insurgency's potential constituency employed by the system it was asked to overthrow. The Venezuelan apparatus did not abstain from politics out of constitutional sainthood; it was bought into the settlement and watched by parties united in denying it a pretext. Read against the common failure, Venezuela is the control case the comparison needed: where the parties jointly policed the apparatus's incentives and the state's revenue let it outbid the insurgency's promises, both roads failed to be necessary, the guerrillas defeated and the elections unthreatened, and the left's eventual road to national office in the following century ran, as elsewhere, through the ballot and a moderated programme. The exception therefore confirms the finding's mechanism. The variable was never Venezuelan virtue; it was the apparatus's situation inside a settlement that made intervention expensive and uninvited, precisely the condition the other republics' strategies had assumed and not secured.

### Why did Venezuela's democracy survive the decades that broke its neighbours?

Because its founding pact paid the armed forces to stay inside the settlement and paid the parties to police one another's invitations to the barracks, while petroleum revenue let elected governments deliver enough of the insurgency's programme to empty its recruiting grounds. The apparatus had no invitation, no grievance with a constituency and no rival bidder.

## The currents outside the duel

The comparison has followed the period's two loudest positions, but the region's left contained other strategic currents whose exclusion from the duel was itself a fact of the debate, and a fair account should register them before closing. Trotskyist organisations, strongest in Bolivia and Argentina, carried a third inheritance into the argument: in Bolivia their party had been, in nineteen fifty two, a significant force inside the miners' movement whose militias defeated the army, and their tradition therefore spoke of insurrection with a local title deed neither the foco nor the parties could match; their strategic vocabulary of transitional demands and the united front proposed to build the revolutionary situation by a programme that connected immediate grievances to the question of power, a road that was neither the column nor the coalition as the duel defined them. Anarchist and syndicalist survivals, older than Marxism in several of the region's labour movements, persisted in unions and mutual societies and regarded both roads' parties with a suspicion their histories had earned. Indigenous and peasant organisations, finally, conducted throughout the period a politics that the duel's categories fitted worst of all, defending land and community through federations, occupations and negotiations with whatever state presented itself, and producing, in the Bolivian and Ecuadorian highlands especially, the durable mass organisations from which the following century's electoral movements would be built. The duel's two roads claimed to exhaust the strategic imagination of the left; the period's actual left was wider, quieter and longer lived than the duel, and its quieter currents inherited the earth the duelists had contested, a distribution of outcomes the comparison records as its final irony.

## What counted as winning

The comparison has graded outcomes as though winning were self evident, and its closing analytical duty is to interrogate the standard, because the movements themselves disputed it and the dispute explains some of their conduct. By the standard of state power, the period's ledger is the one this article has kept: two insurrectional victories in exceptional conditions, one electoral process terminated, the armed attempts destroyed, the settlements negotiated. By the standard of reforms delivered and retained, the ledger changes: land was distributed in several countries under the pressure the movements created, the suffrage was completed, labour rights were entrenched and later partly dismantled, and the region's political vocabulary was permanently altered, so that even the dictatorships governed in the language of the revolution they claimed to have defeated. By the standard of survival and transmission, the ledger changes again: the traditions that survived the period intact enough to govern in the following century were those that had kept an organisation, a press and a memory through the defeats, assets the electoral parties held by structure and the armed organisations held, where they held them, by the prison and exile networks described above. The movements' own internal accounting privileged the first standard while the period lasted, which is why the defeats registered as total; the historian can afford all three standards at once, and should, since a strategy's full grade includes what it built while failing. That concession made, the comparison's finding stands on the first standard because the strategies themselves chose it: both roads promised state power, named it as the objective, and asked to be judged by its attainment. Grading them by the standard they set themselves is the respect this article has tried to pay them throughout, and it is why the common failure, and not the consolations of the other ledgers, is the article's conclusion.

## A note on the evidence

The comparison rests on an evidentiary base whose unevenness a researcher should know and a general reader should sense. For the repression, the base is now strong: the truth commissions and the courts of the transition period established the record country by country, and their reports are the authority this article has repeatedly routed the reader toward for the scale of what was done. For the external sponsor's conduct, the base is the declassified archive, the Senate inquiry into Chile, the released diplomatic and intelligence records, a base that grows by scheduled disclosure and that has already revised several of the period's settled stories. For the movements themselves, the base is the weakest, and the weakness has a shape: the victors of the internal arguments wrote the memoirs, the destroyed organisations left their papers in police archives or in the ground, and the state's files on the movements, captured documents, interrogation records, surveillance photographs, survive in the keeping of the institutions that defeated them, a provenance that must be priced into every use. Oral history has done much to correct the imbalance, and the interview projects with survivors of both roads are among the field's essential sources, but the dead do not sit for interviews, and the comparison's central generation is increasingly beyond their reach. The article's conclusions have therefore been stated at the level the evidence supports most firmly, outcomes and mechanisms, rather than at the level of intentions and inner debates, where the record thins; and the reader who proceeds into the field should expect the mechanisms to be refined, and occasionally overturned in detail, as the archives of the apparatuses themselves are opened, country by country, under the pressure of the democracies the period failed to prevent and finally, on its own hard terms, produced.

## The Chilean road's long approach

The electoral road's confidence in Chile was not a theorist's invention but the product of an electoral history that seemed, to its participants, to be moving in one direction, and recovering that history explains why intelligent people staked so much on legality there. The Chilean left had contested the presidency in coalition since the nineteen fifties, and its candidate, Allende, had come within a few tens of thousands of votes of victory in nineteen fifty eight, running against the combined weight of the right and a centre that still commanded the countryside's delivered vote. In nineteen sixty four he was defeated heavily by the Christian Democrat Frei, in an election in which the right, calculating that only the centre could stop the left, threw its votes to Frei and the campaign was financed in part from abroad, as later disclosures established. The left read the two results as a trajectory: the coalition's vote was the largest single bloc by the later nineteen sixties, the right was declining, the centre was exhausting its reforming novelty, and time, organisation and the completed suffrage, women voting in presidential elections only from nineteen fifty two and the secret ballot secured in the countryside by reforms of the late fifties, were all working in the coalition's favour. The reading was not fantasy; it was an extrapolation from real returns, and it taught the coalition's strategists to think of the presidency as an inheritance falling due rather than a fortress to be stormed. What the extrapolation could not price was the response of the other actors to the same trajectory, since a trend visible to the left was equally visible to the officer corps, the propertied classes and the embassy, each of which drew its own conclusion about the closing window. The electoral road's deepest misreading may therefore have been temporal: it assumed that a majority assembling slowly would be allowed to finish assembling, when in politics a visible trajectory is an alarm bell rung for one's enemies, and the interval between the trend's visibility and its completion is precisely the season in which apparatuses act. The foco's theorists made the mirror error about time, assuming the state's response would be slower than the nucleus's growth. Both roads, in the end, misjudged the clock, and the clock was set by the institution neither had placed at the centre of its theory.

## The doctrine abroad

The region's strategic export travelled beyond the region, and its foreign career is a coda worth a section because it repeated the home pattern in miniature and confirms that the misreading was in the doctrine rather than in the continent. Marighella's minimanual, translated within a few years into the languages of Europe and North America, became a handbook for the urban armed groups that formed in the industrial countries at the turn of the nineteen seventies, in Germany, Italy, Japan and the United States, organisations that reproduced the urban strategy's logic, small compartmented groups, spectacular action, provocation of the state, inside societies whose coercive apparatuses were incomparably stronger and whose publics were incomparably less available than anything the manual's author had faced. Their fates were correspondingly swift, and their principal historical effect, as at home, was to license the expansion of the security state that destroyed them and to burden the legal lefts of their countries with an association they had not chosen. Debray's codification had a quieter foreign career in the universities, where the foco entered the textbooks as the type of voluntarism, the doctrine that will can substitute for conditions, and is taught to students of revolution as a cautionary concept. The electoral road's export ran through the Eurocommunist episode already described and through the solidarity movements, whose veterans carried the Chilean lesson into the labour and social democratic parties of Europe as a permanent suspicion of bare majorities. The region had conducted, at ruinous cost, an experiment whose results the world's left consumed as literature; the comparison's final service is to insist that the results were results, purchased as results are, and not merely the scenery of the century's romance with revolution.

## The heirs and the checklist

The movements that inherited the region's politics after the transitions governed themselves, consciously or not, by the checklist this article extracted from the record, and a brief survey of the inheritance closes the account without trespassing on the neighbouring articles' territory. The electoral projects that won office across the region in the following century were built, almost without exception, by men and women formed in the defeats: trade unionists imprisoned under the dictatorships, exiles of the seminar rooms, survivors of the parties the repression had dissolved. Their programmes bore the period's watermark in their caution about the apparatus: constitutions renegotiated where possible, military budgets and prerogatives trimmed by increments, transformations paced to keep the coalition broader than the government's enemies, and a rhetoric that promised change within the order's forms. Their critics, including the heirs of the armed road's impatience, charged that the checklist had become an alibi, that the apparatus question, invoked at every moderation, now served to excuse the abandonment of the transformation itself, and the charge restaged, in the new century's idiom, the old argument between the roads, this time within governments rather than against them. The comparison does not adjudicate that restaging; its materials end with the period. It records only that the argument's persistence, across the transitions and into office, is the strongest evidence that the question the two roads contested was never the period's property. It is the standing question of any politics that proposes to change who holds what, in a state whose armed core has its own account of the matter, and each generation of the region's left has had to answer it with the instruments and the information of its moment, as the generation this article has followed answered it with theirs.

## The Bolivian miners and the thesis of Pulacayo

The miners of Bolivia deserve a panel of their own in any account of the roads, because their movement held, inside a single federation, the experiences that elsewhere were distributed among rival organisations, and because its founding document stated the period's strategic problem with a clarity the later theories rarely matched. The Thesis of Pulacayo, adopted by the miners' federation in nineteen forty six, argued that Bolivia's revolution would be made by the proletariat of the mines in alliance with the peasantry, that the bourgeoisie of a dependent country was incapable of completing its own democratic tasks, and that the workers must therefore aim at power themselves rather than tail a national bourgeoisie through stages. Six years later the miners' militias were the armed force that defeated the army in the streets and made the revolution of nineteen fifty two, and for a period the federation exercised something close to a dual power beside the government its rifles had installed, with ministers it could veto and mines it substantially ran. The sequel is the comparison's whole argument in one organisation's biography. The government rebuilt the army; the army, in time, massacred miners in their camps and destroyed the militias; the federation survived as a union, led the resistance to successive dictatorships, and was finally broken not by a coup but in nineteen eighty five, when an elected government, facing hyperinflation and the collapse of the tin price, closed the state mines by decree and dispersed the mining population to the cities and the coca fields. The most revolutionary workers' movement in the region's history was thus defeated in the end by an elected government's economic emergency, administered by the very president the miners had helped to power in nineteen fifty two, returned to office in his old age. No strategist of either road had imagined that ending, in which the apparatus that mattered was the treasury and the world tin market, and the article records it as the outer limit of its own finding: the coercive apparatus decided the period's contests, but history kept inventing further apparatuses behind it.

## Mexico: the state that absorbed the question

Mexico stands at the comparison's edge as the great counterfactual of form: a country that had its social revolution early, between nineteen ten and nineteen twenty, and built from it a party state that absorbed the revolution's language, its heroes and eventually its opposition into a single institutional order that governed for seventy years. For the Mexican left, the strategic question was therefore posed in a distinctive key. The ruling party claimed the revolution's inheritance and monopolised its symbols; elections were administered by the state the party inhabited; and the apparatus, an army progressively subordinated and professionalised after the revolutionary decades, stood behind a civilian order whose stability rested on incorporation rather than on the southern cone's methods. The Mexican guerrillas of the nineteen sixties and seventies, in Guerrero and the cities, were defeated by a dirty war whose existence the state long denied and later investigations documented, a war conducted in the shadow of the party's civilian legitimacy and therefore largely invisible to the country's public. The electoral road, meanwhile, was blocked not by coups but by the administration of the vote itself, until the ruling party's own splits and the opposition's accumulation forced an opening at the century's end, and the presidency changed parties by ballot in the year two thousand. Mexico's lesson for the comparison is that the apparatus question can be dissolved, for generations, by a state strong enough to make itself the revolution's executor and the opposition's landlord, and that both roads, against such a state, spent themselves against the wrong walls: the guerrillas against an army that the party had already tamed into an instrument, the electoralists against a count that the party administered. The third road's emergence from Chiapas, the one corner of the republic the absorption had never reached, reads in this light as the exception that maps the rule.

## How the dictatorships ended

The comparison's period closes with the apparatuses' own withdrawals, and the manner of those withdrawals is the last body of evidence about the obstacle's nature. In Argentina, the military government destroyed itself by an external adventure, invading the Falkland Islands, the Malvinas, in nineteen eighty two and losing the war to Britain, after which the regime collapsed in disgrace and handed power to an elected government that put its commanders on trial, a sequence in which the apparatus was weakened not by either road but by its own hubris in the one kind of war it existed, in its own account, to fight. In Brazil, the generals conducted a slow, controlled opening across a decade, liberalising by instalments from above, conceding an amnesty, direct elections for governors, and finally an electoral college transition to a civilian, retaining at every step the initiative and the terms. In Uruguay, the armed forces, having lost a constitutional plebiscite in nineteen eighty that they had expected to win, negotiated their withdrawal through a pact with the political parties at the Naval Club, emerging with an amnesty the restored democracy later ratified at the ballot box. In Chile, the dictatorship was ended by the instrument its own constitution had provided: the plebiscite of nineteen eighty eight, in which the electorate, registered and mobilised by a broad opposition campaign conducted with a discipline the period had taught it, voted no to the general's continuation, and the regime, divided in its own junta at the count, accepted the result and negotiated a transition that left its commander in chief of the army for a further decade and its constitution substantially in force.

The Chilean plebiscite is the comparison's most delicate final datum, and it must be handled exactly. An electoral act removed the dictatorship that had removed an electoral government, and the electoral road's survivors were entitled to their vindication. But the act worked because the apparatus had been weakened by its own economic crisis and international isolation, because the opposition had limited its immediate demand to the single question the plebiscite posed, and because the regime's own legalism, the constitution it had written to perpetuate itself, supplied the mechanism and bound its author when a faction of his own junta refused the alternative of annulment. The conditions the electoral road's theory had presupposed in nineteen seventy, an apparatus divided at the decisive moment, a majority assembled and disciplined, an objective narrow enough to unite the centre with the left, were present in nineteen eighty eight in a way they had not been in nineteen seventy three, and their presence was the product of the intervening catastrophe's education. The roads' argument thus ends not in a verdict but in a specification, which is where this article has argued it always should have been conducted: not arms or ballots in the abstract, but the condition of the apparatus, the breadth of the alliance and the narrowness of the immediate aim, in the concrete country and year. The generation that won the plebiscite had learned the specification in the camps and the exile seminar rooms, and its victory, partial and encumbered as it was, belongs in the ledger as the electoral road's amended form, travelling at last with its eyes on the obstacle.

## The revolution's own house divided

A final panel belongs to the sponsoring revolution's internal argument, because Cuba's conduct toward the continental strategies was shaped by a debate at home that mirrored the region's, and the mirroring explains the sponsorship's inconsistencies better than any theory of hypocrisy. The victorious movement of nineteen fifty nine contained the Sierra's veterans, the urban underground's survivors and, absorbed a little later, the cadres of the old communist party, and the three inheritances carried three strategic instincts: the Sierra's faith in the armed deed, the underground's knowledge of the cities and the party's discipline of stages and alliances. The revolution's continental policy oscillated among the instincts as its internal balance shifted: sponsorship of the focos in the years of the Sierra's ascendancy, quarrels with the old parties conducted in the vocabulary of that ascendancy, and then, after the defeats and the island's own institutionalisation, a gradual reconciliation with the parties and a diplomacy that privileged state relations, including support, in the nineteen seventies, for the Chilean coalition whose road the foco's theorists had denounced. The old party's cadres, whose caution in nineteen fifty eight had been the foco literature's favourite proof of the peaceful road's bankruptcy, ended as administrators of the revolutionary state and counsellors of its moderation abroad. The comparison draws from the panel a general caution about strategic doctrine: that what presents itself as a theory of history is often, in its moment of power, the deposit of an organisation's internal settlement, exported with the confidence of doctrine and revised with the quietness of administration. The region's movements, which received the doctrine as doctrine, graded the revisions as betrayals or as maturations according to their own settlements, and the argument's heat, across twenty years, owed as much to that unacknowledged mechanism as to any disagreement about the enemy.

## The indigenous question beneath both roads

Both strategies theorised the countryside's population as a peasantry, a class category, and the population in question was, across the Andean and Mesoamerican highlands, indigenous peoples whose own political categories were communal and civilisational before they were economic. The mismatch ran quietly under the whole argument and surfaced at its decisive points. The foco's columns arrived in Quechua and Aymara and Guaraní speaking country with Spanish speaking cadres and a programme translated from the coastal cities, and their hosts' calculations, about land, about the community's authorities, about the state's long memory of rebellion and punishment, were made in a political idiom the columns could hear but rarely speak. The electoral parties, for their part, organised the highlands through peasant federations that translated communal demands into the vocabulary of class and citizenship, a translation that delivered real goods, titles, schools, the vote, while subordinating the community's own account of itself to the party's programme. In Bolivia in the nineteen seventies a current arose inside the peasant movement, Katarismo, named for an eighteenth century rebel against the colonial power, which asserted that the highlands' people were not a class awaiting a party but nations with their own history and claim, and which treated both the parties of the left and the military nationalists as rival custodians of a people who intended to be their own. The current's long fruit was the indigenous and peasant movement that eventually carried its own candidates to national office by election in the following century, and its short fruit, within the period, was a standing correction to both roads: the subject of the region's transformation was never only the proletariat of the diagrams, and a strategy that could not hear the communities' own political language would be answered, in the hills, with the silence that so many columns mistook for fear. The third road's doctrine, composed in the Lacandon jungle in dialogue with Tzotzil, Tzeltal, Chol and Tojolabal communities, is unintelligible without this correction, since its central forms, the assembly, the rotating mandate, governing by obeying, were learned from the communities' practice rather than imported from the canon, a genealogy its founders acknowledged and the comparison honours as the period's one genuinely new political technology.

## The Popular Front in the memory

The Chilean coalition's confidence had an older root than the returns of the nineteen sixties, and it explains the electoral road's emotional depth in the country where it was most fully tried. In nineteen thirty eight a Popular Front of radicals, socialists and communists had won the Chilean presidency and governed, enacting reforms, industrialising through a state development corporation and surviving its term, an experience unique in the region at the time and foundational for the left that lived it. The front's memory taught two generations that a coalition of the left and centre could win, could govern and could hand over power constitutionally, and the lesson was carried in families and party schools into the nineteen sixties as the tradition the new coalition claimed to continue. The memory was accurate and incomplete in the way of all founding memories: the front of nineteen thirty eight had governed a smaller state, against a weaker and less organised right, in a world whose dominant power was distracted by depression and war, and with an armed forces whose political abstention had not yet been tested by a programme of expropriation. The coalition of nineteen seventy inherited the confidence without the conditions, and the comparison, which has insisted throughout on grading presuppositions against conditions, finds in the Popular Front's long shadow its most poignant exhibit: a true memory, honestly earned, functioning as a false premise. The armed road's founding memory, Cuba, played the identical role for its adherents, a true event whose conditions were not transferable, and the symmetry completes the article's account of how intelligent movements fail: not by lying about the past, but by trusting it.

## Guatemala's long war reconsidered

Guatemala has appeared in this article in fragments, as the prehistory's warning and the record's darkest case, and it earns a consolidated panel before the close because its war was the period's longest and its lessons the least digestible for both roads. The insurgency's roots lay in the nineteen fifty four termination of an elected reformist government, an origin that gave the Guatemalan left a title to armed resistance the region's other movements could envy: they had tried the legal road first, and it had been answered from outside. The officers who revolted in nineteen sixty were not Marxists but nationalists offended by their government's subservience, and the movement that grew from their revolt, through alliance with the outlawed party of labour and the student movement, became Marxist as the state radicalised it, a sequence the foco's theory never imagined, in which the state manufactured its own gravediggers' ideology by proscription. The war's geography then migrated, from the eastern hills of the first columns to the capital's clandestine networks and finally, in the late nineteen seventies, to the indigenous highlands of the west, where a new guerrilla army rooted itself in Mayan communities whose grievances, land, forced labour's memory, contempt, were older than the republic. The state's answer in the highlands, in the early nineteen eighties, was the period's most terrible: a counterinsurgency that treated the communities themselves as the enemy's sea and drained it by massacre and displacement, on a scale and with a character that the historical clarification commission, established under the peace accords and reporting at the decade's end, documented and judged in terms this article's protocol leaves to that report. The insurgency survived the campaigns as an organisation and signed the peace of nineteen ninety six as a political party in waiting; it had not been destroyed, and it had not won, and its thirty six years stand in the comparison as the limiting case of duration, the proof that survival, the foco's first virtue, could be purchased across a generation without altering the outcome the apparatus imposed. For the electoral road, Guatemala's postwar decades supplied the reciprocal proof, as the former insurgents' party contested elections in a republic whose military retained its prerogatives and whose highlands' survivors voted, in their majorities, for whoever promised the war would not return. The country's whole modern history can be read as the comparison conducted by other means, and its commission's report, with the Chilean and Argentine and Salvadoran reports beside it, forms the documentary foundation on which any future grading of the roads will have to stand.

## The mediators

The negotiated endings introduced an actor the classical strategies had never cast: the mediator, and the comparison should close its cast list with the figure, because the mediator's arrival marks the moment the period's strategic imagination admitted a third party into what had been a duel. In El Salvador the mediation was conducted by the United Nations, with the church and the region's governments in supporting roles; in Guatemala the same machinery presided over a decade of accords; in Nicaragua the end of the Contra war and the election of nineteen ninety were supervised by international observers whose presence both sides had requested. The Salvadoran archbishop whose preaching had made him the war's most famous civilian voice, and whose assassination at the altar in nineteen eighty opened the war's worst decade, belonged to an earlier cast, the prophet rather than the mediator, and his fate measured the distance the country still had to travel before mediation became possible. What made the mediators acceptable, when they came, was the common failure itself, experienced as exhaustion: the insurgencies could not defeat the armies, the armies could not legitimise their victories, the sponsors could no longer fund the stalemate at the old price, and the populations' verdict, delivered in every survey and every migration, was that the war's continuation had become the one outcome nobody could defend. Mediation is the institutional form a shared failure takes when it is finally recognised by all parties at once, and the settlements it produced, with their truth commissions, their demobilisations and their imperfect reforms of the apparatuses themselves, are the period's most durable political constructions. The comparison ends its analytical work there, with the observation that the roads' twenty year duel was settled neither by the rifle nor by the ballot but by the negotiation neither doctrine had theorised, conducted over the obstacle both doctrines had misjudged, in the presence of the dead whose commissions were still being written.

## Costa Rica: the republic without the obstacle

One republic in the region ran the period without the obstacle, and its experience functions as the comparison's natural control, the case in which the deciding variable was removed and the outcomes changed accordingly. Costa Rica emerged from a brief civil war in nineteen forty eight under a junta whose leader, José Figueres, proceeded to abolish the army altogether, inscribing the abolition in the constitution of nineteen forty nine and redirecting the state's expenditure toward schools, health and the electoral machinery. The consequences for the strategic argument were total. A Costa Rican left did not have to theorise the coercive apparatus as its central problem, because the republic had, by a founder's decision ratified across generations, declined to maintain one in the form its neighbours knew; the police that remained were a constabulary, not a political army with a doctrine of internal war. The country's communist movement had been outlawed after the civil war and its successor parties operated for decades at the margin of legality's edge, yet the contests of Costa Rican politics were fought over budgets, coffee prices and the welfare state's extent, and governments of the centre left and centre right alternated by election through the very decades in which the southern cone's republics were governed from the barracks. When the debt crisis of the nineteen eighties struck, Costa Rica negotiated with its creditors, adjusted, protested and voted, and the crisis's political casualties were ministries and programmes rather than constitutions. The comparison does not offer the Costa Rican path as a prescription, since the abolition was itself the product of an unrepeatable conjuncture, a victorious junta leader with the authority to disband the institution that might have resisted him, in a small country under the northern power's tolerant eye. It offers the case as the finding's cleanest confirmation. Remove the apparatus, and the strategic argument between arms and ballots loses its deciding term; the roads cease to be wagers against an armed referee and become ordinary politics, with ordinary politics' frustrations and ordinary politics' survivors. What the rest of the region's left was fighting, beneath every doctrine it debated, was the presence in its republics of an institution that could end the argument at will. Costa Rica is what the argument looked like with the institution absent, and its placid exceptionalism, so easily mistaken for a national temperament, is the comparison's final piece of evidence that temperament was never the variable.

## Colombia's long negotiation

Colombia's armed organisations have appeared in this article as the limiting case of permanence, and the manner of the largest one's ending, decades after the period proper, deserves a closing panel because it restaged every element of the comparison in a single process. The Revolutionary Armed Forces, born in the nineteen sixties from peasant self defence communities and grown across half a century into an army that taxed coca regions and held the state's forces at bay across the peripheries, entered formal negotiations with the Colombian government in Havana in two thousand and twelve, with Cuba and Norway as guarantors and the region's governments as witnesses, a staging that itself summarised the century: the island of the first road hosting the last road's surrender of arms. The talks proceeded for four years through an agenda that read like the period's syllabus in reverse, land first, then political participation, then the drug economy, then the victims and the terms of justice, then the laying down of arms under international verification. The accord signed in two thousand and sixteen was submitted to a plebiscite and rejected by a narrow margin, on a low turnout, after a campaign in which the opposition charged that the guerrillas were being rewarded; a revised accord was then ratified through congress, the fighters concentrated in zones under United Nations verification, surrendered their weapons to the monitors, and founded a political party under a new name to contest elections. The party's first electoral outings returned a vote so small that the organisation's half century of war appeared, in retrospect, to have purchased a constituency that existed mainly inside the war itself, a datum the foco's theorists would have recognised as their doctrine's final refutation in its longest running laboratory: the column had survived everything except the peace, and the population it claimed had never, when offered the ballot in safety, ratified the claim. The demobilised fighters' security, meanwhile, became the process's open wound, as former combatants and social leaders in the countryside were assassinated in numbers the peace's guarantors documented with alarm, the apparatus question returning in the dispersed form of the armed actors the state had never fully monopolised. Colombia's negotiation thus closes the comparison's evidence with a summary in action: an armed road of fifty years converted, by exhaustion and mediation, into an electoral party of negligible weight, in a republic whose coercive landscape remained plural and violent beneath the peace's paper. Neither road's founders would have predicted the ending, and both would have recognised its logic at once.

## How to argue with this article

A comparison that asks to be trusted should say how it can be attacked, and the closing pages owe the reader the article's own points of vulnerability, stated as plainly as the objection it entertained earlier. The first point is the selection of the period: begin the story in nineteen fifty two, with the Bolivian army's defeat and dissolution, or in nineteen thirty eight, with the Popular Front's Chilean victory, and the balance of the evidence shifts, since the longer frame contains an apparatus destroyed and an electoral coalition that completed its term. The article has included both cases and argued that the later apparatuses were rebuilt into something their predecessors were not, but a critic is entitled to reply that the rebuilding is the article's assumption rather than its finding, and that a different periodisation yields a different common failure, or none. The second point is the treatment of the settlements: the article reads the Central American negotiations as the armed road's arrival at the electoral road's starting point, but the veterans read them as the armed road's vindication, proof that only war made the state negotiate the reforms that ballots had never extracted, and the difference between the readings is not factual but interpretive, a disagreement about what a strategy is for. The third point is the article's silence, imposed by its protocol, on the human accounting: a comparison that declines the figures declines also the full weight of what the apparatuses did, and a reader who finds the analysis bloodless is finding the protocol's cost, which the article accepts as the price of its neutrality between the roads and does not ask the reader to accept as a virtue. Each vulnerability is real. The article's defence is the one it has practised throughout: its claims are stated at the level where the evidence is firmest, its presuppositions are on the table, and its central term, the condition of the coercive apparatus, is offered as a variable to be measured in any new case rather than as a spell to be repeated. Argue with it on those terms and the argument itself continues the seminar the period began.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Your open problems begin where this article's protocol forced it to stop. The comparison was graded on outcomes, and the archives that would let you grade the mechanisms at finer grain, the intelligence files of the period's apparatuses, the internal records of the destroyed organisations, the correspondence of the exile seminar, remain unevenly open across the region, catalogued in some capitals and sealed or destroyed in others. The commissions' reports give you the repression's established record; the strategies' internal histories await the same documentary patience, and several of the movements' archives survive in exile collections whose cataloguing is a live scholarly frontier. Ask after the picture, and you will be asking the period's question in its contemporary form.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which strategy worked better in Latin America?

Neither, on the evidence of the two decades in which both were fully tested, and the question is better replaced than answered. The armed road's attempts were destroyed in country after country, and the electoral road's fullest trial ended with the government removed by the armed forces. Where power did change hands, in Cuba and Nicaragua, the winning form was a broad insurrectional alliance against a regime already collapsing, which belongs to neither strategy as its theorists defined it. The useful finding is not a ranking but a shared obstacle: the state's coercive apparatus defeated the one strategy in the field and terminated the other in office, and any honest comparison has to start from that symmetry.

### Q: What is the difference between foco and insurrection?

A foco is a small armed nucleus that begins the struggle on its own initiative and expects its example to generate the mass movement, the crisis and eventually the party around itself. An insurrection is the rising of a mass movement that already exists, armed at the moment when a regime's authority is collapsing across society. The foco therefore claims to manufacture its own precondition, while the insurrection exploits a precondition produced by the regime's disintegration and by years of prior organisation. The distinction decides how the region's cases should be graded: movements that won, in Havana's hinterland and in Managua, behaved as insurrections with broad alliances, whatever their later mythology claimed, whereas movements that behaved as pure focos were destroyed.

### Q: Why did the guerrilla strategy fail so often?

The strategy rested on a chain of expectations, and the chain broke at its links in a repeating order. The nucleus had to survive in terrain where state intelligence services, fed by informers and captured papers, repeatedly located it. The peasantry had to rally, and instead it weighed the column's chances against the reprisals that would certainly follow and often stood aside, particularly where a reforming government had already delivered or promised land. The urban networks that supplied money, medicine and recruits had to hold, and they were the softest target the police faced. Above all, the enemy's army had to prove brittle, and the region's armies, retrained and resupplied for counterinsurgency after the Cuban victory, proved durable instead.

### Q: Did the urban guerrilla strategy provoke repression?

The charge was made at the time by critics inside the left, and the sequence of events in the urban strategy's main theatres supports its central claim. In Brazil, Uruguay and Argentina, armed action in the cities was followed by the transfer of internal war to the armed forces, by emergency legislation, and by repression that fell far beyond the armed organisations onto unions, parties, publications and their members' families. Defenders of the strategy replied that the repression was coming anyway, since the states in question were already governed by or moving toward military rule, and that legality had protected nobody in the coups that preceded the urban campaigns. Both observations can stand together: the states chose the repression, and the urban actions supplied its timing, its public rationale and its first targets.

### Q: How did Nicaragua succeed where others failed?

The Sandinista victory of nineteen seventy nine differed from the failed attempts in its form and in its enemy. In form, the Front that won had revised the foco doctrine into an insurrectional strategy: a broad alliance embracing business sectors, church figures and civic organisations alongside the armed columns, with urban risings timed to a general crisis rather than a rural war of indefinite growth. In its enemy, the Somoza dynasty had spent the decade alienating the very classes that normally defend a regime, profiting from an earthquake's relief, murdering an opposition editor, and converting the state into a family business, until its own social base joined the opposition and its external patron withdrew protection. The army that might have saved it disintegrated with the dynasty. The lesson drawn in this article is that the conditions, not the doctrine, were exceptional.

### Q: What did Chile prove about the electoral road?

Chile proved two things at once, and the polemical literature's habit of keeping only one of them is the source of most confusion about the case. It proved that the electoral road was practicable: a left coalition could win a presidential election, be confirmed by congress, govern for three years and transfer substantial property, copper, banks, industry and land, by legal instruments, in the region's most durable democracy. It also proved that none of this answered the question of the armed forces, which retained the capacity to end the process and, when the transformation's reach and the economy's disorder had built them a civilian constituency, used it. A strategy whose premise was that legality would bind the military was refuted at exactly the joint where the premise bore weight.

### Q: What problem did both strategies share?

Both strategies were, beneath their quarrel, theories about the state's coercive apparatus, and both misjudged the same institution in opposite directions. The guerrilla theory assumed the army would prove brittle under pressure, that conscripts would desert and the apparatus would crack once a nucleus survived and grew; the region's retrained and externally supplied armies did not crack. The electoral theory assumed the same institution could be bound by constitutional loyalty, held to its professed norms by a government that never broke legality; in the decisive case the officers reinterpreted their duty and removed the government. Neither theory had placed the apparatus, its composition, doctrine, intelligence and external support, at the centre of its planning. That shared omission is what this article names the common failure.

### Q: What is the Zapatista alternative?

It is the position announced by the movement that emerged in Chiapas in nineteen ninety four: an armed indigenous movement that explicitly declines to seek state power. Rather than marching on the capital or contesting the presidency, it built autonomous institutions of self rule across its territory, municipalities and councils administering justice, health and education under assemblies, with office holders mandated and recallable under the principle its declarations call governing by obeying. Its founders, who included veterans of the defeated guerrilla organisations of an earlier generation, presented the refusal as the lesson of those defeats: since every road to the palace had ended at the army, the movement would stop travelling to the palace. Whether that is an alternative strategy or an evasion of strategy's question remains disputed, and the movement is described in this article only by the founding position and stated principles it set out at its emergence.

### Q: Does taking state power require arms?

The region's record suggests the question is aimed at the wrong variable. Governments in the period were removed by arms regardless of how they had arrived, and armed movements failed to take power in most of the places they tried, so the presence of weapons in a movement's hands predicted very little. What predicted outcomes was the condition of the weapons in the state's hands: where the coercive apparatus was disintegrating, divided or abandoned by its patron, power changed hands, sometimes to movements with modest armament; where the apparatus was intact, supplied and willing, neither the ballot nor the rifle prevailed against it. The founders' old question, whether the state machine must be broken, survives the period in a sharpened form: not broken by whom, but in what condition must it already be, and what makes it so.

### Q: What would each strategy require to work?

The guerrilla strategy would require the conditions its doctrine claimed to be able to create: an army already losing cohesion, a population already moving toward insurrection, a regime already stripped of external support and internal allies, and an alliance broad enough to govern the morning after victory. Assembled, those are the conditions of a successful insurrection, which the foco was invented to render unnecessary. The electoral strategy would require a military genuinely subordinated to civilian authority, or a governing majority so broad, mobilised and patient that the cost of terminating its government visibly exceeds the cost of tolerating its programme, sustained across the years a transformation needs. Neither set of conditions is imaginary; each has occurred somewhere. The period's finding is that in the region and decades examined, the conditions were rare, shrinking, and actively prevented by the institution they concerned.

### Q: How does the common failure grade both strategic roads?

It grades them evenly, at the joint where each mechanism met the same obstacle, and it refuses the consolation each side offered itself. The armed road is not graded as wicked or foolish; it is graded as unconfirmed in every trial where the state's forces were intact, its central expectation, that a nucleus could create revolutionary conditions by fighting, having failed to produce the peasant rising and the army's collapse it promised. The electoral road is not graded as cowardly or as betrayed innocence; it is graded as unconfirmed in the trial most favourable to it, its central expectation, that legality would hold the military to the constitution, having failed in the region's strongest democracy. Evenhandedness of that kind is not neutrality between the roads' adherents. It is the recognition that the evidence convicts a shared presupposition, the misjudgement of the coercive apparatus, and that convicting the presupposition teaches more than convicting either party to the quarrel.

### Q: What should a student separate between a strategy's record and a verdict on Marxism generally?

Separate the instrument from the doctrine that was invoked to bless it. A strategy is a dated wager by a particular organisation about how power can be taken and held in one country; its record grades the wager, the organisation's reading of its moment, and the balance of forces it faced. Marxism as a body of thought also contains analyses of land tenure, of dependence, of class formation, of ideology and of the state itself, and those analyses stand or fall in other courts: in the archives, in the seminar, in their capacity to organise evidence their rivals cannot. The same canon, read by honest readers, supplied both roads in this article and several positions besides, which is why a strategy's defeat cannot be converted into the doctrine's refutation without an extra argument that the doctrine compelled that strategy. In the Latin American case that extra argument is weak, and examiners reward students who notice its weakness rather than repeat the conversion.

### Q: Why does the coercive apparatus decide both roads' arguments?

Because both arguments were, at bottom, wagers about what that apparatus would do, and events settled the wagers at the apparatus rather than at the level of programme or popularity. The guerrilla argument needed the army to disintegrate under insurgent pressure; the electoral argument needed it to abstain under constitutional pressure; the negotiated endings in Central America needed it to accept a bargain when its sponsors tired. In each case the outcome turned on the institution's cohesion, doctrine, intelligence and external supply, variables that neither road's theory had measured with the seriousness it measured land, class or votes. An institution that can destroy one strategy in the field and terminate the other in office, and that survived the period to bargain over the transitions that followed, is not one factor among others in the strategic equation. In this region and period it was the equation's deciding term, which is why the article builds its comparison around it.

### Q: How does the strategies assessed table order the comparison?

The table orders the comparison by enlarging it beyond the famous duel and then sorting by cause. It sets five strategies in one frame, the rural nucleus, the urban armed campaign, the broad insurrectional alliance, the electoral road and the autonomy strategy, and it gives each the same four entries: the theory it acted on, the cases where it was tried, the outcome, and the reason for the outcome. The ordering work happens in the final column. Read down it, and the reasons sort the strategies more honestly than their banners do: the alliance's successes carry the reason of a collapsing regime, the nucleus and the urban campaign carry the reason of an intact apparatus, the electoral road carries the same reason approached from office, and the autonomy strategy carries the reason that it declined the encounter. A reader who absorbs the table has the article's argument in miniature, including its refusal to let the foco claim the alliance's victories.

### Q: What separates the armed road's objection to elections from the electoral road's objection to arms?

The two objections are mirror images, and what separates them is the direction from which each watches the same institution act. The armed objection looks at the electoral road from the mountains and says: you will win office, govern under a constitution, and be removed by the army you refused to touch, delivering your people to it with the formalities observed; Chile is its exhibit. The electoral objection looks at the armed road from the city and says: you will be located, encircled and destroyed, and your destruction will license the repression of the entire left, including the organisations that warned you; Bolivia and the southern cone are its exhibits. Each objection is the other road's epitaph, written in advance and largely accurate about the fate, while silent about its own. Their symmetry is itself evidence: when both sides' strongest arguments are descriptions of the same obstacle destroying the other, the obstacle, and not the choice between the roads, is where the explanation belongs.
