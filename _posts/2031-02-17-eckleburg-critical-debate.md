This is the eckleburg eyes: a critical debate mapped, position by position, with the evidence each reading rests on and the line of argument its proponents use, so that a reader can see the whole disagreement at once instead of meeting it one confident claim at a time. The pair of giant bespectacled eyes on a billboard above the valley of ashes has drawn more scholarly disagreement than any other image in The Great Gatsby, and the disagreement has a shape worth learning before anyone picks up a side. Some critics read the eyes as God, a moral witness brooding over a corrupt landscape. Others read them as the death of God, a faded sign of a faith that has withdrawn. Others read them as commerce, an advertisement that has replaced worship with trade. Others read them as a void, paint that means nothing and therefore exposes the hunger for meaning itself. A fifth line of argument treats the eyes as the reader, the act of watching staged inside the novel. Each position has textual ground to stand on, each has a serious weakness its opponents press, and the text keeps all of them alive at once. That is the namable claim of this article: one symbol, many scholarly verdicts, and the openness of the image is exactly what generates the debate rather than an accident the debate should fix.

A map is not a verdict, and this article keeps that distinction strict. The temptation with contested symbols is to summarize each position only long enough to dismiss it and then crown a winner, but that move destroys the very thing a researcher needs. A debate map shows where each reading draws its strength, what passages it leans on hardest, what it cannot easily explain, and how the positions answer one another, so that a student, teacher, or researcher can navigate the criticism with the positions intact. The complication this map addresses honestly is the urge to resolve the debate in favor of one reading, because resolving it would require the novel to say something it refuses to say. Fitzgerald gives the eyes a physical description, a commercial origin, a character who calls them God, and another character who calls them an advertisement, and then he lets the disagreement between those two characters stand without a narrator's correction. The critical debate is the scholarly echo of that unresolved scene.

## What the Debate Is Really Asking

Before the positions can be mapped, the question underneath them has to be stated precisely, because the Eckleburg debate is really several debates tangled together and they pull in different directions. The first is a question about the text: what does the image of the eyes do in the scenes where it appears, and how much weight can a painted advertisement carry? The second is a question about method: should a symbol be read for what its first readers could have meant, for what the words most plausibly support, or for what the image has come to mean after decades of criticism? The third is a question about authority: does a character's interpretation of an image bind the reader, so that Wilson calling the eyes God settles the matter, or does the novel stage Wilson's reading as one more human projection? Critics who argue past one another are often answering different ones of these questions, and the map has to keep them separate or the positions blur.

Consider how much turns on Wilson's dialogue in Chapter 8. Wilson, sleepless and grieving after Myrtle's death, stares out his window at the billboard and says "God sees everything," and when Michaelis tries to calm him with "It's an advertisement," Wilson answers, "You can't fool God!" The divine reading takes Wilson at his word and treats the scene as the novel's own identification of the eyes with moral oversight. The commercial reading takes Michaelis at his word and treats the scene as the novel deflating a desperate man's projection. The void reading takes both speeches as evidence of human need rather than evidence about the eyes: Wilson needs a watcher, Michaelis needs a demystification, and the eyes themselves do nothing either way. Notice that all three readings cite the same exchange. They differ not in which evidence they admit but in what they think evidence of this kind can prove. That is why the debate cannot be settled by pointing at the passage; the passage is the thing being fought over, and each side has a coherent account of what the passage shows.

The debate also has a boundary question that rarely gets stated but shapes everything: whether the eyes must mean one thing at all. The divine, absent-God, and commercial readings all assume the image is a symbol with a referent, something it stands for, and they argue about which referent is correct. The void reading denies the premise and argues that the image's power comes precisely from its refusal to resolve into a referent. The reader-as-witness reading shifts the premise sideways and argues that the image's meaning is the act of looking itself, so the question "what do the eyes mean" is replaced by "what does it mean that the novel keeps making characters and readers look at them." These are not five answers to one question. They are five different accounts of what kind of question the image poses. A map that flattens them into a single list of candidates misses the structure of the disagreement, so this article keeps the deeper structure visible: three readings argue about the referent, one denies there is a referent, and one reframes the question entirely.

There is one more layer worth naming before the positions are stated, because it explains why the debate has stayed live for so long. The eyes appear only a few times in the novel, always briefly, and always in the valley of ashes or in Wilson's memory of it, yet they have generated an outsized critical literature. That disproportion is itself evidence about how symbols work. An image that is vivid, placed at a site of moral weight, and left semantically open will attract readings the way a strong magnet attracts filings, and each new theoretical fashion finds in the eyes a ready test case for its method. The debate's history is therefore also a history of criticism itself, with the eyes serving as a fixed point against which successive generations of critics have measured their approaches. To map the Eckleburg debate is, in part, to map how literary criticism has changed its mind about what symbols are for.

Readers coming to this article from the symbol-focused work should know exactly how the two projects relate. The companion article on the eyes of T. J. Eckleburg treats the image as it functions inside the novel: its physical description, its placement, its recurrence, and its immediate narrative role. That article answers what the eyes are and what they do in the story. This article answers what scholars have made of them and why those scholars disagree. The distinction matters because conflating the two is the most common error in student essays: citing a critic's reading as if it were a fact about the novel, or treating the novel's description as if it settled the critical argument. The symbol articles hold the in-novel content; this article holds the debate about that content, and the link between them runs both ways.

## The Eckleburg Eyes: A Critical Debate Mapped Over Time

The debate did not arrive all at once. It accumulated in layers, with each generation of critics reacting to what the previous generation had claimed, and the order of arrival explains why certain positions feel like responses to others. A chronological map keeps the positions from looking like five free-floating opinions and shows instead a conversation with a memory, where new readings are built out of dissatisfaction with old ones. What follows traces that sequence without favoring any stage of it, because the map's job is to show how the disagreement grew, not to declare that later readings improved on earlier ones.

### What did the earliest critics do with the eyes?

The earliest critics treated the eyes as atmosphere rather than argument, noticing their bleak power without building a thesis around them. The divine association appeared as an impression tied to Wilson's dialogue, not as a defended claim, so the period produced scattered remarks that later positions would recruit as ancestors. No debate existed yet, only the material one would need.

In this phase the image belonged to the setting, a strange landmark that deepened the desolation, and commentary on it was brief and impressionistic rather than systematic. The divine association was present from early on, largely through Wilson's dialogue, but it was stated as an observation rather than defended as a thesis. No one had yet built a full reading around the eyes, so there was no debate in the modern sense, only scattered remarks that later critics would mine for precedent. The eyes were noticed, not yet contested.

That early vagueness matters for the map because every later position claims some continuity with it. The divine reading points to the early moralizing remarks and says the first readers saw what was really there. The commercial reading points to the same remarks and says they were uncritical reflexes, the kind of loose allegorizing that later method would discipline. The void reading points to the thinness of the early commentary and says the image was always more evocative than meaningful, which is why no one could say much about it. When a debate's history begins with impressions rather than arguments, the later positions inherit the freedom to recruit those impressions as ancestors, and the map should note that this recruitment is interpretive work, not neutral history.

### How did the symbol-hunting decades change the question?

They changed the question from what the eyes evoke to what they mean, turning atmosphere into a symbol to be decoded. The divine reading hardened from impression into thesis, classroom teaching spread it as settled knowledge, and the commercial counter-reading began forming around the billboard's literal function. The modern debate's shape was born here.

Close reading of symbols became the dominant critical skill in these decades, and the eyes were lifted out of the setting and treated as a discrete symbol to be decoded. In this phase the eyes were lifted out of the setting and treated as a discrete symbol to be decoded, and the divine reading hardened from an impression into a defended thesis with Wilson's dialogue as its proof text. Classroom teaching amplified the effect, since a symbol with a clear meaning photographs well on an examination, and generations of students learned the eyes as God as if it were settled. The commercial counter-reading began to form here too, as critics who noticed the billboard's literal function pushed back against readings that ignored the advertisement. The debate acquired its modern shape in this period: a contest between meanings, with evidence cited and rebuttals offered.

The important thing to see about this phase is that it set the terms every later position still uses. Even the void reading, which denies that the eyes mean anything, is a response to the symbol-hunting assumption that they must mean something. Even the reader-as-witness reading, which reframes the question, is legible only against the background of the decoding project it declines to join. The symbol-hunting decades are the debate's formative period in the strict sense: they did not just add positions to the map, they defined what counts as a position. A researcher who skips this history will misunderstand the later readings as independent discoveries when they are really moves in a game whose rules were written here.

### What did the theory-driven decades add to the map?

They added new kinds of questions rather than new meanings, giving the map its full five-position shape. Materialist critics built the commercial thesis, reader-oriented critics developed the witness reading, and the absent-God and void positions sharpened in response. The positions stopped sharing enough common ground for one decisive argument, which is why adjudication grew harder as criticism matured.

Materialist and historicist critics developed the commercial reading into a serious account of how advertising, urban modernity, and the secularization of public space converge in the billboard, turning what had been a deflationary aside into a thesis about the culture the novel anatomizes. Reader-oriented critics developed the witness reading, asking what the novel does to its readers by staging acts of looking, and found in the eyes a figure for interpretation itself. Meanwhile the absent-God reading refined the divine position from within, accepting the religious language but arguing that the text stages a God who has withdrawn, so that the moral vocabulary and the empty sky belong to the same bleak fact. The void reading sharpened too, borrowing philosophical language to argue that the image's refusal of meaning is its meaning.

Notice the pattern: the new positions did not defeat the old ones but multiplied the dimensions of the disagreement. After this phase a researcher could no longer ask simply which meaning was correct, because the readings were no longer all answers to the same question. The commercial reading had become a claim about culture, the witness reading a claim about reading, the absent-God reading a claim about secularization, the void reading a claim about the limits of symbolism, and the divine reading a claim about moral order. The debate had become interdisciplinary in effect, with each position drawing its standards of evidence from a different intellectual neighborhood. That is why adjudication became harder rather than easier as the criticism matured: the positions stopped sharing enough common ground for a single knockout argument, and the map has to show that structural fact rather than pretending the disagreement is merely about who read the passage most carefully.

### What does the divine reading claim?

The divine reading claims the eyes are God, a moral witness set over the valley of ashes, and treats Wilson's identification as the novel's own. Its proponents argue that the placement, the verb "brood," and the overseeing posture compose a deliberate figure of judgment watching a corrupt world.

That is the thesis in compressed form; the full case unfolds across several kinds of evidence, and its strength comes from how naturally the pieces fit together. Start with the scene that anchors everything. In Chapter 8, George Wilson, whose wife has just been killed, stares through his window at the billboard and says "God sees everything," and when Michaelis objects that the eyes are an advertisement, Wilson insists, "You can't fool God!" For the divine reading, this is not a deluded man projecting onto paint. It is the novel's way of naming the image's function through the character best positioned to name it: the valley's one quasi-religious figure, a man who does not attend church and has no other theological vocabulary, recognizing the only available figure of transcendence in his world. The reading treats the dialogue as authorial identification rather than character error, and it finds support in the fact that the novel never has Nick or any authoritative voice correct Wilson. Michaelis says "It's an advertisement," but Michaelis is a minor, frightened man trying to calm a grieving neighbor, not a spokesman for the text's symbolism. The divine reading hears the advertisement objection as the voice of a demystifying modernity, true on the literal level and beside the point on the symbolic one.

The physical description carries the second strand of the case. Nick describes the eyes as enormous, blue, and set above the valley, and says they "brood on over the solemn dumping ground," a verb with unmistakable overtones of a hovering, judging presence. The word "solemn" does theological work too: dumping grounds are not solemn, but ground watched over by a moral power can be. The eyes are also disembodied, looking "out of no face," which the divine reading takes as the classic iconography of an all-seeing God, the eye detached from a body because the watcher is not a person. Add the placement: the billboard stands at the valley's edge where the road to the city passes, so that every traveler between the eggs and New York moves under its gaze, and the image becomes a figure for a moral order that sees the commerce, the affairs, and the carelessness without intervening. A full exposition of this thesis, with the symbol's in-novel workings kept distinct from the critical claims about it, is the subject of [the article on the eyes as God](/2025/04/28/eckleburg-eyes-as-god/), and readers who want the reading developed at length should go there.

The third strand is structural: the divine reading argues that the novel needs the eyes to do moral work that no character can do. The Buchanans are careless, Gatsby is compromised, Nick is complicit, and the valley's inhabitants are crushed, so the narrative requires some vantage from which the whole waste can be surveyed and judged. The eyes supply that vantage. Without them, the valley of ashes is just a poor neighborhood; with them brooding above it, the poverty becomes a moral fact, a sign of what the glittering world costs. This is why the reading has survived every theoretical fashion: it answers a genuine structural need in the novel, and any account that dismisses the eyes as mere paint has to explain what, if anything, does the judging work in a book so saturated with moral language. The divine reading's answer is that nothing else can, which is why the billboard must be more than an advertisement.

Its proponents also handle the obvious objection, which is that the eyes advertise an oculist's practice, and they handle it by distinguishing levels of meaning. A symbol can have a literal origin and a symbolic function at once; the fact that the eyes were painted to sell spectacles does not prevent them from signifying judgment, any more than a church built with donated money ceases to signify worship. The reading thus absorbs the commercial fact rather than denying it, and this absorption is part of its resilience. It concedes Michaelis's point and claims it is irrelevant to the symbolic register, which lets the divine reading keep both the billboard's literal history and its transcendent meaning. Whether that two-level move is legitimate is exactly what the other positions contest, but stated at full strength, the divine reading is a coherent, textually grounded account with the novel's most emotionally charged scene as its cornerstone.

### Why do some critics read an absent God instead of a present one?

The absent-God reading agrees that the eyes speak the language of divinity but argues the novel stages a God who has withdrawn, a moral vocabulary surviving after its referent has gone. Its evidence is the faded paint, the dimmed eyes, and the way the text pairs religious invocation with a conspicuous absence of any answering presence.

This position grew out of the divine reading rather than against it, and the family resemblance is the point: it keeps the theological frame but reverses the verdict on what the frame contains. The key textual detail is the condition of the billboard. Nick says the eyes are "dimmed a little by many paintless days under sun and rain," which the absent-God reading takes as a deliberate image of decayed faith. A present, watching God would not need paint that is peeling; a God who has withdrawn from the world leaves exactly this kind of weathered remnant, a sign that still points but no longer connects. The eyes are gigantic and they are faded, and the contradiction is the meaning: the culture retains the scale of religious feeling and has lost the substance, so it keeps building enormous hollow forms. Wilson's cry of "God sees everything" is then not the novel's identification of the eyes with God but a grieving man's attempt to summon a watcher who is not there, and the pathos of the scene comes from the gap between Wilson's need and the paint's indifference.

The reading draws further support from the novel's broader religious emptiness. No character in The Great Gatsby practices a living faith; the one funeral is perfunctory, the one wedding is a transaction, and the moral vocabulary of the book floats free of any institutional anchor. The absent-God reading argues that the eyes belong to this pattern: they are the novel's figure for secularization itself, the process by which a culture keeps the forms of judgment while losing the judge. Michaelis's "It's an advertisement" is, on this account, the truer speech, not because the eyes mean nothing but because they mean the replacement of the sacred by the commercial, a God-shaped hole filled by a trade sign. The distinction from the straight commercial reading is subtle but real: the commercial reading says the eyes are about advertising, while the absent-God reading says they are about the loss that advertising now covers, and the grief in that loss is essential to the position.

Its line of argument also explains why the eyes do nothing in the plot, which is a fact every reading must handle. The eyes never intervene, never warn, never punish; Tom and Daisy drive on, Gatsby dies, and the billboard keeps brooding over the ashes. For the divine reading this inaction is a problem to be explained away, usually by saying that judgment in the novel is deferred or displaced onto the reader. For the absent-God reading the inaction is the evidence: a withdrawn God is exactly one who watches without acting, and the eyes' passivity is not a flaw in the symbol but its theological content. The valley suffers under a gaze that sees and does nothing, which is the precise condition of a world after faith, and the horror of the image is that the watching continues after the caring has stopped. This is why the position has attracted critics interested in the novel's modernity: it makes the eyes a figure for the twentieth century's signature spiritual condition rather than a timeless moral emblem.

The absent-God reading also has a distinctive answer to the question of why Fitzgerald painted the eyes so large. Enormity without efficacy is the point: the culture can still produce gigantic religious images, but it can no longer produce the reality they once signified, so the images grow larger as the faith grows thinner, like a voice shouting into an empty room. The yellow spectacles, the vanished nose, the vanished oculist who "sank down himself into eternal blindness, or forgot them and moved away": every detail of neglect becomes theological. Even the oculist's disappearance is recruited, since a God who has forgotten his own sign is the perfect emblem of withdrawal. Whether the text can bear this much weight on a few adjectives is the question its opponents press, but as a line of argument it is internally consistent, emotionally powerful, and responsive to details, the faded paint and the passive gaze, that the straight divine reading must work harder to absorb.

### What does the commercial reading see in the billboard?

The commercial reading sees an advertisement and stops refusing to take the novel at its word, arguing that the eyes signify the colonization of public space and inner life by commerce. Its proponents hold that the billboard's literal function is its symbolic function: in this world, the closest thing to transcendence is a trade sign.

The case begins with a methodological complaint against the other readings: they all treat the billboard's status as an advertisement as an obstacle to be climbed over on the way to a grander meaning, when the novel itself foregrounds that status. Nick's introduction of the image is explicit about its origin: "some wild wag of an oculist set them there to fatten his practice in the borough of Queens." The commercial reading asks why criticism should discard the one causal account the text provides. The oculist painted the eyes to get customers. That is what they are for. Any reading that needs to forget this sentence in order to work is, on this view, importing a meaning the novel did not plant. The reading thus presents itself as the most textually disciplined of the five, the one that takes the novel's own explanation seriously instead of treating it as a false bottom.

From that foothold the reading builds outward into the novel's economic imagination. The eyes watch over the valley of ashes, the dumping ground of the industrial economy, and they watch the road that carries the rich between their playgrounds and the city where the money is made. The commercial reading argues that the billboard is the perfect emblem of a society in which everything, including the gaze that once belonged to God, has been converted into a medium of exchange. The eyes do not judge the commerce; they are the commerce, one more surface selling something to the traffic. Wilson's "God sees everything" is then the scene's irony rather than its revelation: a desperate man mistakes an advertisement for providence, and the novel's point is that in a fully commercialized world people can no longer tell the difference. Michaelis's correction, "It's an advertisement," is the moment of clarity, and the fact that Wilson cannot hear it is the tragedy. On this account the scene is not about God's presence or absence but about a culture's loss of the ability to distinguish the sacred from the sold.

The reading gains depth from the history of the American roadside, where the billboard was a new and conspicuous feature of the landscape in the years the novel describes. The commercial line of argument holds that Fitzgerald chose a billboard deliberately, not as a neutral carrier for a religious symbol but because the billboard was the era's signature form of public address. A church steeple would have signified God without ambiguity; a trade sign signifies the displacement of the steeple, and the novel's choice of the trade sign is the meaning. The eyes are enormous because advertisements are enormous; they are painted because advertisements are painted; they fade because advertisements fade when the business fails or moves on. Every physical detail that the theological readings spiritualize, the commercial reading re-materializes, and it claims this re-materialization is not reduction but precision: the novel is about money, status, and the market's invasion of private life, so its central image should be read as belonging to that invasion.

Its proponents also point to the company the eyes keep in the novel's symbolic economy. The green light, the shirts, the car, the parties: the book's great images are all commodities or displays of wealth, and the eyes fit the pattern if they are read as commerce, while they break the pattern if they are read as theology. The commercial reading thus claims a kind of systemic fit that the divine reading lacks: it makes the eyes continuous with everything else the novel cares about instead of importing a religious register that appears nowhere else with the same force. And it has an answer to the charge of flatness, the worry that calling the eyes an advertisement drains them of power. The power, it says, is precisely in the flatness: the horror is that the most imposing image in the valley is selling eyeglasses, and the novel's moral vision consists in showing a world where nothing higher is on offer.

### What does the void reading refuse to grant the eyes?

It refuses them any stable referent, arguing that their force comes from emptiness rather than hidden meaning. The eyes are paint on a board, the novel knows it, and the critical compulsion to fill them, with God, capital, or the reader, reveals the filler's hunger rather than the image's content. The debate's failure to converge becomes the reading's best evidence.

The void reading refuses to grant the eyes any stable referent at all, arguing that the image's force comes from its emptiness and that every attempt to fill it says more about the filler than the filled. Its proponents hold that the eyes are paint on a board, that the novel knows this, and that the critical compulsion to make them mean something is the real subject.

This is the most austere of the five positions, and its strength is its refusal to do the work the other readings do for it. Start again with the physical facts, which the void reading treats as exhaustive rather than as a surface to be seen through. The eyes are a faded advertisement for a defunct or departed oculist's practice. They depict no face, no body, no person; they are "a pair of enormous yellow spectacles which pass over a nonexistent nose," an image of seeing equipment with no seer behind it. The void reading argues that the novel is explicit about this hollowness and that the criticism's long effort to fill it, with God, with capital, with the reader, is a repeated demonstration of the very hunger the novel diagnoses. Wilson looks at paint and sees God; the divine-reading critic looks at Wilson looking at paint and sees a theological thesis; at each level the meaning is supplied by the looker, and the text's contribution is to stage the supplying.

The reading's signature move is to turn the debate itself into evidence. If the eyes really were God, the void argument runs, the criticism would have converged by now; the fact that serious readers have filled the image with five incompatible meanings across many decades suggests the image is designed to receive meanings rather than to contain one. The novel's own structure supports this: the eyes appear, they are described with unusual intensity, and then nothing in the plot depends on what they mean. No character acts on a correct or incorrect interpretation of them; Wilson acts on his own grief, not on the eyes' instruction. A symbol that does no narrative work and sustains every interpretation is, for this position, not a rich symbol but an empty one, and its richness is an artifact of the attention lavished on it. The void reading thus presents itself as the only position that explains the debate's persistence instead of merely participating in it.

Its proponents are careful to distinguish the void from mere insignificance, because the obvious objection is that calling the eyes meaningless makes them unworthy of the novel's emphasis. The distinction is between semantic emptiness and functional emptiness: the eyes mean nothing, but they do something, which is to reveal the interpretive hunger of everyone who looks at them, characters and critics alike. Wilson's projection is the in-novel demonstration; the critical debate is the real-world demonstration. The image is thus a mirror rather than a window, and its power is reflexive: it shows the looker to themselves. This is why the void reading can sound close to the reader-as-witness reading while remaining distinct. The witness reading says the eyes figure the act of looking; the void reading says there is nothing behind the look, and the looking is all there is. The difference matters because the witness reading still grants the image a positive content, the staging of spectatorship, while the void reading grants it none.

The line of argument also recruits the novel's larger treatment of failed symbols. Gatsby's green light, Daisy's voice "full of money," the shirts: the book is crowded with things characters invest with meanings that dissolve on contact, and the eyes are the purest case because they are nothing but a surface. The void reading places the billboard at the head of this series and argues that Fitzgerald's deepest subject is the human manufacture of significance in a world that does not supply it. On this view the eyes are not an exception to the novel's symbolism but its thesis statement: look how much meaning can be hung on paint, and look how the paint remains paint. Whether that thesis can be sustained without turning the novel into an anti-symbolic tract is the pressure point its opponents press, but the reading's economy is undeniable: it explains the most textual facts with the fewest assumptions, and it is the only position that predicts, rather than laments, the debate's endlessness.

### Where does the reader-as-witness position fit?

The reader-as-witness reading treats the eyes as a figure for spectatorship itself, arguing that the novel uses the billboard to stage the act of looking and thereby implicate the reader. Its proponents hold that the image's meaning is not a referent but a situation: someone watching, someone watched, and the ethical weight of the watching.

This position starts from a structural observation the other readings underuse: the eyes are the novel's most insistent image of vision, and the novel is itself an extended act of vision, Nick watching Gatsby watching Daisy, the reader watching Nick. The billboard literalizes that structure by planting a giant pair of eyes at the site where the classes and the plotlines intersect, so that every act of looking in the book has a monumental counterpart. The reading argues that Fitzgerald is doing something reflexive here, using the image to make the novel's own spectatorship visible to itself. When Wilson looks at the eyes and sees God, when Michaelis looks and sees an advertisement, when the motorists look and see a landmark, the novel is showing that seeing is never neutral, that every gaze brings its need to the object. The reader, who has been watching all of this watching, is then caught in the same structure: our reading of the eyes is one more gaze bringing one more need, and the image's function is to make that complicity felt.

The evidence marshaled is compositional rather than lexical. The eyes appear at moments of moral exposure: the first description comes as Nick and Tom pass through the valley on the way to the city, establishing the watched-over quality of the whole landscape before the plot's betrayals begin; they return in Wilson's window at the moment of his breakdown, when the need for a witness is most acute; and they preside, in a sense, over the aftermath, the valley where the consequences accumulate. The reading does not claim the eyes cause any of this. It claims they frame it, converting the reader's attention into a theme. The enormous scale is functional on this account: the eyes have to be gigantic because they have to stand for the scale of novelistic attention itself, the way a whole invented world is held under a reader's gaze for the duration of the book. The yellow spectacles, the instruments of vision, reinforce the point: this is an image about the apparatus of seeing, not about any particular thing seen.

Its proponents also argue that the position explains a feature the other readings handle awkwardly, which is why the eyes are described with such lavish visual specificity. The blue irises, the yellow spectacles, the yard-high retinas, the paintless days: if the eyes were simply God, the novel could have said so through Wilson and moved on; if they were simply an advertisement, a sentence would suffice. The descriptive excess is, for the witness reading, the point, because the novel is training the reader's eye, teaching us to look hard at surfaces and then asking what our looking does. The passage is a lesson in novelistic attention disguised as a landmark, and its placement early in the book, before the reader has learned how to read this particular novel, makes it a kind of instruction: watch everything, notice the watching, suspect your own gaze. The later critical debate is then the lesson's afterlife, generations of readers demonstrating exactly the dynamic the image stages.

The position's distinctiveness lies in where it locates the meaning: not in the image, not in the culture, not in theology, but in the transaction between text and reader. This is why it can sound like the void reading while differing from it decisively. The void reading says the transaction reveals that there is nothing there; the witness reading says the transaction is itself the content, that the novel is about what it is like to watch other people's lives and to be watched in turn. Wilson's error, on this view, is not that he sees God in paint but that he outsources his judgment to an image instead of exercising it himself, and the reader who outsources interpretation to a critical consensus repeats the gesture. The reading thus has an ethical edge the others lack: it does not just map what the eyes mean, it asks what our meaning-making costs. Its vulnerability, which the table will record, is that it risks making the novel about reading at the expense of making it about the world it depicts, but as a line of argument it is the only one that takes the criticism's own behavior as part of the evidence.

## Where the Positions Collide

Stating each position at full strength is only half the map; the other half is showing where the positions actually strike one another, because a debate is defined by its points of contact. The five readings do not simply sit side by side. They make incompatible claims about the same passages, and tracing those collisions shows why the disagreement has the staying power it does. This section takes the two hardest pressure points in the whole debate and works through them as exchanges, giving each side its best move and its best reply, without declaring a winner. The discipline from the previous section holds: the collisions are mapped, not adjudicated.

### Which objection does each position struggle most to answer?

Each position has one objection it handles worst: the divine reading must explain the advertisement, the commercial reading must explain the moral language, the void reading must justify the descriptive intensity, and the witness reading must avoid flattering the critic. Naming these pressure points keeps the map honest, since strengths alone make a brief rather than a map.

Working through them in turn shows how the debate's pressure is distributed. Take the divine reading's hardest objection first. The novel states, in Nick's own voice, that the eyes were painted by an oculist to fatten his practice. The divine reading's standard reply distinguishes the literal origin from the symbolic function, and that reply works as far as it goes, but it leaves a residue: if the symbolic function is what matters, why did Fitzgerald bother giving the literal origin at all, and in such memorable comic detail? The "wild wag of an oculist" is not a throwaway line; it is one of the passage's most distinctive sentences, and a reading that needs it to be irrelevant is paying a real cost. The divine reading can pay it, symbolism routinely outruns literal origins, but the map should record the price rather than hiding it.

The commercial reading's hardest objection is the mirror image. If the eyes are only an advertisement, why does the novel describe them with language that keeps escaping the commercial register? "Brood" is not a word for advertising; "solemn" is not a word for trade signs; the enormous scale and the valley placement keep inviting the very theological language the reading wants to retire. The commercial reply is that the excess is the point, that the horror is precisely the gap between the grand language and the grubby fact, but this reply concedes that the language is grand, which is what the theological readings needed. The reading thus wins its deflationary point only by standing on the descriptive intensity it is trying to deflate, and the tension is structural, not a matter of emphasis.

The absent-God reading's hardest objection is boundary blur: pressed hard, it slides toward its neighbors and risks losing its identity. Emphasize the withdrawal and it starts to sound like the void reading with theological decoration; emphasize the grief over the loss and it starts to sound like the divine reading with a melancholy twist; emphasize the trade sign filling the God-shaped hole and it starts to sound like the commercial reading with better prose. Its proponents answer that the position is defined by holding all three emphases at once, and that the blur is a feature, since secularization is itself a blurred condition. Whether a position can be both the most nuanced and the most distinct is a genuine question, and the map records it as unresolved.

The void reading's hardest objection is the simplest to state and the hardest to answer: if the eyes mean nothing, why does the novel spend some of its finest descriptive prose on them? The standard reply distinguishes semantic emptiness from functional emptiness, the eyes do the work of revealing projection, but the reply has to carry the full weight of the passage's beauty, and beauty in Fitzgerald is rarely just a delivery mechanism for a thesis about meaninglessness. The witness reading's hardest objection is scope creep: once the eyes become a figure for reading itself, the reading can seem to make the novel about the critic rather than about the world, and its proponents must show that the reflexivity illuminates the valley rather than merely flattering the interpreter. Each of these objections is live, each has a real reply, and none has ended the debate, which is exactly why the map needs them all visible.

### How do the theological readings handle the advertisement objection?

They handle it with a two-level theory of meaning: the commercial origin is literal fact, while the symbolic function as judgment operates separately. A trade sign can signify transcendence, so the oculist's motive does not cancel the theology. The commercial counter is that this demotes the text's own causal sentence, and the exchange exposes rival theories of how symbols work.

The objection, pressed by the commercial line, is straightforward: the novel says the eyes are an advertisement, so readings that make them God or a dead God are ignoring the text's own account. The theological reply distinguishes what the image is on the literal level from what it does symbolically, arguing that a painted trade sign can function as a figure of judgment just as a wooden cross can function as a figure of faith regardless of who paid the carpenter. The reply is coherent, and it has the weight of literary tradition behind it: symbols routinely outgrow their literal occasions, and no serious reader thinks the green light stops meaning longing because it is also a dock lamp.

The commercial counter-move is to deny that the two levels are really separate here. The objection runs that the oculist's motive is not just a literal fact but the passage's own explanation of the image, offered by the narrator without irony, and that the two-level theory is a device for discarding the one causal sentence the text provides. On this view, the theological readings are not distinguishing levels but demoting the level they cannot use, and the "wild wag" sentence is the text's way of telling the reader not to do exactly what those readings do. The force of this counter depends on how much authority one grants Nick's flat statements of fact, which is why the debate keeps circling back to the narrator: if Nick's causal asides are reliable, the commercial reading gains; if his descriptive language is the truer signal, the theological readings gain.

The theological counter-reply then points to the asymmetry of the passage itself. Nick gives the oculist's motive in one comic sentence and gives the brooding, solemn, gigantic presence in a sustained descriptive performance; the passage's energy is overwhelmingly on the side of the image's power rather than its commercial origin. A narrator who wanted to deflate the eyes would not describe them like this, and the fact that the deflationary sentence sits inside a passage of such intensity suggests the deflation is itself part of the intensity, a realistic detail that makes the symbol stranger rather than smaller. The commercial reading can answer that the strangeness is the point, that the gap between the grubby origin and the grand effect is the novel's whole moral vision in miniature, and at this point the exchange has become genuinely productive: both sides are now describing the same gap, and they differ only on what the gap means.

Notice what has happened across this exchange. Neither side has produced a new passage; both have re-read the same sentences with increasing precision, and each round has clarified what is really at stake, which is not a fact about the billboard but a theory of how symbols work. The theological side needs symbols to be able to transcend their occasions; the commercial side needs the occasion to discipline the symbol. That is a disagreement about literary method wearing the clothes of a disagreement about the eyes, and recognizing it as such is one of the map's main services. A researcher who sees the collision clearly can stop hunting for the decisive passage, since the passages are shared, and start examining the theories of symbolism each side presupposes, which is where the real work of advancing the debate would have to happen.

## Why the Novel Sustains Every Position

Mapping the positions raises the obvious follow-up: why has none of them managed to defeat the others? The answer is not that critics have been careless but that the text is built to sustain multiple readings at once, and this section shows the machinery of that sustainment. Understanding the machinery matters for research practice, because it tells a researcher what kind of evidence can move the debate and what kind cannot, and it explains why the honest outcome of studying the disagreement is a better map rather than a winning side.

### Which passages do all five positions keep returning to?

All five return to the same three passages: the billboard's introduction in Chapter 2, Wilson's exchange with Michaelis in Chapter 8, and the valley the eyes overlook. No position can ignore any of them, so the evidence base is shared and the disagreements are interpretive rather than archival. New questions move this debate more than new facts ever will.

That convergence is the debate's most revealing structural fact. The first is the introduction of the billboard in Chapter 2, with the oculist's motive, the enormous spectacles, the nonexistent nose, and the eyes dimmed by paintless days. The second is Wilson's dialogue in Chapter 8, "God sees everything" met by "It's an advertisement" and answered with "You can't fool God!" The third is the valley itself, the ash-grey landscape the eyes overlook, since every reading needs an account of why the image is planted there and not elsewhere. Each position draws different conclusions from these passages, but no position can afford to ignore any of them, which means the debate's evidence base is shared and its disagreements are interpretive rather than archival.

The shared evidence base has an important consequence: new textual discoveries are unlikely to settle the debate, because there is almost nothing left to discover about the passages themselves. The novel is short, the eyes appear briefly, and the relevant sentences have been parsed for decades. What changes the debate is not new evidence but new questions, and the history traced earlier confirms this: each new position arrived carrying a new kind of question, about commerce, about secularization, about reading itself, and re-read the same sentences through it. A researcher hoping to contribute should therefore aim at the question level rather than the passage level, asking what the existing evidence has not yet been asked to show. The setting itself rewards this approach: the valley of ashes as a symbolic landscape is treated at length in the article on [valley of ashes symbolism](/2025/05/05/valley-of-ashes-symbolism/), and any new reading of the eyes has to reckon with how that scholarship has fixed the meaning of the ground the eyes watch over.

There is also a compositional reason the same passages keep serving every position, which is that Fitzgerald wrote them to be overdetermined. The introduction of the billboard gives the commercial origin, the theological verb, and the material decay in a single paragraph; Wilson's scene gives the religious identification and its demystification in a single exchange. A passage that supplies every reading with its best evidence is not a passage that failed to choose; it is a passage designed to hold the alternatives in tension. The novel's method with the eyes is the same as its method with the green light and with Daisy's voice: present an image at maximum intensity, attach several incompatible significances, and withdraw before adjudicating. Recognizing this method is what separates a mature account of the debate from a frustrated one. The frustration assumes the text owes the reader a decision; the mature account sees that the withholding is the technique, and that the critical disagreement is the technique working as designed on its most qualified readers.

This also explains why appeals to authorial intention have never settled the matter. Fitzgerald left no statement assigning the eyes a meaning, and the manuscript record, so far as it is known, does not contain a discarded draft that resolves the image into a single significance. Intention-based arguments therefore have to be reconstructed from the text, which returns the researcher to the same passages everyone else is reading. Biographical speculation, that Fitzgerald meant this or that by the billboard, adds color but not decision, because the published text is what the debate is about and the published text is the thing that withholds. The honest intention-based claim is a modest one: Fitzgerald intended the image to be productively open, and the debate's persistence is evidence that the intention succeeded. That claim supports the map rather than any position on it, which is why it belongs in this section and not in the table.

### How can a researcher compare the readings without picking a winner?

Compare them with three shared tests applied evenly: textual coverage, systemic fit with the novel's other symbols, and predictive power about why rival readings look plausible. Record the results explicitly without letting the tests smuggle in a preferred outcome, and keep popularity, cleverness, and fashion out of the judgment.

The first test is textual coverage: how much of the relevant evidence does the reading account for, and what does it have to minimize or explain away? The divine reading covers Wilson's dialogue beautifully and strains on the advertisement; the commercial reading covers the oculist's motive and strains on the moral language; the void reading covers the inaction and strains on the descriptive intensity. Coverage is a genuine virtue, but no position covers everything, which is why the test informs without deciding. The second test is systemic fit: how well does the reading cohere with the novel's other symbols and concerns? The commercial reading fits the commodity-saturated imagery; the absent-God reading fits the religious emptiness; the witness reading fits the novel's architecture of watching. Fit is illuminating, but different readings fit different systems, so the test multiplies perspectives rather than crowning one.

The third test is the most useful and the least used: what does the reading predict about the rest of the debate? A strong reading should be able to explain why the other readings look plausible, since their plausibility is itself a fact about the text. The void reading does this best, predicting the disagreement's persistence from the image's emptiness; the witness reading does it nearly as well, predicting it from the structure of spectatorship. The divine and commercial readings do it less well, because each has to treat the other's plausibility as a kind of error, Wilson's delusion or the critic's reduction. This test does not pick a winner either, since predictive power about the debate is a different virtue from fidelity to the text, and a researcher should say which virtue they are weighting rather than pretending the tests converge. The point of the three tests is to make comparison disciplined and explicit, so that a literature review can say, of each position, what it covers, what it fits, and what it predicts, without the reviewer's thumb on the scale.

There is a practical corollary for anyone writing about the debate, whether in an essay, a thesis chapter, or a seminar paper. State each position's best case before raising objections, keep the objections symmetrical by applying the same tests to every side, and never let a position's popularity do the work of argument. The divine reading's classroom dominance is a fact about teaching, not a fact about the text, and a researcher who treats it as evidence is confusing sociology with interpretation. Equally, the void reading's cleverness is a fact about its formulators, not a proof of its correctness. The map is a tool for keeping these confusions visible, and the researcher who uses it well will produce an account in which a reader can see exactly where each judgment was made and on what grounds. The broader field rewards this discipline: the survey of Gatsby scholarship shows how the criticism has matured precisely by becoming more explicit about its own procedures, and debate-mapping of this kind is part of that maturation, a way of writing about disagreement that honors every side enough to state it fairly, which can be consulted in [the survey of Gatsby scholarship](/2030/12/02/gatsby-scholarship-survey/) for the wider context.

A final caution belongs here about the difference between mapping and relativism. Saying the debate is live is not saying every reading is equally good or that evidence does not matter. Some positions cover more text, some fit the novel's systems better, and a researcher is entitled to say so, provided the saying is comparative rather than conclusive. The map's discipline is to keep the comparisons visible and the conclusions provisional, because the text has not authorized a final one. This is a demanding standard, harder than picking a side, since it requires holding five strong cases in the mind at once without letting any of them collapse into a straw man. But it is the standard the material requires, and the articles that last in this critical conversation are the ones that meet it.

## A Worked Toolkit for Mapping the Debate

Everything so far has described the debate; this section turns the map into practice. Researchers, teachers, and students need more than positions and collisions: they need to see what good debate-mapping looks like on the page, what bad mapping looks like so they can avoid it, and how to organize the work of studying the disagreement. What follows is deliberately concrete: a before-and-after pair showing a weak map repaired, a model comparative paragraph that can be imitated, and a one-week research plan narrated as prose. The toolkit keeps the article's central discipline throughout, since a toolkit that smuggled in a verdict would betray the map it serves.

### What does a weak debate map look like?

A weak map lists the readings as isolated opinions and lets preference leak through the ordering, walking toward a pre-chosen winner. One position gets its best evidence in full while the others get summaries, and the closing performs an adjudication the evidence never supported. The failure is structural, so the repair is structural: equal strength, symmetrical objections, neutral ordering.

The before version typically runs like this: a paragraph each on the divine, commercial, and void readings, with the divine reading given Wilson's dialogue in full, the commercial reading given a dismissive summary, and the void reading presented as a clever paradox, followed by a closing line that the divine reading "remains the most persuasive." Nothing in the before version is factually wrong, which is what makes it instructive: the failure is structural, not factual. The positions are not stated at equal strength, the objections are not distributed evenly, and the conclusion performs an adjudication the evidence was never asked to support.

The after version repairs the structure without changing the facts. Each position gets its best evidence stated first, in the same amount of space, before any objection is raised; the objections are then applied symmetrically, so the divine reading's advertisement problem sits beside the commercial reading's moral-language problem and the void reading's intensity problem. The ordering follows the debate's history or a neutral logic rather than a rising scale of persuasiveness, and the closing states what the comparison showed instead of who won: which passages each reading covers, where the collisions lie, what remains unresolved. The practical test for the repair is simple: a proponent of any of the five positions should be able to read the map and recognize their own view at its strongest. If one side's proponents would protest that they were shortchanged, the map is still weak, and the fix is more evidence for the shortchanged side, not softer language.

### What does a strong comparative paragraph look like?

A strong comparative paragraph puts two positions on the same passage and shows exactly where they diverge, keeping the evidence shared and the interpretation distinct. It names the common evidence, states each side's use in parallel syntax, and locates the disagreement one level up, in method rather than fact. The template is stable: shared evidence, parallel assignments, precise divergence.

Here is a model, built on Wilson's exchange with Michaelis, that a researcher can imitate: "Both the divine and the commercial readings cite Wilson's 'God sees everything' and Michaelis's 'It's an advertisement,' but they assign the two speeches opposite authority. The divine reading hears Wilson's cry as the scene's revelation and Michaelis's correction as the voice of a demystifying modernity, true on the literal level and beside the point symbolically. The commercial reading reverses the assignment: Michaelis speaks the scene's clarity and Wilson speaks its pathos, a grieving man mistaking paint for providence. The passage itself does not arbitrate, since Fitzgerald gives neither speaker a narrator's endorsement, so the divergence is not about which speech occurred but about what kind of speech each is, revelation or symptom, clarity or coldness."

Notice the paragraph's machinery, since the machinery is what makes it imitable. It opens by naming the shared evidence, so the reader sees the comparison is fair; it states each position's assignment of authority in parallel syntax, so the contrast is structural rather than rhetorical; it closes by locating the disagreement one level up, in the theory of what the speeches are, rather than pretending the passage settles it. A paragraph built this way can be written about any collision in the debate: the faded paint, the verb "brood," the oculist's motive. The template is stable: shared evidence first, parallel statements of what each side makes of it, then the precise point of divergence named at the level of method. Paragraphs like this are the basic unit of debate-mapping prose, and a literature review composed of them will be fair almost automatically, because the form forces symmetry.

### How should a week of research on the debate be planned?

Move from the text outward to the criticism and back: two days on the passages with a five-column notebook, three days reconstructing each position at its strongest, and two days comparing with the shared tests and drafting comparative paragraphs. Read comparatively only after each view is understood from the inside, then check the draft against the fairness test.

The first two days belong to the novel itself: read the Chapter 2 billboard passage and the Chapter 8 Wilson scene slowly, with a notebook divided into five columns, one per position, and record under each column the details that position would emphasize and the details it would have to minimize. This exercise produces a personal map before any secondary reading can shape it, and the columns will already show where the collisions lie. The discipline is to fill every column honestly, including the positions one finds least plausible, since the point is to understand the debate's structure, not to audition favorites.

The middle three days belong to the criticism, and the rule is to read each position at its strongest before reading its critics. Take one position per day, or per half day for the shorter ones, and reconstruct its line of argument from the inside: what it claims, what evidence it marshals, how it answers the hardest objection. Resist the urge to read comparatively at this stage, because premature comparison produces the weak map described above, with the first position read generously and the rest read as foils. Notes from this phase should be organized by position, not by topic, so that each reading's internal coherence is visible before it is set against the others. By the end of the fifth day the researcher should be able to state all five positions from memory, at something close to full strength, which is the real entrance requirement for writing about the debate.

The final two days belong to comparison and to writing. Lay the five reconstructions side by side and apply the three shared tests: coverage, systemic fit, and predictive power about the debate itself, recording the results in prose rather than scores. Then draft the account using the comparative-paragraph template, one collision at a time, and check the draft against the fairness test: would a proponent of each position recognize their view? The last task is to return to the passages with the debate in mind and notice what the week has changed about the reading of them; usually the passages look stranger and richer than they did on the first day, which is the sign the research worked. A week planned this way will not produce a verdict, but it will produce something the debate actually needs: another careful map, drawn by someone who did the looking themselves.

## What the Map Leaves Out

Every map has edges, and an honest one names them. The five positions mapped here cover the critical debate over what the eyes mean in the published text, but there are neighboring questions the map deliberately brackets, and there is a standing worry about whether evenhanded mapping creates a false balance. This section addresses both, because a researcher who understands what the map excludes and why will use it better than one who mistakes the map for the whole territory.

The first exclusion is genesis: where the image came from. The novel's 1925 jacket art shows a pair of disembodied eyes over a carnival cityscape, and some commentary has linked that cover image to the billboard, suggesting the painted eyes entered Fitzgerald's imagination through the artwork rather than through the valley's landscape. That is a composition-history question, and it belongs to the study of how the book was made, not to the debate over what the finished text sustains. A second exclusion is biographical: speculation about Fitzgerald's own religious beliefs, his Catholic upbringing, or his attitudes toward faith, offered as a key to the eyes. Such speculation may illuminate why an author would write this image, but the debate mapped here concerns what the published novel supports, and authorial belief does not bind the text's readers. A third exclusion is pedagogical: the simplified versions of the readings that circulate in classrooms and study guides, which are worth knowing about as sociology but are not positions in the scholarly debate. Each exclusion follows the same principle: the map tracks the interpretive disagreement about the text, and questions of origin, belief, and transmission belong to adjacent inquiries with their own methods and their own articles.

A concrete example shows why the boundary matters. Suppose a student argues that the eyes must signify divine judgment because the jacket art's celestial eyes, commissioned before the novel was finished, depict a heaven-like visage. The inference moves from origin to meaning: because the image may have arrived via the cover, it must carry the cover's connotations. But the move is invalid on its face. An image's source does not fix its function in a new context; the prose reworks whatever it borrows, and the billboard passage gives the eyes a commercial motive, a valley setting, and a faded materiality that the jacket does not share. The student's evidence is real, the jacket exists and the resemblance is visible, yet the conclusion does not follow, because nothing about the cover determines how the sentences behave. Contrast the legitimate composition-history version of the same inquiry: a researcher who documents when the billboard entered the drafts, what the jacket looked like at that date, and whether any correspondence mentions it is doing valuable work that could reshape understanding of the novel's making. That work belongs in a study of composition, where it can be judged by archival standards, rather than in the interpretive debate, where it would be judged, unfairly, by standards it was never meant to meet. Keeping the questions separate protects both.

### Why does the map exclude the cover-art connection?

It excludes it because genesis is a different question from meaning: whether the jacket's eyes influenced the billboard concerns the image's origin, not what the published text sustains. The map tracks the critical debate over interpretation, and origin stories belong to composition history, a neighboring territory with its own article.

The exclusion deserves a fuller explanation because the cover-art connection is genuinely interesting and frequently raised. The jacket's celestial eyes, floating above a lit city, do resemble the billboard's gigantic eyes presiding over the valley, and the resemblance has prompted suggestions that Fitzgerald worked the cover image into the narrative. Treated as composition history, this is a legitimate line of inquiry: it asks how the image entered the book, what the author saw, and how the visual culture of the period shaped the prose. But notice that nothing in that inquiry settles, or even addresses, the interpretive question the five positions debate. If the cover inspired the billboard, the billboard still does what it does in the text: it still broods, still fades, still draws Wilson's projection and Michaelis's correction. Genesis explains the image's arrival; it cannot determine the image's meaning, because meaning in a published novel is a function of how the words work in context, not of where the author first saw something. The map therefore brackets the cover-art connection without dismissing it, and a researcher pursuing it should frame the work as composition history from the start, with the archive and the publishing record as the evidence base rather than the novel's sentences.

The same logic governs the biographical exclusion, and it is worth stating carefully because biographical readings of the eyes have a persistent appeal. Fitzgerald was raised Catholic and drifted from the faith; it is tempting to read the faded, watching eyes as the author's own lost God. The temptation should be resisted at the level of the debate, not because biography is irrelevant to literature in general, but because this particular debate is about what the text sustains, and the text sustains all five positions regardless of what its author believed. A Catholic Fitzgerald and an atheist Fitzgerald would have produced the same sentences, and the same five readings would attach to them. Biographical context can enrich an account of why the image took this form, and it belongs in a full study of the novel, but it cannot adjudicate between the positions, which is the only thing that would earn it a place on the map. The map's edges are thus drawn by a single rule: include what bears on the interpretive disagreement, exclude what answers a different question, and say plainly which is which.

### Does evenhanded mapping create a false balance?

No, because mapping is not relativism: the map records differential strengths through the shared tests, showing which readings cover more text and fit the novel's systems better, without converting those comparisons into a final verdict. Evenhandedness means equal rigor in stating each case, not equal praise, and the table's resistance column exists precisely to keep the differences visible.

The false-balance worry is worth taking seriously because it names a real failure mode. In fields where evidence has settled a question, presenting settled and fringe views side by side misleads the reader, and critics of debate-mapping sometimes import that worry into literary study. But the analogy fails here for a simple reason: the Eckleburg debate is genuinely unsettled, with all five positions live in the literature and none refuted. Evenhandedness in this context is not a courtesy extended to weak views but an accurate representation of the field's actual state. Moreover, the map does not treat the positions as equal in every respect; it differentiates them constantly, through the coverage test, the fit test, the predictive test, and the resistance column of the table. A reader who works through those instruments will have a clear sense of where each position is strong and where it strains, which is a more informative kind of differentiation than a verdict would provide, since a verdict hides the comparisons that produced it.

There is also a revisability condition that keeps the map honest over time. The map is a snapshot of the debate as it stands, and it should be updated when the debate moves: if new archival evidence reshapes a position, if a new theoretical vocabulary opens a sixth, if one reading collapses under criticism, the map changes. Stating the positions at full strength does not freeze them; it gives future revision something definite to revise. A researcher who disagrees with the map's balance should therefore argue with the map on its own terms, showing where a position was overstated or a resistance understated, rather than dismissing mapping as such. That is how the cartographic tradition advances: not by replacing maps with verdicts, but by drawing better maps, each one correcting the last. The map offered here aims to be worth correcting, which is the highest ambition a non-adjudicating article can have.

## Verdict: The Debate Stays Live, and the Liveness Is the Finding

The debate over the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg remains live, and this article does not resolve it, because resolving it would require choosing among readings that the text deliberately keeps in play. The divine reading has Wilson's dialogue and the brooding verb; the absent-God reading has the faded paint and the passive gaze; the commercial reading has the oculist's stated motive and the billboard form; the void reading has the image's narrative inertness and the debate's own failure to converge; the witness reading has the compositional staging of acts of looking. Each of these is a genuine achievement of attention, built from evidence the novel actually supplies, and each is met by a genuine resistance that the other positions articulate. To declare a winner would be to pretend the text has done something it has not done, which is to authorize one of these accounts over the rest.

What can be said, without adjudicating, is why the disagreement has the shape it does and what that shape reveals. The eyes are a small, vivid, semantically open image planted at the novel's moral center of gravity, described with unusual intensity and left without authorial gloss, in a book whose method is to present charged images and withdraw before interpreting them. A text built that way will generate exactly this kind of debate: multiple strong readings, a shared evidence base, disagreements that turn out to be about method rather than about facts, and a history in which each new position is a response to the last. The liveness of the debate is therefore not a failure of criticism but a finding about the object, evidence that the image is doing the work Fitzgerald designed it to do. A researcher who understands this will stop asking which reading is correct and start asking what each reading discloses, about the text, about the culture that produced it, and about the act of reading itself.

That shift, from adjudication to mapping, is the standard this article holds itself to and recommends to others. It is harder than picking a side, because it requires stating every position at full strength, applying the same tests to all of them, and resisting the pull of classroom consensus, theoretical fashion, and the sheer pleasure of a decisive claim. But it is also more honest, and in the long run more useful, since maps outlast verdicts: the positions mapped here will be argued over for as long as the novel is read, while any particular adjudication would be one more entry in the debate rather than its conclusion. The eyes will go on brooding over the valley, the critics will go on disagreeing about what the brooding means, and the disagreement itself will remain what it has always been, the sign of an image strong enough to hold them all.

## Study: Grounding the Readings and Finding the Scholarship

A debate map is only as good as the reader's access to the underlying materials, so this section points to the two places where the work continues. First, the readings mapped here all rest on a small number of passages, and there is no substitute for examining those passages closely, in sequence, with the competing interpretations in mind. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the critical debate over Eckleburg's eyes?

It is the long-running scholarly disagreement about what the giant bespectacled eyes on the billboard above the valley of ashes mean. Critics have read them as God, as a withdrawn or dead God, as commercial advertising, as an empty void, and as a figure for the reader's own watching, and each reading draws on real textual evidence. The debate matters because the image is vivid, morally placed, and semantically open, so it attracts strong interpretations that cannot all be true in the same way. This article maps the disagreement without resolving it, showing what each position claims, what evidence it rests on, and how the positions answer one another, so researchers can navigate the criticism with every side intact.

### Q: What competing readings of the eyes exist?

Five positions make up the current map. The divine reading takes the eyes as God, a moral witness brooding over corruption, anchored in Wilson's dialogue. The absent-God reading keeps the theological frame but argues the novel stages a withdrawn deity, with the faded paint as evidence of decayed faith. The commercial reading treats the billboard as advertising and argues that commerce has replaced transcendence in the novel's world. The void reading denies any stable referent and argues the image's emptiness is its point. The reader-as-witness reading treats the eyes as a figure for spectatorship itself, implicating the reader's gaze. Each has textual ground and each faces honest resistance.

### Q: How has the debate evolved?

The debate accumulated in layers. Early commentary treated the eyes as atmosphere and moral coloring, with the divine association present but undefended. The mid-century symbol-hunting decades turned the image into a puzzle to decode, hardening the divine reading into a classroom thesis while the commercial counter-reading began to form. The theory-driven decades then added new kinds of questions rather than new meanings: materialist critics developed the commercial line, reader-oriented critics built the witness reading, and the absent-God and void positions sharpened in response. Each phase reacted to the last, so the current five-position map is best understood as a conversation with a memory, not five independent discoveries.

### Q: What evidence does each reading rest on?

Each reading leans hardest on different features of the same small evidence base. The divine reading rests on Wilson's "God sees everything" and "You can't fool God," the verb "brood," and the overseeing placement. The absent-God reading rests on the dimmed, paintless eyes, the gaze that never acts, and the novel's broader religious emptiness. The commercial reading rests on the oculist's stated motive to fatten his practice, the billboard form, and the commodity-saturated imagery elsewhere. The void reading rests on the faceless spectacles, the image's narrative inertness, and the debate's failure to converge. The witness reading rests on the placement at moments of moral exposure and the lavish visual specificity.

### Q: Should the debate be resolved for one reading?

No, and the attempt to resolve it misunderstands what the text is doing. Each of the five readings is built from evidence the novel actually supplies, and each faces genuine resistance from the others, so crowning a winner would require the text to authorize a choice it withholds. Fitzgerald gives the commercial origin, the theological language, the material decay, and the staged projection in the same passages, then lets them stand in tension without a narrator's correction. The honest scholarly outcome is a better map, not a verdict: a clear account of what each position claims, covers, and cannot explain. Researchers should compare the readings explicitly rather than adjudicating silently.

### Q: Why does the symbol's openness generate debate?

The openness generates debate because the image is overdetermined: a single paragraph supplies the commercial origin, the theological verb, and the material decay, while a single exchange supplies both the religious identification and its demystification. An image that is vivid, placed at the novel's moral center, described with unusual intensity, and left without authorial gloss will attract strong readings the way a magnet attracts filings. Each new critical method then finds in the eyes a ready test case, re-reading the same sentences through new questions about commerce, secularization, or spectatorship. The debate persists not because critics are careless but because the text is designed to sustain multiple defensible accounts at once.

### Q: How do you cite the novel's edition when quoting the billboard passages?

Cite the specific edition you actually used, giving the publisher and year of that edition in your bibliography, and quote the passages from it rather than from memory or from a quotation site. The billboard's introduction and Wilson's dialogue are short enough that small wording differences between editions rarely change the argument, but scholarly practice still requires the citation to be checkable. If your argument turns on a particular word, such as "brood" or "dimmed," confirm it in a scholarly edition and say which one you are following. For class essays, the course's assigned edition is sufficient; for publication, name a standard text. Precision here signals that the debate is being conducted at the level of the words, where it belongs.

### Q: Can the divine and commercial readings of the eyes coexist in one argument?

They can coexist only if the argument is explicit about operating on two levels, and most attempts to combine them collapse under pressure. The divine reading treats the advertisement as a literal fact that is irrelevant to the symbolic register, while the commercial reading treats the symbolic register as an evasion of the literal fact, so the two positions disagree about what kind of meaning is legitimate. A combined argument has to state a theory of how an image can be both commerce and transcendence without letting one level quietly do all the work. The absent-God reading is sometimes offered as a synthesis, since it keeps the commercial fact and the theological language together, but it is really a third position with its own commitments rather than a compromise.

### Q: What separates the void reading from the absent-God reading?

The difference is whether the image's emptiness is theological or total. The absent-God reading says the eyes mean something specific: a God who has withdrawn, leaving a faded sign of a lost faith, so the emptiness is charged with grief and points to secularization. The void reading says the eyes mean nothing at all and refuses even the theological frame, arguing that the hunger for a referent, Wilson's and the critics', is the real subject. In practice the two can sound alike, since both dwell on the faded paint and the passive gaze, but their claims diverge sharply: one fills the hollow with a departed God, the other insists the hollow is the point. Conflating them weakens both.

### Q: How does the valley of ashes setting feed the debate?

The setting feeds the debate because it gives every reading its moral stakes. The valley is the novel's landscape of industrial waste and human cost, the dumping ground between the eggs and the city, and the eyes brood over it from above. The divine reading needs the valley to be a fallen world worth judging; the commercial reading needs it to be the economy's exhaust, watched by commerce itself; the absent-God reading needs it to be a place faith has abandoned; the void reading needs it to be the site where projection becomes desperate. Because the ground below is so heavily freighted, the image above it cannot be neutral, and each position recruits the setting as the context that makes its reading necessary.

### Q: Why does this debate work well in a classroom?

It works because it makes critical method visible through a single vivid image. Students can grasp all five positions in one session, see that each rests on real evidence, and discover that the disagreement is about what counts as evidence rather than about who read carefully. The debate teaches the difference between a symbol's literal origin and its possible meanings, the way a character's interpretation can be staged rather than endorsed, and the discipline of stating an opponent's case at full strength. It also inoculates against the most common student error, which is citing one critic's reading as a fact about the novel. Few other passages in the book compress so much teachable method into so few sentences.

### Q: What makes a strong essay question about the eyes debate?

A strong question asks the student to map or compare rather than to declare, since declaration questions invite unsupported verdicts. Good prompts include asking how two readings use the same passage differently, what each of the five positions must minimize to work, or how the debate's history shaped the current options. The best questions name the positions and demand textual grounding: they ask for the evidence each side marshals and the line of argument connecting it to the claim, not for a personal favorite. Weak questions ask which reading is correct, because the text withholds the decision and the essay then has nowhere honest to go. Frame the task as cartography and the essays will show their work.

### Q: What archival discovery would most affect the debate?

The discovery most likely to matter would be manuscript or draft evidence showing how Fitzgerald developed the image across revisions, since the published text is the whole of the current evidence base. A draft in which the billboard entered late, or entered with different descriptive language, would reshape accounts of its function, though it would not settle the symbolic question by itself. Letters or notes discussing the eyes' intended meaning would be influential but not decisive, because the debate concerns what the published novel sustains, and authors' intentions do not bind readers of the finished text. Realistically, the archive is more likely to refine the history of the debate than to end it, which is consistent with the map's claim that new questions move this disagreement more than new facts.

### Q: What is the most common misrepresentation of the God reading?

The most common misrepresentation treats it as the claim that Fitzgerald believed in God or intended a Christian allegory, which turns a reading of the image into a claim about the author's faith. The actual divine reading is narrower and more defensible: it claims the novel uses the eyes as a figure of moral oversight within the fiction, regardless of what Fitzgerald believed. A related distortion presents the reading as naive, as if its proponents had simply failed to notice the billboard is an advertisement, when the serious versions of the position address the advertisement directly and distinguish literal origin from symbolic function. Misrepresenting the strongest version as the weakest is the fastest way to lose the debate's trust, and fair mapping requires engaging the position at its best.

### Q: How should you handle a critic who seems to change sides?

Handle it by dating the statements and treating each as a position in its own right rather than as a contradiction to be exposed. Critics revise their views across careers, respond to new methods, and sometimes use a reading provisionally in one essay and abandon it in the next, and the map should record the trajectory rather than forcing a single allegiance. Cite the specific work for each claim, note the shift explicitly, and never place a scholar on both sides of the table for the same claim at the same time. If the change reflects the debate's evolution, say so: a critic who moved from the divine reading to the absent-God reading is evidence of how the conversation developed. The goal is accurate attribution, not gotcha, and the map is more useful when it shows movement.

### Q: How does the eyes debate connect to the green light debate?

The two debates share a structure but differ in their evidence, and confusing them flattens both. The green light's meaning is anchored by Gatsby's own desire: the novel shows a character investing the light with longing, so the debate concerns what kind of longing it figures. The eyes have no such anchor; no character's sustained desire organizes them, and Wilson's projection is brief and desperate rather than central. As a result, the green light debate is about the varieties of a known investment, while the eyes debate is about whether the investment is warranted at all, which is why the void reading has no real counterpart in green light criticism. Studying the two together teaches how differently Fitzgerald builds his symbols.

### Q: Why do some readers resist calling the eyes a symbol at all?

They resist because the word "symbol" smuggles in the assumption that the image stands for something determinate, and the eyes may be doing a different kind of work. For these readers the billboard is better described as an image, a landmark, or a staged provocation: something that organizes attention and feeling without resolving into a referent. The resistance is not philistinism but a methodological caution, a refusal to let the vocabulary of decoding dictate the analysis before the analysis begins. This is the impulse the void and witness readings formalize, each in its own way. Taking the resistance seriously means asking what the novel gains by planting an image that will not settle, rather than assuming every vivid detail must cash out into a meaning.

### Q: What role does Nick's narration play in the debate?

Nick's narration controls everything the debate can know, and his reliability as a describer shapes every position. He introduces the billboard with the oculist's motive stated as fact, describes the eyes with the theologically loaded verb "brood," and reports Wilson's dialogue without correcting either Wilson or Michaelis. Each reading leans on a different aspect of this control: the divine reading trusts the descriptive language, the commercial reading trusts the stated motive, the void reading notes that Nick never interprets the image himself. Because Nick is the only source, no reading can appeal past him to a neutral account, and the debate's disagreements are partly disagreements about how much weight a narrator's word choice should carry.

### Q: How long should the debate section be in a research paper on the eyes?

Long enough to state each relevant position fairly and short enough that the paper's own argument remains the center. For a paper that uses the debate as context, a compact paragraph per position, with the evidence and line of argument compressed, is usually sufficient; the table in this article models that compression. For a paper whose contribution is to the debate itself, the section needs full-strength statements of each position before any criticism, which typically runs to several pages. The common failure is asymmetry: giving one's preferred reading a full exposition and the others a sentence each. Readers and examiners notice the imbalance immediately, and it undermines the paper's credibility more than any single weak argument would.

### Q: What question about the eyes has no one answered yet?

No one has fully explained why this particular image, among all the novel's vivid details, generated a debate of this scale and endurance while equally striking images did not. Accounts exist at the level of individual readings, but the meta-question, what it is about the eyes' specific combination of scale, placement, openness, and moral context that makes them uniquely generative, remains under-theorized. Answering it would require joining textual analysis with a sociology of criticism: showing how the image's features interact with the profession's methods, teaching needs, and theoretical fashions across time. That is a genuinely open research problem, and it is the kind of question debate-mapping is built to surface rather than to settle.
