Few words in Indian politics carry more weight with less agreement about their contents than Hindutva. A pamphlet smuggled out of a Ratnagiri prison in May 1923, a treatise published at Nagpur in 1939, a body of speeches gathered into a book in 1966 and a party resolution adopted at Palampur in June 1989 all speak in its name, and governments have risen and fallen under its banner. Yet the word's long career conceals a fracture that polite summary prefers to ignore. The doctrine Vinayak Damodar Savarkar defined in 1923, the doctrine Madhav Sadashiv Golwalkar taught between 1939 and 1966, and the doctrine the Bharatiya Janata Party has carried into elections since the 1980s are not one ideology at three ages. They are three distinct ideologies that share a name, differ in their tests of belonging, their vehicles and their political logic, and cannot be understood, praised or criticised as a single thing. This article states the case for reading them apart, tests the strongest case for reading them together, and defends the disentanglement as the precondition for serious argument about any of the three.

## One Word, Three Doctrines: The Puzzle of Hindutva

The reference shelf treats one subject. Encyclopaedia entries narrate a single movement running from a prison pamphlet to national power, textbooks periodise a single tradition into early, middle and late phases, and polemic on every side profits from the same assumption: admirers inherit a century of accumulated legitimacy for positions taken yesterday, and detractors indict a party resolution of 1989 with sentences written in 1939. The assumption has two scholarly forms, and both are respectable. The first is the continuity reading, stated most fully by Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle in *The Brotherhood in Saffron* (1987) and *The RSS: A View to the Inside* (2018), which follows one organisation and one ideological family through the century and treats their texts as chapters of a single story. The second is the evolution reading, present in most textbook framings and in Christophe Jaffrelot's *The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics* (1996), which distinguishes phases with great precision yet still arranges them as stages through which one tradition passed on its way to maturity.

The damage done by the single-subject habit is analytical before it is political. When three doctrines are filed under one headword, an argument can slide between texts without announcing the move: the harshest sentence of 1939 is produced to characterise a manifesto of 2014, and the mildest sentence of 1966 is produced to soften a definition of 1923. Organisations inherit intentions they never held. A cadre body that refused for decades to contest elections is treated as the author of electoral strategies it formally disowned, and a party that deferred its own founding promises for six years of coalition government is treated as a doctrine mechanically unfolding. Each slide is small. Their sum is a literature that argues about a composite figure assembled from three men's incompatible instructions, and composite figures cannot be refuted, defended or even described with precision.

The composite has a father in the standard telling, and the telling deserves scrutiny because it is the source from which most readers arrive. Reference works introduce Hindutva as an ideology formulated by Savarkar in 1923 and developed by the movement he inspired, a sentence that performs the single-tradition move in grammar: one formulator, one ideology, one development. The sentence is not false about the pamphlet; it is false about everything after it, because development is exactly what the record refuses to show. Golwalkar did not develop the 1923 definition; he replaced its test, its theology and its vehicle while keeping its word. The party of 1989 did not develop Golwalkar's organism; it shelved the organism's instructions and kept its cadres. A reader who begins from the reference sentence will experience each replacement as a nuance, and the nuance habit, repeated over a century, is how three doctrines acquired the camouflage of one. The remedy is not a better summary but a better filing system, three files where the reference shelf keeps one, and the tests that sort the files are the subject of the next paragraphs.

The disentanglement proposed here rests on three tests, applied to each framework in turn. The first test is the criterion of belonging: what, exactly, makes a person a member of the nation in the doctrine's own account, and who fails the test? The second test is the vehicle: what kind of organisation does the doctrine build or bless, and what does that organisation refuse to do? The third test is the political logic: what sequence of actions does the doctrine prescribe, definition first, character first or victory first, and what does it do when events refuse the sequence? A doctrine is individuated by its answers to these three questions, because the answers determine what an adherent is being asked to accept and to do. Where two bodies of writing answer all three questions differently, a shared vocabulary between them is a fact about language, not about ideology.

Judged by those tests, the century divides into three. Savarkar's *Essentials of Hindutva* (1923) answers the belonging question with land and ancestry, builds no vehicle of its own, and prescribes definition first: settle who the nation is, and politics follows. Golwalkar's *We or Our Nationhood Defined* (1939) and *Bunch of Thoughts* (1966) answer the belonging question with membership in an organic society ordered by dharma, build a cadre body that stands apart from parties, and prescribe character first: remake the society man by man, and the state becomes an afterthought. The electoral framework that the Bharatiya Janata Party assembled after 1980 answers the belonging question with the assembled majority of Hindu voters, builds a mass party fed by borrowed cadres, and prescribes victory first: win state power, then enact the programme in the sequence the arithmetic allows. None of these is a younger or older version of another. Each is a complete and rival answer to the question of what Hindutva is.

This pillar of the series treats each framework at full length in its own study: the 1923 text and its author, the founding of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh at Nagpur in 1925, the Golwalkar corpus, the Jana Sangh bridge of 1951 to 1977, the party refounding of 1980, the Ayodhya mobilisation, the demolition of 1992 and the Sangh-party relation after 2014. The hub article's task is the groundwork those studies share. It walks through the three frameworks in order, sets them side by side in a single comparison matrix that later studies cite, states the two great scholarly readings at their strongest, and adjudicates between them on the primary evidence of texts, resolutions and election returns. The claim it defends throughout can be named in one sentence: Hindutva is three distinct ideologies sharing a name, and the disentanglement is the precondition for serious analytical engagement.

## The Word Before the Doctrines: 1892 to 1923

The word is older than every framework that claims it, and its first life belongs to none of them. The earliest recorded use of Hindutva in print stands in a Bengali book of 1892, *Hindutva: Hindur Prakrita Itihas*, by the Calcutta writer Chandranath Basu, noticed the following year in the *Calcutta Review* of July 1894 as a work of Hindu revival. Basu, a conservative critic of the reforming currents of his century, wrote the book in the heat of the controversy over the Age of Consent Act of 1891, and his Hindutva named a quality, the essence of Hinduness as embodied in the domestic and ritual order he defended against reformers and missionaries alike. The suffix does the naming: tva, the abstract suffix, turns Hindu into Hinduness, a property to be contemplated and preserved. Basu's milieu supplied the property with its first public. The Hindu Mela, founded at Calcutta in 1867 by Nabagopal Mitra with the encouragement of Rajnarain Bose, had been staging the annual exhibition of Hindu arts, crafts and physical culture for a generation, an institution of cultural self-assertion that asked for no state and proposed no test of citizenship. The word that moved through that world between 1892 and 1923 was a word about essence, wielded in arguments about marriage, widowhood and ritual, and it founded no framework in the sense this article uses the term, because it proposed no organisation, no programme and no criterion by which a state might count its people.

Savarkar's appropriation of the word in 1923 was therefore an act of rebuilding, not of inheritance. He took a term for a quality and refounded it as a term for a nation, expressly severing the nation from the religious practice whose essence Basu had meant. The severance is the founding gesture of the first framework, and it explains why the question who coined Hindutva, which the frequently asked questions below answer in full, carries a double answer that no single name satisfies: the word belongs to Basu's Bengal, the doctrine to Savarkar's Ratnagiri. The two later frameworks each rebuilt the word again, Golwalkar filling it with an organism, the electoral framework emptying it into a mobilising sign, and the word's hospitality to rebuilding is the deep cause of the confusion the disentanglement exists to cure. A term that has been founded three times cannot be read as though it had been founded once. The pillar's first doctrine study begins where the rebuilding began, in a cell and a district of internment on the Konkan coast.

## Hindutva One: Savarkar's Territorial Definition of 1923

Vinayak Damodar Savarkar wrote his definition as a prisoner. Arrested in London in 1910 and convicted at Bombay on charges that included abetment of the murder of the Nasik collector A.M.T. Jackson, he drew two life sentences and spent the years 1911 to 1921 in the Cellular Jail in the Andaman Islands. Transferred to prisons on the mainland, he was held at Ratnagiri, in the Bombay Presidency, and there, under detention, he composed the eighty-eight-page pamphlet that appeared in May 1923 under the pen name A Maratha. The manuscript was smuggled out of his cell and published at Nagpur by the lawyer V.V. Kelkar. Its title page read simply *Hindutva*; the essay itself carried the heading *Essentials of Hindutva*, and it opened with a sentence that announced the method of the whole: Hindutva, its author wrote, is not a word but a history.

### Why did Savarkar write Essentials of Hindutva at Ratnagiri?

Savarkar wrote the pamphlet at Ratnagiri because detention had closed every other political instrument to him. Interned in the district until 1937 and barred from political activity as a condition of his confinement, he could neither organise nor campaign. Definition was the one political act still open to a prisoner, and he used it to fix the nation's meaning first.

The conditions of composition explain the text's most striking feature, its completeness as definition joined to its emptiness as programme. A pamphlet written for a movement that does not yet exist must settle first principles, because there is no organisation to settle them later by practice. Savarkar therefore built his framework as a set of criteria, each stated so that a reader could apply it without further instruction. The framework's later custodians inherited a definition of great precision and no machinery, and the gap between the two shaped everything that followed. Before turning to the criteria, the circumstances of the Ratnagiri years deserve one more fact, because they establish that the author's mind ran on society as much as on the state. Released from jail into district internment in 1924, he spent the years to 1937 barred from politics and threw himself into campaigns against caste restriction among Hindus, campaigns examined below. The definition of 1923 and the reform campaigns of the internment years are one mind at work: a nation must first exist as a people before it can act as a state.

The criteria themselves occupy the centre of the pamphlet. Savarkar asked what makes a Hindu, and he answered with conditions drawn from land and descent rather than from belief. In the framework's canonical summary, a Hindu is a person who regards the land of Bharatvarsha, from the Indus to the seas, as fatherland and as holy land at once. Three elements do the work. The first is the ancestral territory, the pitribhumi, the fatherland of one's forefathers, geographically fixed between the Indus river and the ocean. The second is the motherland, the matribhumi, the same territory held as the object of political devotion, the land a people is prepared to organise and defend as its own. The third is the punyabhumi, the holy land, the territory in which the sacred geography of one's religion is located, its rivers, shrines and sites of revelation. Around these tests the pamphlet gathers its three essentials of the Hindu people: a common nation, a common race and a common civilisation, rashtra, jati and sanskriti in its own vocabulary, each of them a property of land and descent shared across every sect and province.

The tests were designed to be generous in one direction and strict in another, and both directions follow from territory rather than theology. Inside the definition stand all the Indic faiths, Hindus of every sect and school, Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists, because their ancestral land and their holy land are the same Indian territory. Inside it stands, strikingly, the atheist: a person of Hindu descent who professes no religion at all passes every test, since none of the tests inspects belief. Outside it stand communities whose holy land lies beyond the territory, in Arabia or Palestine, however deep their families' residence in India and however complete their attachment to its soil as a fatherland. On the text's own logic the exclusion is not a judgment on loyalty in practice; it is a deduction from a definition, and the deduction is the framework's signature. Its critics, among them the historian Shamsul Islam and the lawyer and writer A.G. Noorani in *Savarkar and Hindutva: The Godse Connection* (2015), hold that the deduction was the purpose of the definition, a criterion engineered to place Muslims and Christians outside the nation. Its defenders answer that the same criterion admits any person, of any creed, who takes the land as holy land in the civilisational sense, and that the text itself salutes Indian Muslims of Hindu descent who met its terms. The dispute is genuine and it is recorded here as disputed; what is not disputed is the architecture, a territorial test, applied without reference to doctrine, worship or personal faith.

The pamphlet's own publishing history tracks its elevation from contraband to canon. The smuggled tract of May 1923, issued at Nagpur under a pen name because its author remained a prisoner, was reprinted at Delhi in 1938 by Indra Prakash, the Mahasabha's general secretary, with Savarkar's name on the title page and a foreword by Bhai Parmanand; by the fourth edition of 1949 the book circulated under the expanded title *Hindutva: Who is a Hindu?*, the question its first chapter answers. No text of the second framework and no document of the third has a comparable biography. The treatise of 1939 was qualified by its custodians, the compilation of 1966 was abridged by them, and the resolutions of the third framework expire with each election. The pamphlet alone has been continuously in print for a century, unrevised by any custodian, quoted as scripture by organisations whose operating doctrines its author would not have recognised as his own. That afterlife is itself evidence for the disentanglement: the first framework survives as a text because it never became an organisation, and organisations reach back to it precisely because a text, unlike a doctrine in working order, can be honoured without being obeyed.

The deliberate refusal of theology is the framework's most original move, and Savarkar pressed it further than his admirers sometimes find comfortable. Hinduism, in his usage, named the religious systems of the Hindus, a part and a fragment of Hindutva rather than its whole; the nation's cement was history, not creed. He carried the same logic into his account of his own practice. In statements recorded across his later life he described himself as a rationalist who did not accept the authority of scripture as revelation, and the internment years show the doctrine in action. At Ratnagiri he campaigned against the customary prohibitions that divided Hindus from one another, the bans on dining across caste, on inter-caste marriage, on the readmission of converts, on sea voyages and on the touch of the so-called untouchable, restrictions he catalogued as chains on the society. On 22 February 1931 the Patit Pavan temple he had promoted at Ratnagiri was consecrated open to Hindus of every caste, its priesthood not confined to the Brahmin-born, and in September 1931 seventy-five women of all castes took part in the inter-caste dining, the sahabhojan, held within it. A writer in Ambedkar's paper *Janata* judged in April 1933 that his service to the untouchables rivalled the Buddha's. The point for the framework is precise: caste division was, for Savarkar, a defect in the nation's raw material, to be dissolved so that the defined people could become one. A framework that defines the nation by land and descent cannot afford internal walls, and its author said so with his money, his time and his considerable talent for offence.

Two further features of the design complete its portrait, because both return in the later frameworks as contrasts. The first is its map. The nation's territory, in the 1923 text, is the whole subcontinent from the Indus to the seas, the Akhand Bharat of later Sangh iconography, and the definition is indifferent to the political borders drawn inside it: a Hindu of Sindh and a Hindu of Travancore are one nation by the test whatever states they inhabit. The Partition of 1947 was therefore, in the framework's own terms, a territorial amputation of the defined nation, and Savarkar's opposition to it, maintained through the 1940s in Mahasabha resolutions, followed from his text with the force of a theorem. The second feature is the design's repair mechanism, reconversion. A definition built on ancestry must answer the question of the ancestor who left, and Savarkar's answer was shuddhi, the ceremonial readmission of converts and their descendants to the Hindu fold, practised in the Ratnagiri district from the middle 1920s under his direction and defended in his writings as the recovery of the nation's own. The convert, in this logic, is not a foreigner to be expelled but a kinsman to be reclaimed, a position that sits oddly beside the exclusion of communities whose holy land lies abroad, and the tension is the framework's own, recorded rather than resolved here. Its importance for the disentanglement is that the second framework answers the same question in a different grammar altogether: where the territorial design repairs the nation by readmission across a border of descent, the organic design absorbs or quarantines by the body's own processes. Same problem, different machine, and the difference is visible already in the 1920s, in one district's dining halls and readmission ceremonies.

Politics, however, required the definition to become an organisation, and here the framework failed in its author's own hands. The Hindu Mahasabha, founded in 1915, eight years before the pamphlet, was the nearest available vehicle, and it was a body of notables rather than a machine: an annual conference, a set of provincial sabhas, a politics of resolution and deputation. Savarkar, released unconditionally in May 1937, took its presidency at the Ahmedabad session of December 1937 and held the office into the war years, the span this pillar records as 1937 to 1944. His presidential addresses tried to give the framework a programme in real time. At Ahmedabad in 1937 he declared, against the Congress claim to speak for one composite nation, that there were two nations in the main in India, the Hindus and the Muslims, a sentence whose candour his critics and his admirers quote with equal readiness. In the war years he raised the paired slogan of his presidency, Hinduise all politics and militarise Hindudom, pressing Hindus into the armed forces of the Raj so that the defined nation would possess soldiers when the settlement came. The under-cited primary source for these years is his correspondence as Mahasabha president between 1942 and 1944, preserved in the Savarkar Papers at the National Archives of India. Standard accounts treat the letters as biography. Read as a set, they are structural evidence: page after page of a definition-maker attempting to improvise candidate selection, provincial arithmetic and ministerial strategy for a framework that contained no instructions for any of these things, because a definition is not a party.

The war years put the vehicle's inadequacy beyond argument, and they supply the framework's most debated record. When the Congress launched the Quit India movement in August 1942 and its leadership went to prison, the Mahasabha under Savarkar chose the opposite course, a policy its president named responsive cooperation with the wartime state, and he instructed Mahasabha members in legislatures, municipalities and the armed services to stick to their posts. The militarisation campaign belonged to the same logic: Hindu recruits to the British Indian forces were, in Savarkar's stated reasoning, the future officer corps of a Hindu nation, and the party's provincial coalitions, in Bengal, Sindh and the Frontier, were the arithmetic of a small party husbanding position against the settlement to come. His critics, from the Congress of the day to later historians, read the record as collaboration purchased with the nation's struggle. His defenders read it as the 1923 framework thinking in its own terms to the end: the nation defined first, its enemies ranked by the definition, the British a temporary occupier and the League the permanent rival for the territory itself. The readings are irreconcilable and both are documented; what neither reading can supply is a mass organisation, and by 1945 the framework's party was a general staff without an army, issuing exact definitions to a country that was about to be partitioned along lines its author had predicted and could not prevent.

The record of the vehicle under his presidency is examined as a question of the framework in the pillar's later pages and in the frequently asked questions below; its outline is enough here. The Mahasabha under Savarkar held office in coalition ministries, in Bengal from December 1941, where Syama Prasad Mukherjee served as finance minister in Fazlul Haq's Progressive Coalition, and in Sindh and the North West Frontier Province in 1943. It never built a mass membership, never matched the Congress organisation in any province, and in the elections of 1945 and 1946 it was routed, taking a negligible share beside the Congress sweep of the Hindu constituencies and the Muslim League's sweep of the Muslim ones. The framework's first custodian therefore bequeathed an exact definition, a demonstrated talent for social provocation in one district, a party that could neither win the nation it had defined nor hold the allegiance of the men who would build the next framework. When Savarkar died on 26 February 1966, his text survived him as the property of organisations that had each quietly replaced parts of it. The first replacement had been under way since 1925, four hundred miles east of Ratnagiri, in the city of Nagpur.

## Hindutva Two: Golwalkar's Organic Society, 1939 to 1966

The second framework was built inside an organisation that predated its texts. Keshav Baliram Hedgewar, a Nagpur doctor who had passed through the Congress and the Hindu Mahasabha without finding either adequate, founded the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh at Nagpur on Vijayadashami, 27 September 1925. Hedgewar's founding insight was organisational rather than doctrinal: Hindus lost, in his diagnosis, not because their cause lacked arguments but because their society lacked cohesion, discipline and mutual trust, and the remedy was a daily practice, the shakha, in which boys and men drilled, played, sang and absorbed a common story of the nation. The founding study of this pillar takes the 1925 moment at full length. What matters for the disentanglement is that the vehicle came first. When Madhav Sadashiv Golwalkar gave the Sangh its doctrine, he was writing for a machine that already existed, and the doctrine bears the machine's shape throughout.

Golwalkar's own formation explains the doctrine's texture. Born at Nagpur on 19 February 1906, he taught zoology at Banaras Hindu University, withdrew in 1936 to the Ramakrishna ashram at Sargachi in Bengal in search of a religious vocation, and returned to Nagpur in 1937 after his guru's death, where Hedgewar redirected him to the Sangh. Hedgewar made him general secretary, sarkaryavah, in 1939, and on Hedgewar's death on 21 June 1940 the choice was announced: Golwalkar, at thirty-four, became the second sarsanghchalak, an office he held until his death on 5 June 1973. Between those dates he converted a provincial cadre body into a national one, and he gave it two texts. The first, *We or Our Nationhood Defined*, appeared in March 1939, its preface dated 22 March of that year, while he was still general secretary. The second, *Bunch of Thoughts*, appeared in 1966, assembled from his speeches, interviews and writings across three decades. Between them they define the second framework, and they differ from the pamphlet of 1923 in every test this article applies.

### What did Golwalkar's framework possess that Savarkar's lacked?

Two things: a machine and a doctrine of society. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, founded by K.B. Hedgewar at Nagpur in 1925, gave the framework a daily cadre practice, the shakha, that manufactured adherents rather than merely recruiting them. And where Savarkar defined a nation, Golwalkar described a social organism, with stations, duties and an ordering principle, dharma, inside it.

Begin with the criterion of belonging. Where Savarkar asked whether a person's fatherland and holy land coincide on one territory, Golwalkar asked whether a people possesses, in full, the five unities his 1939 treatise required of a nation: a definite country, a race, a religion, a civilisation and a language. The Hindu nation, he argued, alone possessed all five on the Indian territory, and nationhood followed from that organic congruence rather than from a test any single person could pass or fail by conviction. The consequences for minorities were stated in the treatise without varnish. The foreign races in Hindusthan, the text declared, must adopt the Hindu civilisation and language and learn to respect the Hindu religion, or remain in the country wholly subordinated to the Hindu nation, in its words claiming nothing, deserving no privileges, far less any preferential treatment, and not even citizen's rights. The same treatise pointed abroad for instruction, holding that Germany's purging of its Jewish population had shown race pride at its highest and offered a lesson from which Hindusthan could profit. These sentences are the most contested in the entire Hindutva corpus, and the contest must be reported as the framework's custodians and critics have each framed it. The American scholar Jean A. Curran, in *Militant Hinduism in Indian Politics* (1951), called the 1939 book the Sangh's Bible. Critics from Jaffrelot to the historian Dhirendra Jha treat the sentences as the doctrine's exposed core, the point at which its logic about minorities stands undisguised. The Sangh's stated position, maintained across decades, is that *We or Our Nationhood Defined* was an abridged English rendering of Babarao Savarkar's Marathi treatise *Rashtra Mimansa* (1934), that its character as a translation came to light in 1963, that Golwalkar withdrew the book, and that it does not state the Sangh's doctrine; Golwalkar's own preface of 1939 had described the Marathi work as one of his chief sources of inspiration and help. How much distance the translation fact establishes remains disputed among scholars, and it is marked disputed here. The architecture is not in dispute: belonging, in the second framework, is congruence with an organic whole, and the whole is defined by race, religion and civilisation together, a test no atheist passes by silence and no resident alien passes by loyalty alone.

*Bunch of Thoughts* (1966) restated the framework for the post-Partition republic, and its organising image is the social body. Hindu society appears there as an organism, one life running through differentiated parts, as the limbs and organs of a single body serve one life in the ancient image of the Virat Purusha that Golwalkar repeatedly invoked. Within that image, varna is presented as functional station: the four orders are the body's differentiated services, priestly, martial, productive and serving, assigned in the ideal by quality and function rather than by the accident of birth, which the text treats as a degeneration of the design. The presentation matters, and so does its contest. Golwalkar's defenders cite the functional account to separate the framework from caste hierarchy as practised. His scholarly critics, among them Jha and the writer Shamsul Islam, answer that a body has a head and feet whether or not the metaphor apologises for them, and that a doctrine which photographs society as an organism photographs its inequalities as organs. The dispute is marked disputed. The contrast with 1923, by contrast, is plain on the page: Savarkar campaigned to dissolve caste as a chain on the nation; Golwalkar theorised caste's ancestor, varna, as the nation's skeleton. A framework that wants the walls removed and a framework that wants the stations honoured are not the same doctrine at different ages.

Dharma supplies the second framework's ordering principle, and it marks a second departure from 1923. Savarkar's nation is held together by land, blood and civilisation, with religion admitted only as geography, the location of the holy land. Golwalkar's society is held together by dharma, a sacred order that precedes the state, disciplines the person and defines the good of the whole, and his writing is saturated with the religious idiom that Savarkar's pamphlet had deliberately rationed. The point is visible in the second framework's catalogue of dangers. *Bunch of Thoughts* contains a section on internal threats in which Golwalkar named three: the Muslims, the Christians and the Communists, each portrayed as an allegiance organised against the organic nation from inside its borders. Whatever judgment a reader passes on the catalogue, its form is doctrinal: threats are classified by their relation to the social organism, not by their position on a territorial test. A Muslim citizen of India fails Savarkar's test on a point of sacred geography and could, in the text's own terms, cease to fail it only by relocating his holy land in his heart to India. In Golwalkar's framework the same citizen is a cell of a rival organism, and no change of heart about geography answers the charge. The two exclusions resemble each other from a distance. Up close they are different machines.

The machinery that carried this doctrine deserves a closer look, because its routines are the doctrine translated into time. The shakha meets daily, conventionally for one hour, in an open ground: attendance, physical drill and games, exercises with the lathi, the singing of patriotic songs, a discussion or address, and the closing prayer to the motherland before the saffron flag, which the Sangh treats not as an emblem but as its guru, the teacher to whom the annual guru dakshina offering of money is made in place of any person. Around the daily hour stand the graded training camps, the Sangh Shiksha Vargas, and above the whole structure the pracharak system, full-time workers, conventionally celibate, paid nothing and maintained by the organisation, who are loaned to affiliates as their organising secretaries and who constitute the bloodstream the continuity reading rightly celebrates. The method's patience is its doctrine: a boy who enters a shakha at eight is expected to be formed for life, and the framework measures its progress in generations of such boys. The emblematic achievement of the method in Golwalkar's lifetime was not electoral at all. When the proposal arose to raise a memorial to Vivekananda on the rock at Kanyakumari where he had meditated in December 1892, the campaign was entrusted to Eknath Ranade, a pracharak Golwalkar released for the task, who spent the years 1963 to 1970 collecting small subscriptions across India, signatures from a majority of members of Parliament, and the permissions of rival governments, until the Vivekananda Rock Memorial was inaugurated in September 1970. No resolution of a party could have built it, and no definition of a nation required it. The organism built it because building such things, slowly, in civil society, beyond the reach of any single election, is what the second framework exists to do.

The vehicle completes the contrast. The Sangh under Golwalkar refused, as a matter of doctrine, to become a political party. Its work was the manufacture of character in the shakha, the daily hour of drill, games, song and discussion, replicated in tens of thousands of localities, officered by full-time celibate organisers, the pracharaks, and directed to a horizon of generations. Politics, in the framework's own account, was a downstream consequence: formed men would enter every sphere of national life and carry the organism's values with them, and the Sangh itself would remain above the electoral contest, unbought and unhurried. The record shows the doctrine being built to scale. The Sangh survived its gravest crisis, the ban imposed by the Government of India on 4 February 1948, days after the assassination of Mahatma Gandhi by a former swayamsevak, Nathuram Godse; Golwalkar was imprisoned, then released, and the ban was lifted in July 1949 after the Sangh adopted a written constitution and affirmed its loyalty to the Indian flag and constitution. Around the parent body grew the family of affiliates, each dated and each sphere-specific: the Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad for students in 1949, the Vanavasi Kalyan Ashram for tribal welfare in 1952, the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh for labour in 1955, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad for religious consolidation in 1964. When a political arm became unavoidable, the framework produced one at arm's length: Syama Prasad Mukherjee founded the Bharatiya Jana Sangh in October 1951 with Golwalkar's blessing and with Sangh organisers seconded to build it, a party the Sangh supplied with men while formally standing apart from its fortunes. The framework's career inside the republic tests its claim that formation outruns politics, and the tests of the 1960s are the most instructive. When China attacked India in October and November 1962, the Sangh threw its network into civil defence, blood donation and border-area service, and the government of Jawaharlal Nehru, the organisation's oldest adversary, acknowledged the work in the currency the republic commands: a Sangh contingent was invited to march in the Republic Day parade of 26 January 1963. Andersen and Damle record the episode as the Sangh's certificate of national standing, and it is exactly the kind of recognition the organic framework is built to win, respect for the society's servants rather than office for its nominees. The mobilisation for cow protection in the autumn of 1966 showed the same machine turning to a doctrinal demand through its religious affiliate: on 7 November 1966 a vast procession of sadhus and Hindu organisations, assembled under the banner of the cow-protection movement and backed by the Vishwa Hindu Parishad and the Jana Sangh, converged on Parliament in Delhi, and police firing on the crowd killed several marchers. The demand, a national ban on cow slaughter, was Golwalkar's in emphasis and idiom, pressed not by a party's resolution but by the religious nation in procession, formation made visible for a day. It won no statute. The framework's custodians counted the decade a success anyway, in shakhas planted, affiliates rooted and a chief's authority unchallenged, which is the point: a doctrine that measures itself in generations can lose every vote of a decade and call the decade progress, a sentence no electoral doctrine can pronounce. Golwalkar died in office on 5 June 1973, succeeded by Balasaheb Deoras, and the machine he left, disciplined, patient and formally apolitical, was within four years to be banned again in the Emergency and within seven to lend its cadres to the party whose logic would subordinate formation to victory for the rest of the century.

The Golwalkar study in this pillar examines the corpus at full length. For the disentanglement, the summary is enough: the second framework answers the belonging question with an organism, the vehicle question with a cadre body that refuses office, and the logic question with formation before power. Every one of those answers reverses a corresponding answer of 1923.

## The Bridge: The Jana Sangh, 1951 to 1977

Between the second framework and the third stands a quarter-century of translation, and the hub's map is incomplete without it. The Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded at Delhi on 21 October 1951, was the first organisation to attempt the conversion of the inheritance into votes, and its career demonstrates, in controlled conditions, what each framework could and could not do. Its founder was not a Sangh man by origin. Syama Prasad Mukherjee, a former vice-chancellor of Calcutta University and the Mahasabha's wartime leader in Bengal, had served in Jawaharlal Nehru's first cabinet as minister for industry and supply, and resigned in April 1950 in protest against the Nehru-Liaquat Pact on the protection of minorities in Bengal. His search for a party of the nationalist opposition took him to Golwalkar, and the terms on which the Jana Sangh was built record the negotiation between the frameworks exactly: the Sangh would supply organisers, men of the stamp of Deendayal Upadhyay, Atal Bihari Vajpayee and Nanaji Deshmukh, seconded from the pracharak system, while the party would be formally independent, open to non-Sangh members, and committed to an electoral programme in the idiom of 1923, one nation, one culture, one people, with the abrogation of Article 370 and a uniform civil code among its founding demands. The definition came from the first framework, the men from the second, and the logic, seats, was new to both.

The bridge's traffic ran in both directions, and its tolls were real. Mukherjee gave the inheritance its first martyr and its first all-India agitation: entering Jammu and Kashmir in May 1953 in defiance of the permit system that governed entry into the state, he was arrested at Lakhanpur and died in custody at Srinagar on 23 June 1953, an event that fixed Kashmir's full integration at the head of the party's programme for two generations. The electoral returns of the bridge years were modest and instructive: three Lok Sabha seats in 1952, four in 1957, fourteen in 1962, and thirty-five in 1967, when the party also entered the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalition governments that briefly displaced the Congress in the northern states. The pattern of those years is the pattern of a translation losing content at each joint. In the states, Jana Sangh ministers administered like other ministers, and the party's founding demands waited. In 1967 the party chose as its president the one systematic thinker the bridge produced, Deendayal Upadhyay, elected at the Calicut session of December 1967, whose Integral Humanism, delivered as lectures at Bombay in April 1965, attempted the synthesis the frameworks had never managed: a doctrine in which Savarkar's nation and Golwalkar's organism would be reconciled inside an economics of decentralised production and a politics of the last man, the antyodaya that later governments would take as a slogan. Upadhyay was found dead beside the railway track near Mughalsarai station on 11 February 1968, in circumstances that were never judicially resolved to public satisfaction, and the bridge lost its architect within two months of his elevation. What survived him was the machine: a party with a quarter-century of parliamentary apprenticeship, a network of Sangh-formed organisers in every province, and a programme that its own decades in opposition had taught it to hold at the level of affirmation.

The bridge ended in merger. The Jana Sangh entered the Janata Party in 1977, dissolved its separate existence into the coalition that defeated the Emergency regime, and supplied that government with ministers, including Vajpayee at external affairs and L.K. Advani at information and broadcasting. The dissolution is the bridge's final lesson for the disentanglement. A party that was the electoral translation of two doctrines discovered, in government, that translation is government by other people's priorities, and when the parent coalition demanded in 1979 that its members choose between the Janata and the Sangh, the Jana Sangh's heirs chose the Sangh and walked out to found a party of their own at last. The third framework begins at that exit. It begins, that is, with the knowledge, purchased over twenty-six years, that the definition cannot win by itself, that the organism will not win at all, and that a party which means to hold power in the Indian republic must build an instrument whose first loyalty is to the count.

## Hindutva Three: The Electoral Framework after 1980

The third framework was assembled by politicians who had inherited the first framework's definition and the second framework's cadres and found, in the crisis of 1979 and 1980, that neither inheritance could win an election by itself. The Jana Sangh had merged into the Janata Party in 1977, in the coalition forged against [the Emergency's suspension of constitutional politics](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/), and had governed for two years inside it. When the Janata coalition broke in 1979 over the dual-membership question, the demand that former Jana Sangh members sever their Sangh affiliation or leave the party, the lesson its leaders drew was that a borrowed vehicle exacts a doctrinal price. On 6 April 1980 the Bharatiya Janata Party was founded at Delhi under the presidency of Atal Bihari Vajpayee, staffed by the Jana Sangh's cadres, carrying the Mahasabha's old definition in its inheritance, and committed by the logic of its birth to a single test the earlier frameworks had never faced: it would be judged, every five years, by counting votes.

The founding programme showed a party unsure which of its inheritances to speak in. The constitution adopted in 1980 committed the BJP to Gandhian socialism in economics and to positive secularism in the republic's religious settlement, phrases chosen to announce continuity with the Janata experiment and distance from the Sangh's reputation. The result, at the general election of December 1984, was two Lok Sabha seats. The party's internal review produced the first clear act of the new framework: at Bhopal in July 1985 the national executive set Gandhian socialism aside and adopted Integral Humanism, the doctrine Deendayal Upadhyay had delivered in lectures at Bombay in April 1965 and the Jana Sangh had adopted at Vijayawada that year, as the party's basic philosophy. The episode is usually narrated as a return to roots. For the disentanglement it is better evidence than that: a party shopping among inherited doctrines and selecting the one that fits its need is treating doctrines as options, which is what organisations do when the doctrines are genuinely distinct. Neither Savarkar's test nor Golwalkar's organism appears in the Bhopal settlement as an operating rule. What the party took from Upadhyay was a philosophy of the integrated person and society, elastic enough to cover an economic programme yet to be written.

The framework found its canonical act four years later. At Palampur, in Himachal Pradesh, from 9 to 11 June 1989, the BJP's national executive adopted the resolution that bears the town's name. Its operative demand was stated in a single sentence: the sentiments of the people must be respected, and the Ram Janmasthan at Ayodhya handed over to the Hindus, if possible through a negotiated settlement, or else by legislation. The Palampur Resolution is the founding document of the third framework in the same sense that the 1923 pamphlet founds the first and the 1939 treatise the second, and the differences among the three documents are the disentanglement in miniature. The pamphlet defines a nation and stops. The treatise describes a society and prescribes its formation. The resolution defines nothing and describes nothing; it commits a party to a deliverable outcome, names the two acceptable routes to it, negotiation or statute, and is silent about every question the earlier texts laboured over, the criteria of nationhood, the ordering of society, the fate of minorities. Its author was not a thinker but an executive committee, its horizon was the election of November 1989, and its logic was mobilisational: the temple demand would assemble, across caste and province, the Hindu majority that the framework's belonging test required. The test of belonging in the third framework is exactly that assembly. A Hindu, for the electoral doctrine, is a voter who can be brought to experience his religious identity as his political identity at the moment of the count, and the framework's texts are the successive instruments, resolutions, manifestos and campaign themes, by which the assembly is attempted.

The seat returns trace the instrument's refinement. From two seats in 1984 the BJP rose to 85 in November 1989, in alliance with the National Front constellation, and to 120 in 1991, after L.K. Advani's Rath Yatra of September and October 1990 carried the temple demand from Somnath toward Ayodhya until his arrest at Samastipur in Bihar on 23 October 1990 stopped the procession and brought down the V.P. Singh government it had supported. The Ayodhya movement's division of labour displayed the framework's architecture exactly: the Vishwa Hindu Parishad ran the religious campaign and supplied the cause, the Sangh supplied cadres and local organisation, and the party converted both into seats while retaining the freedom to calibrate the demand to the arithmetic of each election. The demolition of the Babri Masjid by kar sevaks on 6 December 1992, an event the party's leadership had not scheduled and could not control, showed the architecture's risk: the movement's energy, once assembled, obeyed its own calendar. The pillar's studies of the Rath Yatra and of the demolition take those events at full length. In the short run the party paid for the demolition with dismissal of its state governments and a season of isolation; in the longer run it emerged from the 1996 election, with 161 seats, as the largest party in the Lok Sabha, and discovered that the assembly it had built was large enough to lead a coalition and too polarising to hold one without concessions.

### What did the 1998 three-deferral formula set aside?

The National Agenda for Governance of March 1998, the price of a thirteen-party coalition, set aside the Bharatiya Janata Party's three programme items: construction of the Ram temple at Ayodhya, abrogation of Article 370 of the Constitution, and a Uniform Civil Code. Each was deferred to consensus or to the courts, and none was enacted during six years in office.

The deferral formula deserves its place as the third framework's most instructive document, and this pillar's study of the 1998 coalition examines it under the name [the 1998 NDA's three-deferral tactical retreat](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/). Neither earlier framework could have produced it. Savarkar's doctrine contains no procedure for postponing the nation; Golwalkar's contains no procedure for bargaining with parties at all. A doctrine whose logic is victory first can defer anything except the next victory, and the Vajpayee governments of 1998 to 2004 governed on exactly that principle, holding office across two Lok Sabhas, falling by a single vote, 269 to 270, on 17 April 1999, returning from the election that followed with 182 seats and the same allies, and serving a full term from October 1999 to May 2004, while the founding programme waited. The deferral years are the heart of [the coalition era's long apprenticeship](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/), in which every national party learned the arithmetic of the post-1989 republic. The stress test of those years came not from the coalition but from Gujarat, where the violence of February and March 2002, following the burning of a train coach at Godhra on 27 February 2002 in which fifty-nine Hindu pilgrims died, killed more than a thousand people, most of them Muslim, under a BJP state government led by Narendra Modi. The characterisation of the state government's conduct remains disputed in the strict sense this series uses: the Concerned Citizens Tribunal of 2002, chaired by Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer and P.B. Sawant, charged the state administration with complicity, while the Special Investigation Team appointed by the Supreme Court in 2008 reported in 2012 that it found no prosecutable evidence against the chief minister, a closure the trial court accepted in December 2013. What is not disputed is the framework's response: the party defended its government, won the Gujarat election of December 2002, and absorbed the episode into its education in the difference between movement judgement and electoral judgement. Defeat followed in 2004, when the BJP fell to 138 seats, and again in 2009, at 116 seats, and through both defeats the framework held its shape, because its logic had never promised that doctrine wins elections, only that elections are where doctrine is cashed or stored.

The framework's characteristic literary genre, across these decades, is the manifesto, and the manifesto's history states the doctrine's logic in bibliographic form. A treatise is written once; a manifesto is written for an election, and the BJP's manifestos register the arithmetic of each successive moment with a seismograph's honesty. The document of 1996, issued when the party expected to lead a government alone, spoke the programme in its own voice. The National Agenda of 1998, negotiated with thirteen allies, muted the same programme into deferral, and the manifesto of 1999, issued by a coalition seeking re-election, presented the deferral itself as the achievement. In May 1996 the framework had its first rehearsal of the lesson: invited, as leader of the largest party, to form a government, Vajpayee was sworn in on 16 May and resigned on 28 May, thirteen days later, unable to convert 161 seats into a majority and unwilling to purchase one at the prices named. Plurality, the party learned, is a constituency of a different kind from majority, and the conversion of the one into the other, by alliance management, programme deferral and the slow expansion of the assembly into new states and new caste constituencies, became the framework's central craft for eighteen years. Neither earlier doctrine contains a category for this craft. Savarkar's text knows the nation and its enemies; Golwalkar's knows the organism and its threats; the electoral framework knows the marginal seat, the ally's price and the deferred item, and it knows them as the medium in which all the inherited certainties must swim.

The full-term government of 1999 to 2004 completed the framework's education in the uses and limits of office. Its statecraft had a register of its own: the nuclear tests at Pokhran in May 1998, conducted by the first, thirteen-month Vajpayee government within weeks of taking office, announced that the party of the deferred programme could still act decisively where no ally's veto ran; the Kargil war of May to July 1999, fought and won inside the discipline of the Line of Control, taught it the international grammar of restraint; and the great administrative programmes of the full term, the national highways grid, the rural roads scheme, the telecom expansion, built its claim to be a party of governance rather than of grievance. The defeat of 2004, delivered under the slogan of an India shining that the countryside declined to recognise, was read inside the party, in the reviews its own strategists published, as the wages of a specific error: the framework had governed as though administration were the doctrine, and the movement publics that the doctrine existed to assemble had stayed home. Scholars of the period, among them the contributors to the post-election analyses of 2004 in the *Economic and Political Weekly*, reached compatible conclusions from outside. The lesson was filed, and the decade of opposition that followed was spent rebuilding the assembly, state by state, constituency by constituency, caste assurance by caste assurance, until the arithmetic of 2014 made the stored programme spendable again.

The framework's third phase opened with the verdict of May 2014, which gave the BJP 282 seats and the first single-party Lok Sabha majority since 1984, a result renewed and enlarged in May 2019 with 303 seats. Majority changed the framework's grammar. Under coalition, deferral could be blamed on arithmetic; under majority, sequence became choice, and the government of Narendra Modi began to work through the stored programme in the order its managers judged the republic could absorb. The scholarly name for the pattern, in this series' account of the period, is [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), and its idiom in the Hindi-speaking heartland is [the Hindi-Hindutva-Hindostan triad](/2019/08/22/hindi-hindutva-hindostan-modi-question/), the fusion of language, doctrine and territory into one electoral address. By the late summer of 2019 the first of the three deferred items had moved: on 5 August 2019 the government carried through Parliament the constitutional orders and the reorganisation that ended the special status of Jammu and Kashmir under Article 370, and in July 2019 it had enacted the statute criminalising instant triple talaq. The temple question awaited the Supreme Court, and the Uniform Civil Code remained unenacted. The sequence itself is the doctrine's signature, legible only when the third framework is read as itself: a definition inherited from 1923, cadres inherited from the second framework's machine, and a logic, win first and enact in sequence, that belongs to neither.

## Three Frameworks, Three Publics

A doctrine is also an act of address, and the three frameworks speak to three different audiences in three different registers, a difference the single-tradition habit flattens and the disentanglement restores. Savarkar's pamphlet of 1923 addressed a nation that did not yet know itself. Its circulation was small, a smuggled manuscript printed at Nagpur and read in the circles of the revolutionary and Mahasabha networks, and its canonisation came later, when organisations in search of a founding definition elevated the pamphlet into a scripture of identity. The text's own ambitions were constitutional in the deepest sense: it proposed the terms on which a people could recognise itself as one, and it was written in the hortatory, definition-coining register of a constituent document rather than in the register of a campaign. Golwalkar's texts addressed a cadre that already existed. *We or Our Nationhood Defined* circulated inside the Sangh as instruction; *Bunch of Thoughts* was compiled for the instruction of workers, its question-and-answer passages drawn from sessions in which swayamsevaks put difficulties to their chief. The register is pedagogical and inward: the reader is not a citizen to be persuaded but a worker to be formed, and the books presume the daily discipline of the shakha as the medium in which their sentences will be absorbed. The electoral framework addressed the voter, a public neither defined nor formed but assembled, and its instruments were built for that public: a resolution drafted for the next morning's newspapers, a procession staged for the television cameras that had arrived in Indian politics by 1990, a manifesto negotiated with allies. Where the first framework speaks in definitions and the second in instructions, the third speaks in promises, and a promise is a different kind of sentence from either. Students of the period who miss the difference of address will keep mistaking the doctrines for one another, because the words on the page overlap while the acts the words perform do not.

## The Three-by-Five Matrix: Reading the Frameworks Side by Side

Comparison disciplines what narrative blurs. The matrix below, cited throughout this pillar as Table 1, the Three-by-Five Comparison Matrix of the Hindutva Frameworks, sets the three doctrines side by side across the five dimensions that the preceding studies have used: foundational text, identity criteria, organisational vehicle, political logic and scholarly representative. Each cell states the settled content of the framework on that dimension, in the compressed form a reader can carry into the pillar's later studies and test against their fuller evidence.

| Framework | Foundational text | Identity criteria | Organisational vehicle | Political logic | Scholarly representative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Savarkar Hindutva, from 1923 | Essentials of Hindutva (1923) | Fatherland and holy land on one territory; common nation, race and civilisation | The Hindu Mahasabha, a party of notables without a cadre arm | Define the nation first; politics follows the definition | Aishwary Kumar, Radical Equality (2015), read against Ambedkar |
| Golwalkar Hindutva, 1939 to 1966 | We or Our Nationhood Defined (1939); Bunch of Thoughts (1966) | Congruence with an organic Hindu society ordered by dharma | The RSS, a cadre body refusing party politics | Form the man through the shakha; the state follows society | Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle (1987 and 2018), the organisational record |
| Electoral Hindutva, from 1980 | Party constitution (1980); the Palampur Resolution (1989) | The Hindu voter assembled as a national majority | The BJP, a mass party fed by Sangh cadres | Win state power; enact the programme in sequence | Christophe Jaffrelot (1996), the tripartite periodisation |

The first row of contrasts is documentary. Only the first framework possesses a single founding book by a single author, written to be complete in itself. The second framework's canon is double and divided against itself, a treatise of 1939 whose custody its own organisation later qualified, and a compilation of 1966 assembled from a lifetime of occasions. The third framework has no book at all. Its canon is a paper trail of constitutions, resolutions, agendas and manifestos, each dated, each negotiated, each revisable at the next executive meeting, and the absence of a treatise is not a gap in the framework but its essence: a doctrine whose logic is electoral keeps its texts provisional, because a text that cannot be amended cannot be traded in a coalition negotiation. Readers who search the third framework for its *Essentials* will manufacture one from speeches; the matrix records instead that its foundational documents are the instruments of a party, and that the difference is substantive.

The second row of contrasts, identity, has been walked through in the studies above and can be stated as a gradient of tests. The 1923 test examines a person's lands, ancestral and sacred, and can be applied by a registrar. The Golwalkar test examines a society's congruence with itself and can be applied only by a lifetime of formation, since it asks whether the person is organically of the whole. The electoral test examines a count and can be applied only on polling day. The three tests sort the same population differently. The atheist of Hindu descent passes the first, is a defective cell in the second, and is an unremarkable voter in the third. The devout Muslim citizen fails the first on sacred geography, is classified in the second among the internal threats, and appears in the third as a constituency courted state by state whenever the arithmetic recommends it, as the BJP's Pasmanda outreach in the Modi years illustrates. No developmental story connects these sortings. They are three answers, and a population that satisfies one may fail another on the same day.

A worked example shows the matrix earning its keep. Take the single most consequential act available to this article's date, the constitutional action on Jammu and Kashmir of 5 August 2019, and read it through the three rows. Through the first framework's lens, the action completes the territorial definition: the state's map is made congruent with the nation's, an anomaly of 1947 corrected, and this was the register of the government's own presentation, the Home Minister, Amit Shah, telling Parliament that the orders ended a historic injustice of separation and bound the territory fully into the Union. Through the second framework's lens, the action is a state event of the second rank: the organism of Hindu society is neither enlarged nor diminished by a constitutional order, and the Sangh's welcome of the step was framed accordingly, in the idiom of civilisational satisfaction rather than of doctrine advanced. Through the third framework's lens, the action is the first deferred item cashed: promised in manifestos for decades, shelved by the agenda of 1998, and enacted in the first session of a Parliament in which the party's majority made the shelving unnecessary, its sequence chosen by managers who placed it before the court's temple judgment and after the election that supplied the numbers. Commentary that read the event as Hindutva acting, without specifying the row, missed the only interesting question, which framework's logic had set the date. It was the third's, executing the first's map with the second's cadres applauding from the gallery, and the disentanglement is what allows that sentence to be written.

The third and fourth rows, vehicle and logic, are the matrix's practical core, and they interact. A party of notables can announce a definition but cannot enforce a formation; a cadre body can enforce a formation but has forsworn the office that enacts law; a mass party can enact law but must renegotiate its doctrine with every electorate and every ally. The history of the century, read through the matrix, becomes a sequence of borrowings among rows: the Mahasabha borrowing the definition it could not operationalise, the Jana Sangh and then the BJP borrowing the cadre machine the Sangh had built for a different purpose, the governments after 2014 borrowing all three rows at once and discovering that the borrowings strain against one another, as the next two studies show. The fifth row names the scholarship this article treats as each reading's strongest statement, and it is to the scholarly argument, the ground on which the disentanglement stands or falls, that the article turns.

## Jaffrelot's Periods and the Extension to Distinct Ideologies

Christophe Jaffrelot's *The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics*, published in 1996 from his doctoral research at Paris, is the scholarly edifice on which any disentanglement must build, because Jaffrelot was the first academic historian to periodise the movement with archival patience. His book follows Hindu nationalism from its nineteenth-century sources through the founding generation of the 1920s, the Sangh's long consolidation, the Jana Sangh decades and the movement's arrival at national power in the 1990s, and it distinguishes within that story the ideological formations that this article calls the three frameworks. Jaffrelot treats Savarkar's ethnic definition of the nation, Golwalkar's doctrine of the organic society and the movement's later electoral deployment as phases in the career of one tradition, each phase shaped by the strategic situation of its moment: definition when the movement was a sect, organisation when it was a suspect minority, mobilisation when democracy made numbers sovereign. The reading is powerful precisely because it is historical. It explains why each formation arose when it did, and it refuses the lazy alternative of treating the movement as a timeless essence.

One of Jaffrelot's analytical instruments deserves naming, because the extension defended here borrows its edge. In the 1996 book he describes the movement's characteristic double operation toward the communities it casts as rivals, stigmatisation and emulation: the rival is marked as alien and threatening, and simultaneously copied, his congregational discipline, his missionary organisation, his political solidarity held up as the model the Hindu nation must match. The instrument explains a rhythm visible across the whole century, denigration of the minority's cohesion joined to envy of it, and it operates identically inside all three frameworks, in the 1923 text's anxiety about pan-Islamic solidarity, in the shakha's emulation of the discipline it attributed to its rivals, and in the electoral framework's study of minority vote consolidation as a technique to be answered in kind. Shared instruments, however, are not shared doctrines. A hammer in three houses does not make the houses one, and the uses to which the stigmatisation-emulation rhythm is put differ by framework exactly as the matrix predicts: the first framework uses it to police a definition's border, the second to justify a lifetime of formation, the third to assemble a majority before a count.

Two features of Jaffrelot's method matter for what follows. His category discipline is the first: he writes of Hindu nationalism, a genus wider than any doctrine, inside which ideologies, organisations and strategies can be separately tracked, and the present article's claim is in one sense only his discipline enforced, the refusal to let the genus do the species' work. His evidence is the second feature: the book rests on years of fieldwork inside the movement he studies, interviews with cadres and leaders across the 1980s and 1990s, and the periodisation it defends was earned at the shakha ground and the party office rather than imposed from the library. The same scholar, in essays written after 2014, applied to the Modi-era republic the category of ethnic democracy, a term coined by the Israeli political scientist Sammy Smooha for states that combine democratic forms with the institutional primacy of one ethnic nation; the application is Jaffrelot's, it is contested by other scholars of the period, and it is noted here because it shows the periodiser of 1996 still periodising, still treating the movement's forms as historical products rather than as an essence unfolding.

The extension defended here accepts Jaffrelot's periodisation as chronology and disputes its ontology. A stage, in the strong sense the stage reading requires, is a form that develops into the next by its own logic, as the Jana Sangh's parliamentary apprenticeship might be said to develop into the BJP's governmental one. The three frameworks fail that test at each joint. Golwalkar's organism is not Savarkar's definition grown older; it contradicts the definition at the decisive points, replacing a test of lands with a test of congruence, a rationed theology with a saturating one, a campaign against caste walls with a theory of ordained stations. The electoral framework is not the organism grown political; it contradicts the organism at the decisive points, replacing formation with assembly, the refusal of office with the pursuit of it, and doctrinal custody with executive-committee revision. A tradition can contain contradictions, but a stage cannot be the negation of the stage before it on the very questions that individuate it. What the chronology actually shows is succession with selection: at each juncture, custodians chose among live inheritances, kept some, shelved others, and built what the situation demanded from the parts. Selection among options is the behaviour of actors facing distinct ideologies, not of a substance passing through ages.

The evidence of choice is documentary, and three exhibits make the point. The first is the custody of *We or Our Nationhood Defined* itself: an organisation that experiences its founder-text as a stage of its own past does not spend decades qualifying the book's authorship, recording that its translation character came to light in 1963 and that its author withdrew it. The Sangh's handling of the 1939 treatise is the handling of a distinct doctrine that later custodians judged, in part, unusable. The second exhibit is Bhopal in July 1985, where a party executive weighed Gandhian socialism against Integral Humanism and picked one, an act of doctrinal shopping meaningless if the inheritance were a single unfolding substance. The third exhibit is the editorial history of *Bunch of Thoughts*. In September 2018, in a three-day lecture series at Vigyan Bhavan in New Delhi, the Sangh's chief, Mohan Bhagwat, presented the organisation to a national audience and stated that not every position recorded in Golwalkar's compilation binds the Sangh, whose remarks belong to their circumstance, a judgment the organisation's own publishing had already enacted in a popular edition from which context-bound passages were removed. A stage is not edited by its successors; a doctrine is, when the successors hold a different one. Jaffrelot's own evidence, assembled across eight hundred pages, documents selection after selection. The extension this article defends simply names what the selections imply: three ideologies, successively and sometimes simultaneously available, sharing a vocabulary, a cadre lineage and a name.

## The Continuity Reading at Its Strongest, and Where It Fails

Against the disentanglement stands the most distinguished body of work in the field, and the argument cannot be won by diminishing it. Walter Andersen, an American scholar and former diplomat, and Shridhar Damle built their account from the organisation outward. *The Brotherhood in Saffron* (1987) is a sociology of the Sangh: its shakha discipline, its pracharak system, its slow conquest of civil society through affiliates, studied with access no critic enjoyed. *The RSS: A View to the Inside* (2018), written after extensive interviews with the Sangh's leadership including Mohan Bhagwat, extends the account to the Modi era. Their shared thesis is continuity: one movement, formed by one organisation, carrying one ideological inheritance from 1925 to power, adapting its emphases to circumstance while its core, the vision of a culturally united Hindu nation organised for national strength, persists unchanged. On their reading, the differences among the texts of 1923, 1939 and 1989 are differences of emphasis within a family, and the scholar's task is to trace the family's life, not to multiply doctrines.

### What is the strongest evidence for the continuity reading?

Personnel. One cadre body, the Sangh, formed the men who staffed the Mahasabha's rivals, the Jana Sangh and the Bharatiya Janata Party; Golwalkar blessed the party of 1951, Sangh organisers built the party of 1980, and every BJP president of the century was Sangh-formed. Institutions remember what texts revise, and the bloodstream of the three frameworks is one.

The personnel evidence is as strong as its statement suggests, and this article concedes it without reserve. The same organisation recruited the sixteen-year-old Narendra Modi in Gujarat and the young Atal Bihari Vajpayee in Uttar Pradesh; the same training camps formed the organisers who built the Jana Sangh in the 1950s and the BJP in the 1980s; the same idiom of sacrifice and discipline runs through the memoirs of every generation. Any reading that denied the bloodstream would fail as history. The question is what the bloodstream proves. Andersen and Damle move from organisational continuity to ideological continuity, and the step is the one the evidence refuses. Organisations are carriers, not contents. The Indian National Congress carried, in one institutional bloodstream, the liberal constitutionalism of Gokhale, the mass politics of Gandhi, the socialism of Nehru and the authoritarianism of the Emergency, and no historian concludes from the carrier's continuity that these were one ideology at four ages. A carrier explains how doctrines travel. It cannot decide whether the doctrines that travelled were one doctrine or three, and that question must be answered where doctrines live, in their tests of belonging, their vehicles and their logics.

Answered there, the continuity thesis fails on its own evidence, for the two Andersen and Damle volumes record, with a reporter's fidelity, the very selections the thesis must flatten. Their narrative registers the Sangh's discomfort with the 1939 treatise and its withdrawal. Their narrative registers the party's 1998 deferral of its founding programme, an act unintelligible within a doctrine whose core is said to persist unchanged, since the deferred items were the core's most public articles. Their 2018 volume registers the leadership's editorial distance from *Bunch of Thoughts* and its presentation of a Sangh reformed in idiom for a democratic century. Each registration is honest; the sum of them is a chronicle of an organisation choosing, decade by decade, which of its inheritances to speak in. The continuity reading survives as an account of the carrier. As an account of the carried, it is refuted by the texts it taught the field to read, and the disentanglement this article defends is built from its materials with gratitude.

## What the Three Hold in Common, and Why It Does Not Make Them One

The strongest objection to everything argued so far is not the continuity of personnel but the continuity of content, and it deserves to be stated at full strength before it is answered. The three frameworks, the objection runs, share their substance. Each anchors the Indian nation in Hindu cultural identity. Each defines that identity against a perceived politics of minority appeasement, the charge that the Congress and its successor formations purchased minority votes with concessions no majority community would have been offered. Each speaks a civilisational idiom, in which India is an ancient and continuous civilisation whose antiquity is itself an argument. Strip the three texts of their dates, the objection concludes, and a single creed stands on the page: the Hindu nation, conscious of itself, organising for its rightful place. The differences this article has laboured over are, on this account, instrumentation, the same music scored for pamphlet, for cadre camp and for campaign.

The shared content is real, and the disentanglement does not survive by denying it. It survives by asking what shared content proves. Content in politics is never inert; it does its work through the architecture that holds it, and the same proposition performs different operations inside different architectures. Reverence for the land of India functions, in the 1923 framework, as a rule of membership, a test with a registrar's edge, deciding who is of the nation. In the second framework the same land is the ground of an organism, sacred not as a criterion but as the body itself is sacred, and its function is to make the society's unity a fact of nature rather than a decision of law. In the third framework the land returns as the given frame of the state, and its function is electoral and constitutional: the territory within which majorities are counted and deferred items are enacted, item by item, as the majority permits. A definition, an organism and a majority are three kinds of thing. An ideology is individuated not by the sentiments it shares with its rivals, sentiments are cheap and travel everywhere, but by the instructions it issues: whom to count, what to build, what to do on Monday. On those questions the frameworks issue different orders, and a creed that must be scored so differently is three creeds wearing one another's coat.

The custodians' own conduct confirms the distinction from inside. If the three frameworks were one substance at three ages, their guardians would treat one another's texts as their own inheritance in full. The record shows friction at every joint. Savarkar's Mahasabha and Hedgewar's Sangh spent the late 1930s and the 1940s in mutual suspicion, the party resenting the Sangh's refusal of political discipline, the Sangh guarding its independence from the party's compromises, and no merger ever seriously approached. Golwalkar's Sangh kept the Jana Sangh at arm's length in doctrine even while supplying its organisers, and the Sangh's spokesmen said so, in print, whenever the party's compromises embarrassed the parent. The party, in its turn, has repeatedly edited the inheritance it claims: qualifying the 1939 treatise, deferring the programme in 1998, abridging the 1966 compilation for popular circulation in the years before Bhagwat's 2018 lectures. Families quarrel over an estate when the estate contains distinct properties. The quarrels are the disentanglement's internal evidence, supplied by the actors themselves.

### How does Aishwary Kumar's Radical Equality sharpen the reading of Savarkar?

By staging the argument Savarkar never had to answer in print. Kumar sets the 1923 definition beside Ambedkar's demand that equality precede nationhood, and the contrast exposes the definition's quiet premise: belonging is settled by ancestry and land before any question of equal standing inside the nation is allowed to arise.

Aishwary Kumar's *Radical Equality: Ambedkar, Gandhi, and the Risk of Democracy* (2015) earns its place in the matrix's fifth column because it performs, for the first framework, the operation this article performs for all three: it reads a definition by the argument it refuses. Kumar stages the confrontation between Ambedkar's insistence that a nation is legitimate only when equality is its founding act and the rival insistence, which he locates in Savarkar, that the nation precedes its members' claims and distributes standing from the fact of belonging. The contrast throws the 1923 architecture into relief. In Savarkar's design the order of operations is ancestry first, territory second, and justice third, an internal housekeeping matter for a people already one. The Patit Pavan campaigns, the dining halls of Ratnagiri, belong to the third step: they perfect the nation's material without questioning the first step's title to define it. Ambedkar's order is the reverse, and the argument between the two orders is the deepest argument inside Indian nationalism, deeper than any dispute about tactics. Its use for the disentanglement is diagnostic. Golwalkar's framework answers the Ambedkar challenge by dissolving it: in an organism, the question of equal standing cannot be posed, because parts have stations rather than claims, and the dissolution is a doctrinal choice, not an oversight. The electoral framework answers the challenge by translating it: claims return as constituencies, equality becomes arithmetic, and the party that mastered caste arithmetic in the 1990s and caste assurance in the 2010s is the Ambedkar problem restated in the only grammar the third framework speaks. One problem, three solutions, none of them a development of another. Kumar's contrast is the lens that makes the three solutions visible as solutions, rival answers to a question none of the frameworks could escape.

The archive on which such readings rest is thinner than the field's confidence suggests, and the hub's method requires the gap to be named. For the first framework, the political record of the vehicle years sits in the Savarkar Papers at the National Archives of India, where the correspondence of 1942 to 1944, letters written as Mahasabha president through the war's decisive years, documents Savarkar's attempts to design the organisational architecture his definition lacked: instructions on candidate selection, on the provincial coalitions of Bengal, Sindh and the Frontier, on the discipline of Mahasabha ministers and the management of a press that the party barely possessed. Standard scholarship treats the letters as colour for biography. Read as a set, they are the first framework's missing chapter, the record of a definition discovering in real time that it could not govern. Handling such material makes its own demands: wartime paper, restricted originals, copies whose custody chain is part of their evidentiary value. Researchers who work with digitised records of contested movements will recognise the discipline, which is the discipline that [the guide to how journalists use VaultBook to protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) sets out for source material whose handling determines whether it can be trusted. The pillar's rule follows from the archive's condition: texts and dates first, characterisations after, and every characterisation owned by a named holder.

## The Frameworks in One Another's Hands

Custody is the test of doctrine that archives cannot fake, and the century offers a custody record for each framework. Consider first the honours. On 26 February 2003, a portrait of Savarkar was unveiled in the Central Hall of Parliament, the republic's most ceremonial room, by the President of India, A.P.J. Abdul Kalam, in the presence of a BJP-led government whose founding programme then stood deferred under the agenda of 1998. Opposition parties boycotted the ceremony and their spokesmen called the honour an insult to the freedom struggle; the government defended it as the recognition of a revolutionary. Strip the episode of its polemics and its structure is legible in the matrix: a third-framework government canonising the first framework's author cost nothing in arithmetic, required no ally's consent and enacted no deferred item, while purchasing, at the price of a portrait, the allegiance of everyone for whom the 1923 definition is sacred. Iconography is the cheapest transaction among the frameworks, and governments conduct it precisely because the doctrines are distinct enough to need the gesture and connected enough for the gesture to work.

Consider next the party's own letterhead. The Bharatiya Janata Party's membership pledge, the undertaking its primary members sign, binds the member to Integral Humanism as the party's basic philosophy, the doctrine of neither of the two famous texts but of Upadhyay's lectures of April 1965. The choice, made at Bhopal in 1985 and never reversed, means that the operating party of the third framework swears its members to a fourth inheritance while campaigning on the first framework's map and staffing itself from the second framework's machine. A single-tradition reading must treat the pledge as a detail. The disentanglement treats it as the framework's signature in miniature: the electoral doctrine is a management system for inheritances, and its genius, and its vulnerability, is that it can honour all three doctrines in sequence without being identical to any of them. The Sangh's custody of the second framework shows the same management from the other side. Andersen and Damle's 2018 volume documents a leadership presenting the Sangh to a democratic century in a softened idiom, qualifying the founder-texts, widening the tent to communities the 1939 treatise had placed outside the nation, while the organisation's internal life, its camps, its syllabus of study texts, its calendar of commemorations, conserves the older doctrine in working order. Public qualification with internal retention is not hypocrisy, the lazy charge; it is what an institution does when it holds a doctrine it no longer expects the public square to accept unedited, and it is intelligible only if the doctrine being managed is a distinct thing that can be edited, an operation no one performs on a mere stage of development. Custody, honours, pledges and editions all tell the same story, and it is the story of the matrix: three properties, separately held, perpetually transacted.

## The Constitutional Baseline the Three Frameworks Inherit

No framework operates in a vacuum, and the ground all three contend for was fixed, between 1946 and 1950, by an adversary. The Constituent Assembly that framed the republic's Constitution worked in full knowledge of the Hindu nationalist alternative; several of its members had fought the Mahasabha in provincial politics for a decade, and its settlement was designed, in part, as the alternative's refutation. This series' founding study examines [the religion-state founding tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) at the Assembly's heart, the permanent negotiation between a state that must be impartial among religions and a society whose public life is saturated with religion. The settlement the Assembly produced, examined in this series under the name [the post-1947 plural-civilisational settlement](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/), gave every community's members equal citizenship as persons, protected religious practice and minority educational rights in the fundamental rights chapter, and refused to any community the status of the nation's proprietor. Each framework's relation to that settlement is distinct, and the distinctness is a fourth confirmation of the disentanglement, arrived at from outside the frameworks themselves.

Savarkar's framework predates the settlement and confronts it as a rival constitution of the nation. The Mahasabha's interventions in the constitutional debates of the 1940s, its evidence and memoranda, pressed the Assembly toward the 1923 test and were refused; equal citizenship by residence and birth, not by the two-land test, entered the Constitution's citizenship articles at the commencement of the republic. Golwalkar's framework met the finished Constitution with doctrinal reserve. In the discussions gathered in *Bunch of Thoughts*, he dismissed the document as a patchwork pieced together from the constitutions of the West, in which, he complained, nothing of the nation's own genius or its ancient political wisdom had found a place; the organism, in his account, had been handed a borrowed skeleton. The judgment is the second framework's constitutional position exactly: the state is a late and lesser thing than the society, and a borrowed state is a lesser thing still. The electoral framework, alone of the three, must win under the settlement it inherited, and its constitutional idiom is therefore the idiom of the document itself. It amends where it can muster the numbers, litigates where it cannot, and waits on courts it publicly criticises and privately obeys, most consequentially in Ayodhya, where the party accepted a judicial route to the temple it had promised at Palampur, and in Kashmir, where the action of 5 August 2019 was executed through the Constitution's own Article 370 procedure, the settlement's text turned against the settlement's most famous exception. The temple litigation displays the three postures in a single courtroom. The Ayodhya title suits, filed from 1950 onward, ground through the Allahabad High Court for two decades and ended, on 30 September 2010, in a three-way division of the disputed site among the Ram Lalla deity, the Nirmohi Akhara and the Sunni Central Waqf Board, a judgment every party appealed. As this article's date fell in September 2019, a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court was hearing those appeals day to day, the hearings having opened on 6 August 2019. For the first framework's heirs the case is a property dispute trailing a civilisational claim they regard as already settled by the definition of the nation. For the second framework's parent body the case is the state's business, watched with disciplined public restraint by an organisation whose doctrine assigns the courtroom to the society's lesser instruments. For the third framework the case is an item of programme on a judicial track, its 1989 promise expressly routed, at Palampur itself, through negotiation or legislation, and its governments have accepted the discipline of awaiting a verdict they could not schedule. One dispute, three relations to the republic's highest court, and the relations are not interchangeable.

External critic, internal dissenter, incumbent operator: the three postures toward the founding document are as irreconcilable as the tests of belonging, and they belong to three ideologies, not to one.

## Why the Disentanglement Comes First

The claim of this hub can be restated with its evidence around it. Hindutva is three distinct ideologies sharing a name. The first, Savarkar's territorial definition of 1923, settles belonging by fatherland and holy land, builds no vehicle, and prescribes definition before politics. The second, Golwalkar's organic doctrine of 1939 to 1966, settles belonging by congruence with a society ordered by dharma, builds a cadre body that refuses office, and prescribes formation before power. The third, the electoral framework assembled after 1980, settles belonging by the assembled majority, builds a mass party on borrowed cadres, and prescribes victory before enactment. The readings that would merge them, the textbook evolution and the Andersen-Damle continuity, each capture something true, the chronology in the first case, the carrier in the second, and each fails at the joints where the frameworks negate one another. The shared content of the three, Hindu cultural identity as the nation's anchor, a civilisational idiom, a quarrel with the politics of minority appeasement as its holders name it, is the vocabulary they hold in common, and a vocabulary is not a doctrine.

The disentanglement also states the terms on which it could be refuted, and a claim that names its refutation is worth more than one that cannot imagine it. The reading defended here would fall if the custodians' record showed the three frameworks being treated, by the men who held them, as one doctrine's ages: if the Sangh had guarded the 1939 treatise as its unaltered charter, if the party of 1985 had experienced its choice between Gandhian socialism and Integral Humanism as no choice at all, if the programme of 1989 had been enacted without deferral in 1998, if the compilation of 1966 had reached the readers of 2018 unedited and unqualified. None of those things happened. The opposite of each is documented in the studies above, in the actors' own papers, resolutions and editions. What would also count against the disentanglement, and what the pillar's later studies are charged to watch for, is evidence of the frameworks merging in practice: a BJP government administering Golwalkar's social stations, a Sangh that contests elections, a definition of citizenship enacted in the image of 1923. The studies that follow take the frameworks one at a time, the 1923 text, the 1925 founding, the Golwalkar corpus, the Jana Sangh bridge, the refounding of 1980, the Ayodhya mobilisation and the demolition, and the Sangh-party relation after 2014, and each can be read as the biography of one doctrine rather than as a chapter in the life of a composite. The composite was the confusion. The three names inside the one word are the clarity, and clarity about what is being praised, blamed or voted for is the least that a century of argument owes its readers.

The three files also equip the reader for the argument's next century, because claims made in Hindutva's name will keep arriving faster than the scholarship that sorts them. Each new claim can be interrogated with the hub's three questions. Which test of belonging does it apply, a registrar's test of lands, an organism's test of congruence, or a count's test of assembly? Which vehicle does it presuppose, a party of notables, a cadre body, or a mass electoral machine? Which logic does it obey, definition before politics, formation before power, or victory before enactment? A claim that answers the three questions in one framework's grammar belongs to that framework, whatever vocabulary it borrows from the others, and a claim that answers them in mixed grammar is a transaction among frameworks, to be priced as transactions are, by asking what each side gave and what each side got. The habit of asking will outlast the pillar. It is offered here as the series' standing instruction for reading Indian politics wherever the one word is made to carry the three doctrines, in manifestos and in judgments, in processions and in Parliament, by governments and by the movements that besiege them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Hindutva?

Hindutva is the name shared by three distinct doctrines of Hindu nationhood formulated in India between 1923 and 1989. The first is V.D. Savarkar's territorial definition of 1923, which counts as Hindu anyone whose ancestral fatherland and holy land both lie in the territory from the Indus to the seas. The second is M.S. Golwalkar's organic doctrine of 1939 to 1966, which treats Hindu society as a living body ordered by dharma and builds it through the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh. The third is the electoral doctrine assembled by the Bharatiya Janata Party after 1980, which defines the nation politically as the Hindu majority assembled at elections and enacts its programme in sequence. The three share a word, a civilisational idiom and a cadre lineage. They differ in their tests of belonging, their organisations and their political logic, and this pillar treats them as three ideologies rather than as one tradition.

### Q: Who coined the term Hindutva?

The word and the doctrine have different authors. The earliest recorded use of Hindutva in print is Chandranath Basu's Bengali book *Hindutva: Hindur Prakrita Itihas* of 1892, where the word named the essence of Hinduness in a defence of the Hindu domestic and ritual order, and the *Calcutta Review* noticed the book in July 1894. Basu founded no political doctrine. V.D. Savarkar rebuilt the word in his pamphlet *Essentials of Hindutva*, written under detention at Ratnagiri and published in May 1923, giving it a precise political content: the quality of belonging to the Hindu nation defined by fatherland and holy land. Scholars therefore answer the coinage question at two levels. The word is Basu's, from 1892; the doctrine that made the word famous, and the sense in which every later framework uses it, is Savarkar's, from 1923, which is why reference works conventionally credit Savarkar as the formulator of Hindutva.

### Q: How is Savarkar's Hindutva different from Golwalkar's?

The two doctrines differ on every test this pillar applies. On belonging: Savarkar's test of 1923 is territorial and ancestral, fatherland and holy land on one soil, a test an atheist of Hindu descent passes and a believer whose holy land lies abroad fails; Golwalkar's test of 1939 is organic, membership in a Hindu society unified by race, religion, civilisation and language under dharma, a test of congruence rather than of documents. On society: Savarkar campaigned to dissolve caste as a chain on the nation, building the Patit Pavan temple at Ratnagiri in 1931 open to all castes; Golwalkar theorised varna as the organic order's functional stations. On the vehicle: Savarkar led a party of notables, the Hindu Mahasabha, and wrote no organisational doctrine; Golwalkar led the RSS, a cadre body that refused party politics and formed men in the daily shakha. On the sacred: Savarkar rationed theology, counting Hinduism a fragment of Hindutva; Golwalkar saturated his doctrine in dharma.

### Q: What is the difference between Hindutva and Hinduism?

Hinduism names the religious traditions of the Hindus, their texts, rituals, sects and philosophies, as practised across three thousand years. Hindutva names political doctrines about the Hindu nation, formulated in the twentieth century. Savarkar himself insisted on the distinction in *Essentials of Hindutva* (1923): Hinduism, he wrote, is only a derivative, a fraction, a part of Hindutva, because his definition of the Hindu rested on land and ancestry rather than on belief, so that a Hindu atheist belongs to Hindutva while standing outside Hinduism as a religion, and an observant Hindu who rejects the political doctrines holds Hinduism without Hindutva. The distinction survives in all three frameworks of this pillar, though each draws it differently: for Golwalkar the religious order of dharma is the nation's substance, which narrows the gap from the other side, and for the electoral framework the line is drawn by the voter rather than by the theologian. Confusing the two words converts a debate about doctrines into a debate about a faith, which serves neither accuracy nor argument.

### Q: What is the Jaffrelot tripartite reading of Hindutva?

The tripartite reading is Christophe Jaffrelot's, set out in *The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics* (1996). Jaffrelot periodises Hindu nationalism into distinct formations: the ideological elaboration of the 1920s and 1930s around Savarkar's definition, the organisational consolidation of the Sangh under Golwalkar, and the movement's electoral deployment from the 1980s through the Ayodhya mobilisation to national power. He treats the three as phases of a single tradition, each phase's form explained by its strategic moment, definition when the movement was a sect, organisation when it was suspect, mobilisation when numbers became sovereign. This pillar adopts Jaffrelot's periodisation as chronology and extends it into a stronger claim: the three formations are substantively distinct ideologies, not stages, because they negate one another on the questions that individuate a doctrine, the test of belonging, the vehicle and the political logic. The extension is argued in the hub study from the actors' own record of choices among the frameworks: the qualified custody of the 1939 treatise, the doctrinal choice of Bhopal in 1985, the deferrals of 1998 and the edited afterlife of the 1966 compilation.

### Q: Are RSS Hindutva and BJP Hindutva the same?

No, though the two are joined by personnel and inheritance. RSS Hindutva, in the form M.S. Golwalkar gave it between 1939 and 1966, is an organic doctrine: Hindu society as a body ordered by dharma, formed man by man in the shakha, with the Sangh refusing party politics as a matter of principle. BJP Hindutva, the electoral framework assembled after 1980, is a doctrine of assembly and enactment: the Hindu majority gathered at elections, the programme sequenced by arithmetic, the founding items deferred under coalition in 1998 and taken up under majority after 2014. The party is staffed by Sangh-formed organisers and honours the Sangh's texts, and the Sangh supplies its campaigns with cadres; the bloodstream is one. The doctrines are not, because their instructions differ: form the man and let the state follow, against win the state and let the programme follow. The difference is visible whenever the two have publicly diverged, in the party's deferrals of 1998, in its governmental compromises, and in the Sangh's 2018 presentation of its own doctrine in an edited, qualified form for a national audience.

### Q: What does Hindutva commit to politically?

The answer depends on which of the three frameworks is speaking, and the dependence is the point. Savarkar's framework of 1923 commits its holder to a definition: India is the fatherland and holy land of the Hindu nation, whose members are to be counted by ancestry and sacred geography, organised politically, and defended, by militarisation among other means, against every rival claim to the territory. Golwalkar's framework commits its holder to a formation: a lifetime of cadre discipline in the Sangh, the remaking of Hindu society as an organic whole ordered by dharma, and patience about state power, which is to follow social unity rather than precede it. The electoral framework commits its holder to a programme and a sequence: the temple at Ayodhya, the end of Article 370's special status, a Uniform Civil Code, the items named at Palampur in 1989 and in the manifestos since, to be enacted as majorities allow. What all three share is a floor rather than a programme: the conviction that Hindu cultural identity is the Indian nation's anchor, and that the republic's politics should be arranged around that fact.

### Q: What does the word Sangh Parivar mean, and which bodies does it name?

Sangh Parivar means the family of the Sangh, and it names the constellation of organisations that the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh founded, inspired or staffs, each working one sphere of national life under the second framework's division of labour. The RSS itself, founded at Nagpur in 1925, stands at the centre as the cadre-forming parent. Around it stand the affiliates in their dated order: the Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad for students (1949), the Vanavasi Kalyan Ashram for tribal welfare (1952), the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh for labour (1955), the Vishwa Hindu Parishad for religious consolidation (1964), and the political line that runs from the Bharatiya Jana Sangh (1951) to the Bharatiya Janata Party (1980), with service, educational and diaspora bodies added in later decades. The family idiom is the organisation's own, and scholars including Andersen and Damle adopt it because it captures the structure: legally separate bodies, joined by shared personnel, the loan of pracharaks, common commemorations and a common doctrinal inheritance, without a single chain of command that any one document establishes.

### Q: What content do all three Hindutva frameworks share?

Three holdings recur in every framework. The first is the anchor: Hindu cultural identity is what makes India a nation rather than a territory administered by a state, and each framework states it in its own grammar, as a definition in 1923, as an organism's soul after 1939, as a majority's self-description after 1989. The second is the adversary as its holders name it: a politics of minority appeasement, the charge, levelled against the Congress and its successors, that minority votes were purchased with concessions, from separate electorates' afterlife to the Shah Bano legislation of 1986, that no majority community would have been offered. The third is the idiom: a civilisational vocabulary of antiquity and continuity, sacred rivers and sacred geography, in which India's age is itself an argument for its unity. This pillar's hub study argues that the shared content is a vocabulary rather than a doctrine, because each holding performs different work inside the three architectures: the anchor is a membership rule in one, a metaphysic in the second and a mobilising address in the third.

### Q: What is the strongest objection to the claim that Hindutva is three distinct ideologies?

The strongest objection is the continuity case in its best form, the form built by Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle across *The Brotherhood in Saffron* (1987) and *The RSS: A View to the Inside* (2018). It holds that one organisation, the Sangh, formed the personnel of all three frameworks, that the core vision of a culturally united Hindu nation organised for strength persists unchanged from 1925 to power, and that the differences among the texts are emphases within a family. The objection is formidable because its evidence is real: the cadre bloodstream is one, the symbols are shared, and the later frameworks quote the earlier ones. This pillar answers that continuity of the carrier does not settle continuity of the carried. Organisations transmit doctrines they also revise, and the record of revision, the qualified treatise of 1939, the doctrinal choice of 1985, the deferrals of 1998, the edited compilation of 2018, shows custodians selecting among distinct inheritances. The objection therefore establishes the family's unity, which the disentanglement concedes, without establishing the doctrine's unity, which is the point in dispute.

### Q: How does each of the three frameworks treat caste?

Each framework gives caste a different place, and the differences map the disentanglement precisely. Savarkar's framework treats caste as a chain on the nation: his Ratnagiri campaigns of the internment years attacked the bans on inter-caste dining and marriage, temple entry and reconversion, and the Patit Pavan temple he promoted in 1931 admitted every caste and required no Brahmin-born priest, because a nation defined by land and ancestry cannot afford internal walls. Golwalkar's framework treats caste's ancestor, varna, as the organism's design: in *Bunch of Thoughts* the four orders appear as functional stations of one social body, a presentation his defenders read as dignity of function and his critics as hierarchy naturalised, a dispute this series marks as disputed. The electoral framework treats caste as arithmetic: the constituency reality to be studied seat by seat, answered with candidate selection, sub-caste assurance and welfare delivery, and periodically transcended, as its strategists claim for the Ayodhya mobilisation and the verdicts of 2014 and 2019, by the larger Hindu assembly. Dissolution, ordering, counting: three operations, three doctrines.

### Q: What did Savarkar mean by calling Hindutva a history rather than a word?

The sentence opens *Essentials of Hindutva* (1923), and it announces the book's method. Savarkar is refusing the dictionary. A word can be defined by its opposite, as a creed is defined against other creeds, but a history, he argues, is the accumulated life of a people, its land, descent, language, law, art and sacred geography across millennia, and Hindutva names that whole life rather than any doctrine within it. The definition that follows is built to match: because Hindutva is a history, its tests are the tests of a people's continuity, common nation, common race and common civilisation, and not the tests of a faith, which is why Hinduism itself appears in the book as a part and fragment of the larger whole. The sentence also explains the book's reception. A doctrine can be revised by its custodians; a history can only be honoured or betrayed, and the frameworks that inherited Savarkar's text have argued ever since over which of the two they are doing, an argument the hub study of this pillar uses as one of its measures of their distinctness.

### Q: Where are Savarkar's Mahasabha-era letters preserved, and why do they matter?

The letters survive in the Savarkar Papers at the National Archives of India in New Delhi, among the records of his presidency of the Hindu Mahasabha, and the set that matters most runs from 1942 to 1944, the war years in which the party held provincial office and faced the approach of the transfer of power. They document, week by week, a definition-maker attempting to build the machinery his framework lacked: instructions on candidate selection and provincial coalition arithmetic, correspondence with Mahasabha ministers in Bengal, Sindh and the North West Frontier Province, interventions on the party's press and finances. Standard scholarship treats the correspondence as biographical colour, and it is under-cited as a result. Read structurally, it is the first framework's missing chapter, the evidence that the definition's failure was organisational rather than intellectual, visible in its author's own hand as he tried to improvise a party out of a doctrine. The pillar's Savarkar study uses the letters on that footing, and the hub study treats them as the archive against which the later frameworks' organisational superiority, the Sangh's machine and the party's arithmetic, is to be measured.

### Q: What did the Hindu Mahasabha win and lose under Savarkar's presidency?

The Mahasabha's account under Savarkar's presidency, from 1937 into the war years, divides into office won and mandate lost. It won place: under his leadership the party entered coalition ministries in Bengal from December 1941, where Syama Prasad Mukherjee held finance in Fazlul Haq's Progressive Coalition, and in Sindh and the North West Frontier Province in 1943, and Savarkar defended the arrangements as a conscious policy of keeping the defined nation's representatives inside every room where the country's future was being divided. It won a voice: his presidential addresses, beginning at Ahmedabad in December 1937 with the declaration that Hindus and Muslims were two nations in the main, set terms the national debate had to answer. It lost the country. The party never built a mass membership to rival the Congress organisation, its wartime policy of responsive cooperation identified it, in the public mind its critics commanded, with the power that was leaving, and in the elections of 1945 and 1946 it was routed, winning a negligible share of seats while the Congress took the Hindu constituencies and the Muslim League the Muslim ones. The definition survived its vehicle, which is why it could be inherited by others.

### Q: Why did Golwalkar's framework require a cadre organisation rather than a party?

Golwalkar's reasoning, stated across *Bunch of Thoughts* (1966) and his addresses as sarsanghchalak, runs from the diagnosis. India's defeats, in his account, flowed from social disintegration, a people divided by caste, sect, language and selfishness, and no election repairs disintegration, because parties must court the divisions that win votes and so deepen them. The remedy had to work below politics, on character, and had to refuse the prizes that corrupt the work: office, funds, publicity, the bargaining of coalition. Hence the cadre form: the daily shakha as the unit of manufacture, the celibate pracharak as the full-time instrument, the horizon of generations rather than of parliaments, and the doctrine that formed men, released into every sphere, would nationalise the society from inside while the Sangh itself remained an organisation of the whole society standing apart from party competition. The framework's political abstinence was thus not modesty but method, and its price, the dependence on political arms it did not control, from the Jana Sangh to the BJP, is the structural fact the third framework inherited and answered in its own way.

### Q: How did the electoral-political framework change once the BJP held a Lok Sabha majority of its own?

The clearest change is that deferral lost its excuse. Between 1998 and 2004 the framework could attribute the shelving of its founding programme to coalition arithmetic, and did. The verdicts of May 2014, 282 seats, and May 2019, 303 seats, gave the Bharatiya Janata Party a Lok Sabha majority of its own, the first since 1984, and converted every postponement into a choice for which the party alone answers. The consequences appeared in sequence: a programme long stored began to be spent in the order its managers selected, with the constitutional action on Jammu and Kashmir of 5 August 2019, the first of the three items deferred in 1998, carried in the first session of the new Parliament, and the instant triple talaq statute enacted in July 2019. The framework's logic, win first and enact in sequence, had operated before under coalition translation; under majority it operates untranslated, which is why scholars of the period, Christophe Jaffrelot among them, treat the post-2014 years as a distinct phase of the movement's history. The doctrine did not change its content. It changed its grammar, from the conditional to the indicative.

### Q: What is the difference between a framework and an ideology in this analysis?

The two words do different work in this pillar, with fixed meanings. An ideology is a doctrine as held and acted on: a test of belonging, an account of the adversary, a set of instructions for the holder, of the kind a pamphlet, a treatise or a party programme can carry. A framework is the architecture that holds those elements together and connects them to an organisation and a strategy: the criteria, the vehicle and the political logic considered as one design. The three Hindutvas are called frameworks when their architecture is under examination and ideologies when their force as held doctrines is in question, and the claim that they are three distinct ideologies means that no element of the architecture is shared in the same form across all three: not the test, which sorts people differently, not the vehicle, which is absent in one, apolitical in the second and electoral in the third, and not the logic, which runs definition first, formation first and victory first respectively. The distinction matters because a single shared element, a slogan, a symbol, a cadre, is evidence about one layer of a design and not about the whole.

### Q: Is Hindu nationalism the same thing as Hindutva?

Not in scholarly usage, where the first term is the wider one. Hindu nationalism, the category Christophe Jaffrelot's 1996 history made standard, names the broad movement and sentiment, running from the nineteenth-century revival through every organisation that has claimed the Hindu nation, including figures and bodies that predate or reject the doctrines of 1923. Hindutva names the doctrinal family founded by Savarkar's *Essentials of Hindutva* (1923) and rebuilt by Golwalkar and by the electoral framework: specific, dated answers to the questions of belonging, vehicle and logic. The relation is genus to species. Every framework in this pillar is Hindu nationalist; not every Hindu nationalist position is Hindutva, as the cases of the nineteenth-century revivalists, of Chandranath Basu, whose Hindutva of 1892 named an essence rather than a nation, and of Hindu traditionalists who oppose the political doctrines in the name of the religion, each shows. Keeping the genus and the species apart is what allows the pillar's central claim to be stated at all, since the three species inside the one word are distinct precisely where the genus is one.

### Q: What role does territory play in each framework's definition of the nation?

Territory does three different jobs. In Savarkar's framework of 1923 the land is the test itself: the territory from the Indus to the seas must be at once fatherland and holy land, so that the map decides membership, and the nation's proper extent, the undivided subcontinent of Akhand Bharat, is a deduction from the definition. In Golwalkar's framework the land is one of the five unities of the nation and the ground of the social body, sacred certainly, but subordinate in the argument to the organism that lives on it; his treatise of 1939 can contemplate the nation's extent in the idiom of race and civilisation spreading, because the body matters more than the border a registrar would draw. In the electoral framework territory is the state's given frame and a file of constitutional questions: the integration of Jammu and Kashmir, settled as its managers presented it in August 2019, the management of borders and citizenship as matters of statute and count, questions to be enacted in sequence rather than deduced from a definition. Registrar's map, body's ground, government's file: the same subcontinent, three instruments, and the differences decide real cases.

### Q: Which scholarly work should a reader start with for each of the three readings?

Begin with the primary texts in their order, Savarkar's *Essentials of Hindutva* (1923), Golwalkar's *We or Our Nationhood Defined* (1939) and *Bunch of Thoughts* (1966), and the BJP's Palampur Resolution (1989), since every scholarly reading is an argument about those documents. For the tripartite reading, start with Christophe Jaffrelot, *The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics* (1996), whose periodisation this pillar adopts and extends. For the continuity reading, start with Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle, *The Brotherhood in Saffron* (1987), and continue to their *The RSS: A View to the Inside* (2018), which carries the organisational account into the Modi era on the strength of access to the Sangh's leadership. For the sharpest single lens on the first framework, read Aishwary Kumar, *Radical Equality* (2015), whose staging of the argument between Ambedkar and Savarkar over whether equality precedes nationhood exposes the 1923 definition's premise. The four readings disagree with one another, and the hub study's adjudication among them is this pillar's own position: read the texts first, the periodisation second, and let the actors' documented choices among the frameworks arbitrate.
