Class consciousness is usually introduced as one of the simplest ideas in Marxist theory and then quietly becomes one of the hardest. The simple version says that workers have class consciousness when they recognise that they belong to a class, understand that the class has interests of its own, and see those interests as opposed to the interests of the employers who confront them. On that definition the concept looks like a piece of social psychology, a state of mind that could in principle be measured by asking people questions, and its history looks like a long disappointment, because the recognition has repeatedly failed to arrive in the form the theory seemed to predict. Almost everything about that simple version is misleading. The developed concept that the Marxist tradition actually uses was not written by Marx at all. It was constructed by Georg Lukacs in a book published in 1923, and in Lukacs's construction class consciousness is explicitly not a description of what workers believe. It is an account of what a class would grasp if it understood its own position in the social whole, whether or not any individual member of the class grasps it. That single move, from belief to imputed understanding, changes what kind of evidence could ever count for or against the concept, and it generates the deepest problem the concept carries, the problem of who is entitled to speak for a class's mind. A generation later E. P. Thompson rejected the whole construction and rebuilt the idea from historical experience, arguing that class is not a position waiting for correct awareness but a relationship that happens between people as they live through shared conditions together. This article works through the three models that result, because nearly every confused discussion of class consciousness is a discussion in which two or three incompatible models are being used in the same paragraph without anyone noticing.

## The glossary definition, and why it cannot carry the weight placed on it

The glossary definition has genuine virtues, and it is worth saying what they are before saying why it fails. It is clear. It distinguishes class consciousness from mere membership, since a person can occupy a working class position by every objective criterion and still not think of themselves in class terms at all. It connects the concept to action, because a worker who understands their situation as shared with millions of others, and as opposed to the interests of those who employ them, is a worker disposed to join a union, to support a strike, and to vote in ways that a worker who understands their situation as purely private is not. And it captures something real about political life, which is that collective action on any scale requires people to conceive of themselves as members of a collectivity whose fortunes rise and fall together. Any adequate account of class consciousness has to preserve these insights, and the more sophisticated models examined in this article preserve them in different ways.

The definition fails at three points, and the failures compound. The first failure is historical. If class consciousness means awareness of belonging to a class and of that class's interests, then the concept should be found fully formed in the writings of the thinker whose name the tradition carries, yet Marx uses the phrase rarely, never defines it, and never builds an argument on it. The vocabulary of consciousness in Marx is dominated by other terms, by ideology, by the distinction between a class in itself and a class for itself, and by scattered remarks about how the ideas of an age are formed. A reader who goes to Marx looking for the developed theory of class consciousness will not find it, and textbooks that present the theory as Marx's own are repeating an attribution error that the scholarship corrected long ago. The second failure is evidential. On the glossary definition, the natural test of the concept is opinion research, and opinion research in advanced economies persistently finds that class self identification tracks occupation, education, accent, and culture at least as strongly as it tracks any relation to ownership, and that large numbers of people whose position is working class by the relational criterion describe themselves in other terms entirely. If the concept were really the glossary concept, those findings would be close to a refutation, and much popular writing treats them as exactly that. The third failure is strategic. The glossary definition makes class consciousness a precondition that either exists or does not, and so it cannot explain the phenomenon that most interested the tradition's serious writers, which is the uneven, contradictory, and reversible character of working class thought, the way the same workforce can be militant on Monday about a closure and deferential on Friday about a wage claim, radical about the firm and conservative about the nation, lucid about its immediate opponent and mystified about everything beyond the factory gate.

A concept that cannot handle unevenness is a concept that will be discarded the first time it meets evidence, and the history of the idea is largely the history of attempts to build versions that can handle it. Lukacs built his version to explain why a class could fail to recognise its position without the failure counting as simple ignorance. Gramsci built his account of contradictory consciousness to explain how two incompatible conceptions of the world could coexist in one person's practical activity and speech. Thompson built his version to explain how class could be real, and could transform a society, without ever taking the form of a correct doctrine arriving in workers' heads. Each construction answers a genuine difficulty. Each also creates difficulties of its own, and the point of distinguishing them is not pedantry. It is that arguments which are sound against one model are worthless against another, and a debate in which the models are not distinguished is a debate that can continue indefinitely without either side landing a blow.

## What Marx actually wrote: a sparse usage and two famous scenes

Marx's contribution to the concept is real but indirect, and precision about it matters because the tradition's later constructions claim his authority. The most important Marxian text is also one of the earliest. In The Poverty of Philosophy, published in 1847 as a polemic against Proudhon, Marx describes how economic conditions transform a mass of producers into a class as against capital. The mass shares conditions, shares an opponent, and develops common interests it defends, yet in Marx's careful phrasing it is a class against another class before it is a class for itself, organised and conscious of its unity as a political force. The distinction between the class as an objective formation and the class as a self aware political actor is present here in embryo, and later writers, including the article in this series on [class in itself and for itself](/2011/10/01/class-in-itself-and-for-itself/), have treated the passage as the seed of everything that followed. But a seed is not a theory. Marx does not say what the transformation consists in psychologically, does not specify who certifies that it has occurred, and does not return to the distinction with the systematic attention he gives to value, rent, or the working day.

The second famous scene is darker, and it concerns not workers but peasants. In The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, published in 1852, Marx analyses the French smallholding peasantry as a vast mass whose members live in similar conditions without entering into manifold relations with one another, a mode of production that isolates households instead of interconnecting them. His notorious image is of a sack of potatoes, a class formed by simple addition of homologous magnitudes rather than by community, communication, or organisation. The political conclusion he draws is severe: such a class cannot represent itself and must be represented, and its representative arrives as an authority standing above it, which in the French case meant Louis Bonaparte and the executive power he embodied. Read carefully, the passage is less about consciousness than about the material preconditions of collective self representation, about roads, markets, language, and organisation as the infrastructure without which shared conditions remain politically inert. That materialist emphasis is one of the most fertile ideas in the whole discussion, and Thompson's later work can be read as an attempt to write the positive version of it, an account of how the infrastructure of connection actually gets built in lived experience.

Beyond these scenes, Marx's usage is scattered and mostly indirect. He writes about the working class learning through struggle, about strikes and combinations as schools, about the way the factory system itself gathers workers together and disciplines them into a collective actor. He writes in the first volume of Capital about the point at which the mass of misery, exploitation, and degradation grows alongside the revolt of a working class constantly increasing in numbers and trained, united, and organised by the very mechanism of capitalist production. None of this amounts to a concept of class consciousness in the developed sense. There is no term in Marx corresponding to the German Klassenbewusstsein doing systematic work, no distinction between actual and imputed awareness, no account of what a class would think if it thought correctly. The honest summary is that Marx supplied the objective theory of class, a set of vivid observations about the conditions under which classes become political actors, and a vocabulary that later writers found suggestive. The theory of consciousness that bears his name was built by others, in circumstances he never faced, to answer questions his lifetime only began to pose. The objective theory itself is set out in the companion article on [the Marxist theory of class](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/), and it is the necessary background here, because every model of class consciousness is an answer to a single question that the objective theory creates and cannot itself resolve: what is the relation between where a class stands and what its members think?


## Why the concept had to be invented: revolution in the east, failure in the west

The developed concept of class consciousness is a product of a specific historical shock, and it cannot be understood apart from that shock. In 1917 the working class of Russia, small, concentrated in a few industrial centres, and surrounded by an immense peasantry, became the social base of a revolution that seized and held power. In the advanced capitalist countries of western Europe, where the working class was larger, better organised, longer schooled in unions and parties, and by every prediction of the older theory riper for revolution, nothing comparable occurred. The German revolution of 1918 and 1919 was defeated. The Hungarian soviet republic of 1919 collapsed within months. The Italian factory occupations of 1920 ended in retreat and were followed by fascism. Writers formed in the Marxist tradition faced a pattern that the objective theory alone seemed unable to explain, since the class whose position was most developed displayed the least revolutionary consciousness, while a class in a backward country had acted with world changing decisiveness. Two broad explanations were available. One held that the western working class had been bought, corrupted, or integrated, its labour aristocracy bribed by imperial superprofits and its organisations captured by officials with a stake in stability. The other held that the problem lay in consciousness itself, in the way capitalist society systematically shapes what its members can perceive about their own situation, so that position and awareness could diverge not accidentally but necessarily. The second explanation is the one Lukacs developed, and its appeal is obvious: it converts an embarrassing anomaly into evidence for a deeper theory, provided the theory of distorted perception can be made rigorous rather than serving as an excuse available for every failure.

### Why did the concept become central after the First World War?

Because revolution succeeded where the working class was smallest and failed where it was largest, organised, and longest established. Objective position visibly failed to predict political awareness, so theorists needed an account of how consciousness could diverge from position systematically, and Lukacs supplied the most ambitious one.

Georg Lukacs was a Hungarian philosopher of considerable reputation before he was a Marxist theorist, and the essays collected in History and Class Consciousness, published in 1923, bear the marks of that formation on every page. The book is difficult, Hegelian in vocabulary, and written at a level of abstraction that has kept it a specialists' text even as its central ideas have passed into the common currency of the tradition. Its author served briefly as a deputy commissar for education in the Hungarian soviet republic, fled into exile after its fall, and eventually repudiated parts of the book under political pressure, a biographical fact that matters because the book itself is partly an attempt to understand, from inside, how a revolutionary moment forms and dissolves in the minds of those who live it. The essay at the centre of this article, the essay on class consciousness itself, asks a question of deceptive simplicity. What does it mean to say that a class, as a class, is conscious of something? Individuals are conscious. Classes are not skulls with contents. Any use of the phrase class consciousness therefore involves a construction, and Lukacs's originality lies in stating openly what the construction is rather than pretending the phrase describes an observable mental state.

## Lukacs's construction: consciousness imputed to a position

Lukacs begins by refusing the two most natural interpretations. Class consciousness is not the sum or average of what the members of a class happen to think, and it is not the thought of the class's most advanced or articulate members either. Empirical investigation of workers' opinions, however careful, yields what he calls the psychological consciousness of the class, a factual record of beliefs that may be confused, contradictory, borrowed, or lagging, and that belongs to the domain of sociology rather than to the theory he is building. Against this he sets what the standard translations render as imputed or ascribed class consciousness, in German the zugerechnetes Klassenbewusstsein. Imputed class consciousness is the awareness that would be appropriate to a class's objective position, the thoughts and feelings people would have if they were able to assess both their own situation and the totality of the society that produces it, together with the actions that would rationally follow from that assessment. It is, in the strictest sense, a theoretical construction. The theorist works out, from an analysis of the class's place in the process of production, what interests the class has, what understanding of society those interests would generate in a mind that grasped them fully, and what course of action would serve them. That constructed awareness is then attributed to the class as its consciousness, not as a report of its members' mental states but as a statement of the rational content of its position.

The audacity of the move should not be understated, and neither should its internal logic. Lukacs is not claiming that workers secretly think what the theorist attributes to them. He is claiming that a class position has implications, that those implications can be derived, and that the derivation gives the analyst a standard against which actual beliefs can be compared and their distance measured. The comparison does real work. It explains why a class can act against its own interests without the analyst having to describe its members as stupid, since the gap between position and perception is itself produced by the society, by the fragmentation of experience, by the reified forms in which social relations appear as relations between things. It explains why consciousness can change rapidly in a crisis, when the routines that sustain the gap break down and the implications of a position force themselves on attention. And it explains why the bourgeoisie, in Lukacs's account, faces a structural limit that the proletariat does not. The bourgeoisie also has an imputed consciousness, the awareness appropriate to its position, but that awareness, pursued honestly, runs into a wall, because a full understanding of the totality would reveal the historically transient character of bourgeois society itself, and so the class that rules is compelled to stop its own thought short of completion. The proletariat, by contrast, is the class whose self knowledge and knowledge of society coincide, since to understand its own position completely is already to understand the whole and to perceive the possibility of abolishing class society altogether. This is the famous identity of subject and object in history, the claim that the proletariat is the first class positioned to know society truly because its emancipation requires that knowledge, and it is the philosophical engine of the entire construction.

### Is class consciousness the same as what workers actually believe?

Not in the tradition's main version. Lukacs defined class consciousness as the understanding appropriate to a class position, which may differ from any worker's actual beliefs. Thompson rejected this, treating class consciousness as what people make of their shared experience.

Several features of the construction deserve emphasis because they are routinely lost in summary. First, imputed consciousness is ascribed to classes, not to individuals, and Lukacs is explicit that no individual worker need ever embody it fully. It functions the way a rational model functions elsewhere in social theory, as a benchmark constructed by the analyst, and individuals approach or recede from it as circumstances change. Second, the construction is meant to be objective in a specific sense: the imputation is not the theorist's wish but a derivation, answerable to the analysis of the mode of production, and two analysts who accept the same account of the class's position should arrive at substantially the same imputed consciousness. Third, the construction builds the possibility of false consciousness into itself from the start. If consciousness can be imputed from position, then actual consciousness can fall short of position, and the shortfall is not random error but a structured feature of a society that presents its relations in misleading forms. The neighbouring concept receives its own treatment in the article on [false consciousness](/2012/02/15/false-consciousness-explained/), and the two ideas should be kept distinct, since imputation is a method for specifying what a class's awareness would be, while false consciousness is a diagnosis of why actual awareness diverges from it. Fourth, and most consequentially, the construction locates the authority to describe a class's consciousness outside the class's own utterances. The theorist, or the party that claims the theory, can in principle know the content of a class's consciousness better than the class's members do, because the members have access only to their psychological consciousness while the theorist commands the derivation. Everything politically dangerous in the concept flows from this fourth feature, and everything analytically powerful in it does too, since a theory that could only repeat what workers already say could never explain why they say it or predict when they will stop.

Lukacs connects the construction to his account of reification, developed in the book's longest essay and treated separately in the guide to [Lukacs on reification](/2015/11/15/lukacs-reification-guide/). Reification names the process by which relations between people take on the character of things, governed by apparently natural laws, so that the worker confronts the labour market, the wage, and the working day as fixed features of a landscape rather than as products of a social arrangement that people made and could remake. A consciousness formed inside reified relations perceives fragments. It sees the individual bargain, the individual employer, the individual grievance, and it cannot see the totality because the totality never appears as such in anyone's daily experience. Class consciousness, in the imputed sense, is precisely the penetration of that fragmentation, the point at which the class grasps the fragments as moments of a whole whose logic it can state. This is why Lukacs insists that the difference between bourgeois and proletarian thought is not a difference of intelligence or information but of standpoint. From within the standpoint of capital, the fragments are all there is, and the science built on them, however sophisticated, remains contemplative, recording the laws of a world it treats as given. From the standpoint of the proletariat, the same fragments become intelligible as a process, because the worker experiences their own activity as the substance that the reified forms conceal. Whether one accepts this epistemology or not, it gives the concept of class consciousness a definite content: consciousness is class consciousness when it grasps the totality from the standpoint of a class position, and its degrees are degrees of that grasp, not quantities of anger, information, or self labelling.


## The three models of class consciousness, and why they must not be mixed

The literature on class consciousness contains three distinct models of what the thing is, and the first step toward clarity is to lay them side by side. The imputed model, which is Lukacs's, treats consciousness as the rational content of a position, derived by the theorist and attributed to the class. The experiential model, which is Thompson's, treats consciousness as something made by people in the course of living through shared conditions together, embodied in institutions, customs, values, and traditions rather than in propositions that could be listed. The empirical or attitudinal model, which dominates quantitative sociology, treats consciousness as a distribution of beliefs and identifications in a population, discoverable by survey and interview. Each model has its own criterion for saying that a class has consciousness, its own kind of evidence, and its own political implications, and they diverge most sharply on the question of authority. Who is entitled to say what a class thinks? The imputed model answers: the analyst who has derived the position's implications, even against the testimony of the class's members. The experiential model answers: the class itself, in its historical practice, whose consciousness is proved in what it builds and defends rather than in what it tells an interviewer. The attitudinal model answers: the respondents, aggregated, whose self descriptions are the data and cannot be corrected by the researcher's theory without ceasing to be research. The table below states the comparison in the form this series uses for its findable artifacts, and readers who keep their own study notes on the three models will find a structured workspace for comparisons of exactly this kind in the [VaultBook study notes for Marxism](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), where the columns of a table like this one can be expanded into full notes on each model as the reading proceeds.

| Model | What consciousness is | Who has authority to say a class has it | What evidence bears on it | What it implies for political strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Imputed (Lukacs) | The understanding rationally appropriate to a class position, derived from an analysis of the totality | The theorist or party that performs the derivation from the class's objective position | The coherence of the derivation and the class's conduct in crises, not its stated opinions | Consciousness must be developed toward the imputed standard, which licenses an educating or leading organisation |
| Experiential (Thompson) | The way a class handles shared experience in cultural and institutional forms: traditions, values, unions, chapels, customs | The class itself, speaking through its historical practice over time | The historical record of institutions built, defended, and transmitted, and of struggles actually fought | Consciousness grows from within experience and organisation; it cannot be delivered ready made from outside |
| Empirical or attitudinal | A measurable distribution of self identification, beliefs, and preferences among a class's members | The surveyed population, whose answers constitute the phenomenon | Survey responses, interviews, voting and membership data | Strategy begins from opinion as found, and persuasion is a matter of addressing existing beliefs |

The models are not three descriptions of one object that could be averaged into a compromise. They disagree about what would count as the presence of class consciousness in a single concrete case. Consider a workforce that strikes with great solidarity over a plant closure, maintains a rich institutional life of clubs and mutual aid, and yet tells survey researchers that it thinks of itself as middle class, distrusts unions in the abstract, and believes its difficulties are caused by foreign competition rather than by the structure of ownership. The attitudinal model records weak class consciousness, and on its own terms it is right. The experiential model records strong class consciousness, embodied in practice and institution, coexisting with a verbal self description that belongs to a different register, and on its terms it too is right. The imputed model records a specific distance between the class's practice, which approaches the rational content of its position in the moment of struggle, and its reflective beliefs, which remain within the reified forms, and it interprets the whole pattern as the normal condition of a class whose consciousness is developing unevenly. Three verdicts, each internally defensible, none reducible to the others. A paragraph that moves between them without signalling the transitions, praising the strike as proof of consciousness, lamenting the survey as proof of its absence, and invoking imputation to reconcile the two, is not synthesising the literature. It is failing to notice that the literature contains an argument.

## The imputation problem: who may speak for a class's mind

This article's namable claim can now be stated in its full form. The imputation problem is the problem that every account of class consciousness must answer, explicitly or by default, the question of who is entitled to say what a class's real interests and real awareness are, and the three available answers generate three incompatible research programmes that are routinely mixed in a single paragraph. The answers correspond to the three models. If the theorist is entitled, then research into class consciousness is primarily theoretical work, the derivation of positions and their implications, and empirical material enters as illustration or as evidence of the gap between the imputed and the actual. If the class itself is entitled, through its experience and practice, then research is historical work, the reconstruction of how a class made itself and what it made of its conditions, and theory enters as a set of questions put to the record. If the respondent is entitled, then research is empirical social science, and theory enters as hypotheses about the distribution and causes of beliefs. None of these programmes is disreputable. Each has produced work of lasting value. The intellectual offence is not choosing one but drifting among them, using the theorist's authority to dismiss inconvenient testimony, the historian's richness to avoid stating a derivation, and the survey's numbers to claim a scientific status that the argument being made has not earned.

The imputation problem has a second layer that is less often noticed. Imputation is not unique to Marxism, and recognising its wider presence clarifies what is and is not objectionable in Lukacs's version. Economists impute preferences to consumers from their purchases and will calmly report that a household's revealed preference contradicts its stated aims. Lawyers impute intentions to corporations and to reasonable persons who exist only as standards. Historians of earlier centuries impute interests to classes and nations in almost every explanatory paragraph they write. In each case a construction is attributed to a collectivity or a stylised agent because the construction organises evidence and supports inference, and nobody supposes that the collectivity has been consulted. Lukacs's imputation differs from these in two respects. It is applied to a class that exists, whose members can speak, and can therefore reply that the attribution is wrong, a possibility that the reasonable person of law never exercises. And it is connected, in the tradition that received it, to organisations claiming a mandate to act on the attribution. A revealed preference attributed to a dead consumer authorises nothing. A consciousness imputed to a living class has been used to authorise a great deal, and the political history traced later in this article, from the theory of the vanguard party to the practice of states that ruled in the proletariat's name, is the history of that authorisation being claimed and contested. The analytic question and the political question must therefore be kept apart with unusual discipline. Whether imputed consciousness is a legitimate theoretical construction is one matter. Whether anyone is licensed to act on it against the expressed will of the class concerned is another, and a writer who answers the first question has not thereby answered the second.

## Gramsci and contradictory consciousness: the middle construction

Between Lukacs's construction and Thompson's rejection stands the account developed by Antonio Gramsci in the prison writings he produced under Mussolini's regime, and it deserves separate treatment because it answers the unevenness problem differently from either. Gramsci observed that the ordinary person holds, in his phrase as usually translated, two theoretical consciousnesses, one implicit in their practical activity, which unites them with their fellows in transforming the world, and one superficially inherited from the past and uncritically absorbed. The worker who cooperates with comrades on the line, who knows exactly which managerial claims are false because the work itself refutes them daily, and who yet repeats at the kitchen table the common sense of the newspapers about the necessity of sacrifice and the danger of foreigners, is not simply in error on Gramsci's account. They are divided, and the division has a structure: the practical consciousness generated by activity is embryonic and fragmentary, while the inherited consciousness is coherent in form because it has been elaborated by institutions, churches, schools, and press over generations, even though its content serves interests other than the worker's own. Hegemony, the concept for which Gramsci is chiefly known and which the companion guide to [Gramsci and hegemony](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/) treats in full, names the organisation of that inherited consciousness, the way a ruling class's conception of the world becomes the common sense of an epoch.

Contradictory consciousness changes the analyst's task. On Lukacs's model the gap between actual and imputed consciousness is measured against a derivation, and the practical problem is how the class can be brought to the standard its position defines. On Gramsci's account there is no single gap, because the material to be worked with is already present inside the divided mind: the practical consciousness implicit in cooperative activity is a beginning that organisation and education can elaborate into coherence, while the inherited conception must be criticised from within rather than denounced from without. The political figures that correspond to the two accounts differ accordingly. Lukacs's theory points toward the party as the bearer of the imputed standard, the organisation that embodies the class's consciousness in its developed form. Gramsci's points toward the organic intellectual, the thinker produced by the class itself, and toward a long war of position in the institutions of civil society where common sense is formed. Neither figure is innocent of the imputation problem. The organic intellectual still claims to elaborate what the class only implicitly knows, and the claim can harden into the same authority Lukacs's party asserted. But the direction of travel is different: elaboration begins from utterances the class actually makes and treats them as evidence to be honoured, whereas imputation in its pure form can treat utterances as symptoms to be discounted. That difference, between honouring testimony as material and discounting it as symptom, is the practical hinge on which the whole debate turns, and it will recur when the strongest objection to the concept is stated in its full force.


## Thompson's counter account: class as a relationship that happens

Edward Palmer Thompson published The Making of the English Working Class in 1963, and its preface contains the most influential counter statement the concept of class consciousness has received. Thompson announces that he does not see class as a structure, or even as a category, but as something which in fact happens in human relationships. Class, on his account, is a historical phenomenon, an active process, and class consciousness is the way in which the experiences generated by that process are handled in cultural terms, embodied in traditions, value systems, ideas, and institutional forms. The book that follows is the demonstration: nine hundred pages on the artisans, weavers, Methodists, radicals, and correspondents of England between the seventeen eighties and the eighteen thirties, showing a class being made, not found, as people discovered in Luddism, in illegal unions, in Owenite halls and Chartist newspapers, new ways of understanding what was happening to them and who was responsible for it. The target of the preface's polemic is named plainly enough in the book's reception: a Marxism, dominant in the period's orthodoxies, that treated class as a thing defined by the sociologist's criteria and then complained that the thing refused to behave as the definition required, or worse, supplied the missing consciousness by theoretical decree and called the decree science.

### What does Thompson mean by class as a relationship that happens?

He means that class is not a box defined by occupation or income into which people are sorted, but an event in relationships over time: people who live through shared conditions together come to feel their interests as common and opposed to others, and articulate that feeling in institutions and traditions.

Thompson's account relocates every element of the problem. Where Lukacs derives consciousness from position, Thompson watches it being produced in experience, and experience is a category he insists on with some passion, since it is the term orthodox theory kept omitting, the middle term between being and consciousness where people actually live. Where Lukacs treats actual beliefs as a lagging indicator of a standard set elsewhere, Thompson treats the beliefs people formed, however theologically framed or constitutionally archaic their language, as the consciousness itself, because there is nothing else for it to be. The English workers who framed their grievances in the language of the free born Englishman, of a lost constitution and violated rights, were not displaying a defective version of the awareness a theorist would impute to them. They were making the political culture available to them do real work, and the historian who corrects their vocabulary from the standpoint of a later theory has, Thompson thought, committed an act of enormous condescension, a word he used with intent and which this article will take up in its proper place. Where the imputed model looks forward, asking how far a class has travelled toward its rational standard, the experiential model looks along, asking what a class made of what befell it, with the materials its history supplied. And where Lukacs's construction is deliberately indifferent to survey evidence, Thompson's is answerable to a different tribunal altogether, the archive, in which consciousness leaves traces in the form of institutions founded, funds subscribed, petitions signed, and customs defended at real cost.

The opposition between the two writers should not be softened into a difference of emphasis, because it is a difference about where consciousness resides and who may certify it. For Lukacs, consciousness in its developed form is a property of a position, accessible to theory, and the class's members may fail to possess what is nonetheless theirs. For Thompson, consciousness is a property of a historical community's lived culture, accessible to historical reconstruction, and a theorist who attributes to the community an awareness it never formed has not discovered a deeper consciousness but invented one. Each writer saw the other's position as the characteristic error of a political tendency. Lukacs wrote against the passive, contemplative Marxism that waited for economic collapse to deliver a revolution that consciousness was supposed to ratify automatically. Thompson wrote against a Stalinised Marxism that had converted imputation into a state doctrine, in which the party's knowledge of the class's interests justified the party's power over the class, indefinitely and without appeal. Both polemics hit their targets. The question the reader is left with is not which writer wins but which model is being used, for what purpose, and with what warrant, in any given argument about class consciousness, including the arguments in this article.

## The empirical challenge: what surveys actually find, and what they cannot show

The empirical challenge to class consciousness is genuine and should be stated without softening. Survey research across the advanced economies repeatedly finds that occupational identity, educational culture, and region organise self description at least as powerfully as any relation to the ownership of productive assets. Large proportions of people whose position is working class by the relational criterion, hired labour without significant property, describe themselves as middle class, or decline class labels altogether, or reserve class language for a group they place below themselves. Identification, where it exists, often attaches to the skilled trades, to public sector professionalism, or to respectability rather than to labour as such. Voting studies add a second layer: the correlation between class position and party choice, robust in the middle decades of the twentieth century in several countries, weakened markedly in later decades as other cleavages, over nation, religion, education, and cultural values, cut across it. A literature of considerable sophistication, associated with the embourgeoisement thesis in its successive forms, has argued that affluence, home ownership, and consumption integrate workers into the existing order at the level of identity itself, so that the absence of class consciousness is not a lag awaiting correction but a finding about what kind of society the advanced economies have become. Any honest treatment of the concept must concede that if class consciousness means what the glossary says it means, its measured presence in the populations where theory most expected it is weak, uneven, and by several indicators declining across the period in which it has been measurable.

### Why does the survey evidence not settle the question?

Because each model predicts a different relation between opinion and consciousness. Surveys measure stated identification and belief, which the attitudinal model takes as the phenomenon itself. The experiential model looks instead at institutions and practice, and the imputed model expects stated belief to diverge from position as a matter of theory.

What the surveys cannot show follows from the models' disagreement. They cannot show that the experiential record is empty, since the same populations that answer questions in individualist terms sustain unions, mutual organisations, and collective action whose existence the surveys do not register as consciousness because the instruments were not built to register it. They cannot show that the imputed model is false, since that model predicts, as a theorem, that psychological consciousness will fall short of the position's rational content under conditions of reification, so that disappointing survey results are exactly what the theory leads one to expect; a theory confirmed by every possible answer is in trouble of a different kind, which the objection section below will press, but the trouble is not refutation by questionnaire. And they cannot substitute for the historical question, which is comparative and dynamic: not whether a population endorses a class label at a moment, but how its members' understanding of their situation changes under the pressure of events, whether a closure, a defeat, a strike, or a war reorganises identity faster and more durably than decades of ordinary time. The survey is a snapshot of the register in which people describe themselves to strangers. Class consciousness, on every model except the attitudinal, partly lives elsewhere: in what people do together when the description is tested, in what they build, and in what they refuse. To treat the snapshot as the whole phenomenon is to have adopted the attitudinal model without announcing it, which is the imputation problem returning through the methodological back door, with the researcher's instrument rather than the theorist's derivation silently deciding what the class's consciousness is allowed to be.

## Class consciousness and false consciousness: neighbours, not synonyms

No pair of terms in this territory is more persistently confused. False consciousness, whose own history is stranger than most summaries admit, since the phrase appears in a letter of Engels rather than as a developed concept in Marx, names a diagnosis: an agent's understanding of their situation is false in ways that serve interests other than their own, and the falsity is socially produced rather than a personal failure of intelligence. Class consciousness, in the imputed model, names the standard against which such a diagnosis could be made specific, the awareness appropriate to the position, relative to which actual awareness can be characterised as partial, distorted, or undeveloped. The relation is therefore asymmetrical. False consciousness is defined by contrast with an implied true counterpart, and Lukacs's construction is one attempt, the most rigorous, to say what that counterpart consists in. Writers who use false consciousness without any account of the standard it falls short of are borrowing Lukacs's machinery while declining to acknowledge the debt, and the borrowing matters because the standard is exactly where the difficulties live. Once the standard is made explicit, the questions follow at once: derived by whom, from which analysis, and with what appeal if the class disputes the derivation? Those are the imputation problem again, and they explain why careful writers in the tradition tend either to develop the theory of imputation openly or to abandon the vocabulary of falsity in favour of accounts, like Gramsci's, in which inherited consciousness is criticised as contradictory and partial rather than condemned as simply false.

### How is class consciousness different from false consciousness?

False consciousness is a diagnosis of error: a socially produced misunderstanding of one's situation that serves other interests. Class consciousness is the positive term, the understanding a class forms, or on the imputed model would form, of its position and interests. One names a falling short; the other names the standard or the achievement.

There is a further asymmetry worth stating, since it governs how the two terms behave in argument. False consciousness is almost always attributed to others, and class consciousness in its flattering form is attributed to one's own side, so that the pair can degenerate into a device for sorting populations into the lucid and the duped with the analyst's allegiance deciding the sort. The degeneration is visible in popular usage far from any theory, in which voters whose choices disappoint the commentator are said to be voting against their interests and to require explanation by manipulation, while voters whose choices please the commentator are credited with clear sighted understanding. Every model in this article condemns that usage. The imputed model condemns it because imputation is a derivation from an analysis of positions, not a compliment paid to agreement. The experiential model condemns it because the historian's task is to reconstruct how people understood their world, not to grade them against the commentator's politics. The attitudinal model condemns it because a preference the researcher dislikes is still data. The discipline the concept demands, in all its forms, is that the analyst's own position be submitted to the same analysis: if consciousness is shaped by situation for everyone else, it is shaped for the theorist too, a point Lukacs built into his account by treating the standpoint of the analyst as itself a problem to be justified rather than a privilege to be assumed.


## Lenin, trade union consciousness, and the question of the party

Any account of this concept must pass through Lenin, because the political form in which imputation became consequential was largely his design. In What Is To Be Done, published in 1902, Lenin argued that the working class, by its own unaided effort, is able to develop only trade union consciousness: the conviction that workers must combine, fight employers over wages and conditions, and press governments for protective laws. Socialist consciousness, the understanding of the system as a whole and of the irreconcilability of class interests at the level of the state, does not arise spontaneously from the economic struggle, and Lenin stated, in a formulation he borrowed from Karl Kautsky, that it must be brought to the workers from without, by an organisation of revolutionaries armed with theory. The pamphlet was a polemic in a specific Russian argument against a tendency that wished to confine the movement to economic demands, and Lenin later cautioned against treating its formulations as a complete philosophy of the matter, but the distinction it drew proved durable because it answered an organisational question every movement faces. If the understanding the movement needs does not generate itself from daily struggle, someone must generate it, preserve it, and teach it, and the institution that does so will claim an authority grounded in its possession of the understanding. The [vanguard party](/2014/04/01/vanguard-party-explained/) is that institution in its developed form, and the theory of the party and the theory of imputed consciousness are two faces of one construction: the party is the organisational bearer of the consciousness the class would have if it grasped its position, and its warrant to lead is exactly the derivation Lukacs later supplied in philosophical form.

Trade union consciousness deserves a fairer description than the polemical label usually gives it. The knowledge embodied in a functioning union, of the employer's margins and methods, of the legal terrain, of the solidarity required to sustain a dispute and the discipline required to settle one, is real knowledge, hard won and collectively held, and on Thompson's model it counts without qualification as class consciousness in formation, the class handling its experience in institutional form. The Leninist distinction is not between knowledge and ignorance but between horizons. Trade union consciousness understands the relation with the employer; its demands, however militant, are addressed to the terms of the wage relation and leave the relation itself intact. What Lenin called socialist consciousness takes the further step of understanding the wage relation as one moment of a total order, political as well as economic, whose transformation is the condition of the class's emancipation rather than an improvement of its terms. Whether that further step is a deepening of the same consciousness or the importation of a different one is precisely the question on which the models divide. The experiential model sees a continuum, in which generalisation is one thing movements sometimes achieve and sometimes do not, with no theoretical guarantee attaching to either outcome. The imputed model sees a threshold, defined by the derivation, which struggle alone does not cross. The historical record contains movements that crossed from the defence of terms to a challenge to the order, and movements of great militancy that never contemplated the crossing, and each model reads the record with profit provided it announces which reading it is giving.

## How class consciousness develops, and what blocks it

Across all three models, a convergent account of development can be assembled, because the models disagree about what consciousness is more than about the conditions that change it. The first condition is concentration and communication, Marx's sack of potatoes problem in reverse: workers gathered in large workplaces, dense districts, and shared institutions acquire the means to discover that their grievances are common, and every historical account, from the factory towns of Thompson's England to the mining valleys and dock districts of later movements, shows organisation arising where daily life itself assembles the class. The second condition is struggle, which functions in the literature less as an expression of consciousness than as its school. A dispute forces questions that routine suppresses: whose account of the firm's finances is true, whether the law is neutral, what other workplaces will do, what the state will permit. Participants regularly describe strikes as the period in which they learned how their society worked, and the learning is durable in a way that instruction rarely is, because it was purchased at cost and tested against events. The third condition is organisation that outlasts the episode: unions, parties, newspapers, clubs, and educational institutions that preserve what a struggle taught and transmit it to members who were not present, converting an event into a tradition. Without such vessels, consciousness formed in a crisis drains away with the crisis, a pattern historians record so often that the founding of durable institutions, rather than any single victory, is usually the correct measure of a movement's maturity. The fourth condition is a language in which the new understanding can be stated: every period's movement thinks in the vocabulary available to it, constitutional, religious, economic, or national, and the work of intellectuals, organic or otherwise, is largely the work of forging and circulating formulations in which experience becomes articulate.

The blockers are the mirror image of the conditions, and naming them precisely matters more than denouncing them. Fragmentation of the labour process and of labour markets divides workers into grades, trades, sectors, and statuses whose immediate interests genuinely differ, so that the discovery of common interest must overcome real divergences and not merely imagined ones. Competition within the class, for jobs, housing, and security, sets members against one another along lines that employers and states have repeatedly learned to deepen. The institutions of the wider society work continuously in the other direction: schools, media, and law present the existing order as natural or as the only practical arrangement, and present collective action by workers as an aberration requiring special explanation, while identical action by firms passes as the ordinary conduct of business. Repression raises the price of organisation itself. Defeat, finally, is a teacher with two lessons, and movements that lose catastrophically often learn the second: that the endeavour is hopeless, a conclusion that can govern a generation's expectations long after the material circumstances that produced it have changed. Lukacs's reification belongs on this list as the deepest blocker in his account, since it operates before any of the others, shaping the categories in which experience is registered at all. But a treatment that reaches for reification first explains nothing in particular. The discipline of the subject is to begin with the specific blockers a case presents, the divisions, institutions, and defeats that can actually be documented, and to invoke the deeper mechanisms only where the specific ones run out.

One more feature of development deserves record because every model observes it and none explains it completely, and that is the role of the exemplary event: the single dispute, trial, or atrocity that condenses a class's situation into a story portable enough to travel. Movements remember their history disproportionately through such condensations, a lockout in one town standing for the employers' offensive in a decade, a prosecuted speaker standing for the state's partiality, a single funeral standing for the price of the whole arrangement. The mechanism is narrative rather than theoretical, since a story can be carried by people who would never read a derivation, and retold in settings, the kitchen, the pub, the chapel anniversary, that no organisation's education programme could reach. Episodes acquire this status only when they answer to a widely held experience, which is why attempts to manufacture them fail so reliably, and why the genuine ones are usually recognised in retrospect, by their survival in a class's memory when the minutes and manifestos around them have been forgotten. The historian reads their survival as evidence of what the class made of its experience; the theorist reads them as the points where imputed and actual consciousness came nearest to meeting; the sociologist notes that they function in interviews as the examples respondents reach for when abstract questions fail. On all three readings, the exemplary event is a real unit of the process, and an account of development that omits it has omitted the form in which consciousness most often changes hands.

## Consciousness in the other classes, and the rivals to class

The concept is usually discussed as a property of the working class, but its logic applies to every class, and applying it symmetrically is a useful test of whether an analyst is theorising or cheering. The bourgeoisie, on Lukacs's account, possesses a class consciousness of great sophistication within its limits: a disciplined understanding of its interests in property, contract, and the stability of the conditions of accumulation, elaborated by its lawyers, economists, and statesmen, and defended by institutions of formidable durability. Its limit is structural rather than intellectual. It cannot think the totality through to the end, because the end of that thought is the recognition of its own historical transience, and so its consciousness, however accomplished, must arrest itself at the point where the existing order is naturalised. Middle strata present the opposite configuration: positions that are ambiguous in the relational structure, small property combined with hired work, credentials combined with dependence, generate consciousness that oscillates, attaching itself now to the classes above and now to those below, and the political volatility of such strata across modern history is unintelligible without the category. Even a class whose members rarely use the word can therefore be analysed for its consciousness, provided the analyst states which model is in use and accepts the discipline the model imposes.

Class is also not the only identity in the field, and the tradition's weaker treatments, which handle national, religious, and racial identification as mere disguises that class consciousness would dissolve if it were strong enough, fail empirically and analytically at once. The identifications are rival sources of solidarity and enmity with their own histories and institutions, and they cross cut class in both directions: national solidarity has dissolved class organisation in wartime with a speed no theory of false consciousness adequately explains, and class organisation has at other times held together workforces divided by language and faith that every prediction said would fragment. The honest treatment regards the relation as an open historical question, settled case by case by evidence about institutions and events, rather than as a doctrinal ranking in which class must always be the real identity and the others epiphenomenal. Gramsci's framework is the most serviceable here, precisely because contradictory consciousness expects a person to hold a class practical consciousness and a national or confessional inherited consciousness simultaneously, and treats the political question as which element organisation succeeds in elaborating. Thompson's framework converges from the other side, since a class making itself in history makes itself out of the cultural materials at hand, which always include chapel, nation, and custom alongside the workshop. The models that handle rivals best are those that never promised that class consciousness, once achieved, would be the only consciousness a person has.


## The strongest objection: imputation as licensed condescension

The objection to the imputed model must now be stated at the full strength its proponents intend, because it is the most serious objection in this entire subject and a treatment that weakens it has understood nothing. The objection has an epistemological edge and a political edge, and they sharpen each other. Epistemologically, imputed consciousness is unfalsifiable in a way that should alarm anyone who wants the theory to be answerable to the world. If workers endorse the imputed content, the theory is confirmed. If they repudiate it, the repudiation is itself predicted, as the expected shortfall of psychological consciousness under reification, and the theory is confirmed again. If they act on it, consciousness is developing; if they act against it for decades, consciousness is blocked. A construction that every possible observation fits is not a discovery about classes but a licence for the theorist, since no testimony from the class itself can ever count against the attribution, and the class has been placed in the position of a patient whose denials are charted as symptoms. Thompson's word for the scholarly version of this was condescension, and his charge was precise: the historian or theorist who corrects the past's vocabulary from the standpoint of a later orthodoxy is not recovering what people thought but replacing it, and the replacement flatters the replacer.

The political edge is worse, because the construction did not remain in books. If a party embodies the imputed consciousness of the class, then disagreement between the party and actual workers is, by the theory's own logic, a disagreement between the class's real consciousness and its merely psychological one, and the party's victory in that disagreement can be represented as the class becoming itself rather than as a minority imposing its will. The substitution of the party for the class, and then of the leadership for the party, and then of a single will for the leadership, is a sequence that critics inside the revolutionary tradition itself mapped early and accurately, and the history of the states that ruled in the proletariat's name supplied the evidence at a scale no polemic could have invented: organisations claiming to incarnate the working class's true consciousness suppressed independent unions, forbade the class's own organisations, and shot strikers, while continuing to describe these acts as the class exercising its rule through its conscious vanguard. The objection concludes that a concept with this entailment is not merely risky but corrupt at the root: any theory that permits a minority to discount the expressed beliefs of millions, on the ground that the minority has derived what those millions would think if they understood themselves, hands that minority an instrument that will be used exactly as it was used, and the intellectual elegance of the derivation is part of the danger rather than a mitigation of it.

### Who is entitled to say what a class really thinks?

On the imputed model, the theorist who derives the rational content of the class's position, even against the class's testimony. On the experiential model, the class itself, in its practice and institutions over time. On the attitudinal model, the class's members, whose stated beliefs are the only consciousness there is.

The reply begins by separating what the objection joins, and the separation is this article's defended position. Imputed consciousness has two uses that share a vocabulary and nothing else. In its analytic use, it is a model of a position: the analyst derives what interests a class has and what understanding would correspond to them, and uses the derivation as a benchmark to describe and explain actual beliefs, exactly as an economist uses a model of rational choice to describe and explain actual decisions without claiming that any consumer performs the calculation. Used this way, the construction is neither unfalsifiable nor arrogant. The derivation itself can be attacked, at the level of the account of the class's position, and rival derivations can be compared by their explanatory yield, by whether the gap they posit illuminates the pattern of actual belief and conduct better than competing accounts, including the null account that takes beliefs at face value and explains them by their holders' reasons. A benchmark does not silence testimony; it is a way of asking why testimony takes the form it does. In its political use, by contrast, imputation becomes a warrant: the derivation is treated as a title to act, the benchmark becomes a mandate, and the organisation holding it claims the authority the objection describes. That use does not follow from the analytic one. It follows only with the addition of a further premise, that the bearer of a correct derivation is entitled to rule those who have not reached it, and that premise is a political claim that must be argued on political grounds, where it faces the whole weight of the democratic objection and the historical record the objectors cite.

The concession the objection is owed must then be stated without evasion, because it is substantial. The tradition has repeatedly slid from the analytic use to the political one, and the slide is not an accidental misuse by lesser minds but a temptation built into the construction itself: a benchmark that describes what millions would think if they understood themselves exerts a gravitational pull toward the claim to speak for those millions, and organisations under pressure, in illegality, war, and power, have rarely resisted the pull for long. Lukacs's own biography is part of the evidence, since the philosopher of imputed consciousness spent his later career accommodating himself to an order that governed in the concept's name. A reader is therefore entitled to a rule of handling, and this article proposes the strictest available: imputed consciousness may be used to explain, never to overrule. The moment an attribution is deployed to set aside what a class's members actually say about their own interests, in a union ballot, a strike vote, or an election, it has left analysis and become the thing the objection condemns, whatever vocabulary it wears. Held to that rule, the concept survives the objection in its analytic form, and the objection stands permanently against its political form, undefeated and, on the historical evidence, undefeatable. That is not a comfortable verdict for any camp, which in this subject is some evidence that it is the right one.

## Consciousness in episodes: what formation looks like when it happens

Because the concept is so often debated in the abstract, it is worth fixing what the formation of class consciousness looks like in the concrete episodes the historical literature knows best, always with the models kept distinct. The pattern that recurs, from the English handloom weavers through the great strike waves of the twentieth century, is not the arrival of a doctrine but the transformation of an interpretation. A grievance that was previously narrated as misfortune, the act of a hard master, or the decree of providence comes to be narrated as policy: the predictable outcome of decisions taken by identifiable interests, which could be taken otherwise and can be resisted collectively. The materials of the new narration are typically local and inherited before they are theoretical: a violated custom, a broken agreement, a customary price or pace treated as a right. Thompson's account of the moral economy of the crowd, developed in his later work on eighteenth century food riots, showed crowds enforcing a legitimating notion of fair prices and just practices with discipline and ritual, an entire popular political economy operating decades before the vocabulary of socialism was available to state it. On the experiential model this is class consciousness in one of its real historical forms, inarticulate only to those who require consciousness to arrive in their own terminology. On the imputed model the same episode registers as an early and partial approach to the standard, its customary limits marking exactly the distance still to travel. The episode does not adjudicate between the models. It disciplines both, by showing each what its abstraction must be able to describe without remainder or contempt.

A second pattern concerns speed. Consciousness, on every model, changes slowly in ordinary time and with startling rapidity in crises, when the routines that reproduce the existing interpretation collapse and events themselves conduct the argument. The literature of strike waves and revolutionary situations is filled with testimony of populations whose settled expectations dissolved in weeks, not because pamphlets improved but because the state's violence, the employer's lockout, or the army's fraternisation rearranged what it was possible to believe. Lukacs built this into his construction: the crisis is the moment at which the imputed and the psychological can converge, because the totality that reification conceals in stable times forces itself into experience. Gramsci built it into his distinction between organic crises and conjunctural movements. Thompson's narrative method shows it at the level of biography, in artisans radicalised by a single prosecution or a single winter of hunger. The convergence of the models on this point is instructive. Whatever consciousness is, it is not a stock that accumulates by steady addition, and any strategy or research design that treats it as one, whether by counting leaflets or averaging survey responses across quiet decades, will miss the phenomenon at exactly the moments when it matters most.


## The standpoint argument examined: can a position confer knowledge

Underneath Lukacs's construction sits an epistemological claim that deserves examination in its own right, because versions of it recur far beyond Marxism. The claim is that social knowledge is standpoint dependent in a principled way: what can be seen about a society depends on where the observer stands within it, not because of bias in the disreputable sense but because each position discloses some relations and conceals others, the way a vantage point discloses one face of a building and conceals the rest. The employer experiences the wage as a cost and the labour market as a supply condition; the worker experiences the same wage as the price of their life activity and the market as a compulsion. Neither experience is a hallucination. Each is a veridical registration of the relation from one side, and a social science that averages them produces not the whole truth but a blur. Lukacs's further claim is the controversial one: that the standpoints are not symmetrical in their epistemic yield, because the worker's position, fully thought through, requires an account of the whole system that produced it, while the employer's position, fully thought through, requires that the system be taken as given, its historically specific forms mistaken for the permanent furniture of economic life. The proletariat's standpoint is privileged not morally but cognitively, in the way the standpoint of the person who must repair a machine is privileged over the standpoint of the person who merely owns it, when the question is how the machine works.

The claim has been developed, criticised, and partially rebuilt by several generations of writers, and its fortunes are a fair index of the concept's health. Feminist theorists of the later twentieth century took up the standpoint structure while revising its content, arguing that the labour of social reproduction, performed largely by women outside the wage relation, discloses aspects of the social order invisible from the standpoint of production alone; the revision both confirms the form of Lukacs's argument and demonstrates that his particular assignment of the privileged standpoint was a choice, contestable on the theory's own principles. Sociologists of knowledge have pressed the opposite worry, that standpoint arguments systematically overclaim, since a position may motivate a question without guaranteeing the answer, and the history of science is littered with standpoints that disclosed grievances vividly and mechanisms not at all. The defensible residue, and the position this article takes, is a distinction between two things the standpoint argument might mean. As a claim about agenda, it is strong: positions determine which facts are salient, which puzzles press, and which explanations feel sufficient, and no analyst writes from nowhere. As a claim about verdict, that thought conducted from the correct standpoint is guaranteed true, it fails, because derivations can be contested only by other derivations and by evidence, and the class whose standpoint is invoked retains the standing to reject the result. The middle position sounds like a weakening, and is better described as the argument's survival in the only form in which it can be held by someone who also accepts the objection stated earlier: standpoint explains why the imputed content takes the shape it does, and confers no authority on whoever states it.

## Class consciousness and ideology: the wider atmosphere

Class consciousness is one concept inside a larger theory of how societies think, and placing it in that theory prevents two common errors. The larger theory, whose full treatment belongs to the study of ideology in this series, begins from the observation that the ideas circulating in a society are not a random sample of the ideas its members might have formed. They are produced by institutions with owners and purposes, filtered by what can be said in the available media, and stabilised by their fit with the routines of daily life, so that the conception of the world a person absorbs uncritically is usually the conception elaborated by the classes whose position the existing order serves. Marx's own formulation, in the joint work with Engels known as The German Ideology, written in the eighteen forties and published only long after, is that the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, since the class that controls material production controls the production of ideas as well. Class consciousness, against that background, is the name for the process by which a subordinate class ceases to think entirely in the ruling ideas and produces an account of the world from its own experience: its own press, its own intellectuals, its own institutions for forming and testing interpretations. The concept therefore has an institutional dimension that purely psychological treatments miss. A class's consciousness is partly stored outside heads, in the newspapers it reads, the songs it sings, the precedents its unions cite, and the heroes and traitors of its remembered history, and it can be damaged by destroying the storage as surely as by persuading the individuals.

This placement corrects the first error, which is to treat class consciousness as an individual mental property that happens to be widely shared, like a taste or a mood. On the institutional account, two populations with identical private opinions may differ entirely in class consciousness, because one possesses the organisations in which opinions are compared, corrected, generalised, and converted into a common line, while the other holds its opinions in isolation, where each person's grievance remains a private trouble and dies with the conversation that expressed it. The difference explains a pattern that purely attitudinal research finds paradoxical, the speed with which apparently quiescent workforces can act coherently when an organisation exists through which a shared interpretation can form, and the equal speed with which enraged populations without such vessels dissipate into gesture. It also corrects the second error, which is the mirror image: treating class consciousness as a free floating doctrine, manufactured by theorists and injected into passive material. Doctrines that find no answering experience do not take, as the history of sects demonstrates at exhausting length, while interpretations that name what people are already living through can spread faster than any apparatus could carry them. The relation between organisation and experience is reciprocal throughout: experience without organisation remains fragmentary, organisation without experience becomes a shell reciting formulas, and the tradition's most useful concept, Gramsci's elaboration of the practical consciousness already implicit in activity, is precisely the name for the work done at the junction between them.

## Whose experience counts: the boundaries of the class

A further question, pressed by critics the tradition initially ignored, asks whose experience the concept of class consciousness silently takes as the class's experience. The canonical scenes of formation are masculine and industrial: the pit, the mill, the dock, the picket line. Yet the reproduction of the working class runs through households, neighbourhoods, and communities in which women performed the unpaid labour that made waged labour possible, and the historical record, when historians learned to read it for this purpose, shows class consciousness being formed and defended in those settings as surely as at the point of production: in rent strikes organised from tenement stairwells, in cooperative housekeeping and communal kitchens during disputes, in the consumer boycotts and price protests whose enforcement was community work, and in the transmission to children of the movement's memory and enmities. A concept of class consciousness calibrated only to the workplace will systematically under record the consciousness of the class it claims to describe, and will misread disputes in which the community, rather than the union branch, is the acting subject. The correction does not dissolve the concept's analytic core, since the imputation problem and the three models apply unchanged to the wider field, but it changes the evidence each model must consult: the experiential historian must read the tenement as carefully as the trade journal, and the attitudinal researcher must ask who maintains a dispute's morale and memory, and not only who holds its formal memberships.

The same discipline applies at the boundaries drawn by race and migration, where the historical record resists every simple formula. Employers and states have repeatedly organised labour markets so that the newest and most vulnerable workers are set in visible competition with the established, and the resulting divisions are material facts about the class's structure, not failures of anyone's enlightenment. Consciousness that forms across such divisions, when it forms, is an achievement with identifiable conditions: common institutions that refuse the segmentation, struggles whose demands are framed to raise the floor for all rather than to protect a section, and leaderships drawn from across the divide. Consciousness that fails to form is equally explicable in the same terms, and the analyst who reaches directly for false consciousness to explain the failure has skipped the analysis the concept exists to provoke. The general lesson is the one this article has drawn at each stage. Class consciousness is not a substance that classes have in quantities. It is a set of processes, institutional, experiential, and theoretical, whose presence and direction must be established case by case, with the model in use declared and the evidence appropriate to that model actually produced.


## Organisation, education, and the press: the machinery of formation

If consciousness is partly stored in institutions, then the institutions deserve the same analytical attention as the ideas, and the historical literature supplies a detailed inventory of the machinery by which classes have formed and held an interpretation of their situation. The workers' press is the oldest item in the inventory and in many periods the most important. A movement newspaper does more than report: it selects which events count as the class's events, connects a dispute in one town to the pattern it belongs to, prints the correspondence in which ordinary members test formulations against one another, and maintains, in its file of back numbers, the precedents by which the present is judged. Thompson's England ran on such papers, produced in defiance of stamp duties designed to price them out of working class hands, and the ferocity of the state's campaign against the unstamped press is itself evidence of what contemporaries thought the machinery was worth. Educational institutions form the second item: the reading rooms, mechanics' institutes captured or founded, socialist Sunday schools, labour colleges, and study circles in which a class trained its own interpreters of the world, and in which the boundary between the theorist and the class was at its most porous, since the lecturers were frequently weavers, miners, and engineers teaching their workmates after shifts. The third item is ritual and memory: anniversaries of martyrs and victories, banners carried and preserved at real expense, songs whose verses encode a history, funerals conducted as demonstrations. Ritual is easily dismissed as decoration on the real business of organisation, and the dismissal is an error, since it is through ritual that a class's consciousness is transmitted to members too young to have lived its forming events, and that the costs of membership are converted into identity.

The machinery can also be turned off, captured, or allowed to decay, and the concept of class consciousness earns its keep partly by making those processes visible as a single phenomenon. A press bought by interests outside the class does not merely change its opinions; it changes which events its readers experience as shared. An educational institution that passes under the control of a hostile administration changes what the next generation of the class's interpreters will find thinkable. A union whose internal life atrophies, whose meetings empty and whose journal becomes a bulletin of services, may retain every formal attribute of the class's organisation while ceasing to perform the function that made it a vessel of consciousness, the comparison and generalisation of members' experience. Analysts who measure class consciousness by membership totals alone will miss the hollowing entirely, since the shell persists after the function lapses; analysts who measure it by doctrine alone will miss it from the other side, crediting a fluent official vocabulary that no longer answers to anything members live. The experiential model, with its attention to institutions as they actually function, is best equipped to register the change, and this is one of the points at which the models stop being rivals for a definition and become instruments to be chosen, case by case, for the question actually being asked.

Set against the machinery of formation stands the machinery of the wider society, and symmetry requires that it be described with the same institutional concreteness rather than as a vapour of ideology. Commercial media select and frame by criteria of ownership and audience that are documented and analysable. School curricula settle, through processes that are political and occasionally contested in public, which history the class's children will receive as the national story and which they will never meet. The law defines the boundary between legitimate combination and conspiracy, between a picket and an obstruction, and thereby teaches, with sanctions attached, a lesson about which collective actions are normal and which are deviant. None of this requires a conspiracy, and Gramsci's account is valuable precisely because it does not: hegemony is exercised through the ordinary operations of institutions doing what their position disposes them to do. Class consciousness forms against this standing machinery or not at all, which is why its formation is historically rare, uneven, and reversible, and why the tradition's serious writers treated its appearance as an achievement to be explained rather than a default to be assumed.

## How historians judge a past class's consciousness

The experiential model obliges its users to answer a methodological question that the other models can postpone: by what evidence does a historian, working after the fact, judge that a class in a past period possessed consciousness of a given kind and degree? The question matters beyond the seminar, because confident historical claims about the consciousness of past classes pervade political argument, usually with the evidence left unexamined. The practised answers form a hierarchy of kinds of trace. At the strongest end stand costly actions sustained over time: strikes maintained against hunger, funds subscribed from wages that could not spare them, illegal organisations rebuilt after each suppression, since conduct that costs the actor dearly is difficult to explain except by a shared understanding that the cause is common and the sacrifice rational within it. Next stand institutions and their internal records, the minute books, rule books, and correspondence in which a class's members stated their purposes to one another rather than to posterity, evidence whose value lies in its freedom from the retrospective shaping that memoirs impose. Then come the public texts, the pamphlets, newspapers, trial speeches, and songs, read not as transparent statements of belief but as interventions in arguments the historian must reconstruct, since a speaker's categories are chosen for an audience and an occasion. Weakest, and most abused, are the retrospective testimonies and the records of opponents, police reports and employers' correspondence, which are indispensable for facts and systematically misleading about motives, crediting conspiracy where the participants experienced solidarity and fanaticism where they experienced duty.

Two disciplines govern the use of all four kinds of trace. The first is the discipline of the actors' categories: the historian must establish what the people concerned could have meant by their words, which forbids translating a defence of customary rights into a claim about socialism, or a religious idiom into a mere code for economic interest, without showing the translation at work in the evidence. Thompson's severity on this point was methodological before it was moral, since a translation applied from outside destroys precisely the information the record contains, namely how experience was actually handled by those who lived it. The second discipline is the discipline of the counterfactual: a claim that consciousness caused an outcome must survive the question of what the same people, with the same material pressures and a demonstrably different understanding, would have done, and where the record contains comparable groups whose understandings did differ, the comparison is the closest the subject comes to an experiment. Historians who observe both disciplines can disagree productively, because their disagreements locate themselves in evidence. Those who dispense with them produce the literature of assertion, in which past classes are found to have possessed exactly the consciousness the writer's politics requires them to have possessed, an imputation practised on the dead, who are the one class that can never reply.


## The affluent worker studies: the attitudinal model at its best

The empirical model of class consciousness has its own classic literature, and its most instructive episode is the study of affluent workers conducted in Britain in the nineteen sixties by John Goldthorpe, David Lockwood, and their collaborators. The research began from the embourgeoisement thesis, then widely canvassed, which held that well paid manual workers in new industries were becoming middle class in outlook, identification, and politics, so that the working class as a conscious collectivity was dissolving from within. The investigators chose prosperous manufacturing towns and interviewed workers whose incomes overlapped with those of the white collar employees against whom they were compared, exactly the population in which embourgeoisement should have been furthest advanced. What they found was more interesting than either the thesis or its simple denial. The affluent workers had not become middle class in identification or sociability; their friendships, leisure, and self descriptions remained within the working class world, and their union membership was high. But the meaning of their collectivism differed from the traditional pattern the researchers had documented in older industrial communities. Their orientation to work was instrumental: the job was a means to a standard of life centred on the home and family, the union was valued as an instrument for defending pay and conditions rather than as the institutional heart of a way of life, and solidarity was real but calculative, extended to workmates as fellow parties to a common bargain rather than as members of a community of fate. The investigators described a privatised worker, home centred and instrumentally collectivist, whose class consciousness was genuine within its register and limited in its horizons in ways that neither the embourgeoisement thesis nor the older image of the traditional worker predicted.

The episode is a model of what the attitudinal method can do when it is practised with theoretical care, and it also displays the method's limits with unusual clarity. The findings corrected a confident public thesis by evidence, which is the method's glory. But the classification of the findings as a kind or degree of class consciousness depended, then as always, on a standard imported from outside the data: instrumental collectivism counted as a limited form of consciousness because the analysts measured it against a fuller form, communal and solidaristic, drawn from the historical literature on older working class communities, which is to say from the experiential model's archive. The attitudinal programme, at the moment of its greatest rigour, thus quietly employed a benchmark of exactly the kind the imputed model makes explicit, differing from Lukacs's chiefly in that its standard was historical rather than derived. The lesson generalises, and it is one of this article's recurring findings. Every serious use of the concept employs some standard of what developed class consciousness would look like, against which the case in hand is judged. The intellectual choice is not between having a standard and not having one. It is between stating the standard openly, with its source and its warrant, and smuggling it in through the design of a questionnaire or the periodisation of a narrative. Lukacs stated his. That candour, whatever else is said about the construction, is the quality the whole subsequent discussion has struggled to match.

## Formation under fragmented conditions: services, dispersion, and the new sack of potatoes

The concept's hardest testing ground is the structure of employment in economies where the large factory, the classic site of formation, employs a shrinking minority and where work is dispersed across small units, subcontracted chains, domestic premises, and platforms that assemble labour without assembling workers. The analytical question is precise: which of the conditions of formation identified earlier survive the change, and which must be rebuilt in new forms or lost? Concentration, the first condition, plainly weakens where a workforce shares an employer but never a workplace, or shares a workplace but not an employer, and the sack of potatoes problem returns in a modern form, since workers connected only through an allocation system confront their common counterparty one at a time, each in ignorance of the others' experience. Communication, which the older movements built through the press and the meeting, is transformed rather than abolished by digital means: the same channels that fragment attention also permit dispersed workers to compare terms, discover that a grievance is general, and coordinate stoppages across sites that have never exchanged a delegate, and the empirical record of organising among couriers, warehouse staff, and care workers shows both the new machinery working and its fragility, since channels owned by the counterparty can be monitored or closed in ways the unstamped press, once printed, could not.

Struggle and organisation, the second and third conditions, show the same double character. Disputes in fragmented sectors repeatedly display the educational velocity the literature leads one to expect, with participants generalising from a single grievance to the structure of the allocation system within days, because the system's rules are experienced directly and their common authorship is visible. What is scarce is the vessel: organisations capable of surviving the dispute, holding its lessons, and defending its participants against retaliation distributed invisibly through future allocations. The fourth condition, a language, is being contested in real time, as legal categories of employee, worker, and independent contractor decide in advance which collective descriptions are even available to be thought with, a reminder that the infrastructure of consciousness includes the classifications of the law and not only the sentiments of the class. None of this requires the analyst to declare the concept obsolete or vindicated. It requires the older discipline: to specify which conditions of formation a given workforce possesses, which it lacks, and what follows for the consciousness that can be expected of it, with the models kept apart and the evidence matched to the model in use. A workforce atomised by design that nonetheless builds durable organisation has demonstrated more, on any model, than a concentrated workforce that merely inherits it, and the comparison, conducted honestly, is the kind of judgement the concept exists to make possible.


## The dimensions of consciousness: identity, opposition, totality, and an alternative

One reason discussions of class consciousness generate more heat than agreement is that the phrase bundles several distinguishable achievements, which can be present or absent independently, and a scheme associated with the sociologist Michael Mann offers the most serviceable unbundling. The first element is class identity: the conception of oneself as a member of a class, sharing its situation. The second is class opposition: the perception that the class's interests conflict with those of another class, and that the conflict is structural rather than a matter of individual friction. The third is class totality: the acceptance of identity and opposition as the defining features of one's whole social situation, the lens through which society as such is interpreted. The fourth, which Mann treats as the completion of the sequence, is the conception of an alternative: a picture of a social order organised on different principles, toward which the class's action could be directed. The scheme's value is that it converts a global verdict, conscious or not conscious, into a profile. A workforce may score high on identity and opposition while possessing no conception of an alternative beyond a fairer version of the present arrangement, which describes the condition Lenin called trade union consciousness with more precision than his polemic supplied. A population may hold an alternative in the abstract, inherited from a tradition, while its lived identity attaches elsewhere, a profile visible in periods of decline when the vocabulary of a movement outlives the experience that sustained it. And the elements need not develop in sequence: crises can produce totality and alternative in a rush among people whose settled identity, measured in quiet times, was weak, which reconciles the survey record with the record of sudden movements without forcing either into the other's framework.

The scheme also exposes, by contrast, what is distinctive in each of the three models. The attitudinal programme can in principle measure the first two elements and gesture at the third, since they concern states of belief that respondents can report, though the fourth element, an alternative social order, strains the questionnaire form, since a respondent's ability to endorse a described alternative tells little about whether the alternative functions in their practical reasoning. The experiential programme reads all four elements off the historical record where they exist, and is at its most persuasive on identity and opposition, which leave dense institutional traces, and at its most conjectural on the alternative, which movements often possessed in fragments, a cooperative commonwealth imagined in a hymn, a constitution, or a remembered golden age rather than in a programme. The imputed programme, alone of the three, assigns all four elements by derivation and treats their absence in actual minds as the normal case to be explained, which is its strength as theory and its vulnerability as politics, for the reasons the objection section established. A reader equipped with the profile can perform a service the literature perpetually needs: whenever a writer announces that class consciousness is rising, falling, present, or absent, the reader can ask which element is meant, on which model, and on what evidence, and will find that a large share of published pronouncements cannot answer any of the three questions.

## Teachers of the class: intellectuals, education, and the authority question restated

Every model must also give an account of the people who work on consciousness professionally, since a theory that cannot place its own producers is incomplete in a way its rivals will notice. The tradition's most durable category here is Gramsci's distinction between traditional and organic intellectuals. Traditional intellectuals, the clergy, academics, and men of letters who present themselves as an autonomous caste standing above the social struggle, are in Gramsci's analysis the deposits of earlier social formations, carrying into the present the authority of orders that have vanished or been absorbed. Organic intellectuals are produced by a class itself as it organises: the technician and manager thrown up by industry, and on the other side the union educator, the movement journalist, the shop steward who becomes a theorist of the workplace, and the party organiser whose function is to elaborate the class's practical consciousness into coherence. The distinction matters for this article because it restates the imputation problem at the level of personnel. The organic intellectual's authority, in the ideal form of the concept, is internal and answerable: it arises from the class's own activity, is exercised in forums the class controls, and lapses when the elaboration ceases to answer to the experience it claims to elaborate. The traditional intellectual's authority is external and self certifying. The history of movements supplies both figures in abundance, and supplies also the hybrid that causes the trouble: the organic intellectual who, carried upward by an organisation's success, acquires the habits and immunities of the traditional caste while continuing to speak in the name of the class that produced him. A theory of class consciousness that includes a theory of its own teachers, and of their characteristic corruption, is not a refinement of the subject. It is the subject, since the question of who may speak for a class's mind is finally a question about identifiable people in identifiable institutions, and about the presence or absence of the mechanisms by which the class can talk back.

Education in the formal sense enters the same analysis from the other side. A class that must form its consciousness against the standing machinery of the wider society confronts in the school the most universal of those machines, and the literature records every possible relation between the two: schools experienced as alien territory whose certificates sort children for their stations, adult education movements built precisely to contest that sorting, and the recurring hope, renewed in each generation, that the children of the class educated within the system will return as its interpreters rather than depart as its emigrants. The evidence resists the hope and the despair in equal measure, which is what makes the question empirical rather than doctrinal. What can be said at the level of the concept is only this: consciousness, on any of the three models, is not transmitted by information alone, since each model locates its formation in a practice, whether the derivation tested in argument, the experience handled in institutions, or the identification rehearsed in daily life. Teaching that ignores the practice it addresses decorates the mind without altering the consciousness, a distinction every honest educator in the tradition has had to learn from the receiving end.


## One workforce, three readings: a worked comparison

Abstract comparison sharpens when it is forced through a single case, so consider a case assembled from the common features of many documented disputes rather than copied from any one of them. A components plant employing several hundred people, the largest employer in a small town, announces closure on grounds of cost, with production to move to a lower wage site abroad. The workforce, mixed in age and length of service, contains a union organisation of long standing but modest recent activity. Over the following months the town sees a campaign: mass meetings, a march, an occupation of the plant for some weeks, support committees drawing in shopkeepers and clergy, and a delegation received, without result, by the relevant ministry. The closure proceeds. The plant is demolished; some workers find inferior employment, some retire early, some leave the town. Surveys conducted during the campaign by a regional newspaper find that most respondents blame foreign competition and government indifference, that a minority blame the owning group by name, and that almost none describe the event in the vocabulary of class. Union membership in the town rises during the campaign and falls below its former level within three years. A brass band funded by the union branch in the plant's prosperous years still plays at the commemoration held each year on the anniversary of the occupation.

The attitudinal reading is the most straightforward, and it is not wrong. Measured by stated belief, class consciousness in the strong sense was weak: the dominant attributions were national and governmental rather than relational, the owners' property rights were rarely questioned as such, and the campaign's own slogans concerned fairness, community, and betrayal rather than the wage relation. A researcher confined to this model would report a population with high solidarity in conduct and low class consciousness in belief, and would probably add, correctly, that the solidarity was defensive and local, extended to the town rather than to the class as a whole. The experiential reading starts from the other end. A class fraction, over months, built institutions at speed, discovered in practice which institutions of the wider society were open to it and which were not, maintained an occupation that required continuous collective discipline, and created a commemoration that still transmits the episode's meaning to people who were children when it happened. On Thompson's criteria this is consciousness being made in the only way it ever is, through experience handled in cultural and institutional forms, and the fact that its language was the language of community and fairness rather than of the seminar is, for this model, a fact about the materials at hand and not a discount on the achievement. The reading also notes what the attitudinal snapshot misses about time: the consciousness formed in the campaign did not survive the demolition in organisational form, since the union branch withered with the plant, but it survived as memory and disposition, the reserve on which any future mobilisation in the town would draw.

The imputed reading asks the question neither of the others asks: what understanding corresponded to the workforce's position, and how far did the campaign travel toward it? The derivation is not difficult to state. The closure was a decision about the allocation of capital, taken by owners whose property allowed them to treat the plant as a quantity of value to be moved, against a workforce whose members could not move their lives with comparable freedom; the asymmetry is the class relation itself, visible in an unusually pure form. An understanding adequate to the position would therefore identify the property relation, not the nationality of the competing site or the disposition of a ministry, as the operative cause, and would draw the conclusion the derivation supports about where a remedy would have to act. Measured against that standard, the campaign travelled part of the distance: the occupation asserted, in action, a claim that the plant was in some sense the workforce's, an intuition of enormous theoretical weight expressed in the idiom of fairness, while the reflective account remained at the level of betrayal and competition. The imputed model names this configuration precisely, an advanced practical consciousness within an undeveloped reflective one, and Gramsci's category of contradiction describes the same configuration from inside. What the case demonstrates, and the reason for dwelling on it, is that the three readings are not competitors for a single fact. They are answers to three questions, about belief, about practice, and about position, and the analyst's obligation is to know which question is being answered at each step, especially when the answers are combined into a judgement about strategy, where confusing the practice's strength with the belief's content, or either with the position's implications, produces the characteristic errors of movements and of their commentators alike.

## The tradition examines itself: recantation, reception, and the long argument

No treatment of this concept is complete without the strange afterlife of the book that founded it, because the afterlife is itself evidence in the argument about authority. History and Class Consciousness was condemned by the Communist International's leadership within a year of its appearance, at the organisation's Fifth Congress in 1924, where its Hegelian vocabulary and its treatment of consciousness were attacked as idealist deviations from a materialism that preferred its causation economic and its philosophy settled. Lukacs, whose position in exile politics depended on the movement that was condemning his book, retreated in print, and across the following decades, in Moscow and after his return to Hungary, he repeatedly accommodated his stated views to the requirements of survival and standing, disowning the work that later generations would regard as his masterpiece. The manuscript in which he had defended the book's argument against its first critics, written in the middle nineteen twenties, remained unpublished in his lifetime and appeared only after his death, a sequence that gives the debate about imputation a biographical edge it would be dishonest to soften: the theorist of the class's true consciousness spent much of his career publicly affirming judgments he is widely believed not to have held, under pressure from an organisation that claimed to embody the consciousness he had theorised. Readers may draw different morals. That the theory was too weak to protect its author is one. That the political use of imputation corrupted everyone it touched, beginning with its inventor, is another, and it is the moral the objection section of this article has already drawn in general form.

The book's second life began when the political conditions of its first were gone. Republished in the nineteen sixties and translated widely, it became a founding text of what came to be called Western Marxism, read by students and scholars who valued precisely what the original condemnation had targeted: its insistence that consciousness is not a reflex of economics but a terrain on which history is decided. Lukacs himself, in a preface written for the reissue late in his life, judged the book severely even while authorising its republication, criticising its attempt to outdo Hegel, its residual idealism, and its treatment of the proletariat as the identical subject and object of history, which he came to regard as a metaphysical construction rather than a finding. The spectacle is unusual and instructive: the author applying to his own text, in public, the distinction this article has pressed throughout, between a construction's analytic yield and the claims made in its name, and concluding that the yield survived while the grandest claim did not. Thompson's generation received the book through this second life, and The Making of the English Working Class can be read as the empiricist answer to it, written by a historian who shared Lukacs's conviction that consciousness decides history and rejected, root and branch, his account of where consciousness lives. The argument between the book of 1923 and the book of 1963 is therefore not an episode in the concept's history that a student may skip. It is the concept's history in its essential form, a derivation and an archive confronting one another across forty years, with a revolution, a world war, and the experience of states ruling in the class's name lying between them, and every position taken on class consciousness since has been taken, knowingly or not, inside the space those two books define.

## The problem of quiescence: explaining the consciousness that does not form

A theory of class consciousness is tested as severely by the cases in which nothing happens as by the movements it was built to explain, and the literature's most famous question concerns a non event. In 1906 the German sociologist Werner Sombart published an inquiry into why there was no socialism in the United States, meaning no mass socialist movement of the European type in the country where capitalism was most advanced and the industrial working class already immense. His answers ranged across high wages and cheap food, the openness of the political system to working class voters before industrialisation had hardened class lines, the availability of land and mobility, and the two party structure's absorption of protest, and the question he posed has organised a century of comparative research. The case matters here because each answer presupposes a model of consciousness. Explanations from wages and mobility treat consciousness as a function of grievance levels, an assumption the attitudinal model makes explicit and can test. Explanations from political institutions treat consciousness as formed in the encounter with the state, which is an experiential claim about where the handling of experience occurs. Explanations from the absence of a feudal past, the most durable of the family, argue that American workers never confronted a society visibly organised as an order of ranks, and so lacked the experience against which a total conception of class could define itself, a thoroughly Thompsonian argument deployed before Thompson. The quiescence literature is therefore not an embarrassment external to the concept. It is the concept working comparatively, and its best findings are conditional statements of exactly the kind this article has recommended: given these institutions, these divisions within the class, and these channels for grievance, this profile of consciousness, element by element, is what formed.

The comparative discipline also protects the concept against its defenders. A tradition that explains every quiescence by blockage risks the unfalsifiability the objection identified, since consciousness eternally on the point of forming, and eternally prevented, is indistinguishable from consciousness that was never a tendency of the situation at all. The defence against the charge is to specify, in advance of the evidence, what the unblocked formation would look like and over what period its absence should count against the theory: the institutions that would have to exist, the episodes that would have to generalise, the profile on the dimensional scheme that would have to shift. Where decades pass with the conditions present and the profile unmoved, the honest conclusion is that the derivation of interests, or the account of the class, or both, require revision, and the history of the subject contains honourable examples of exactly such revision, including Lukacs's own late judgment on his construction. Quiescence, handled this way, is not the concept's graveyard but its laboratory, since a theory of how awareness forms that cannot say when it will not form has not yet become a theory.

One further phenomenon belongs to the study of quiescence, because it inverts the usual picture of blockage. Consciousness can be demobilised as well as never mobilised, and the mechanisms of demobilisation are as concrete as those of formation. Defeat disperses the institutions in which consciousness was stored, and the generation that lived the defeat transmits caution as its chief inheritance. Prosperity reorganises daily life around private consumption and the household, withdrawing the time and the venues in which collective interpretation was practised, a process the affluent worker studies caught in the act. Legal and administrative change can dissolve the categories in which a class recognised itself, reclassifying its members as consumers, taxpayers, or individual contractors, and a class that cannot find itself in the public vocabulary will struggle to find itself in fact. And movements can be demobilised by their own victories, where the gains of one period are institutionalised as services delivered to passive members, converting an organisation of participation into an apparatus of provision and its members into clients. The last mechanism deserves emphasis because it implicates the vessels of consciousness themselves in its decay, and because it is invisible to every measure that counts membership or votes while ignoring the internal life through which consciousness, on the experiential model at least, actually consists. The workforce that retains its union, its party, and its vote, and has lost the practice of interpreting its situation in common, presents the analyst with the concept's subtlest case: all the forms of class consciousness present, and the thing itself, on inspection, gone. Only a theory that locates consciousness in practice rather than in paraphernalia can see the case at all, and seeing it is among the strongest arguments that the experiential relocation of the concept, whatever its costs, purchased something real.

## Reification in daily life: how the gap is produced

The claim that actual consciousness falls short of imputed consciousness is empty unless the mechanism of the shortfall can be described in the texture of ordinary experience, and Lukacs's account of reification is the tradition's most sustained attempt at that description. Its starting point is the commodity form's expansion until it becomes the universal structuring principle of society, so that every relation is increasingly mediated by exchange and appears to those inside it as a relation between things with properties of their own. The labour market confronts the worker as an objective environment with a going rate, like the weather, rather than as the aggregate outcome of decisions by employers and workers that could be taken in concert and otherwise. The wage appears as the price of labour, a natural magnitude attached to the job, concealing the division of the day into paid and unpaid portions that the theory of surplus value reconstructs. Time itself is reified in the account: the working day is experienced as a sequence of measurable units to be filled and sold, space and time homogenised into the grid across which labour power moves as one commodity among others. And the worker's stance toward the process becomes contemplative, in Lukacs's technical sense: the process runs according to laws the worker can learn to predict and adapt to, as one adapts to a climate, but which present themselves as unalterable by the activity they govern, although that activity is their entire substance. The gap between position and perception is therefore not a failure of attention or intelligence. It is the faithful registration of a world that genuinely presents itself in thing like forms, and any consciousness that begins from experience, as every consciousness must, begins from material that misleads in systematic directions.

The description has not stood still since 1923, and its most convincing extensions concern the forms in which a later capitalism presents social relations to those who live them. The career is one such form: a biography narrated as the unfolding of individual merit through a sequence of positions, in which the structure that supplies the positions, rations them, and sets their rewards disappears behind the story of the person who traversed them, so that success and failure alike are registered as personal attributes and the class pattern they compose becomes statistically visible and experientially invisible at once. The market as reported in the daily news is another: an entity that rises, falls, worries, and approves, endowed with moods to which governments and populations must adapt, although the entity is nothing but the aggregate of transactions by identifiable actors pursuing identifiable interests. Human resources vocabulary supplies a third: the conversion of the employment relation into a language of talent, fit, growth, and separation, in which every structural feature of the relation reappears as a fact about individuals, their development, their attitudes, their resilience. None of these forms requires anyone's intention to deceive, and that is the point the concept of reification exists to make. A society does not need propagandists where its ordinary operations generate the misleading appearance as a by product, and class consciousness, on the imputed model, is the name for the arduous process by which the appearance is penetrated and the relations it conceals are recovered as relations, made by people and therefore open to being remade.

The experiential model has its own version of the same insight, reached from the opposite bank. Thompson's workers did not begin from a blank: they began from the customary and legal consciousness of their world, the belief that prices ought to be fair, that agreements ought to be kept, that the constitution protected them, and the making of their class consciousness consisted not in exchanging false ideas for true ones delivered from outside but in discovering, through the repeated frustration of those expectations, that the world did not work as its own professions said it did. The gap, on this account, is between a society's professed norms and its operative ones, and experience is the instrument that detects it. The two accounts converge on a proposition stronger than either's polemic usually admits: that the ordinary consciousness of a class is not an arbitrary error but a structured response to a structured appearance, whether the appearance is the reified form of the commodity or the broken promise of the paternalist order. Any politics or pedagogy built on the concept must therefore treat the beliefs it hopes to change as evidence about the world that produced them, and not merely as obstacles in the minds that hold them. That conclusion, reached independently by the concept's two opposed founders, may be the most durable finding in this entire field, and it is the appropriate note on which to turn from the models to the verdicts they support.

## Inheritance and interruption: consciousness across generations

Class consciousness, on the experiential model, is among the things a generation hands to the next, and the mechanics of the handover explain patterns that purely structural accounts find puzzling. The transmission runs through families before it runs through organisations: through the stories told of a grandfather's strike and a grandmother's picket, through the enmities and loyalties absorbed with the language itself, through the district in which the union hall, the club, and the chapel of a particular allegiance stand within a short walk of one another. Where industry, district, and family coincide across generations, a class's consciousness acquires the density of a culture, reproducing itself with little deliberate effort, and movements in such settings display a depth that astonishes outside observers, holding through defeats that dissolve less rooted organisations in a season. The obverse is equally documented. When an industry closes or a district is dispersed by redevelopment and migration, the chain of transmission breaks at every link at once: the workplace that assembled the class disappears, the institutions lose their constituency, and the stories lose the setting that made them intelligible, so that the grandchildren of a militant generation may inherit the vocabulary of class as a family heirloom without any of the experience that once filled it. Analysts who measure only stated belief will record this as a decline in consciousness and will be correct, but the experiential account explains what the measurement cannot, which is that nothing in the descendants has been argued out of anything. The vessel broke, and the contents went with it.

The interruption cases also refine the imputed model in a way its founder would have recognised. A derived consciousness presupposes a class whose position can be derived, and where the material basis of a class is dissolving, the derivation itself becomes unstable: the imputed content of a position that will not exist in twenty years points in two directions at once, toward the defence of the position and toward the interests of the dispersed in their new locations, and movements in declining industries characteristically split on exactly that line, between those who fight for the pit, the mill, or the yard as the class's home and those who carry its traditions into new employment and rebuild them there. Neither faction is the other's dupe, and a theory that must classify one as the true consciousness has mistaken the situation. The dimensional scheme handles the case better: identity and opposition may remain at full strength in both factions while the conception of an alternative diverges, since the alternative each imagines is anchored in a different future for the class itself. The lesson generalises beyond decline. Consciousness is always partly an inheritance, carried in institutions and stories that predate every living member's experience, and always partly a construction, rebuilt by each generation out of what it lives through, and the proportion between the inherited and the constructed is itself a variable that distinguishes periods of stability, when inheritance dominates and consciousness appears as common sense, from periods of upheaval, when construction dominates and consciousness appears as discovery. A complete account of any class's mind must therefore be a generational account, and the historian who finds in the record only the ideas of a single moment has read a palimpsest as though it were a page.

## Misreadings that recur, named and corrected

Five misreadings account for most of the confusion in popular treatments, and each can now be corrected in a sentence or two. The first attributes the developed concept to Marx: Marx supplied the objective theory of class and the in itself and for itself distinction, but the theory of imputed consciousness is Lukacs's construction of 1923, and citing Marx as its author is an error of fact, not of interpretation. The second reduces class consciousness to workers realising they are exploited: exploitation is one relation within the totality the concept concerns, and a worker may grasp exploitation vividly while possessing no class identity, no conception of an alternative, and no solidarity beyond the gate, while a class may display developed consciousness organised around other experiences entirely. The third treats the measured weakness of class identification as a straightforward refutation of Marxism: it refutes, at most, the glossary concept, since the imputed model predicts divergence between position and stated belief and the experiential model locates consciousness in institutions and practice rather than in self description, and the critic who wishes to refute those models must engage their actual claims. The fourth treats class consciousness and false consciousness as mirror images, the true and false versions of one mental state: the relation is asymmetrical, since false consciousness is a diagnosis that presupposes a standard, and the interesting question is always what standard, supplied by whom. The fifth treats Thompson and Lukacs as two flavours of one position, the experiential as a warmer restatement of the imputed: they are opposed accounts of where consciousness resides and who may certify it, and the opposition is the most instructive in the whole literature, since each writer's polemic exposes the other's genuine weakness, the imputed model's licence to discount testimony and the experiential model's difficulty in explaining why classes so often fail to make of their experience what their situation seems to invite.

## What is settled, what remains open, and the discipline the concept demands

A reader who has followed the argument is entitled to a statement of where it leaves the concept. Settled, first, is the attribution: the developed theory of class consciousness belongs to the tradition's second generation, above all to Lukacs, and its use should carry his name rather than Marx's. Settled, second, is the plurality of the models: imputed, experiential, and attitudinal accounts are distinct research programmes with distinct tribunals of evidence, and the imputation problem, the question of who may say what a class thinks, is the axis on which they differ and the question any serious treatment must answer openly. Settled, third, is the conditional legitimacy of imputation in analysis and its illegitimacy as a political warrant: a derived benchmark may organise inquiry and may never be used to overrule the class it describes. Open, by contrast, is almost everything empirical in the strong sense: how the dimensions of consciousness combine in particular workforces and periods, which conditions of formation can be rebuilt under fragmented employment and which cannot, and whether the concept's explanatory yield, set against rivals drawn from the study of identity, institutions, and collective action generally, justifies its continued centrality or only its honoured place in the tradition's history. Those questions will be answered, if they are answered, by work that observes the discipline this article has tried to practise: declare the model, state the standard, produce the evidence appropriate to both, and let the class's own testimony count as testimony, to be explained where the analyst disagrees with it and never to be deleted.

A final word is owed on why the concept continues to matter outside the tradition that produced it. Every politics that speaks of interests must answer, in practice, the question this article has called the imputation problem, since every claim that a policy serves or injures a group implies a standard of the group's good against which its members' expressed preferences may be found wanting, and democracies argue about pensions, wages, and public services in exactly those terms whenever they argue seriously. The Marxist literature on class consciousness is the most sustained attempt in modern thought to conduct that argument honestly, with the standard stated, the authority questioned, and the historical costs of getting it wrong kept in view. Its neighbouring concepts repay study in the same spirit: the account of how a class exists objectively before it acts, the analysis of the ideas that rule an epoch, and the theory of the organisations that claim to speak for classes are each treated elsewhere in this series, and the reader who follows the links from this article will find the same discipline applied there, of naming the model in use and refusing to let any writer, including this one, hold a standard they have not declared.

## For students and exam candidates

The three models are the answer structure for almost any question on this topic, and the attribution is the detail that lifts a script. State early that the developed concept is Lukacs's, from History and Class Consciousness in 1923, not Marx's, and define imputed consciousness precisely: the understanding appropriate to a class position, derived by the theorist, which may diverge from every member's actual beliefs. Set Thompson against it as a rival account, not a supplement: class as a relationship that happens through experience, consciousness as what people make of shared conditions in institutions and traditions. Use Gramsci's contradictory consciousness as the middle term that explains unevenness. Where a question asks whether the concept survives the evidence, deploy the imputation problem as the organising device: surveys answer the attitudinal question, archives answer the experiential one, and the imputed model expects stated belief to lag position, so the same evidence cannot refute all three. Close with the distinction between the analytic and political uses of imputation, and concede the slide between them, since examiners reward the candidate who states the strongest objection before answering it.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The most productive seminar exercise in this territory is to run the survey challenge as a genuine counter argument rather than a foil. Give the group the empirical findings on class self identification first, at full strength, and let the glossary definition fail against them in discussion before any model is introduced. Then present the three models as three rescue operations, each saving the concept by relocating it: into a derivation, into historical practice, into the data themselves, and require each participant to say which relocation they accept and what it costs. Lukacs costs the authority to discount testimony; Thompson costs the ability to say a class misjudges its situation; the attitudinal model costs the concept's critical edge, since a distribution of opinion cannot be wrong about itself. A second exercise assigns the three positions on a single episode, a strike, a closure, a riot from the record, and grades the results on whether the models were kept distinct. Watch for the characteristic error of the strong student, which is synthesis by drift, a paragraph that uses whichever model is winning at that sentence, and treat it as the teaching opportunity the concept exists to provide.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the operative demand is declaration: state the model, state the standard of developed consciousness being used, and state its source, whether derived, historical, or stipulated, before presenting findings, since an undeclared standard converts measurement into imputation without warrant. Historians should observe the two disciplines set out in the methods discussion, fidelity to the actors' categories and an explicit counterfactual, and should treat opponents' records as evidence of fact rather than motive. Journalists face the sharpest version of the trap, because the news cycle rewards a verdict on whether a class is conscious, angry, or asleep, delivered on the evidence of a poll or a single crowd. The poll measures self description to strangers; the crowd measures an episode; neither measures what the tradition means. A report that says which model its claim rests on, and notes when survey identity and institutional practice diverge, will be rarer and more accurate than the commentary around it. When workers' stated views disappoint an argument you are covering, the professional discipline of this subject is to report the views and explain them, in that order, and to leave the word duped out of the copy entirely.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is class consciousness?

Class consciousness is a class's understanding of its own position, its interests, and its relation to other classes, but the tradition uses the phrase in three distinct senses. In Lukacs's imputed sense it is the understanding rationally appropriate to the position, derived by theory, whether or not members hold it. In Thompson's experiential sense it is what people make of shared experience, embodied in institutions, traditions, and values. In the empirical sense it is the measured distribution of class identification and belief in a population. Most confusion on the topic comes from using one sense while being judged by another.

### Q: Did Marx use the term class consciousness?

Only sparsely and without developing it into a concept. Marx's systematic vocabulary concerns ideology, the ruling ideas of an epoch, and the distinction between a class in itself, sharing objective conditions, and a class for itself, organised and aware of its common interests against an opposing class, a distinction sketched in The Poverty of Philosophy in 1847. The Eighteenth Brumaire adds the analysis of a class, the French peasantry, whose isolation prevented it from representing itself. The developed theory of class consciousness, with imputation at its centre, is Lukacs's construction of 1923, built on Marxian foundations but not found in Marx's texts.

### Q: What did Lukacs mean by imputed class consciousness?

Imputed, or ascribed, class consciousness is the awareness that would be appropriate to a class's objective position: the thoughts people would have if they could assess their own situation and the totality of society together, and the actions that would rationally follow. The theorist derives it from an analysis of the class's place in production and attributes it to the class as a benchmark. It is not a report of what members believe, which Lukacs calls psychological consciousness, and no individual need ever embody it fully. The gap between the imputed and the actual is, in his account, produced by reification and is the space in which politics operates.

### Q: Is class consciousness the same as what workers actually believe?

Not in the tradition's main version. Lukacs defined class consciousness as the understanding appropriate to a class position, which may differ from any worker's actual beliefs. Thompson rejected this, treating class consciousness as what people make of their shared experience. The dispute is the imputation problem in miniature: if consciousness is defined by actual belief, surveys settle the question and the concept becomes a branch of opinion research; if it is defined by position, the theorist's derivation settles it and testimony cannot refute the attribution. Stating which definition is in use is the first obligation of any treatment.

### Q: How does class consciousness develop?

Across the rival models, four conditions recur. Concentration and communication let workers discover that grievances are shared, dissolving the isolation Marx pictured in the sack of potatoes. Struggle acts as a school, forcing questions about finance, law, and the state that routine suppresses. Organisation that outlasts the episode, in unions, parties, press, and clubs, preserves what struggle taught and transmits it to new members. And a language must exist in which the new understanding can be stated, whether constitutional, religious, or economic. Consciousness formed in crises without durable institutions characteristically drains away when the crisis passes.

### Q: What blocks the development of class consciousness?

The blockers mirror the conditions of formation. Fragmentation of work and labour markets creates real divergences of immediate interest within the class, and competition for jobs, housing, and security sets members against one another. The institutions of the wider society present the existing order as natural and collective action by workers as aberrant, while identical action by firms passes as ordinary business. Repression raises the price of organising, and defeat teaches a durable lesson of hopelessness. In Lukacs's deeper account, reification shapes the very categories in which experience is registered, so that social relations appear as fixed things before any argument about them begins.

### Q: Did E.P. Thompson reject class consciousness?

He rejected the imputed model, not the phenomenon. In The Making of the English Working Class, published in 1963, Thompson argued that class is a historical relationship that happens through experience rather than a structural position awaiting correct awareness, and that class consciousness is the way shared experience is handled in cultural terms, embodied in traditions, values, and institutions. His polemic targeted the condescension of theorists who corrected past workers' vocabulary from the standpoint of a later orthodoxy. On his account a class's consciousness is proved in what it builds and defends over time, and no theorist may attribute to it an awareness it never actually formed.

### Q: Is national or religious identity a rival to class consciousness?

It can be, and the honest treatment regards the relation as an open historical question rather than a doctrinal ranking. National solidarity has dissolved class organisation in wartime with a speed no theory of deception adequately explains, while class organisation has at other times held together workforces divided by language and faith. Gramsci's contradictory consciousness is the most serviceable framework here, since it expects a person to hold a practical class consciousness and an inherited national or confessional consciousness simultaneously, and treats politics as the contest over which element organisation elaborates. Neither identity is a mere disguise for the other.

### Q: Can a capitalist have class consciousness?

Yes, on every model, though the tradition discusses the bourgeois case less sympathetically. On Lukacs's account the bourgeoisie possesses a sophisticated consciousness of its interests in property, contract, and stability, elaborated by its lawyers, economists, and statesmen, but one with a structural limit: pursued honestly, understanding of the totality would reveal the historically transient character of bourgeois society itself, so the ruling class's thought must arrest itself by naturalising the existing order. Middle strata, whose positions mix small property with dependence on wages or credentials, characteristically display an oscillating consciousness, attaching now to the classes above and now to those below.

### Q: Does class consciousness require a party?

On the imputed model, effectively yes: since socialist understanding does not arise spontaneously from economic struggle, which Lenin argued produces only trade union consciousness, an organisation bearing the developed theory is required to bring it to the class, and Lukacs gave that organisational claim its philosophical form. On the experiential model, no single organisation is required, since consciousness forms through the class's whole institutional life, though parties are among the institutions that can carry it. The attitudinal model treats the party as one influence among others on opinion. Which answer a writer gives is therefore a reliable sign of which model they are using.

### Q: How can historians tell whether class consciousness was present in a past strike?

By weighing kinds of trace in a rough hierarchy. Costly sustained action is the strongest evidence: a strike maintained against hunger, funds subscribed from wages that could not spare them, and organisation rebuilt after suppression are hard to explain without a shared understanding that the cause is common. Institutional records come next, the minute books and correspondence in which members stated purposes to one another. Public texts, pamphlets, newspapers, and songs, follow, read as interventions in arguments rather than transparent belief. Retrospective testimony and opponents' records are weakest on motive, since police and employers systematically credit conspiracy where participants experienced solidarity.

### Q: What should a teacher do when students treat class consciousness as automatic?

Break the assumption with the record. Students often absorb a picture in which shared conditions generate shared awareness by a kind of chemical necessity, and the corrective is a case in which conditions were shared and awareness did not follow, or followed in a form the theory did not predict: Marx's isolated peasantry, a militant workforce with conservative reflective beliefs, a sudden radicalisation with no change in conditions beforehand. Then introduce the three models as rival explanations of the gap between position and awareness, and require students to identify which model any given claim about consciousness is using. The concept becomes interesting to students at exactly the point where it stops being automatic.

### Q: Why does imputed consciousness risk becoming a licence to ignore what workers say?

Because the construction places the authority to describe a class's consciousness outside the class's own utterances. If the theorist or party holds the derivation of what the class would think if it understood its position, then any disagreement from actual workers can be reclassified as the predictable shortfall of merely psychological consciousness under reification, and no testimony can ever count against the attribution. The analytic use of imputation, as a benchmark for explaining belief, does not require this move. The licence appears when the benchmark is converted into a mandate to act on the class's behalf against its expressed will, a conversion the tradition has repeatedly performed.

### Q: How does media ownership bear on the formation of class consciousness?

Media are part of the machinery by which a society's interpretations are produced and distributed, and ownership shapes that machinery's output through selection and framing rather than through dictation of opinions. A class's own press historically performed the opposite function, connecting disputes into a pattern, printing members' correspondence, and preserving precedents, which is why states and employers treated the workers' press as a strategic target. Where a class lacks media of its own, its members encounter their collective situation mainly in descriptions produced by institutions positioned elsewhere, and grievances remain private troubles for want of the connective account that would make them a common situation.

### Q: What distinguishes class consciousness from trade union consciousness in Lenin's usage?

The distinction is one of horizon. Trade union consciousness, in Lenin's argument in What Is To Be Done in 1902, is the understanding workers develop from economic struggle unaided: the need to combine, to fight employers over wages and conditions, and to press for protective laws. Its demands, however militant, are addressed to the terms of the wage relation and leave the relation itself intact. Socialist consciousness takes the further step of grasping the wage relation as one moment of a total political and economic order whose transformation is the condition of emancipation. Lenin held that the second understanding must be brought to the movement by an organised bearer of theory, a claim Lukacs later supplied with a philosophy.
