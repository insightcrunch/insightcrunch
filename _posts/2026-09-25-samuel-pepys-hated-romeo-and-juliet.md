On 1 March 1662, Samuel Pepys took his wife Elizabeth by coach to the Duke's Company's theatre in Lincoln's Inn Fields and saw Romeo and Juliet for the first time in his life. That night he wrote the most famous hostile sentence the play has ever received: he judged the tragedy, in its own substance, the worst play he had ever heard, and the performance of it the worst acting he had ever seen from that company. The verdict matters because it is early, precise, and double. Pepys did not merely dislike an evening at the theatre. He split his condemnation into two findings, one against the play and one against the playing, and the whole subsequent argument about what his entry proves is an argument about how much weight each finding can bear. The reading defended here is exact: Pepys's diary entry is the record of a failed first night witnessed by a spectator whose taste was still forming and whose standards rewarded music, polish, and rehearsal. It is genuine evidence of Restoration impatience with an unadapted Shakespearean tragedy, and it is worthless as a measurement of the play's quality. Both things are in the entry, and both have to be kept.

The entry deserves its fame for a second reason. It is nearly alone. The decades after Shakespeare's death left almost no extended eyewitness testimony about this play in performance, and the testimony that survives from the reopened theatres is dominated by prompter's lists, title pages, and the adapters' prefaces. Pepys gives the reception historian something rarer than a judgment: a dated, situated, first-person account of the play failing in front of an intelligent listener who had every reason to enjoy his evening. Reception history quotes the sentence constantly and reads it carelessly, as if a superlative in a private diary were a box-office report or a critical consensus. It is neither. It is one man's verdict, delivered with unusual rhetorical care, and its care is the clue to its meaning. Pepys was a systematic diarist who graded nearly everything he saw, heard, ate, and bought, and his theatrical grades follow a pattern across hundreds of entries. Once that pattern is reconstructed, the entry of 1 March 1662 stops looking like a thunderbolt and starts looking like a measurement taken with a particular instrument, on a particular night, under conditions the diarist himself recorded and partly blamed. The hostility is real. Its object is mixed. Untangling the mixture is the work of the pages that follow.

## The Diary Entry of 1 March 1662, Clause by Clause

The full entry for the day moves from money to family quarrel to theatre, in the diarist's usual flat sequence. Pepys paid a debt of forty pounds to Sir William Batten in the morning, worked at the Navy Office, dined at home, and quarrelled with his uncle Thomas over dinner. Then, in his own sequence of events, he and Elizabeth went by coach to view a portrait drawing of himself, and from there to the playhouse he calls the Opera. The verdict that follows is short and built like a legal finding. Paraphrased closely, it runs: the play they saw was Romeo and Juliet, performed that day for the first time; as a play in itself it was the worst he had ever heard in his life; it was also the worst acted performance he had ever seen those actors give; and he resolved to attend no more first performances of plays, because the whole cast had been more or less lost in their parts. Every element of that sentence carries information, and reception history loses evidence whenever it quotes only the superlative and drops the rest.

The opening clause fixes the occasion: a first performance. The second clause, the one the anthologies keep, assigns the superlative to the play "of itself," a phrase that does deliberate work. Pepys distinguishes the work from its execution, a distinction he also draws, in the opposite direction, when he praises a weak script carried by fine acting or condemns good material wasted by a poor company. The third clause turns on the company with a comparative drawn from his own playgoing: not the worst acting in the abstract, but the worst he had seen "these people do," which presumes a standard he had watched the same troupe meet on other nights. The fourth clause then explains the third: the actors were "out," the period's word for having lost one's lines, more or less across the whole cast. Pepys therefore wrote, in a single breath, a condemnation of the text, a condemnation of the performance, and a diagnosis of the performance's failure that had nothing to do with the text at all. A first night with an under-rehearsed cast, fluffing lines in a long verse tragedy, stands between Pepys and every judgement he formed that evening. Any reading of the entry that ignores the diagnosis while repeating the superlative is quoting half a witness.

Pepys's superlatives also need calibrating against his diary's house style. He dealt in absolutes the way other men deal in small change. Within the same year he called A Midsummer Night's Dream, seen at the rival King's Theatre on 29 September 1662, the most insipid and ridiculous play he had ever seen in his life, a verdict that sits oddly beside that comedy's subsequent career and beside his own later pleasures. His diary records plays as the best, the worst, the most pleasant, the most tedious, with a freedom that tells the reader as much about the diarist's temperament as about the repertory. Robert Latham, who with William Matthews produced the standard modern edition of the diary, presents Pepys's judgments as the quick, confident verdicts of a clever amateur writing for no audience but himself, valuable precisely because they are unguarded. An unguarded superlative is still a superlative from a sample of one evening. The clause-by-clause reading restores the proportions: Pepys heard a tragedy he disliked, performed badly, on a night when the performers had not yet learned it, and he said so in the strongest words his diary's idiom supplied.

### Did Pepys condemn the play itself, or only its performance?

Pepys condemned both, and he said so in separate clauses. The play "of itself" drew the famous superlative, the worst he had ever heard in his life, while the acting drew a second superlative, the worst he had seen that company do. Neither clause retracts the other.

His own diagnosis then blamed the evening's shakiness on a first performance in which the actors were more or less out of their parts. Text and production are both indicted in the entry; the production is also partly excused, and the excuse is the part that quotation most often leaves behind.

## What 'The First Time It Was Ever Acted' Actually Means

The entry's first clause has misled casual readers for generations, because "the first time it was ever acted" sounds like a premiere. It was not the premiere of Romeo and Juliet, a play then roughly sixty-seven years old, already printed in three quartos and the First Folio, and already described on its own early title pages as a popular stage success. Pepys meant what a regular playgoer means: this was the first performance of this production, the opening night of the Duke's Company's revival of the play at its Lincoln's Inn Fields house. His resolution to avoid first nights only makes sense on that reading. No spectator resolves to stop attending premieres of old plays; a spectator resolves to stop paying for under-rehearsed opening nights, and Pepys, who attended the theatre with a connoisseur's regularity, had learned the cost of them. The phrase therefore dates the entry precisely in the play's Restoration biography: not the tragedy's first life but its return, after the eighteen-year closure of the public theatres, before a company still assembling its craft and an audience relearning its habits.

The contrast with the play's first life is instructive, and the early printed books preserve it. The second quarto of 1599 advertised on its title page that the tragedy had been often played publicly with great applause, a publisher's boast that the surviving evidence of the early stage supports; readers following [what the title pages tell us](/2026/08/28/title-pages-romeo-and-juliet/) about the play's early fortunes meet a record of success that Pepys's evening seems to flatly contradict. The contradiction dissolves once the occasions are separated. A play can be a proven popular success in the 1590s and still fail on a raw March night in 1662, in a converted tennis court, spoken by actors who had inherited the old repertory without inheriting the unbroken tradition of playing it. Between the applause recorded on the quarto title page and the contempt recorded in the diary lies the Civil War, the closure of the theatres in 1642, and the dispersal of the profession. The boy who had first played Juliet was long dead; the part in 1662 went to a young woman, Mary Saunderson, in a company whose oldest members had learned their trade before the wars and whose youngest had learned it in a hurry. Pepys was not judging the same theatrical object the quarto title page praised. He was judging its revenant.

His date needs one clerkly caution that reception history often skips. Pepys wrote the entry under the Old Style calendar, in which the legal year began on 25 March, so his contemporaries dated that March day 1661; modern editions print the year as 1661/62 and the day is now conventionally given as 1 March 1662. The detail matters for more than pedantry. It places the performance in the second full season of the reopened theatres, early enough that the Duke's Company was still working through the small stock of Shakespeare plays allotted to it, and late enough that Pepys had already formed, from dozens of visits, the comparative standard by which he found this cast wanting. He had seen the same company triumph and fail. The entry is a regular's review of a bad night, preserved by accident of genius.


## The Opera in Lincoln's Inn Fields: The Stage Pepys Sat In

Pepys called the playhouse simply the Opera, the name Londoners gave Sir William Davenant's theatre in Lincoln's Inn Fields, converted from a tennis court and opened in June 1661 with changeable scenery of a kind the pre-war public stages had never used. The word mattered. Davenant had made his name under the Protectorate with entertainments that called themselves operas, musical spectacles with painted scenes, and his patent house carried the brand forward: scenery that moved, a proscenium that framed the picture, actresses in the women's parts, and music woven through the afternoon as a matter of course. The Duke's Company, named for its patron the Duke of York, was the junior partner in the Restoration theatrical duopoly. Thomas Killigrew's King's Company had taken the larger share of the old stock plays and the senior actors; Davenant received his own works and nine Shakespeare titles, Romeo and Juliet among them, together with Webster's The Duchess of Malfi. Amanda Eubanks Winkler and Richard Schoch, in their study of Davenant and the Duke's Company, stress how seriously the company was disadvantaged from the outset in the range of drama it could offer, and how Davenant compensated with novelty: new scenery, new music, new women on the stage, and old plays remade to display all three.

Romeo and Juliet on 1 March 1662 was therefore an experiment conducted in public. The tragedy had to fill a scenic stage built for spectacle, with a company whose women were novices by definition, since professional actresses had existed in England for barely a year. John Downes, the company's prompter, whose Roscius Anglicanus of 1708 is the period's great backstage record, preserves the casting: Henry Harris played Romeo and Mary Saunderson, later Mary Betterton, played Juliet. Harris was a respected young tragedian of the company, and Saunderson was becoming its leading actress, but neither could draw on a living tradition of the play, because there was none. The last professional Juliets before the wars had been boys of a company disbanded in 1642. What the first audience of the revival saw was a reconstruction in the strict sense: a famous old text reassembled by a new kind of theatre, with new kinds of performers, under production conditions that the text had not been written for and that the company had barely had time to master. Pepys, sitting in that house as one of its most faithful customers, was watching the Restoration stage invent itself, and on this afternoon the invention faltered.

The house and the season shaped the faltering. The Duke's Company was playing a punishing schedule, rotating a small stock of titles while rehearsing new ones, in a repertory system that gave a reviving tragedy few chances to settle before its first audience arrived. Pepys's own diary documents the rhythm: he was at the Opera repeatedly through February 1662, seeing Davenant's adaptation The Law Against Lovers on 18 February and praising it warmly, barely a fortnight before the Romeo and Juliet he damned. The same house, the same company, the same spectator, opposite verdicts, a fortnight apart. That proximity is the strongest single clue the diary offers about the entry's meaning. Whatever failed on 1 March was not the venue, the troupe, or the diarist's willingness to be pleased in that room. It was the conjunction of this play, this cast, and this night, and the entry's own clauses say exactly that: the play displeased him, and the playing displeased him more measurably, because he had watched the same players do far better in the same building within the month.

### Why did the failure run through the whole cast at once?

Because the failure was systemic, not personal. A revival learned from sides, rehearsed thinly, and conducted by a prompter will fail together when it fails at all, since each actor's cues depend on the others' security. Pepys's phrase "more or less" grades the collapse: nobody was safe, and the least prepared dragged the rest.

"Out" was the period's word for an actor who had lost the thread of a part: lines missed, cues dropped, speeches paraphrased or dried on. Pepys records the whole cast out to a greater or lesser degree, which points to a company under-rehearsed rather than untalented, and supports his resolution to avoid first performances thereafter. The phrase is the entry's built-in discount on its own verdict: the diarist knew the performance had never properly happened.

## Pepys the Playgoer: The Taste That Did the Judging

A verdict is only as informative as the judge, and Pepys's diary is generous evidence about its own author. Samuel Pepys in March 1662 was twenty-nine years old, Clerk of the Acts to the Navy Board, rising briskly through the Restoration administration, and a playgoer of extraordinary frequency and system. He recorded what he paid, where he sat, whom he met, and what he thought, with the same clerkly completeness he brought to his accounts. His theatrical judgments across the diary years follow discernible criteria, and the criteria explain the verdict on this play better than any theory of Restoration taste in the abstract. He rewarded, above all, acting that had been rehearsed into ease, music and dancing integrated into the spectacle, wit that landed in the moment, and novelty of staging. He punished length without variety, verse that held up the action, and performances in which the machinery showed. He was, by his own cheerful confession across hundreds of entries, a consumer of theatrical pleasure rather than a critic of dramatic literature, and his diary's candour about that fact is what makes it such a precious and such a treacherous source.

His Shakespeare judgments show the instrument at work. He adored Hamlet, which he saw repeatedly, and his praise fastened on the actor: Thomas Betterton, in the entry of 24 August 1661, performed the Prince's part beyond imagination. He loved Davenant's operatic Macbeth, with its witches' dances and flying, returning to it again and again and calling it, in January 1667, a most excellent play for the stage and the variety of its dancing and music. He praised The Law Against Lovers, Davenant's free adaptation of Measure for Measure married to Much Ado About Nothing, within a fortnight of the Romeo and Juliet failure. The pattern is not hostility to Shakespeare. The pattern is a preference for Shakespeare mediated: adapted, musical, spectacular, and above all securely played by a settled company. Give Pepys the unmediated article, a long verse tragedy spoken from memory not yet secure, and the diary's language curdles. His famous dismissal of A Midsummer Night's Dream belongs to the King's Company's unadapted revival of that comedy, and his insipid and ridiculous verdict there repeats the pattern of 1 March: where the mediation was absent and the polish uncertain, Pepys's patience failed completely.

His deepest passion explains the punishment he dealt to this particular play. Pepys was a musician before he was a critic: he played the viol and the flageolet, sang, composed settings, kept instruments in his chambers, and measured entertainments partly by their music. Romeo and Juliet offers a musician little to hold. Its famous music is internal, the poetry's own patterning, plus a short comic scene for the hired musicians in the fourth act that a shaky company could easily muff. A Davenant production promised song and dance by brand; the tragedy as written supplies neither in the quantities Pepys craved, and a first-night cast fumbling verse could not supply the compensating pleasure of beautifully spoken poetry either. Jill L. Levenson, introducing the play in the Oxford edition, treats the Restoration record as a story of the drama being remade for new stages; Pepys's diary is the remaking observed from the stalls, by a man whose pleasures the unreconstructed text was never designed to serve. He did not hate the play because he was a philistine. He hated it because it asked for a kind of attention, sustained and verbal, that his theatrical formation had not taught him to give and that the evening's performance gave him no help in giving.

### Why does Pepys write 'heard' instead of 'saw'?

Because hearing was his primary theatrical sense. Pepys judged plays as spoken events: the verse, the wit, the singing, and the audibility of a cast secure in its lines reached him before staging did, and his diary's verdicts run consistently to pleasure or boredom in the ear.

The word also fits his practice as a concertgoer and singer. When the cast was out of its parts, the thing he had come to hear never in fact arrived, and his superlative names the failure at the point where he felt it.

## Play or Production? The Question Inside the Entry

The brief's complication, whether Pepys judged the play or the production, dissolves under the entry's own grammar, and dissolving it correctly is the analytical centre of the whole question. He judged both, said both, and then supplied the evidence that should govern the weight a reader gives to each judgment. The judgment on the production is firm and corroborated: first nights were rough, this cast was out of its parts, the same company's polish on other nights is documented in his own pages, and his resolution to come at the third performance at the earliest is the resolution of a man who believed the playgoing problem could be fixed by timing. The judgment on the play is sincerely meant and evidentially weak. It rests on a single hearing of a long verse tragedy delivered by actors who had not mastered it, by a listener whose diary shows him repeatedly undervaluing unadapted verse drama and overvaluing musical mediation. A modern court would call the first judgment expert testimony and the second a lay impression. Reception history has generally done the reverse, quoting the impression and forgetting the testimony.

The strongest reading available takes the diarist at his whole word. Pepys believed, that night, that the play itself was contemptible, and the belief was honestly formed on the evidence his senses gave him. But his senses were being fed a corrupted text in the only sense that matters for a listener: the words arrived disordered, paraphrased, and insecure. A man cannot judge the poetry of a play whose lines the actors cannot remember, and Pepys, to his credit, recorded the corruption in the same breath as the judgment. Gary Taylor, in Reinventing Shakespeare, reads the Restoration record as an era rewriting the dramatist because it found the originals crude, and Pepys's sentence is one of the exhibits such an argument naturally reaches for: here is the cultivated playgoer of the new age, finding the old tragedy beneath contempt. The diary itself supplies the correction. The same playgoer, in the same weeks, was praising the same company's Shakespeare when it arrived adapted, rehearsed, and sung. What the entry documents is not an era's settled contempt for the play but the precise conditions under which the play, in that era, could fail: unadapted, under-rehearsed, and offered to a taste formed by Davenant's musical stage. That is a narrower finding than Taylor's rhetoric wants, and a more useful one.

Claire Tomalin, in her biography Samuel Pepys: The Unequalled Self, treats the diary's theatrical verdicts as the record of a sensibility being educated in public, its owner brisk, confident, and frequently wrong in ways he would sometimes catch himself. The Romeo and Juliet entry fits her portrait exactly, with one aggravating feature: he never caught himself. There is no second visit in the diary's remaining seven years, no revised verdict, no softening. The play that failed before him in March 1662 stayed failed in his private canon, because the occasion for revision never arose. His judgment therefore entered the record with the fixity of print while remaining, in substance, a first impression. That combination, a first impression preserved with a great diarist's authority, is the entry's peculiar danger and its peculiar value. It shows a reputation at its most vulnerable moment, the first night of a revival, and it warns every later reader how much of what passes for a period's taste is the residue of occasions.


### Was the acting text of 1662 Shakespeare's text, unaltered?

No acting copy from that night survives, so certainty is impossible, but the balance of evidence favours a straight, ordinarily cut text rather than a formal adaptation: no altered version from the 1660s is recorded, and the first documented rewriting, James Howard's, followed the revival instead of preparing it.

The production belonged to Davenant, whose instinct and commercial method was adaptation, and his company remade other Shakespeare plays freely in the same seasons, which is why the question stays open rather than closed. Pepys's complaints, aimed at delivery and substance rather than at any novelty of ending or structure, fit a familiar text insecurely spoken, and they name no surprise that an adapter's hand would have explained.

## A Private Verdict: The Diary Nobody Read for Generations

One fact about the entry is quoted least and matters most: nobody at the time could read it. Pepys wrote his diary in Thomas Shelton's shorthand, a private system he used for a private book, and the six volumes sat in his library at Magdalene College, Cambridge, written in cipher against casual eyes, for a century and a half. The diary was first transcribed in full by Mynors Bright in the 1820s and first published, in a selected and expurgated form, in 1825 under Lord Braybrooke's editorship. Pepys's hatred of Romeo and Juliet therefore influenced nothing. It did not depress the play's stock in 1662, provoke a pamphlet, or comfort an adapter. It slept in shorthand while the play's reputation was made and unmade by other hands, and it entered public argument only in the nineteenth century, when the diarist had become a celebrity and his every superlative acquired retrospective authority. Reception history must handle it as archaeology, not as influence: it is a fossil of one evening's feeling, beautifully preserved, and fossils record organisms, not ecosystems.

The preservation itself deserves a sentence of respect. The standard modern text of the diary, edited by Robert Latham and William Matthews across eleven volumes between 1970 and 1983, established what Pepys wrote with a completeness earlier editions, which silently omitted and smoothed, had denied readers. It is through that edition that the entry's exact construction, superlative, comparative, and diagnosis in one breath, is available for the clause-by-clause reading on which any serious use of the evidence depends. Earlier printings had sometimes softened Pepys's theatrical severities or buried them among the day's business. The Latham and Matthews text lets the verdict stand in its full severity, and, just as importantly, lets the surrounding verdicts stand beside it: the Hamlet rapture, the Macbeth delight, the Dream dismissal, the fortnight-old praise of The Law Against Lovers at the same house. A single entry quoted alone is propaganda. The diary read whole is an instrument panel, and the instrument panel is what the Pepys note at the end of this article sets out to display. Researchers collating the diarist's scattered theatrical judgments across nine years of entries, the way the appendix to the Latham and Matthews edition arranges them, do this work best with the documents held in one private research space, and a local-first vault such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) suits exactly that habit of keeping a whole archive searchable at one's elbow.

The diarist's privacy also explains the entry's tone, and the tone explains its quotability. Pepys was performing for an audience of one, with no reputation to protect and no critical school to answer. His verdict has the ring that only unperformed prose gets, and that ring has seduced generations of quotation. A published critic of 1662, had one reviewed the performance, would have hedged, balanced, and cited beauties; the hedges are where the information about a period's considered taste usually lives. Pepys gives the unconsidered taste, the taste of the stalls at the end of a long day, and reception history needs both kinds and must not mistake one for the other. His is the earliest full-throated boo in the play's surviving record. A boo is data. It is not a review, and it is not a box-office figure, and the play's subsequent fortunes, which the next section traces, show how little the boo predicted.

## The Afterlife Around the Entry: Howard, Otway, and the Altered Endings

The strongest evidence against reading Pepys's verdict as the era's settled judgment is what the era did next. If the Restoration had simply agreed that the tragedy was beneath notice, the play would have vanished from the stage, as several of its fellows nearly did. Instead, the adapters descended on it almost immediately, and adaptation in the Restoration theatre was a compliment paid in the only currency the period trusted: labour. James Howard, brother-in-law to Davenant, prepared a version in the years just after the 1662 revival in which the lovers did not die. His Romeo and Juliet remade the tragedy as a tragicomedy, and the two versions, the original with its double suicide and Howard's with its reprieve, were performed on alternate nights, a scheduling experiment recorded by the Yale edition's stage history from the early records. The experiment is usually retold as a curiosity, but it documents something essential: the play was valuable enough to be worth rewriting, and its ending was felt to be a negotiable feature rather than the whole point. Pepys had judged the play worthless. The market, within the same few seasons, judged it worth two endings.

The alternate-nights arrangement did not last, and the reason it failed clarifies the play's real position in the period's taste. Howard's happy ending, on the Cambridge account of the play's afterlife, was unsuccessful; audiences evidently did not want the lovers saved at the price of the story's meaning. What the period wanted was the tragedy with its machinery modernised, and it got that, comprehensively, from Thomas Otway. His History and Fall of Caius Marius, first performed in 1679, lifted the lovers' story bodily into ancient Rome, renamed them Marius and Lavinia, and folded long stretches of the original's verse into a Roman political tragedy shaped by the succession crisis of its moment. Otway's graft held the stage so completely that, as the Royal Shakespeare Company's stage history puts it, his was the only version of the play to be seen for roughly seventy years. The original text left the boards not because the age despised it but because the age preferred it ventriloquised: the story, the tomb, and above all the ending, in which Otway, following one of the Italian novelle behind the play, let Juliet wake before Romeo died, so the lovers could speak their farewells over the mistake.

That waking scene is the thread that runs through the whole afterlife Pepys did not live to see. Theophilus Cibber, restoring something nearer the original play in 1744, kept Otway's awakening and cut Rosaline; David Garrick's celebrated version of 1750, substantially Cibber's, kept both choices and added the funeral procession and dirge for Juliet that became fixtures of the staging tradition. For two centuries the performed Romeo and Juliet was a palimpsest in which the tomb scene's most beloved beat was Otway's invention, not Shakespeare's. Set Pepys's entry against that history and its proportions change. The diarist condemned the play of itself on a single under-rehearsed hearing in 1662; the profession spent the next hundred and eighty years proving, by continuous labour, that the play of itself was the most workable tragic property in the old stock. The adapters were not refuting Pepys, of whom they knew nothing. They were simply voting, season after season, with the only ballot the theatre recognises, and their votes drowned his.

Two of the play's other famous judges frame the entry from either side and show, by contrast, what kind of document it is. A decade later John Dryden, the period's professional critic, was writing about the play as a working dramatist, praising and blaming with a craftsman's specificity; his remark on Mercutio, the subject of [Dryden's judgment that Shakespeare killed Mercutio](/2026/10/02/dryden-on-mercutio-1672/), argues with the play from inside the trade, as Pepys never pretended to do. A century later Samuel Johnson, editing the works, delivered [Johnson's considered verdict on the play](/2026/10/09/samuel-johnson-on-romeo-and-juliet/) from the study, with the whole text before him and a lifetime's criticism behind the sentence. Pepys's judgment sits between those poles and belongs to neither: it is earlier than both, ruder than both, and based on less evidence than either, which is exactly why it needs the careful handling this article has given it. And behind all three stands the stage Pepys could not have imagined, the platform of the 1590s where the tragedy first played to the applause its title page boasts of; readers tracing [the play on the Elizabethan stage](/2027/08/06/romeo-juliet-elizabethan-stage/) meet the theatrical world whose distance from Lincoln's Inn Fields is the real subject of the diary entry.

### Did Pepys ever revise his opinion of the play?

The diary gives no sign of it. Across the remaining seven years of the journal, with its hundreds of theatre visits, Pepys never records a second attendance at Romeo and Juliet, and no later entry returns to the verdict of 1 March 1662 to soften, repeat, or retract it.

The judgment therefore stands in the record with the completeness of a considered position, although its substance was a first impression formed on a first night. That gap between the verdict's permanence and its evidential basis is the entry's central caution for reception history.

## The Play Pepys Heard: Verse Built for a Different Ear

Fairness to Pepys begins with admitting what was asked of him that afternoon. Romeo and Juliet makes its effects through patterned verse of extraordinary density, and it makes them fast. The Prologue warns the audience in a sonnet that the story is already known and already fatal, "a pair of star-cross'd lovers" taking their life (1.0.6), and then the play spends its length converting that foreknowledge into pressure. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, the set piece a Restoration audience might have been expected to relish, is a ninety-line aria of miniature folklore that begins "O, then I see Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4.53) and accelerates through a catalogue of dreams until Romeo has to cut it off: "Thou talk'st of nothing" (1.4.96). Juliet's great invocation to night opens with a gallop, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2.1-2), and runs for thirty lines of sustained erotic metaphor. Her potion speech in the fourth act, "I have a faint cold fear thrills through my veins" (4.3.15), builds its terror through a single accumulating period. The Prince closes the play with the flat summary that has become its epitaph: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-10). Every one of these effects depends on an audience following verse at speed, held by actors who own the lines. Every one of them collapses when the lines are half-remembered.

Now place that text in Pepys's ear. His diary shows a listener who delighted in verbal clarity, in songs he could carry away, in dances he could describe, and in acting whose ease made artifice invisible. The Duke's Company's scenic stage and musical brand had trained him, over two seasons, to expect the pleasures of mediation: scenery changing, voices singing, a professional polish that kept the machinery out of sight. An under-rehearsed Romeo and Juliet inverted every term of that contract. The verse arrived in the least mediated form imaginable, raw from insecure memories, and its density, which rewards a prepared listener, punished an unprepared one. Where the play's first audiences had met the Queen Mab speech as a bravura novelty from a company at its peak, Pepys met it as one more stretch of talk in an afternoon that had already exhausted his patience. His superlative records the collision honestly. It cannot record the play's quality, because on the evidence of his own diagnosis the play, as a verbal artefact, had only intermittently reached him.

There is a further mismatch that the entry does not state but the season explains. The tragedy is a young people's play built on speed, its action compressed into a handful of days, its lovers played in the 1590s by the company's leading tragedian and a gifted boy. The Restoration revival cast it from a company whose strength was maturing rather than youthful brilliance, and presented it in a scenic house whose intervals, music, and stage pictures stretched the afternoon. Nothing in the record suggests Davenant's staging was inept in conception; everything in Pepys's entry suggests a conception that had not yet been rehearsed into life. The distinction matters for how the failure should be filed. A conception failing in execution on a first night is the commonest accident in theatrical history. Pepys, uniquely among the accident's witnesses, wrote the accident down in a form quotable enough to be mistaken, for three centuries, for a judgment on the conception and the text alike.

## The Other Witnesses: Downes and the Silence Around the Entry

Pepys's entry is often described as the Restoration's verdict on the play, and the description survives only because the Restoration left so few verdicts of any kind. No newspaper reviewed the performance. No pamphlet answered it. The professional record of the revival comes from John Downes, the Duke's Company's prompter and bookkeeper, whose Roscius Anglicanus, published in 1708, lists the production's cast and places the play among the company's early offerings without recording triumph or disaster. Downes is a witness of a different species from Pepys: an insider, writing decades later, interested in personnel and lineage rather than in verdicts, and his calm is itself evidence. A catastrophe of the proportions Pepys's superlative suggests, a launch so bad it entered company legend, might be expected to leave some trace in the prompter's memory beyond a cast list. It leaves none. The production appears in his pages as one revival among many in a busy second season, cast with the company's rising principals, and the silence around Pepys's thunderbolt suggests the evening registered, in the trade's own memory, as an ordinary rough first night rather than a scandal.

The wider silence has a structural cause that reception history must keep in view. Restoration London had no reviewing profession. The periodical essay and the theatrical notice belong to the next century; in 1662 the taste of the town survives in diaries, letters, prologues, and prefaces, forms that record the exceptional rather than the typical and the articulate rather than the crowd. Pepys is the loudest surviving voice about the early Restoration stage not because he was typical but because he was tireless, and his diary's survival in shorthand archives one man's taste with a completeness no audience survey could match. Every generalisation built on his entry therefore carries a sampling warning that honest scholarship states and popular quotation drops. He tells us what the most assiduous playgoer in London thought on one night. He cannot tell us what the pit thought, what the company thought, or what the same playgoer would have thought at the third performance he resolved to wait for and never recorded attending.

Downes also supplies the counterweight in kind. His book is the record of a company that kept the old plays in its working stock and measured them by serviceability; the tragedy that Pepys heard once and damned remained in the Duke's Company's hands, available for the Howard experiment and the seasons that followed. The two witnesses, the diarist in the stalls and the prompter at his desk, describe the same object at different exposures, and the entry's modern misuse begins whenever the stalls are quoted without the desk. Read together, they support the narrow finding this article has defended: a famous play, revived by a stretched company, failed on its first night before its most quotable spectator, and then went on being worked, adapted, and fought over by the profession for the rest of the century. Neither witness records a masterpiece disgraced or a dud exposed. They record a working theatre having a bad afternoon with a demanding text, and posterity, preferring thunderbolts to afternoons, promoted the boo into a verdict of the age.

## The Quotation's Second Life: How a Boo Became a Verdict of Record

The entry's modern career began when the diary itself became public property. The selected edition of 1825 introduced Pepys to the reading public as a personality, and through the nineteenth century, as fuller editions followed, his theatrical verdicts were mined for exactly the quality editors prized in him: quotable certainty. The Romeo and Juliet sentence was perfect ore. It was short, it was superlative, it was about a famous play, and it appeared to license a pleasant irony, that the archetypal love story had once been hissed by a man in a wig. Introductions to editions of the play took it up as the standard exhibit of the tragedy's Restoration fortunes, generally in the stripped form this article has been resisting: the superlative alone, dated, attributed, and left to imply a consensus. Each retelling sanded the entry smoother. The comparative about the acting went first, since it complicated the joke; the diagnosis about the cast being out went next, since it excused the play; what remained was the pure superlative, travelling through stage histories like a coin worn blank.

The full text, once Latham and Matthews had established it, made the stripped form untenable for scholars while leaving it available to everyone else, and the gap between scholarly and popular citation is the entry's second life in miniature. Scholars who quote the whole sentence have to argue with it, and the argument, as the next section shows, produces the interesting criticism. Popular use prefers the coin. There is a lesson in the split about how reception evidence degrades: a document's authority grows with its fame while its content shrinks to its most portable clause, until the authority is being borrowed by a version of the document the document itself contradicts. Pepys's entry is a nearly perfect case. The sentence that reception history quotes against the play contains, in its unquoted half, the play's complete defence, supplied by the prosecutor. Honest citation does not require loving the diary less. It requires quoting the diagnosis with the verdict, the fortnight's praise of Davenant with the damnation of the revival, and the shorthand's privacy with the superlative's publicity. Given those companions, the entry remains what it always was: the most vivid single document of the play's hardest decade.

It is worth pausing on why the stripped version proved so durable, because the durability is not mere laziness. The superlative serves a story audiences enjoy, the story of genius unrecognised by its heirs, and it flatters the modern reader twice over: once by confirming that taste has improved since Pepys, and once by suggesting that even great diarists make fools of themselves about great plays. Both comforts depend on treating the entry as a considered critical judgment that happened to be wrong. Treat it instead as what it declares itself to be, a first-night report from a listener whose favourite pleasures the production could not supply, and the flattery dissolves into something better, an accurate picture of how reputations actually move: not by verdicts but by occasions, revivals, adaptations, and the slow accumulation of evenings, most of them unrecorded. Pepys's evening is recorded, and its record, kept whole, is the exception that teaches the rule.

## What the Entry Still Teaches the Play's Keepers

Editors, directors, and teachers keep returning to this entry because it poses, in miniature, the standing problem of the play's performance life: the tragedy lives or dies in the speaking of it. The lesson Pepys teaches a director is not that audiences are fickle but that this play's contract with its audience is unusually verbal, and that the contract fails fastest where preparation is thinnest. A company that cannot yet ride the Queen Mab speech's acceleration, or land the potion speech's single accumulating sentence, will lose a listener of Pepys's type in the first act and never recover him, whatever the scenery does. The entry is the earliest surviving documentation of a failure mode that every subsequent century has rediscovered in its own way, from the adapters who rebuilt the text to be speakable by their companies to the editors who annotate the verse so that actors can find its joints. Pepys, meaning to record a bad night, accidentally wrote the play's permanent production note: this text punishes the under-rehearsed more swiftly than any other in the repertory.

For editors, the entry disciplines a different habit, the habit of treating early testimony as transparent. The diarist's words are evidence of the highest class, contemporary, specific, and unguarded, and they still arrive mediated by a shorthand system, a nineteenth-century decipherment, and a temperament addicted to superlatives. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford introduction models the right handling, placing Pepys within a reception narrative that includes the title pages' applause, the adapters' labour, and the critics' slow revaluation, so that no single witness carries the period. The method generalises. Pepys on Hamlet is evidence about Betterton; Pepys on Macbeth is evidence about Davenant's musical stage; Pepys on Romeo and Juliet is evidence about a first night, a company, and a listener, in that order of reliability, and an edition that quotes him in any other order misleads its readers with authentic words.

For the general reader the entry offers a final, simpler gift: it breaks the spell of inevitability that hangs over famous plays. The tragedy now arrives wrapped in the assurance of its own greatness, and the assurance does the play a disservice, because it hides the contingency of the survival it celebrates. One March afternoon the play's whole future hung on evenings as fragile as this one, and on other evenings, unrecorded, when the lines were known and the house leaned in. Pepys's boo, preserved in shorthand and published by accident of scholarship, is the scar that proves the survival was earned. The reader who knows the scar reads the play more accurately: not as a monument that was always worshipped, but as a working script that outlasted the worst review it ever received, delivered by the most honest spectator of its hardest age.

## Two Companies, One Town: The Season Around the Entry

The entry's context sharpens further when the whole town's theatrical map of that winter is laid out. London in 1662 supported two patent companies and no others. Killigrew's King's Company, playing first at the converted tennis court in Vere Street and then at the Theatre Royal in Bridges Street, held the senior actors and the great bulk of the pre-war repertory, the inheritance of the King's Men whose stock the patents divided. Davenant's Duke's Company held the future: the scenic stage at Lincoln's Inn Fields, the first professional actresses, and a manager whose genius ran to packaging. The division of the old plays, hammered out before the Lord Chamberlain, left Davenant with his own works and the nine Shakespeare titles, and Winkler and Schoch's account of the settlement shows a manager making a virtue of a thin hand, using spectacle and music to compete against a rival rich in scripts and experience. Romeo and Juliet was one of the few major tragedies in that thin hand, and its revival in the company's second season was a statement of intent: the Duke's men meant to show that the scenic stage could carry the old repertory's heaviest goods. The failure Pepys recorded was therefore not a marginal event in a full repertory. It was a flagship production faltering in public, in a small theatrical world where everyone knew everyone, and where the same spectators, Pepys conspicuously among them, crossed between the two houses comparing standards.

Pepys's own movements that season trace the comparison. He was a customer of both houses and graded them against each other with a punter's loyalty to quality rather than to companies. The King's Company gave him the Dream he damned in September; the Duke's gave him both the Macbeth line of pleasures and the Romeo and Juliet he damned in March. His diary across 1661 and 1662 reads as a league table written by its most regular spectator, and the table's volatility is the point. Verdicts swung with nights, casts, and his own digestion. A reception history built on Pepys must therefore be a history of variance, not of means: he is the period's best witness precisely because he shows how unstable the experience of the old plays could be while the new stage was abuilding, how the same tragedy could be a title-page legend in print and a liability in a draughty tennis court. The town that season was not rejecting Shakespeare. It was negotiating, production by production, what Shakespeare was going to be in the new theatrical world, and Pepys's entry is the negotiation caught at its roughest moment.

## The Adaptation Pepys Would Have Liked: Davenant's Method

The counterfactual is written in the diary itself, in the entries Pepys made at the same house in the same season. What he praised at Lincoln's Inn Fields, repeatedly and without reservation, was Davenant's method of mediation: Shakespeare taken apart and reassembled with music, dance, scenery, and modernised plotting, performed by a company that had rehearsed the result into sheen. The Law Against Lovers, which he enjoyed a fortnight before the Romeo and Juliet, wove Measure for Measure together with Much Ado About Nothing, lightened the moral weather, and gave the house singing and dancing to admire. The Macbeth he adored in later seasons kept the tragedy's spine and dressed it in operatic witches, flying machinery, and a singing Hecate. Had Romeo and Juliet reached him in an equivalent package, trimmed, scored, and polished, the diary's pattern predicts a kinder sentence; the prediction is as close as reception history gets to a controlled experiment, since house, company, spectator, and season are held constant and only the mediation varies.

The method matters beyond the counterfactual, because it explains what the unadapted revival was competing against in Pepys's head. He did not arrive on 1 March innocent of expectations; he arrived as Davenant's formed customer, trained by the house style to look for the pleasures the house brandished. The tragedy that met him had no songs to carry away, no dances to describe to his wife on the coach ride home, no machinery to marvel at, and a cast whose insecurity threw all the weight onto the one element the house style had taught him to treat as the vehicle rather than the destination: the words. Judged as a Davenant evening, the afternoon was a breach of contract. Judged as a Shakespeare revival, it was a brave company attempting the old repertory straight, a season too early. Pepys filed it under the first description, because the house had taught him the filing system. The adapters who followed, Howard with his endings and Otway with his Rome, were in effect supplying the mediation the diary's pattern demanded: they rebuilt the play into the forms its new audiences, Pepys at their head, had learned to enjoy. The entry, read against the season, is thus not merely a record of the play failing the period. It is a record of the period's most articulate spectator specifying, by his pattern of pleasures, the terms on which the play would be allowed to succeed, terms the profession spent the next decades meeting.

## Pepys's Resolutions: The Diarist Against First Nights

The entry's last clause, the resolution to attend no more first performances, deserves the same close handling as its superlative, because it shows the diarist auditing his own evidence in real time. Pepys was a connoisseur of resolutions. His diary is a ledger of vows taken, kept, broken, and renegotiated, about wine, about plays, about money, about his wife's music lessons, and the theatre resolution of 1 March belongs to that household of self-government: he identified a recurring source of wasted afternoons, named its mechanism, and legislated against it. The mechanism he named was not the play and not the company but the occasion. First performances, in his analysis, systematically under-delivered because the performers had not yet grown into their parts, and the analysis is corroborated by everything theatre historians know about the repertory system's rehearsal economy. Pepys, in other words, left his readers the correct methodological warning inside the very document they misquote: evidence gathered at first nights is evidence about preparation, and a verdict formed there should be discounted accordingly. He formed the discounted verdict anyway, and never revisited it, which is the human part of the story; but he wrote the discount down, which is the scholarly part.

The rest of that day's entry frames the theatre visit in the proportions the diarist himself assigned it. The evening closed with supper, letters written by the post, and the half-yearly casting up of his accounts, at the end of which he found, to his relief, that he stood some fifteen hundred pounds to the good in the world. The worst play he had ever heard occupies a handful of lines in a day dominated by money, family, portraiture, and self-congratulation, and the lines' brevity is itself a calibration. This was not a trauma or a conversion. It was a consumer's note, entered between the quarrel and the balance sheet, in the flattest prose Pepys commanded. Reception history, which has built a small industry on those lines, might take the hint about their weight in the life that produced them. The diarist moved on by supper. The play moved on by the next season. Only the shorthand survived to insist, three centuries later, that the afternoon had been a landmark, and the insistence is a tribute to the diary's fame rather than to the evening's importance at the time.

## A Woman in the Part: Mary Saunderson and the New Juliet

One element of the evening deserves separate weight, because it was the element no spectator in the house had grown up with: Juliet was played by a woman. Mary Saunderson, not yet twenty-five, had been among the first professional actresses in England, appearing as Ianthe in Davenant's The Siege of Rhodes when the Lincoln's Inn Fields house opened in 1661, and Downes's cast list gives her the Juliet of the 1662 revival. She would marry Thomas Betterton and become, as Mrs Betterton, the leading tragedienne of the Restoration stage, a performer whose Lady Macbeth and Ophelia were remembered for decades. In March 1662 all of that lay ahead. On the afternoon Pepys attended, the most demanding young heroine in the old repertory was being attempted by a performer with roughly a year of professional experience, in a part created for a trained boy of the old King's Men, opposite a Romeo she may barely have rehearsed with, since the whole cast was, by the diarist's testimony, out of its lines. The experiment was historic and the odds were stacked, and both facts belong in any fair account of what Pepys heard.

The casting also changed what the play could mean, in ways Pepys's diary does not remark but the reception historian must. A boy Juliet had made the tragedy's eroticism a matter of verse and convention; an actress Juliet made it a matter of presence, and the Restoration stage knew what it had gained. The adapters and managers who rebuilt the play in the following decades were building for actresses, and the tomb scene's expansion under Otway, with its waking Juliet given lines to speak over the dying Romeo, is unthinkable without the new kind of performer to sustain it. Pepys, who noted actresses in his diary with a connoisseur's attention elsewhere, left no word about Saunderson's Juliet specifically, and the omission fits the pattern of his evening: a cast that could not get its lines out could not make its novelties felt. The first woman to play the part professionally in England deserved a better first night, and the diarist's silence about her, inside a verdict about the acting generally, is one more reason to file the entry as a production report rather than a judgment on anyone's talent, hers included.

## The Play in Print While Pepys Listened

While the stage revival faltered, the printed play sat in the bookshops in a state of rude health, and the contrast between the two media is the period's reception in a single image. Romeo and Juliet had been printed in 1597, again in the enlarged second quarto of 1599, again in 1609, in the First Folio of 1623, in a further quarto of 1622 and another of 1637, and in the Second Folio of 1632; few plays of the old repertory can match that record of reissue across the very decades when its stage life was interrupted. Print kept the applause of the 1590s in circulation while the profession that had earned it was dispersed. Pepys himself was a bookman of appetite, building the library that would end as the Pepys Library at Magdalene College, and the culture he inhabited read the old plays even when it could not yet stage them well. The diarist's verdict, delivered from the stalls, therefore never represented even his own whole relationship with drama; it represented one medium's bad afternoon against another medium's steady accumulation.

The print record also disciplines the claim, often lazily attached to the entry, that the Restoration rescued a neglected play. The play had never been neglected by readers. What the Restoration had to rescue was a performance tradition, and performance traditions, unlike texts, die when their carriers disperse. The queue of adapters, Howard, Otway, Cibber, Garrick, is the tradition being rebuilt by the only method available, successive approximation, each generation's acting version standing closer to or further from the printed text as taste dictated. Pepys caught the rebuilding at its first public attempt and booed the scaffolding. The building, as the print record already promised and the next two centuries proved, was sound.

## The Desert Around the Verdict: Why One Boo Looms So Large

Pepys's sentence dominates the play's early reception for a reason that has nothing to do with its justice: the surrounding record is nearly empty. Between the applause boasted on the 1599 quarto's title page and the professional criticism of the later seventeenth century, Romeo and Juliet attracted almost no surviving commentary of any kind. Heminges and Condell included the play among the tragedies in the First Folio of 1623, an act of canon-keeping rather than criticism. Leonard Digges, in commendatory verses printed with the poems of 1640 and again in the Second Folio's preliminaries, praised the dramatist's scenes in general terms and named other plays, not this one, as the pieces audiences would not tire of. The tragedy that would become the most famous love story in English passed through the middle of the seventeenth century leaving traces in print runs, repertory lists, and allusions, but no reader's sustained judgment that has survived. Into that evidentiary desert stepped, posthumously, a diarist with a superlative, and the desert did the rest: a single voice in an empty room sounds like a chorus.

The series thesis that runs through the play's critical history, that the modern cliché assumes universal and unbroken love, finds in Pepys its necessary corrective, and the corrective is healthy exactly as far as it goes. The play was not always worshipped; it was applauded in the 1590s, printed steadily, shelved by the wars, booed on its return, rebuilt by adapters, and revalued by editors across two centuries before the modern reverence set in. Pepys's entry is the boo's preservation, and its existence refutes the cliché's smooth curve. But the corrective becomes its own cliché when the boo is promoted from specimen to verdict of the age, because the same desert that magnifies Pepys also hides the audiences who filled the house for the alternate-nights experiment, the readers who bought the quartos, and the professionals who kept choosing the play for labour. Reception history's discipline, applied to this entry, is the discipline of the denominator: one hostile witness, however vivid, divided by an unrecorded multitude. The multitude left no superlatives. It left reprints, revivals, and adaptations, the majority vote of a culture that expressed its judgments in actions rather than in shorthand.

## An Education in Public: Pepys's Judgments Across the Diary

The diarist's later entries supply the final calibration, because they show the instrument of 1 March 1662 maturing without ever being re-aimed at the play it had condemned. Across the diary's nine years Pepys saw more Shakespeare than any diarist of the age, and his responses grew more differentiated as his experience widened: the same man who dealt superlatives in 1662 was, by the diary's later seasons, distinguishing productions of the same play, revising his estimate of actors, and recording mixed evenings with mixed language. His enthusiasm for Betterton deepened into something like criticism, his pleasure in Davenant's musical Shakespeare remained constant, and his impatience with flat revivals of old plays in the rival house never softened. Nothing in the record suggests the mature Pepys would have reversed the verdict of 1662 had he returned to the tragedy; everything suggests he would have written a better one, in both senses: fairer to the text, because his ear had been educated by seven more years of verse spoken well and badly, and more dangerous to it, because his authority as a spectator had grown. The play was spared the rematch by his resolution and by the repertory's movement onward to Otway's Rome. It keeps the first-impression verdict forever, unrevised by the older and better judge its author became, a fossil of the exact moment when the least experienced Pepys met the least prepared production of the diary's whole run.

There is a closing symmetry in the dates that reception history may fairly savour. The diary ends in May 1669, when Pepys, fearing for his eyesight, laid down his pen. Within a decade Otway had rebuilt the play for the new stage, within two the professional critics had begun the long editorial argument, and within two centuries the diarist's private shorthand had become the most famous review the tragedy ever received. Pepys never knew he had written a review. He had written a memorandum to himself, in cipher, between a quarrel and a balance sheet, and the memorandum's journey from privacy to authority is the whole cautionary tale of reception evidence in one document: the words did not change, the context kept being stripped, and the authority kept growing. Keeping the context attached is the only tribute the document actually asks for.

## Reading Pepys Reading: Shorthand, Decipherment, and the Entry's Own Textual History

The entry has a transmission history as chequered as the play's, and the parallel is more than a conceit. Pepys wrote in the shorthand system published by Thomas Shelton, a tachygraphy of arbitrary signs that functioned, for the diary's first century and a half, as an effective cipher. When Magdalene College's Mynors Bright undertook the transcription in the 1820s, the work was closer to decipherment than to copying, and the first published edition, Lord Braybrooke's of 1825, printed a selection, softened and omitted with a freedom that Victorian readers accepted and modern scholarship regrets. Henry B. Wheatley's edition of the 1890s gave the public a far fuller text, and the Latham and Matthews edition of 1970 to 1983 finally printed the diary entire, with the companion volume that keys its people, places, and plays. The verdict on Romeo and Juliet thus reached its modern authority by stages exactly comparable to the tragedy's own textual journey: an early form partly illegible to posterity, successive editors recovering more of it, and a definitive modern edition establishing the full text with the apparatus to read it. The man who booed the bad quarto's descendant was preserved by his own good quarto, three centuries in the making.

The transmission matters for the entry's use, because each stage invited a different misreading. Braybrooke's readers met a Pepys curated for respectability, his theatrical asperities trimmed among much else; Wheatley's readers met the verdict in a text still silently normalised; only the readers of Latham and Matthews meet the sentence in its full construction, with the comparative and the diagnosis standing in their original shorthand order. The scholarly moral is the one this article has pressed throughout: the entry's authority is an editorial achievement, not a natural fact, and it depends on the completeness of the text edited. Quote the entry from a stripped edition, or strip it oneself in quotation, and the authority borrowed is authority the evidence does not confer. The same rule governs the play, whose modern texts rest on editors' recoveries from the quartos and the Folio. In both cases the document that seems to speak most directly from 1662 speaks through the most mediation, and honesty consists in remembering the mediation while enjoying the directness.

## The Double Life: Adapted on Stage, Restored in the Study

The century after Pepys split the play's existence in two, and the split explains how his verdict could be simultaneously deserved and irrelevant. On the stage, the tragedy lived as Otway's Rome, then as Cibber's and Garrick's palimpsest, its tomb scene crowned with an awakening its author never wrote; the play Pepys might have booed again, had he lived, was rarely the play Shakespeare printed. In the study, meanwhile, the editors were quietly restoring the original: Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709 began the modern editorial tradition, and his successors, Pope, Theobald, Johnson, and Malone, worked from the early printed texts toward the tragedy as written, arguing over its readings in prefaces and notes while the theatres played another work under its name. The diarist's complaint, that the play of itself was worthless, was thus never tested by his century in the form he made it. The stage would not present the play of itself, and the study, which did, returned the opposite verdict, enshrining the text in the canon the editors were building. Pepys judged a performance of the thing itself and damned the thing; the age declined to stage the thing itself and praised the text. Between his stalls and their studies the play survived by being two different objects, and reception history goes wrong whenever it forgets which object a given witness actually met.

That doubleness also frames the verdict's proper shelf in the play's biography. Pepys is the tragedy's great witness for the prosecution in the case of the stage, and he has no standing in the case of the study, where the evidence runs the other way from Digges onward. Later critics who quote him as though he had voted in both cases inflate a bailiff's report into a judgment of the court. The article's own verdict, argued from the entry's grammar, the season's record, the diarist's pattern of pleasures, and the profession's subsequent labour, stands as the sum of the sections before it: Samuel Pepys hated Romeo and Juliet on 1 March 1662, honestly, articulately, and on evidence his own diary shows to have been corrupted by the occasion; the hatred is a permanent document of the play's most vulnerable hour and a worthless measure of the play; and the two facts together, not the superlative alone, are what the entry has to teach.

## How a First Night Worked: Parts, Prompters, and Preparation

The diagnosis in Pepys's entry, that the cast was more or less out, describes a predictable accident of the period's working method, and reconstructing the method shows how little the accident had to do with the play. Restoration actors learned their roles from sides, manuscript copies containing only their own lines with brief cues, a practice inherited from the pre-war stage that economised on copying and guarded the company's property in its texts. Rehearsal for a revival was correspondingly thin: the company assembled the pieces in a small number of group rehearsals, leaning on the prompter, Downes's office, to feed, correct, and conduct from the book during performance. A long verse tragedy revived after twenty years, with no living tradition of its business, no actor who had played his part before, and a text whose length strained any schedule, was the repertory's hardest assignment under that system. The wonder is not that the first night wobbled. The wonder is that the period attempted such revivals at all, and the explanation is the one Winkler and Schoch supply: Davenant's company, poor in scripts, had to attempt them, because the nine Shakespeare titles and his own works were nearly all it owned.

Pepys knew the system from the audience's side as well as any man in London, which is why his resolution is phrased as a rule of attendance rather than a judgment on the troupe. Regulars understood that a play grew into itself across its first performances, that the third or fourth night offered the article finished, and that first nights were for the curious and the fashionable rather than for connoisseurs of execution. His resolution to wait for the third performance at least is a consumer applying the trade's own knowledge, and it quietly retracts, in advance, the authority of the verdict recorded two clauses earlier. A critic who announces that the evidence was gathered under conditions he henceforth refuses to repeat has told his readers exactly how to file the evidence. Posterity filed it as a thunderbolt instead. The trade's knowledge, the diarist's self-knowledge, and the superlative all stand in the same short paragraph, and the paragraph, read as the period would have read it, is a routine bad-night memorandum with a quotable first clause.

## The Music That Was Not There

Pepys's formation as a listener had a centre the tragedy could not reach, and naming it completes the account of his standards. He was a practising musician of real attainment, a singer good enough to take professional instruction, a player on the viol and flageolet, a collector of instruments and songs, and a man whose diary returns to music as other men's returns to food. The entertainments that reliably transported him were musical at their core: Davenant's operatic spectacles, with their composed scores and choreographed wonders, stood at the top of his scale of pleasure, and even spoken plays earned his warmest language when they carried songs, dances, and instrumental variety. The Duke's Company's brand, the Opera, promised that centre on its very name. A spectator formed by that brand, arriving at a tragedy whose music is a brief comic turn for hired fiddlers in the fourth act and a dance at a ball reported rather than relished, was being offered the house's plainest fare under its richest label, and then being offered it badly played. The disappointment was overdetermined before the first line was fluffed.

The play's defenders need not concede that its poverty in music is a defect; its music is in the verse, and the verse on a good night supplies what Pepys missed. But the defence only restates the production problem. Verse music is the one kind an under-rehearsed company cannot counterfeit, since it exists nowhere but in secure delivery, while Davenant's kind, scored and staged, can survive a shaky cast on the strength of its orchestra and machinery. The tragedy therefore stood at the maximum distance from Pepys's pleasures at the exact moment it most needed indulgence, and his superlative, translated out of its idiom, says something precise and almost technical: that on 1 March 1662 the play's only music failed to sound. A listener who came for music and heard none, in a house that called itself an opera, might be forgiven the word worst. Forgiveness is not agreement, and the distinction is the one this article has maintained throughout.

## The Audience Around Pepys

Pepys did not sit alone, and the entry's silence about his neighbours is the last of its lessons in the handling of evidence. Elizabeth Pepys sat beside him and left no verdict; the diary rarely reports her judgments at length, and the reception record of the afternoon is thus one spouse's prose wearing the appearance of a couple's outing. Around them sat the mixed public of the new patent houses, courtiers and citizens, the king's example having made playgoing a fashion of the restored court, and the house's receipts for that afternoon, like its temper, are unrecorded. Whether the pit shared Pepys's contempt, endured the evening politely, or enjoyed what he could not, no document says. The adapters' subsequent industry suggests the town found the property promising whatever it thought of the premiere, and the alternate-nights experiment, which required audiences for both endings, suggests curiosity at the least. The diarist's boo, solitary in the archive, may have been solitary in the house as well, or may have led a chorus whose other voices kept no shorthand diaries. Reception history cannot know, and the not-knowing is the point with which any honest account of the entry should end its evidence: the loudest surviving voice is not thereby the representative one, in 1662 any more than in the centuries of quotation that followed.

## The Broken Chain: A Company Without a Living Tradition

The deepest cause of the evening's failure was genealogical. When the theatres closed in 1642, the transmission of the old repertory from actor to actor, the living chain by which business, emphasis, and timing had passed down since the 1590s, was broken for eighteen years. The rival King's Company reassembled with genuine links to the old world: Michael Mohun and Charles Hart, its leading men, had been bred in the pre-war theatre, Hart as a boy player of women's parts with the King's Men, and their company could mount the old plays with something of the inherited manner. Davenant's Duke's Company had no such inheritance in its ranks. Its actors were young men trained since 1660, several of them gathered first by the bookseller John Rhodes, and its leading tragedian, Betterton, was a performer of genius formed entirely within the new dispensation. What the company had instead of a tradition was a manager who traded on one: Davenant, who had known the Caroline stage and encouraged the story of his own special descent from the dramatist, presented himself as the tradition's trustee, and supplied by scenery, music, and adaptation what his actors could not supply by inheritance.

A tragedy like this one exposes a broken chain mercilessly, because so much of its working knowledge was never written down. The quartos and the Folio preserve the words; they do not preserve the tempo of the balcony scene's exchanges, the business of the orchard wall, the handling of the tomb's darkness, the exact pressure at which Mercutio's raillery turns to dying. Companies carried that knowledge in bodies, and bodies had been scattered by war, plague years, and time. Saunderson and Harris were not failing a text so much as reinventing, in public and at speed, a performance that their grandfathers' generation could have mounted from muscle memory. Pepys, who had no way to price the missing inheritance and no reason to try, priced the result. His verdict is what a broken tradition sounds like from the stalls, recorded by the one spectator certain to write the sound down. The adapters who rebuilt the play over the following decades were, among other things, manufacturing a replacement tradition, new business and new emphases that could be inherited in their turn, and the seventy-year reign of Otway's version is the measure of how badly the replacement was needed.

## The Adapters' Apologies: The Rhetoric of Rescue

The generation that rebuilt the play also left a rhetoric for the rebuilding, and the rhetoric completes the period's testimony where Pepys's diary leaves it one-sided. Nahum Tate, defending his happy-ending King Lear in 1681, described the original as a heap of jewels, unstrung and unpolished, yet so dazzling in their disorder that he knew he had seized a treasure. The sentence is the adapter's manifesto for the whole era, and its doubleness is the point: the old text is a treasure and a heap, priceless and unusable, at once. Otway's work on the lovers' story embodies the same doubleness without stating it, keeping the verse he could use, discarding the structure he could not sell, and trusting the story's power to survive its transplanting to Rome. Set Tate's metaphor beside Pepys's superlative and the period's real position emerges between them. The diarist, meeting the heap unstrung on a bad night, reported the disorder. The adapters, meeting the same heap with leisure and tools, reported the jewels. Both testimonies are true to their occasions, and the occasions differ in the one variable this article keeps isolating: preparation. Pepys saw the play at its least prepared. The adapters read it at their most prepared. The reception of Romeo and Juliet in the Restoration is the story of those two encounters, and any history that keeps only the first has mistaken a first night for a verdict of the age.

## The Price of the Afternoon: Pepys as a Paying Customer

The verdict's final context is a bill. Pepys was a paying customer of a commercial theatre, and his diary's judgments belong to the literature of consumption as much as to the literature of criticism. The patent houses charged on the scale the new theatres had set, boxes at four shillings, the pit at half a crown, the galleries below that, prices that made an afternoon at the Opera a considered purchase for a clerk whose accounts, cast up that same evening, so relieved him. Pepys recorded his theatrical spending with the same care as his verdicts, grumbled at prices, compared value between the houses, and understood his custom as a transaction in which pleasure was owed. That commercial frame explains the verdict's tone better than any aesthetics. The superlative is a dissatisfied customer's superlative, the language of a man who had paid for the finished article and received the rehearsal. His resolution, to come at the third performance at the earliest, is a buyer's rule for a repeatable product, and it assumes the product would improve, which is to say it assumes the play was worth a second purchase once properly made. Few documents in the play's reception state their economics so plainly, and none of its famous verdicts makes sense entirely without them: the worst play he had ever heard was also, by the logic of his own resolution, a play whose finished version he expected to be worth half a crown of his money. The hatred and the expectation coexist in the entry, and the expectation is the half the quotation leaves behind.

The diary's wider spending pattern supports the reading. Pepys returned to productions that pleased him with a loyalist's purse, paying repeatedly for Davenant's musical Shakespeare and for Betterton's Hamlet, and his custom followed quality of execution rather than allegiance to house, author, or genre. He was, in the modern sense his diary almost invents, a reviewer whose star ratings moved box office of exactly one: his own. The tragedy lost that one customer's custom entirely, which is the measurable extent of the damage the verdict records. Everything beyond it, the implication of a period's contempt, is inference piled on a private ledger. Reception history should invoice the entry for what it actually documents: one afternoon, one customer, one company caught unprepared, and one sentence whose fame has been compounding interest ever since. The customer, to give the diary its due, was the most observant the house contained, and his bill of complaints, itemised clause by clause, remains the finest surviving audit of what the early Restoration stage owed its audience and, on that March afternoon, failed to deliver.

## The Critics' Quarrel Over One Sentence

Modern scholarship divides over the entry in ways that map neatly onto the entry's own divided clauses. Gary Taylor's Reinventing Shakespeare argues that the Restoration and eighteenth century remade the dramatist to suit themselves, finding the original texts rough, irregular, and in need of the improvements the adapters supplied; Pepys's superlative is the kind of testimony that argument treasures, the cultivated insider caught finding the undiluted article worthless. Michael Dobson's The Making of the National Poet answers from the other direction: the adapters' labour, on his reading, was a form of appropriation that built the dramatist's national monument rather than slighting it, since no one rewrites at such length what the culture holds cheap. The theatre historians, Robert D. Hume in his account of the period's repertory economics and Winkler and Schoch in their study of the Duke's Company, supply the third term the other two lack: the material record of a disadvantaged company, a punishing rotation, and first nights that everyone in the trade knew were lotteries. On their evidence the entry's theatrical half, the half with the diagnosis in it, is the half that explains the evening.

The adjudication this article defends gives each party its due and then ranks them. Taylor is right that a real current of Restoration impatience with unadapted Shakespeare runs through the period, and Pepys, who cheered Davenant's adaptations and damned two unadapted revivals in a single year, is a genuine specimen of that current; the entry cannot be explained away as mere production noise. Dobson is right that the labour market refutes contempt as the period's dominant note: Howard's two endings and Otway's seventy-year graft are not the treatment a culture gives a play it despises. Hume and Winkler and Schoch are rightest about the specific document, because the document itself, read whole, is a production report with a prejudice attached, and the production report is corroborated by everything known about the Duke's Company in its second season. The verdict on the entry follows: Pepys's hatred is real evidence about the conditions of the play's early Restoration reception, above all about the fragility of an unadapted verse tragedy before a taste formed by musical spectacle; it is weak evidence about the play, and it is no evidence at all about a consensus, since the diarist wrote for no one, influenced no one, and was contradicted in his own decade by the adapters' industry. Reception history should quote the whole sentence or none of it.

What survives the quarrel, and what gives the entry its permanent place in the play's biography, is its earliness and its honesty. It is the first extended verdict on the tragedy from the age that inherited it, and it records, without varnish, that the inheritance was not reverence. The cliché of an unbroken line of worship from 1595 forward dies on that March page, and that is the service Pepys performs for the play he hated: he proves its fame was rebuilt, not inherited, and rebuilt in the teeth of evenings like his. The tragedy that the modern world treats as the archetypal love story spent its first Restoration night being called the worst play a bright young civil servant had ever heard, by a listener who then went home and balanced his accounts and found himself fifteen hundred pounds to the good. The play survived him, his century, and his superlative. His diary, by one of reception history's better ironies, now survives partly as the tragedy's most quoted review.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: On what date did Samuel Pepys see Romeo and Juliet, and at which theatre?

He saw it on 1 March 1662, dated 1661 by the Old Style calendar his contemporaries used, at the Duke's Company's theatre in Lincoln's Inn Fields, which Pepys and London generally called the Opera. The entry records that he went with his wife Elizabeth by coach after viewing a portrait drawing of himself. The date places the performance in the second full season of the reopened public theatres, and the venue was Sir William Davenant's scenic house, converted from a tennis court and opened the previous June. The performance was the first night of the company's revival of the play.

### Q: What does Pepys's phrase 'the first time it was ever acted' actually mean?

It means the first performance of that production, not the play's premiere. Romeo and Juliet was by then some sixty-seven years old, had been acted often in the 1590s to the applause its early title pages advertise, and had appeared in three quartos and the First Folio. Pepys, a regular playgoer, was noting that the Duke's Company's revival was receiving its opening performance that day, a fact that explained the cast's insecurity in its parts and grounded his resolution to avoid first performances in future. Reading the phrase as a claim about the play's first ever staging misreads a regular's shorthand and invents a premiere that never happened.

### Q: Which company performed the production Pepys saw, and who managed it?

The Duke's Company performed it, under the management of Sir William Davenant, at its patent theatre in Lincoln's Inn Fields. The company took its name from its patron, the Duke of York, and was the junior house in the Restoration duopoly, holding Davenant's own plays and nine Shakespeare titles allotted to it after the patents were granted, Romeo and Juliet among them. Thomas Killigrew's King's Company held most of the remaining pre-war repertory. Davenant's company compensated for its thin stock of scripts with scenic novelty, music, and the first professional actresses, a strategy that shaped both the production Pepys saw and the standards by which he judged it.

### Q: Who played Romeo and Juliet in the performance Pepys attended?

John Downes, the Duke's Company's prompter, records Henry Harris as Romeo and Mary Saunderson as Juliet in his Roscius Anglicanus of 1708, the period's great backstage memoir. Saunderson, who became Mary Betterton on her marriage to the company's leading actor, was among the first professional actresses in England and had played Ianthe in The Siege of Rhodes when the Lincoln's Inn Fields house opened in 1661. She grew into the Restoration stage's leading tragedienne. On the afternoon Pepys attended she had roughly a year of professional experience, in a part written for a trained boy of the old King's Men, opposite a cast that the diarist reports was out of its lines throughout.

### Q: Why did Pepys resolve to attend no more first performances of plays?

Because the performance taught him that first nights systematically under-delivered. His resolution states its own reason: the actors were all of them more or less out of their parts, so the play he had paid to hear had never properly been performed. In the repertory system, revivals were rehearsed thinly, actors learned from sides containing only their own lines and cues, and a production settled across its first several performances. Pepys resolved to wait until at least the third performance before judging a play, applying the trade's own knowledge as a consumer's rule. The resolution is the entry's built-in warning that its verdict was formed under conditions its author distrusted.

### Q: What does it mean that the actors were 'all of them out'?

In the theatrical vocabulary of the period, an actor who was out had lost the thread of the part: lines forgotten, cues missed, speeches paraphrased or dried altogether. Pepys records that the failing was general, the whole cast being out to a greater or lesser degree, which indicates under-rehearsal rather than individual incompetence, since the same company earned his praise on other nights at the same house. The diagnosis matters because it stands between the diarist and the text he condemned: a listener cannot judge the poetry of a play whose words arrive disordered from the actors' memories, and Pepys recorded the corruption in the same sentence as the judgment it should discount.

### Q: Did Pepys ever see the play a second time or revise his verdict?

No second attendance appears in the diary, and no later entry revises the verdict. Across the journal's remaining seven years, filled with hundreds of theatre visits, Pepys never records returning to Romeo and Juliet, and the judgment of 1 March 1662 stands unsoftened and unretracted. That permanence is misleading, because the verdict was in substance a first impression formed on a first night under conditions the diarist himself blamed. His opinion of other plays shifted with repeat viewings elsewhere in the diary, but the tragedy never received a rehearing, so its private sentence was never appealed. The record preserves the least experienced version of his judgment with the fixity of a considered position.

### Q: Why does Pepys call the play the worst he had 'heard' rather than the worst he had seen?

Because hearing was his primary theatrical sense and the diary's consistent vocabulary of judgment. Pepys measured plays as spoken and sung events: verse delivered securely, wit landing in the ear, music and song carried away from the house. He was a practising musician, a singer and player on the viol and flageolet, and his entries grade performances by what reached his hearing before what reached his eye. The word also fits the failure he recorded. A cast out of its parts fails precisely at the ear, so the superlative names the sense in which the evening's damage was done. His criticism is auditory throughout, and this verdict is its extreme case.

### Q: How does his verdict on the play compare with his verdict on A Midsummer Night's Dream?

The two verdicts are twins in idiom and circumstance. On 29 September 1662, at the rival King's Theatre, Pepys saw an unadapted A Midsummer Night's Dream and called it the most insipid and ridiculous play he had ever seen in his life, language as absolute as the Romeo and Juliet superlative of the previous March. Both condemnations fell on straight revivals of Shakespeare without the mediation of adaptation, music, and spectacle that Davenant's house supplied, and both were delivered by a diarist whose superlatives were a house style rather than a measured scale. The pairing shows a pattern of taste, not a pair of considered critical positions, and it warns against quoting either verdict as a period consensus.

### Q: Which Shakespeare productions did Pepys genuinely admire?

He admired Shakespeare when the plays reached him mediated, musical, and securely played. He saw Hamlet repeatedly and recorded, in August 1661, that Thomas Betterton performed the Prince's part beyond imagination. He returned again and again to Davenant's operatic Macbeth, with its witches' dances and singing, calling it a most excellent play for the stage and the variety of its dancing and music. He praised Davenant's adaptation The Law Against Lovers at the same Lincoln's Inn Fields house barely a fortnight before the Romeo and Juliet he damned. The pattern is consistent: adaptation, music, spectacle, and rehearsal polish earned his praise, and their absence earned his superlatives of contempt.

### Q: Was the acting text of the 1662 production an adaptation of the play?

No adapted text from that production is recorded, and the evidence points to a straight, if cut, revival. Davenant's company adapted Shakespeare freely in those seasons, and his The Law Against Lovers shows the method, but the first documented rewriting of Romeo and Juliet is James Howard's version, which followed the 1662 revival rather than preparing it, and no acting copy from the March performance survives. Pepys's complaints are aimed at the play's substance and at the delivery, never at novelties of plot or ending, which supports the reconstruction of an ordinary cut acting text. The question cannot be closed beyond that balance of evidence, and honest accounts keep it open.

### Q: How did James Howard's version give the story a happy ending, and how was it scheduled?

James Howard, Davenant's brother-in-law, remade the tragedy as a tragicomedy in which the lovers survived, and the early records, summarised in the Yale edition's stage history, report that his version and the original were performed on alternate nights, so that audiences could choose their ending. The experiment documents the play's value to the company: a text worthless in the period's judgment would not have earned two acting versions in rotation. The happy ending, on the Cambridge history of the play's afterlife, failed to hold its audience, evidently because the story's meaning depended on the deaths. The scheduling remains the period's most curious tribute to the play Pepys had called the worst he ever heard.

### Q: Why did Otway's Caius Marius displace the original play for decades?

Thomas Otway's The History and Fall of Caius Marius, first performed in 1679, transplanted the lovers' story to ancient Rome, renamed them Marius and Lavinia, wove long stretches of the original's verse into its Roman political tragedy, and gave the ending a new shape drawn from the Italian novelle behind the play, in which Juliet wakes before Romeo dies and the lovers speak their farewells. The graft suited the new stage's performers and the political anxieties of its moment so well that, as the Royal Shakespeare Company's stage history records, Otway's version was the only form of the play seen on stage for roughly seventy years, until the original began its return in the 1740s.

### Q: When was Pepys's diary first published, and why does the delay matter?

The diary was first published in 1825, in a selected edition prepared by Lord Braybrooke from Mynors Bright's transcription of the shorthand, more than a century and a half after the entry was written. The delay matters because it fixes the verdict's kind of importance. Written in Shelton's shorthand for no reader but its author, the judgment influenced nothing in its own age: it depressed no stock, provoked no answer, and comforted no adapter. Its authority is entirely retrospective, the fame of a document published when its author had become a celebrity. Reception history must therefore treat the entry as evidence about one evening, preserved by accident, and not as a force in the play's seventeenth-century fortunes.

### Q: Who deciphered and edited the diary's shorthand for modern readers?

The shorthand was Thomas Shelton's tachygraphy, and its first full transcription was the labour of Mynors Bright of Magdalene College, Cambridge, in the 1820s, from which Lord Braybrooke's selected edition of 1825 was printed. Henry B. Wheatley's edition of the 1890s gave readers a far fuller text. The standard modern edition is that of Robert Latham and William Matthews, published in eleven volumes between 1970 and 1983, which prints the diary entire with a companion volume of commentary. It is through their text that the Romeo and Juliet entry can be read in its full construction, superlative, comparative, and diagnosis together, rather than in the softened forms earlier printings sometimes offered.

### Q: How do theatre historians explain the failure of that first night?

Theatre historians explain it materially rather than aesthetically. Robert D. Hume's account of the period's repertory economics, and Amanda Eubanks Winkler and Richard Schoch's study of Davenant and the Duke's Company, describe a junior company disadvantaged from the outset in scripts and experience, playing a punishing rotation in a new scenic house, rehearsing revivals thinly while its actors learned from sides. A long verse tragedy with no living performance tradition was the hardest assignment such a system could set, and its faltering first night was a predictable product of conditions. On this evidence the entry documents the machinery of the early Restoration stage straining, not a culture delivering a considered judgment on the play.

### Q: What does Gary Taylor argue about the Restoration's treatment of Shakespeare?

In Reinventing Shakespeare, Gary Taylor argues that the Restoration and the century after it remade the dramatist to suit their own tastes, finding the original texts rough and irregular and valuing the improvements supplied by adapters, so that the Shakespeare the age revered was substantially a Shakespeare rewritten. Pepys's superlative is the kind of testimony the argument naturally exhibits: the cultivated playgoer of the new age finding the undiluted tragedy beneath contempt. The counter-evidence, pressed by Michael Dobson and the theatre historians, is that adaptation was also appropriation and homage, and that Pepys praised the same company's Shakespeare warmly whenever it arrived adapted, rehearsed, and sung, a pattern that narrows what his verdict can prove.

### Q: Did Pepys's verdict influence how his contemporaries treated the play?

It influenced nothing, because no contemporary could read it. The diary was written in shorthand for its author's eyes alone and stayed unpublished until 1825, so the verdict played no part in the decisions of the managers, adapters, and audiences who determined the play's Restoration fortunes. Those decisions, on the surviving evidence, ran the other way: Howard's alternate endings within a few seasons, Otway's seventy-year graft, and the steady reprinting of the text all proceeded in ignorance of the diarist's opinion. The verdict's influence belongs entirely to the centuries after its publication, when it became the most quoted hostile judgment in the play's reception and acquired an authority its author never sought for it.
