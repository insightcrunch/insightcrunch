The New Left names a break, and the break has a date. In a single year, 1956, the two traditions that had divided the socialist world between them lost their claim on a generation's loyalty at the same moment. The first was the communism of the Soviet type: disciplined, state-bearing, armed with a doctrine that treated its own history as beyond reproach. The second was the social democracy of the parliamentary labour parties: respectable, governmental, committed to managing a reformed capitalism rather than replacing it. The current that called itself new defined itself against both at once, and the simultaneity is the point. It was not a refinement of either tradition, nor a compromise between them, but a refusal of the choice they jointly imposed, made by people who concluded that the choice itself was the obstacle. Everything the current later became, its journals and campaigns, its concepts and quarrels, its strange afterlife inside universities, descends from that double refusal and from the predicament it created: how to be a socialist when both existing vessels of socialism have been judged and found wanting.

The label can mislead, and this guide begins by correcting the picture it usually summons. The New Left was not the student revolt of the late nineteen sixties, though that revolt inherited its name and some of its personnel; the founding moment came a decade earlier, and the founders were not students but established intellectuals, historians, critics, and organisers in their thirties and forties. Nor was it one movement with branches. The British and American formations were made by different pressures, spoke in different idioms, and produced different legacies, and a good deal of confusion in the literature comes from treating them as a single phenomenon with two addresses. What they shared was the founding refusal and a set of preoccupations that followed from it: attention to culture and everyday experience as political terrain, scepticism toward the claim that the industrial working class remained the automatic agent of change, and an anti-imperialism that the older traditions had subordinated to other loyalties. The sections that follow separate the two formations, grade their Marxism, and ask what, in the end, they built.

## What the New Left was

Begin with the plain definition, because everything else in this guide is a commentary on it. The New Left is the current of socialist thought and politics that emerged from 1956, rejecting both Soviet communism and established social democracy, and characterised by attention to culture, experience, and agency, by new political agents beyond the industrial working class, and by anti-imperialism. Each clause carries weight. The dating fixes the origin in a specific crisis rather than in a mood. The double rejection fixes the identity: this was a current defined by what it refused before it was defined by anything it proposed. The attention to culture marks its distance from an older Marxism for which culture was decoration on an economic structure. The appeal to experience marks its quarrel with doctrine: against systems that deduced what workers must think from what workers were, it set what people actually felt, made, and endured. The new agents mark its sociological wager, that change might come from quarters the classical map left blank. And the anti-imperialism marks its geography, for the founding generation had watched empires defended by labour governments and liberation movements crushed by a socialist state, and drew conclusions about both.

A definition by refusal sounds like a defect, and the current's critics have always said so. But a negative definition can be exact when the thing refused is exact. In 1956 the socialist who would not follow Moscow and would not settle for the managed capitalism of the postwar settlement had, institutionally speaking, nowhere to go: no party, no international, no publishing apparatus, no recognised place in the political order. The New Left was the attempt to build that nowhere into a somewhere, with journals instead of a party card, campaigns instead of a parliamentary fraction, and a recovered Marx, the young humanist of the Paris manuscripts, instead of the catechism of dialectical materialism. Its incoherence, which was real, was the incoherence of a coalition united by refusal. Its energy, which was also real, came from the same source: having declined both orthodoxies, it had to think for itself, and it did.

### What made the New Left new?

Its novelty was positional rather than doctrinal. It occupied ground that the two older traditions had jointly vacated: outside the communist parties and their discipline, outside the parliamentary labour parties and their governmental caution, it claimed the socialist inheritance while refusing both of its institutional custodians and their respective orthodoxies.

Positional novelty explains a feature of the current that doctrinal histories find embarrassing: its members disagreed about nearly everything except the refusal. Pacifists and advocates of industrial militancy, humanists and early structuralists, Christians and atheists, historians of the seventeenth century and sociologists of the housing estate could all plausibly wear the label, because the label certified an address rather than a creed. This is why the question of whether the New Left was really Marxist, taken up in a later section, has no single answer: some of its founders were Marxists reconstructing their tradition after a catastrophe, some were socialists of other lineages who found Marx indispensable but insufficient, and some passed through the current on their way out of Marxism altogether. What holds them in one story is the year that made them, and the institutions they built together before they dispersed.

## The year everything broke

Movements rarely get a birthday, but this one comes close. In February 1956, at a closed session of the twentieth congress of the Soviet party, Nikita Khrushchev delivered a long report on the crimes of Stalin: the purges, the torture, the fabricated cases, the murdered comrades, all of it confirmed from the very rostrum of the system that had denied it for three decades. The speech was secret and leaked everywhere. For communist parties across Europe, whose members had defended the Soviet record against all accusers, the effect was not a revision but a detonation. If the centre itself now admitted that the accusations were true, then the loyalty demanded of the rank and file had been loyalty to a lie, and every local leadership that had enforced the lie stood compromised with it.

Eight months later the second blow fell, and it fell on the other side of the argument. In October 1956 Hungary rose: workers' councils, a reforming communist government under Imre Nagy, a declaration of neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact. In November, Soviet tanks crushed the rising in Budapest. The sequence mattered as much as the events. The secret speech had invited communists to believe the system could reform itself from within; Hungary showed what happened to those who acted on the invitation. A generation of party intellectuals concluded that the problem was not Stalin's personality but the structure that had produced and protected him, and that conclusion, once drawn, could not be undrawn. In Britain the Communist Party lost roughly a third of its membership within two years, and among the departing were its most distinguished historians.

The third event of that autumn concerned the other tradition. In the Suez crisis, a British government attempted to retake by force a canal nationalised by Egypt, colluded with allies to manufacture a pretext, and was forced into humiliating retreat by American financial pressure. Suez was not a socialist grievance in itself, but it completed the founding year's education. It displayed the governing consensus of postwar Britain, the bipartisan imperial reflex that a Labour government had done nothing to dismantle and much to continue, as both brutal and obsolete. For the younger founders, the lesson of the year was symmetrical: the communist option stood disgraced by Budapest, and the established alternative stood exposed by Suez as the manager of a declining empire it dared not renounce. Two disillusions, arriving together, made a founding possible.

### What triggered the New Left?

the events of 1956. The denunciation of Stalin at a Soviet party congress and the Soviet intervention in Hungary destroyed the credibility of the communist option, while established social democracy was already regarded as inadequate. The New Left is defined by rejecting both at once.

### What did the two crises destroy?

Between them they destroyed the alibi of each old tradition. Communism could no longer plead ignorance of the terror, since its own congress had confessed it, nor plead necessity, since Budapest had shown the terror's machinery turned on workers. Social democracy could no longer plead innocence of empire, since Suez had shown the imperial reflex intact under its management.

The founding generation's biographies record the year's work in miniature. The historians who left the British party had spent a decade inside its Historians' Group, producing scholarship of real distinction while submitting, with increasing discomfort, to a discipline that reached into footnotes. The younger Oxford circle around what became Universities and Left Review had no party to leave; their formation was the postwar university itself, expanded, confident, and already sceptical of the pieties of both Cold War camps. What 1956 gave both groups was each other. The ex-communist historians brought erudition, political experience, and a wound; the university group brought a generational restlessness and a feel for the new cultural terrain of affluence, television, and youth. The New Left in Britain was the institution they built at the meeting point.

It is worth pausing on the negative capability of that year, because later sections return to it. The founders did not agree on what socialism was. They agreed, with unusual precision, on what it was not: not the bureaucratic command state defended as history's verdict; not the welfare capitalism administered as history's terminus. A movement that begins by clearing ground can move fast on the cleared ground, and the early years of the British current, its journals, clubs, and campaigns, have an intellectual fertility out of all proportion to its numbers. The same ground-clearing also fixed the movement's permanent weakness. When the time came to say what should stand on the site, the coalition discovered that its members had been clearing it for different buildings.

## The wartime formation

Behind the decade inside stands a formation still earlier, and it explains why the break of 1956 fell on prepared ground. The senior founders were the children of the nineteen thirties and the soldiers of the nineteen forties. They had come to politics through the hunger marches' aftermath, the Spanish war, and the popular front's promise that fascism could be beaten by a broad alliance of the decent; they had then served, many of them, in a people's war whose official purposes were democratic and whose unofficial education was radical, an army of citizens debating politics in the barracks while the state promised them a new Britain as the price of victory. The election of 1945, which dismissed the war's leader and installed a Labour government with a mandate for transformation, was their generation's founding political experience, and its sequel was their education in disappointment at the pace of a different kind: the settlement built, the empire retained, the bomb pursued, the alliance with America mortgaging the independence the programme had promised. By the time the Cold War froze the camps, the wartime generation had already learned to distinguish between a government's professions and its practice from inside its own side's victory.

### What had the wartime years taught the founders' generation?

They had taught that ordinary people could run a war and imagine a new society while doing it, that the state would promise transformation and administer continuity, and that the gap between the two was the permanent terrain of politics, lessons the following decade's party service only deepened.

This formation distinguishes the British founders from every caricature of them as seminar innocents. Thompson had commanded tanks in Italy; Hoggart had served in North Africa; the younger Hall arrived from a Jamaica where the war had accelerated the independence argument. When such men broke with the parties of their youth in 1956, they broke as veterans of the century's central experiences, and the moral authority with which the founding generation spoke, its contempt for those who had never had to choose under pressure, was earned in that earlier school. The New Left's founding documents breathe the atmosphere of men who had seen a society mobilise itself for a common purpose and refused, ever after, to believe that apathy was the natural condition of their countrymen.

## The decade inside

The founders did not arrive at 1956 from nowhere, and the British wing's prehistory explains the peculiar authority with which it broke. For a decade before the secret speech, the Communist Party's Historians' Group had gathered an extraordinary concentration of talent: Christopher Hill on the seventeenth century, Eric Hobsbawm on the nineteenth, Thompson on the making of the working class, Rodney Hilton on the medieval peasantry, and around them a penumbra of archaeologists, economists, and teachers. The group met, argued, and published under a discipline that grew harder to bear as the members' scholarship matured. Its internal journal carried real debate; its collective projects, on the transition from feudalism to capitalism and on the standard of living in industrialisation, set agendas the profession still works on. The members were not innocents awaiting disillusion. They were practitioners who had learned, year by year, exactly which questions could not be asked aloud, which archives could not be cited, which conclusions had to be reached before the evidence was examined.

That education is the key to the violence of the break and to its quality. When the secret speech confirmed what the regime's critics had alleged, the historians experienced the admission not as news but as an indictment of their own decade of accommodations, and their response was correspondingly thorough: they did not merely resign a membership, they set about reconstructing the entire method by which they had been formed. The scholarship of the founding books is unintelligible without the decade inside, because its central move, the rescue of ordinary people's agency from the condescension of posterity and of doctrine alike, was worked out first as an argument with the members' own teachers and with themselves. The New Left's historiography was an autocritique before it was a programme.

### What did the Historians' Group achieve before the break?

It built a school. Working inside a disciplined party, its members produced the foundational debates of modern British social history, on the transition to capitalism and on living standards during industrialisation, and trained one another in an archival craft that the universities of the period rarely matched.

The group's fate carried a lesson the founders never forgot about the relation between organisation and truth. A party that had given these scholars their subject, their audience, and their fellowship had also required the periodic sacrifice of their judgment, and the sacrifice had been made so routinely that its cost was visible only when the centre confessed. Hence the founding generation's institutional minimalism afterward: journals rather than parties, argument rather than discipline, an almost superstitious fear of the apparatus. Critics called the fear a luxury of intellectuals. The founders called it the tuition they had paid, and the whole architecture of the first New Left, its refusal of membership, programme, and line, is that tuition made visible.

## The revisionist alternative

The second rejection needs its own specification, because social democracy in the nineteen fifties was not standing still. It was revising itself, and its most intelligent statement appeared in the founding year itself: Anthony Crosland's argument that capitalism had been transformed by managerial control, full employment, and the welfare state into a system whose remaining injustices could be remedied by growth and redistribution, so that public ownership, socialism's historic badge, had become a means mistaken for an end. Revisionism was the Labour right's answer to affluence, and on its own terms it was formidable: honest about the new society, fluent in its economics, and contemptuous of the fundamentalism that treated nationalisation as a creed. The founders' quarrel with it defines the second half of the double rejection precisely. They held that revisionism had described the transformation and misjudged it entirely: that a capitalism which had learned to distribute satisfactions had not ceased to distribute power, humiliation, and stunted lives, and that a socialism which stopped at equality of consumption had abandoned the field on which the new order was actually secured, the field of culture, education, and everyday experience.

### What did the revisionists conclude from the same decade?

They concluded that capitalism had changed its nature and that socialism should change its programme in reply: from ownership to equality, from transformation to growth, from class rhetoric to distribution. The founders accepted the description and rejected the conclusion, which is why theirs was an argument within socialism rather than across its border.

The confrontation clarifies what the New Left was for. Between the communist's frozen plan and the revisionist's managed contentment, the founders staked a third claim: that the point of socialism was neither the plan nor the contentment but the expansion of human agency, the capacity of ordinary people to direct their own lives in common, and that by this measure both a nationalised industry run as a ministry and a consumer society run as a market fell short in the same way. It was a demanding standard, useless for drafting manifestos and excellent for judging them, and it explains the current's otherwise puzzling combination of maximal ends and minimal programmes. The founders were not holding back a strategy they dared not state. Their strategy was the standard, applied everywhere, beginning with their own institutions.

## The affluence question

The founders' Britain posed an empirical problem that their theory had to survive: the working class was getting richer. Real wages rose through the nineteen fifties, the new estates filled with televisions and washing machines, and the revisionists drew the conclusion that the old solidarities were dissolving into privatised contentment, a diagnosis the Conservatives' electoral success among workers seemed to confirm. The question was put to a direct test at the decade's end by sociologists who studied the affluent workers of the Luton car and chemical plants, the supposed vanguard of the new contentment. Their finding cut against every party to the argument in a different place. The affluent workers were not becoming middle class in their values or their sociability; they remained instrumental collectivists, loyal to their unions as to a service, voting Labour as a calculation, and privatised in their leisure not from embourgeoisement but from the organisation of their working lives. Affluence had changed the texture of working-class existence without dissolving its separateness, which was very close to what the founders' cultural analysis had predicted against both the revisionists' celebration and the orthodox dirge.

### What did the affluent worker studies find?

They found a working class transformed in its comforts and continuous in its divisions: better housed, better fed, and more private in its pleasures, yet still enclosed within its own networks, still voting its calculation of interest, and still available to a politics that addressed its experience rather than its mythology.

The episode deserves its place because it shows the current's method earning its keep. Where the orthodox awaited an immiseration that never arrived and the revisionists announced a contentment that never quite formed, the founders' insistence on describing experience whole, in the factory and the front room together, produced the account the evidence eventually ratified. It also taught the complementary lesson the founders were slower to learn: that a class can be accurately described and still not be mobilised by the description, because between the sociology and the politics stands the question of organisation, on which the current's account remained, to the end, a brilliant improvisation.

## The double rejection

The namable claim of this guide can now be stated in its strongest form. The New Left is defined not by any positive doctrine but by the simultaneous rejection of two existing options in a single year, which is why its content varied so widely between countries while its negative definition remained constant. Doctrines travel badly; refusals travel well. A French existentialist, a British historian, and an American campus organiser shared no programme, but each could recognise, in the others, the same two closed doors. The double rejection was the passport each carried into the others' countries, and it explains the otherwise puzzling speed with which the label crossed the Atlantic and the Channel while the positive content stayed stubbornly local.

Consider what each half of the rejection excluded. Rejecting Soviet communism excluded the theory of the vanguard party as the unique form of socialist organisation, the subordination of national movements to a centre, the doctrine that history's stages were known and their order guaranteed, and the treatment of dissent as pathology. Rejecting established social democracy excluded the theory that socialism was the sum of welfare reforms administered indefinitely, the identification of the national interest with the interests of the labour movement, the reflex that defended empire as a trust, and the treatment of capitalism's cultural order as somebody else's department. What remained after both exclusions was a space with certain fixed stars but no map: the conviction that capitalism deformed human relations at every level, that ordinary people's experience was a form of knowledge, that culture was a material force, and that any socialism worthy of the name had to be made by its subjects rather than delivered to them.

### How does a refusal become a definition?

By elimination under pressure. When both available vehicles of a tradition are judged unfit in the same season, those who will neither board nor abandon the journey must describe themselves by the judgments they share. The refusal was specific, dated, and public, and it proved more durable as a bond than any programme the coalition later attempted.

The double rejection also predicts the movement's characteristic arguments. A current defined against two orthodoxies will fight on two fronts permanently, and the New Left did: against the communist inheritance it defended experience, culture, and the young Marx; against the social democratic inheritance it defended the possibility of a society beyond managed capitalism. Its theoretical production bears the marks of that two-front war. The recovery of the early Marx armed it against the doctrine of stages and necessity. The turn to culture armed it against the doctrine that raising living standards exhausted the socialist case. Each front generated genuine discoveries, and each generated its own exaggerations, because an argument built to refute a custodial orthodoxy tends to overcorrect. The humanism of the first New Left overtrusted experience; the theoreticism of its successor overtrusted systems. Both overcorrections are legible as moves in the same two-front war.

Finally, the double rejection explains the movement's relationship to Marx himself. The founders were not, in the main, innovators who happened to cite Marx; they were readers who believed the tradition's custodians had buried the author under the doctrine. Hence the characteristic gesture of the early current: a return to texts, to the manuscripts of 1844, to the Grundrisse when it became available, to a Marx who wrote about alienation, senses, and human powers rather than about the falling rate of anything. That gesture connected the New Left to the broader current treated in this series under the heading of [Western Marxism: the complete guide](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/), and it gave the movement's Marxism its distinctive texture: philological, humanist, and oriented to consciousness. Whether that texture was a recovery or a reinvention is one of the standing arguments of the literature, and a later section takes it up directly.

## Two journals and a merger

A movement without a party needs another skeleton, and the British New Left found its skeleton in print. Its first organ was an act of internal dissent. During 1956, while still members of the Communist Party, the historians E. P. Thompson and John Saville produced a cyclostyled journal called The Reasoner, arguing from inside for democratic reform of the party and an honest reckoning with the secret speech. The party suspended the journal; its editors resigned, after Hungary, with thousands of others. Relaunched independently as the New Reasoner, the journal carried the ex-communist generation's reckoning into the open: Thompson's long manifesto for a socialist humanism, historical essays, documents of the Polish and Hungarian opposition, and a running argument that the tradition had to be remade from its moral foundations upward.

The second organ came from a different world. Universities and Left Review grew out of an Oxford discussion circle of younger socialists, among them Stuart Hall, Raphael Samuel, Charles Taylor, and Gabriel Pearson, and its first issue appeared in the spring of 1957. Where the New Reasoner looked backward to a tradition to be rescued, Universities and Left Review looked sideways at a society to be understood: affluence, advertising, the new media, the changing texture of working-class life in the age of the television set and the coffee bar. Its pages treated a film or a magazine as seriously as a strike, a bias that the older generation initially found frivolous and later found unavoidable. The two journals embodied the two halves of the coming formation: the ex-communists carried the argument about Stalinism and the socialist tradition; the university group carried the argument about capitalism's new cultural forms.

In January 1960 the two journals merged to launch New Left Review, with Hall as its first editor. The merger was itself a thesis: that the reckoning with the old left and the analysis of the new capitalism were one project, requiring one address. Around the journal grew a penumbra of New Left clubs in towns and universities, reading groups, and a famous coffee house in Soho that served as the movement's informal headquarters. For roughly two years the formation had something like a national presence: a review, a network, a campaign in nuclear disarmament, and a shared vocabulary. It had no membership card, no discipline, and no agreed programme, by design. The founders had seen what discipline did to truth in the old parties and preferred a movement held together by argument. The preference was honourable, and it was also, as events showed, a wager about how long argument alone can hold a coalition together.

### Who founded the British New Left?

Two generations in succession. The first comprised communist intellectuals, above all the historians around Thompson and Saville, who broke with their party over Hungary and carried its scholarly culture into exile. The second comprised younger university socialists around Hall and Samuel, formed by affluence and empire rather than by the party's internal wars.

### Why did the two journals merge?

Because each held half of the founding insight. The ex-communist journal understood why the old left had failed but wrote for a shrinking world of party argument; the university journal understood the new society but lacked historical depth. A single review promised the historians an audience and the younger writers a tradition.

Count the founders and a common error dissolves. These were not students in revolt against their elders' world; the senior figures were the elders, veterans of wartime service and a decade of party scholarship, and the younger were recent graduates working as teachers and researchers. The British New Left began as an insurrection of the tradition's own intellectual cadre, which is why its arguments were fought at such a high level and why its wounds were so personal. When Thompson and his successors quarrelled, as they soon did, they quarrelled as men who had shared a formation and lost it twice.

## The founding generation and its books

Between the late nineteen fifties and the middle sixties, the British formation produced a shelf of books that permanently altered several disciplines, and any account of the movement's Marxism has to reckon with the fact that its theory was largely written as history and criticism. Richard Hoggart's study of working-class culture in Leeds, published in 1957, treated the magazines, songs, and speech of an industrial community as evidence about what capitalism was doing to inner life. Raymond Williams, a Welsh railwayman's son teaching in adult education, published in 1958 a history of the idea of culture itself and followed it with an argument that the long revolution of literacy and democracy was the real drama of modern British life. Thompson's great work of 1963 reconstructed the making of the English working class as an act of human agency, men and women making themselves in struggle, against every account, Marxist or otherwise, that treated them as the reflex of economic forces.

Three features unite these books beneath their differences. Each insists that experience is evidence: that how people live a structure is part of the structure's reality, not a subjective mist over it. Each treats culture as a whole way of life rather than a garnish, so that a novel, a chapel, or a music hall becomes a document of the social order. And each performs the recovery of agency against the determinisms of both orthodoxies, the communist doctrine of stages and the social democratic doctrine of gradual administration. If the New Left had a doctrine in these years, this was it, and it was held most powerfully not as propositions but as a method demonstrated on archives. The method had a name in Thompson's later polemics: socialist humanism, the insistence that Marxism is a tradition of moral argument about human possibility or it is a technique of rule.

### What did the founding generation bring from the old left?

It brought the old left's virtues stripped of its obedience: historical scholarship of professional standard, a lived knowledge of party organisation and its corruptions, wartime experience, and a conviction, learned inside the machine, that doctrine enforced by discipline ends by deforming both the doctrine and the people who enforce it.

Their institutional base deserves emphasis, because it shaped the work's audience and tone. Williams and Thompson both taught in adult education, in the extra-mural classes run for trade unionists, housewives, and servicemen returning to study, and the idiom of the early New Left, lucid, argumentative, allergic to jargon, was formed in rooms where obscurity lost the audience by the second week. A Marxism addressed to adults who had worked all day had to earn its abstractions. Later, as the founding generation moved into the expanding universities, the idiom changed with the audience, and something of the movement's original pact with a lay public was lost. The trajectory from the adult education classroom to the seminar room is the biography of the whole formation in miniature.

## Empire comes home

The founding year's third crisis was imperial, and the empire's aftershocks reached the metropolis itself within two years. In the late summer of 1958, white mobs attacked Black residents in the Notting Hill district of London and in Nottingham, and the riots confronted the younger founders with a fact their analysis had underweighted: that the imperial order had a domestic address, that the racial hierarchy organised abroad was reproduced in the housing queues, lodging-house signs, and police practices of the capital. Hall, who had come to Britain from Jamaica as a Rhodes scholar at the decade's start and who edited the university journal, wrote about the riots with a precision that marked the current's turn. The empire was not an external policy to be opposed at meetings; it was a structure of feeling at home, and the migrants arriving from the Caribbean were not its symbols but its citizens, remaking the working class whose culture the founders had set out to describe.

This strand matters to the grading of the current's anti-imperialism, which is sometimes dismissed as solidarity at a distance. In the British case the distance collapsed early. The founders' milieu overlapped with the first organisations of Caribbean political life in London, with the journals and campaigns that followed the riots, and with the slow recognition that the long revolution of British democracy would be fought partly over who counted as belonging to the people whose history the historians were writing. The recognition was imperfect and its working out took decades, in which the Birmingham centre's later work on race and policing played the leading part. But the seed was planted in the founding years, and it distinguishes the British current's anti-imperialism from a merely diplomatic anti-Americanism: it began, unusually early, as an argument about the constitution of the metropolitan working class itself.

### Why did events at home change the current's map of empire?

Because they abolished the geography on which the older anti-imperialism rested. If the colony's hierarchy operated in a London lodging house, then solidarity was not foreign policy but a domestic division inside the working class, and the analysis of British culture and the analysis of empire became, permanently, a single inquiry.

The point connects to the current's most quoted later formulation, Hall's insistence that race is the modality in which class is lived. That sentence belongs to the Birmingham years, but its premise was set in 1958: that in the post-imperial metropolis, the experience the founders had placed at the centre of their method could not be described in a vocabulary from which race was absent. A movement founded on the recovery of experience had acquired, by its own logic, an obligation it could not decline.

## The Trinidadian in London

One figure stands behind the British formation's understanding of both Marxism and empire like a presiding spirit, and his absence from the standard rosters is itself a datum. C. L. R. James, the Trinidadian historian of the Haitian revolution, had spent the nineteen thirties in Britain building a Trotskyist current, writing masterpieces on cricket, revolution, and Hegel, and arguing, with his American collaborators, that the Soviet Union was a state capitalism and that the self-activity of ordinary people, workers occupying factories, slaves making revolutions, was the tradition's true subject. Expelled from the United States in the anti-communist years, he returned to London in the middle fifties and then to an independent Trinidad, and his London circle touched the founding generation at several points: Hall knew and learned from him, and the themes the founders made their own, the creativity of the crowd, the refusal of stages, the insistence that the colonised were history's authors and not its objects, had been James's themes for two decades.

### What did the Trinidadian exile contribute?

He contributed the proof of concept. A Black Marxist from the colonised world had already written the history the founders were attempting, of ordinary people making themselves in revolution, and had already broken with every custodianship on offer, demonstrating that the double rejection had a predecessor and a method.

James matters to this guide for a further reason: his career is the comparison case for the whole formation. He had performed the double rejection avant la lettre, against Stalinism from the thirties and against liberal empire from birth, and his reward had been the margin, the small group, the unpublished manuscript, the influence exerted through persons rather than institutions. The founders, arriving at his positions by their own road in 1956, built the institutions he never had, and the difference between his fate and theirs measures what the conjuncture gave them: a mass audience, a university expansion, a publishing world. Genius is not the variable; the audience is. A tradition should be honest about the luck of its timing, and the James case is where the British New Left's luck stands out in the record.

## Disarmament: the first mass form

The new current acquired a mass body through the campaign against nuclear weapons. The Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, founded at the start of 1958, and the Easter marches from the weapons establishment at Aldermaston to London, drew tens of thousands onto the roads: students, clergy, trade unionists, teenagers, and a conspicuous contingent of the new journals' writers, who marched, spoke, and organised. For the New Left the campaign was several things at once. It was a school of extra-parliamentary politics for a generation that had been offered only the ballot and the party card. It was proof that a moral argument, made outside the established channels, could move people whom no party had reached. And it was a working model of the politics the founders imagined: single-issue in form, universal in implication, since the bomb concentrated every question about science, empire, the state, and the value of human life into a single symbol.

The campaign also administered the formation's first lesson in limits. In 1960 the Labour Party's conference voted for unilateral disarmament, and its leader promised to fight, and fight again, to reverse the decision, which was duly reversed the following year. The episode taught the younger founders that capturing a resolution was not capturing a party, and it accelerated a drift already visible in the journals: away from the attempt to renovate the old institutions and toward the construction of a politics alongside them. Meanwhile the direct action wing, organising mass sit-downs through the Committee of 100, pressed the campaign toward civil disobedience and split its liberal centre. By the middle sixties the disarmament movement had ebbed, but it had permanently marked the current's style: moral witness, symbolic action, suspicion of the official channels, and a constituency younger and more various than any the old left had assembled.

### Why did disarmament give the current its first mass form?

The bomb was the one issue on which the double rejection could march. It indicted the communist state's arsenals and the Western alliance's strategy in a single gesture, required no agreement on doctrine, and admitted everyone the old parties had bored or expelled. A coalition defined by refusal found, in refusal of the bomb, its crowd.

Looked at coldly, the disarmament years also fixed the formation's demographic centre of gravity outside the industrial working class it still hoped to win. The marches were strongest where the universities, the churches, and the professions supplied recruits; the great unions, with honourable exceptions, kept their distance or their hostility. The founders noticed. Their writings of the period circle repeatedly around the question of what political subject could possibly correspond to a politics of culture, education, and peace, and the answers they rehearsed, youth, the new salariat, the colonised world, anticipate the debates of the following decade. The New Left did not set out to substitute new agents for the working class; it discovered, campaign by campaign, that the substitution was happening underneath it, and its best minds spent years deciding whether to mourn the fact or theorise it.

## The editorship changes hands

In 1962 the founding editorship ended. Hall departed, and control of New Left Review passed to a younger group around Perry Anderson, who remade the journal in a different image. The change is the hinge of the British story, and it is usually told as a coup; it is better understood as a changing of the formation's centre of intellectual gravity, from the historians and their England to a generation formed by continental philosophy and comparative history. Under the new editorship the review became the great importing house of European Marxism into English. Issue by issue it translated and expounded the traditions the first New Left had barely known: the Frankfurt School's critique of mass culture, Sartre's later philosophy, Gramsci beyond the selections, Althusser's structural reading of Marx, the Italian workerists, the German and French debates entire. A reader who took only the review could receive, across the nineteen sixties, a systematic education in the Marxism of the continent, much of it unavailable in English from any other source.

The achievement was enormous and the price was real. The journal's centre of attention moved from the moral and historical texture of British life to the high theory of the European tradition, and its prose changed accordingly, becoming denser, more systematic, and more remote from the adult education idiom of the founders. Thompson, from outside, watched the institution he had co-founded turn into something he regarded as a betrayal of the founding purpose: an import agency for foreign systems, he charged, where the original project had been to remake an indigenous socialism. The quarrel was never only personal. It posed the standing question of the whole current: whether the renovation of Marxism required recovering a national tradition's buried resources or submitting that tradition to the discipline of the most advanced theory available anywhere. Both answers were Marxist. They were not compatible in one journal, and the journal chose.

### What changed when the editorship changed hands?

The journal changed its question. Under its first editor it asked how a socialist sensibility could be formed inside an affluent, media-saturated Britain. Under its second it asked what the most developed Marxist theory, wherever produced, could explain about the whole history of capitalist society, Britain included as a case.

Historians of the movement sometimes mark the change by distinguishing a first New Left, the formation of 1956 to about 1962, from everything that followed, and the distinction does real work. The first formation was a movement with a journal; what succeeded it was a journal with a diminishing movement attached. Yet the successor performed a service the movement could not have performed for itself. When the student explosion came at the decade's end, it found waiting for it, in English, an entire library of continental Marxism that the importing years had naturalised. The second phase of the New Left, in Britain and far beyond, thought with concepts the review had spent a decade translating. Institutions outlast the arguments that build them, and the translation programme is the clearest case in this whole history of an institution quietly deciding what a later generation would be able to think.

## The quarrel over the English revolution

The new editorship arrived with a thesis, and the thesis detonated the formation's deepest quarrel. In a series of essays in the middle nineteen sixties, Anderson and Tom Nairn argued that England's bourgeois revolution had come too early and been left incomplete: the bourgeoisie, arriving before its class had matured, had made its peace with the landed aristocracy, absorbed its manners, and never carried through the thoroughgoing transformation of state and ideology that France had known. From that incompletion everything else was said to follow: an empiricist intellectual culture innocent of system, a conservative labour movement content with corporate bargaining inside an unreformed order, a working class hegemonised, in the Gramscian vocabulary the essays imported, by the values of its rulers. The theses were offered as the explanation of Britain's long decline and of its socialism's peculiar timidity, and they implied a strategy: since the national tradition was the problem, deliverance would have to come from outside it, from the continental theory the journal was importing.

Thompson's reply, published in 1965 under the title of the peculiarities of the English, remains one of the great polemics of the British left, and it answered the theses on every level at once. As history, he charged, the account selected its evidence to fit a model borrowed from an idealised France and Germany, countries whose revolutions had failed as often as England's had succeeded. As method, it treated classes as things with fixed essences rather than as relationships made in struggle, committing exactly the sin of reification the movement existed to oppose. As politics, it licensed contempt for the actual traditions of the English working class, its chapels, unions, and radicalisms, in the name of a maturity those traditions supposedly lacked. And beneath the argument ran a generational grief: the founding historian was being told, by the journal he had helped to found, that the history he had spent his life recovering was a record of failure.

### What was at stake in the quarrel over the English revolution?

Nothing less than where a British socialism could come from. If the national past was a story of incompletion and deference, renewal required foreign theory and a rupture with inherited radicalism. If that past held real resources of resistance and self-making, renewal required recovering them. The two answers divided the formation permanently.

The quarrel's long result was paradoxical. The theses lost the argument among historians, where the evidence for a simple story of bourgeois failure never assembled itself, yet they won the institution, since the journal and its publishing house carried the importation programme forward for decades. Thompson withdrew to his Warwick centre for the study of social history, gathered younger scholars around the methods of history from below, and in 1978 published a vast philosophical polemic against the structural Marxism then ascendant, the quarrel's final escalation. The formation's two halves thus ended by founding two different academic fields, social history and the systematic theory of the review, each descended from the same founding year and each regarding the other as the road not taken. A reader who wants the whole British story has to read both descendants, which is why this guide treats the quarrel as the formation's central document rather than as a family embarrassment.

## The break-up of Britain

The English quarrel had a long fuse, and its last charge detonated a decade later in an unexpected place. Tom Nairn, the co-author of the incompletion theses, extended their logic in the nineteen seventies to the multinational structure of the British state itself: the archaic, composite monarchy-state that the incomplete bourgeois revolution had preserved was now, he argued, breaking up under the pressure of the national movements of Scotland and Wales, and the left's task was to welcome the dissolution of the old order's shell rather than defend a unity that had always been the form of its rule. The argument carried the founding generation's quarrel into a second generation: Thompson and the English historians heard in it the old contempt for the national popular tradition, now armed with a theory of the state, while the younger readers heard the first adequate account of a kingdom visibly decomposing around them.

### Why did the national question reopen the founding quarrel?

Because it posed the same choice in a new arena. If the British state and its culture were the unreformed shell the theses described, its dissolution was an opportunity; if the national tradition held the resources of resistance the historians had recovered, its defence was a duty. The formation's two answers had each acquired a country.

The episode's interest for this guide is that it shows the incompletion framework doing real predictive work, whatever its standing among historians. A state whose modernisation the theses had declared arrested did, in the following decades, shed an empire, enter and leave a European union, and devolve its government to its constituent nations, each event legible in the framework's vocabulary even to those who rejected the framework. Theories in this tradition are not falsified like hypotheses; they are worn out or borne out by the company they let their holders keep with events, and by that standard the quarrel of the nineteen sixties remained productive long after its principals had stopped speaking.

## The ancestors the founders claimed

Every renovation invents a pedigree, and the British founders' pedigree was a native radical tradition recovered against the charge of incompletion. Its stations were the Levellers and Diggers of the revolutionary seventeenth century, the corresponding societies and the unstamped press of the early nineteenth, the Chartists, and above all William Morris, the designer-poet whose lectures and romances had fused the critique of industrial capitalism with a vision of unalienated work a generation before the manuscripts of 1844 were available to be recovered. Thompson's first large book, published in 1955, was a biography of Morris, and its argument was the founding programme in embryo: that the English tradition contained, in Morris, a Marxism already moralised, already centred on the quality of life and labour, which the twentieth century's custodians had forgotten and the founders proposed to remember. When the architects of the incompletion theses later told the founders that the national past was a record of failure, the reply wrote itself, because the founders had spent their apprenticeships proving the past contained another England entire.

### Which ancestors did the founders claim?

They claimed the losers of English history as a usable past: the Levellers and Diggers, the radical artisans and Chartists, and supremely Morris, in whom they found a native Marxism of unalienated labour and joyful work that answered the imported doctrine of stages on its own ground.

The pedigree was not antiquarianism. It did strategic work in the formation's central quarrel, supplying the evidence that a British socialism had indigenous resources and need not be imported as a finished system, and it did methodological work too, since the recovery of lost traditions of resistance was exactly the operation the founding historians performed on the working class itself. The tension should be noted honestly: a movement that defined itself by a break with the pasts on offer was simultaneously in the business of manufacturing a past of its own, and the manufacturing was no less selective for being scholarly. The founders would have pleaded guilty and cited the alternative: the official pasts, Stalinist and Labourist alike, were also manufactured, with the difference that theirs had footnotes and admitted its argument in public.

## The European echoes

The founding was British in its institutions, but the crisis of 1956 was European, and the founders knew themselves to be one national case of a general earthquake. In Poland the same October that brought tanks to Budapest brought a reforming communist leadership to power in Warsaw by the threat of resistance, and Polish revisionism, the philosophy of Leszek Kolakowski and the economics of the market socialists, supplied the British journals with documents and with hope. In Yugoslavia, outside the bloc since 1948, the Praxis philosophers were building a humanist Marxism of self-management that paralleled the British recovery of the young Marx from an entirely separate starting point. In France, where the communist party had met the secret speech with denial and the Algerian war with complicity, the dissident journals Arguments and Socialisme ou Barbarie carried the same double refusal in a different idiom, and the phrase nouvelle gauche circulated there before it was naturalised in English. And over the whole scene stood the decade's most translated heresy, the Yugoslav exile Milovan Djilas's anatomy of the new class, which argued in 1957 that the communist bureaucracy had become a possessing class in its own right: the single most economical statement of what the secret speech had confessed and Hungary had proved.

### Was the founding a British event or a European one?

The crisis was European and the disillusion general; the institutions were British. Other countries produced the same refusal in journals and circles, but the particular combination of ex-communist scholarship, university expansion, and a mass disarmament campaign gave the British case its durability, which is why the label's history is usually written from London.

The traffic among these cases was one of the founding generation's educations. Translations, conferences, and exiles carried arguments from Warsaw and Belgrade and Paris into the London journals, and carried the English historians' work outward in return. The lesson the founders drew was that the tradition's crisis was structural rather than national: wherever the custodians had confused the doctrine with their custody of it, the same reckoning was owed. That conviction underlies the current's internationalism, which was less a programme of solidarity than a recognition of simultaneous biography, and it is the reason the label, though coined for the British case, fitted so many others closely enough to travel.

## The American formation

Cross the Atlantic and the pressures change their order. The United States had no mass communist party to break with in 1956 and no labour party to be disappointed by; its left had been broken earlier, by the anti-communist purges of the late nineteen forties and fifties, which had emptied the unions, the universities, and the culture of an organised socialist presence. The American New Left therefore formed not from a double rejection performed in a single year but from a vacuum filled by successive shocks: the sit-ins and freedom rides of the civil rights movement from 1960, which taught white students in the South and then the North that the republic's promises were unenforced; the nuclear fear that organised the early campus groups; and then, overwhelming everything, the escalation of the war in Vietnam from 1965, which gave the formation its mass scale, its urgency, and eventually its destructive fever.

The organisational vessel was Students for a Democratic Society, refounded in 1960 from the student wing of an older socialist league, and its founding document, the Port Huron Statement of 1962, remains the American formation's canonical text. Drafted principally by Tom Hayden and adopted at a retreat in Michigan, the statement is remarkable for what it is not. It is not a Marxist document; its vocabulary is democratic and moral, its grievances are apathy, bureaucracy, the corporation, the arms race, and the racial order, and its remedy is a democracy of participation in which people share in the decisions determining the quality and direction of their lives. Marx enters its pages as one resource among others, and the working class appears without the automatic centrality the older tradition assigned it. The American formation, in short, began where the British founders' successors had not yet arrived: with new agents, new issues, and a theory to be built rather than inherited.

### Why did the American formation take a different road?

Because its inheritance was absence rather than orthodoxy. Where the British founders had to escape a party and a doctrine, the Americans had to invent a left in a country whose organised socialism had been destroyed a decade earlier, and they built it from the materials at hand: the civil rights movement's example, the campus, and democratic moralism.

### What did the Port Huron Statement ask for?

It asked that democracy stop meaning periodic voting and start meaning participation: that individuals share in the social decisions determining their lives, in the workplace, the university, and the polity, and it indicted apathy, the corporation, and the military establishment as the powers such a democracy would have to displace.

The difference in starting points explains the difference in products. The British formation, born among historians, made books and a review; the American formation, born among organisers, made a movement and a style of politics. Where the British argued about the seventeenth century, the Americans argued about the draft. The traffic between them was real but one-directional in theory: the American movement consumed European Marxism hungrily in translation as it radicalised, while contributing back an example of mass student revolt that electrified the European formations in 1968. By then each had become the other's legend, which is a relationship that survives poorly the discovery that the legend is also a country with its own arguments.

## The letter that attacked the labour metaphysic

The American formation received its most influential theoretical gift early, and from an unlikely donor: a Texan sociologist in his forties, already famous, who held no party allegiance and owed the Marxist tradition nothing but attention. C. Wright Mills had spent the nineteen fifties anatomising his country's power structure, its interlocking elites of corporation, army, and state, and arguing that the intellectual's vocation was to confront that structure with reason. In 1960 the new British review published his open letter to the New Left, and the letter did two things at once. It gave the young formation a transatlantic blessing from an established scholar, and it attacked, with a bluntness no insider had managed, the assumption on which the older Marxism's strategy rested: that the industrial working class was the appointed agent of historical change.

Mills named that assumption the labour metaphysic and called it a legacy of Victorian Marxism, a belief carried into conditions that no longer supported it. In the advanced countries, he argued, the working class had been integrated, its organisations bureaucratised, its revolutionary capacity a faith rather than a finding. To wait for its awakening was to excuse oneself from politics. The possible agents of transformation were elsewhere: among the young intelligentsia forming in the expanding universities, among the movements of the colonised world, wherever people were acquiring the education and the grievance together. The letter's sociology can be disputed, and the following section's subject, the theory of new agents, disputes and refines it still. Its rhetorical effect is not in dispute. A generation on two continents read permission to stop apologising for not being the class their doctrine had assigned to history, and to ask instead who might actually move.

### Why did Mills attack the labour metaphysic?

Because he judged it an empirical failure preserved as a loyalty test. The industrial working class of the advanced countries had been incorporated into the postwar order, he argued, and a strategy that waited on its revolutionary awakening condemned the left to passivity while other agents, students, intellectuals, and the colonised, were already in motion.

Mills died in 1962, before the American formation had a mass existence, and he never had to answer for the uses made of his letter. Some of those uses were careless: an argument that the working class was not automatically revolutionary became, in weaker hands, the claim that it was politically negligible, a claim the British founders, with their roots in labour history, never accepted and events have never supported. But the letter's core move survives every correction. It separated the question of who benefits from change from the question of who can make it, and insisted the second question be answered by looking. That insistence, more than any single text, is the hinge between the old left's Marxism and the new one's.

## Participatory democracy

If the American formation had a characteristic political demand, it was participatory democracy, the phrase at the centre of the Port Huron Statement and the banner under which its organisation understood itself. The demand sounds modest and is not. It holds that a democracy in which citizens periodically choose among managers, while the decisions that shape their days are taken in corporate boards, administrative agencies, and military staffs, is a democracy of form without substance. Against it the statement set a standard: that the individual share in those social decisions determining the quality and direction of his life, that politics be brought out of the isolation of the voting booth into the institutions where life is actually governed, the workplace, the university, the city, the army's purposes themselves.

The practice preceded the phrase. In the Southern civil rights movement, in the organising tradition associated with Ella Baker and carried by the student volunteers of the freedom campaigns, the Americans had seen a politics in which the people affected by a decision made it, in mass meetings where sharecroppers spoke the strategy into being. Participatory democracy was the generalisation of that experience into a political theory, and its force came from the generalisation's audacity: what had worked in a Mississippi church basement was proposed as the constitution of a good society. The demand also supplied the formation's internal constitution, or attempted to. A movement preaching participation organised itself by consensus and rotation of tasks, with consequences examined below: extraordinary moral energy, a crippling slowness in decision, and a vulnerability to the most committed, who could always outlast the meeting.

### What does participatory democracy demand in practice?

It demands that decision rights follow consequences. Wherever a decision shapes people's working and civic lives, those people gain a standing share in making it: in firms, universities, and municipalities as in parliaments. It therefore indicts every arrangement in which formal political equality coexists with private government in the economy and the administration.

The idea's weaknesses were visible early and its critics, friendly and hostile, catalogued them. It named a procedure more readily than an outcome: it could say how decisions should be made without settling what should be decided about ownership, investment, or war. It scaled badly, working by acclamation in a chapter meeting and by exhaustion in a national convention. And it carried an unexamined premise that participation is its own education, true often enough to sustain the faith and false often enough to wreck organisations that relied on it. What cannot be said is that the demand was a pose. It organised real sacrifices, it described something real about the poverty of merely representative politics in an administered society, and it remains the term through which the American formation is remembered wherever its name survives.

## The organising years in America

Between the founding statement and the mass movement stands a forgotten middle act, and restoring it corrects the picture of the American formation as a campus affair. From 1963 the student organisation sent its cadres off campus, into the poor neighbourhoods of northern cities, in a project that proposed to build an interracial movement of the poor: community unions organising the unemployed, tenants, and welfare recipients around the grievances of rent, rats, and relief. The organisers lived in the neighbourhoods on subsistence, held endless meetings in the participatory manner, and attempted to generalise the Southern movement's method into a strategy for the industrial North. The projects mostly failed on their own terms; the poor did not unite across race on the organisers' timetable, the funders withdrew, and the cadres returned to the campuses radicalised and exhausted. But the episode fixed the American formation's character at its best: a moral seriousness that treated its own members' comfort as an obstacle, and a theory of organising that began from the grievances people already had rather than the consciousness they were supposed to acquire.

The same years taught the counter-lesson. Organising the powerless by participation is slow, and the war was not slow. As the escalation in Vietnam quickened from 1965, the formation's centre of gravity shifted from the neighbourhood to the campus and the demonstration, from building to protesting, and its slogan travelled with it, from participation to resistance. The shift was understandable and it was fatal to the founding project: a movement that had proposed to remake democracy by patient local construction found that history had offered it a larger stage and a shorter script. The later factional catastrophe was prepared in those years, when the habits of emergency displaced the habits of construction and the most impatient interpretation of the emergency began to outbid every other.

### What did the community organising projects attempt?

They attempted to prove participatory democracy as a method among adults rather than students: to organise the poor of the northern cities, across the colour line, around rents, welfare, and housing, into community unions that would make their members the authors of the demands made in their name.

## The two New Lefts

The argument of this guide has insisted that the British and American formations differed in origin, composition, and product, and the point is best fixed by laying the two side by side. The comparison is the brief's findable artifact for this article: **The two New Lefts**, a table comparing the formations on the dimensions that decide what a political current can do: what triggered it, what carried it, what it characteristically demanded, what theory it drew on, what it produced, and how it ended. Read down the columns and the shared negative definition stands out precisely because everything positive diverges. Read across the rows and the divergences explain themselves: different triggers recruited different carriers, and different carriers produced different legacies.

| Dimension | British formation | American formation |
|---|---|---|
| Founding trigger | The double shock of 1956: the secret speech, Hungary, and Suez, breaking a party generation and a governing consensus together | The vacuum left by a destroyed older left, filled by the civil rights movement's example and then by the escalation in Vietnam |
| Principal vehicles | Two dissident journals merged into a single review in 1960, with clubs, campaigns, and adult education around it | A student organisation refounded in 1960, its founding statement of 1962, and the campus chapter network |
| Characteristic political demand | A socialist humanism: the recovery of agency, culture, and moral argument within a remade socialist tradition | Participatory democracy: a standing share for individuals in the decisions shaping their working and civic lives |
| Theoretical resources | The recovered young Marx, native traditions of history and criticism, and later the imported continental systems | Democratic moralism, Mills's sociology of power, the civil rights movement's practice, and European Marxism absorbed in translation |
| What it produced | Cultural studies as a field, history from below, a durable review and publishing house, a generation of scholarship | A mass movement practice, a durable political vocabulary, and cadres scattered into feminism, academia, and public life |
| How it ended | By fission: the founding quarrel expelled the historians into social history while the journal pursued systematic theory | By implosion: escalation, factional war, and repression dissolved the organisation at the end of the sixties |

### What does the comparison show that narrative hides?

It shows that the sameness was chronological and negative while the difference was institutional and positive. One label covered a historians' renovation of a national tradition and a student movement's invention of one, and most errors about the New Left come from reading the record of either formation as the record of both.

Two cells of the table repay a slower look. The first is the vehicles row. The British current was carried by journals, objects that select for people who write and argue, accumulate slowly, and can survive their founders; the American current was carried by an organisation, an object that selects for people who mobilise, accumulates quickly, and can be destroyed by its own growth. Nearly every contrast in the other rows follows from that one. The second is the final row. The British formation did not so much end as divide, its halves emigrating into adjacent academic fields where they worked for decades; the American formation ended in the strict sense, its organisation torn apart in 1969 amid factional struggle and state attention, its people dispersed into the movements of the following decade. A current carried by print leaves libraries; a current carried by organisation leaves veterans. The two New Lefts left both, and the legacies section of this guide is an attempt to inventory the estate.

## Culture as politics

Every account of the New Left must explain why a socialist current spent so much of its energy on novels, pop songs, advertisements, and the design of coffee bars. The answer lies in the founding diagnosis. The older Marxism had treated culture as a reflection: the real action was in the economy, and ideas, arts, and entertainments were the shadows the action cast. The postwar world refused to cooperate with that picture. In the affluent democracies the working class was being remade not only at work but at home, by television, hire purchase, the youth market, and a mass media that addressed it as consumers rather than as citizens or producers. A politics that ceded that terrain to the market, the founders argued, ceded the formation of the very people in whose name it spoke. Culture was not the decoration on the structure; it was one of the workshops in which the structure's inhabitants were made.

Williams gave the position its canonical vocabulary by insisting that culture is ordinary: not a special category of refined achievement but the whole way of life of a society, its meanings and values as they are lived in institutions, relationships, and arts alike. From that premise a research programme followed, the patient description of how a society's meanings are made and remade, which the following generation institutionalised as cultural studies. From the same premise a politics followed: if meanings are material, then the struggle over them is not a distraction from the serious business of economics but part of its substance, and the socialist who sneers at a song or a serial has misunderstood where consent is manufactured. The older left's criticism, that this was a migration from the real to the trivial, is heard at full strength in a later section; the founders' conviction, that they had located the real more exactly, should be registered first.

### Why did culture move to the centre of a socialist politics?

Because the terrain of consent had moved. In societies where the classic immiseration was receding and the mass media, advertising, and the youth market were forming desires directly, a socialism that spoke only of wages and ownership addressed a shrinking part of its audience's experience, and knew it.

There was also a biographical logic. The British founders were, professionally, readers of texts: historians, critics, teachers of literature. They reached for the tools they had, and the tools proved better fitted to the new society than the political economy they had inherited, which described the capitalism of the mill better than the capitalism of the supermarket. Critics have made much of this, as if the analysis were a rationalisation of the analysts' skills. The founders' reply is built into their best work: the skills were turned on the society that had formed the analysts too, its schooling, its broadcasting, its hierarchies of taste, and the results survived the reply because the descriptions were accurate. When the analysis of a television serial or a women's magazine still illuminates, the question of why its author was qualified to write it loses its sting.

## The novel of the break

The founding crisis has a novel, and the novel is evidence of a kind the journals cannot supply. Doris Lessing had been a member of the Communist Party in two countries, had written the first volumes of a great sequence about a woman's formation inside the movement's hopes, and left the party in the founding season. Her novel of 1962 took the whole experience into a fractured form: notebooks within notebooks, a writer protagonist anatomising her own political and erotic life, the movement's certainties and the century's madnesses examined in the first person. The book became, on its long-delayed recognition, one of the century's most argued-over novels, claimed by the feminism that followed as a founding text and quarrelled with by its author for the claiming. For this guide its value is diagnostic. It shows what the double rejection felt like from inside a gifted life: not a syllogism but a weather, of loyalty curdling, of comrades becoming strangers, of the self rebuilt after the building it lived in was condemned.

### Why does the break have a novel and not only journals?

Because the founding generation's question was finally about experience, and the novel is the instrument that registers experience at full resolution: the texture of loyalty, the smell of the meeting room, the cost of the confession. The journals argued the break; the novel lets a reader inhabit it, which is why the two records complete each other.

Set the novel beside the documents and a gap in the official record becomes visible. The journals of the founding years are overwhelmingly a men's archive arguing in public voices; the interior history of the period, the households that sustained the arguments, the women whose typing, cooking, and patience underwrote the movement's institutions, enters the record late and largely through fiction and memoir. The feminist reckoning described elsewhere in this guide began, among other places, in the recognition that the great novel of the break had been written by a woman about experiences the movement's own history had no room for. A guide that used only the journals would reproduce the omission while describing it.

## The arts of the movement

A current that held culture to be a primary battleground was bound to produce practitioners as well as analysts, and its fringe of theatres, film societies, folk clubs, and little magazines is not decoration on the main story. In Britain the dramatists and directors who gathered around the new stages brought the founding generation's questions into the repertoire: history plays that treated the crowd as protagonist, documentary forms that put working-class speech on the stage unmediated, a celebrated attempt to build a workers' theatre organisation out of the trade union movement itself. The Free Cinema filmmakers of the late fifties, several of them formed in the same Oxford and London milieu as the journals, declared for a cinema of everyday life and made it, in short films about markets, funfairs, and jazz clubs whose attention to ordinary pleasure was itself a polemic. The folk revival, organised in part by veterans of the same left, collected and sang the industrial ballads that the founding historians were simultaneously rescuing in print, so that a song and a monograph could carry the same argument about whose experience counted as history.

### Why did the movement invest in theatre and song?

Because its theory required it. If culture was where a society's meanings were made, then making culture was politics in the primary sense, and a movement that only analysed plays and ballads while producing none would have conceded the workshop to the market it criticised.

The American fringe was differently stocked, song above all: the freedom songs adapted from the Black churches of the South, the folk revival's topical balladeers, the benefit concert as an institution of the movement's economy. In both formations the arts performed the office the journals performed for the intellectuals: they recruited by feeling before argument, they carried the analysis into rooms print could not enter, and they gave the movement a memory in forms that outlasted its organisations. When the history of the period is told in its songs and films, as it often is, the telling is not a softening of the record. It is the record, in the media the current itself designated as primary.

## The recovery of the young Marx

The current's Marxism had a textual foundation, and the foundation was a recovery. The Marx available to the older traditions had been edited by his custodians: the systematic economist of the later volumes, the philosopher of dialectical laws, the prophet of the stages. Against that figure the postwar decades made available another: the young writer of the manuscripts composed in Paris in 1844, unpublished for nearly a century, whose subject was alienation, the estrangement of human beings from their work, their products, their fellows, and their own capacities under the rule of private property. Here was a Marx who wrote like a moralist and a phenomenologist, for whom communism meant the restoration of human senses and powers rather than the administration of nationalised industry. For a generation fleeing the doctrine built in the older Marx's name, the younger text was an emancipation permit: one could be a Marxist of the manuscripts against the Marxism of the catechism.

The recovery gave the early New Left its philosophical signature, the humanism that Thompson defended as the tradition's moral core and that the guide to [Marxist humanism explained](/2011/07/15/marxist-humanism-explained/) treats in its own right. It also fixed the early current's characteristic blindnesses. A Marxism centred on alienation spoke powerfully about the quality of experience under capitalism and haltingly about its mechanisms: crisis, accumulation, the state. The founders knew this and mostly accepted the trade, reasoning that the mechanisms had been theorised to death by the orthodoxies while the experience had been theorised not at all. Their successors reversed the trade with a vengeance, and the structural Marxism of the later sixties, with its anti-humanist slogan that history is a process without a subject, is best read as the pendulum of the same argument swinging through its arc.

### What did the recovery of the young Marx make sayable?

It made a moral language available inside Marxism again. Alienation named what was wrong with affluence as well as with poverty, so socialists could criticise the society of the full refrigerator in Marx's own words, and could defend experience, culture, and human need against a doctrine that had treated them as superstructure.

It is only fair to the older traditions to note that the recovery was itself an act of editing. The young Marx of the manuscripts led, in his own lifetime, to the older Marx of the critique of political economy, and a reading that enthrones the first and brackets the second performs the same selective custody the founders condemned in their opponents. The honest position, occupied by the best of the founding generation in their practice if not always in their polemics, is that the corpus contains both Marxes in tension, and that the tension is the tradition's engine. The New Left's contribution was to make the tension impossible to ignore: after the manuscripts entered general circulation, no custodial orthodoxy could plausibly present its Marx as the whole of him.

## New agents of change

The sociological wager of the current can be stated as a question the older tradition thought it had answered in advance: if not the industrial working class in its classical form, then who? The answers offered across the two formations make a revealing catalogue. Students, first as a constituency in formation and later, in the decade's fever, as a substitute proletariat. The colonised and postcolonial world, whose revolutions in China, Cuba, Algeria, and Vietnam seemed to have relocated history's engine to the periphery. The new technical and professional strata created by the expanded state and the knowledge industries, salaried, educated, and without property, a class the classical map had no colour for. Women, whose organised entry into the argument at the decade's end exposed how much of the current's own practice had relied on their uncounted labour. And the marginal and excluded, the figures Marcuse gathered under the heading of the substratum, those outside the integrated majority whose refusal might crack its consensus.

Each candidacy had its evidence and its season, and the honest verdict is that none bore the weight the classical proletariat was supposed to bear, the weight of a universal class whose particular emancipation emancipates everyone. The students graduated. The periphery's revolutions built states that reproduced the dilemmas of the centre. The new strata divided by income and allegiance. What the catalogue achieved, and it was not nothing, was the destruction of the question's premise. After the New Left, no serious socialist theory could simply assign agency by location in the relations of production; agency had to be argued for, group by group and conjuncture by conjuncture, which is a more demanding discipline and a more truthful one. The older left called the whole search an evasion of the class question. The searchers replied that the class question, honestly pursued, had led them to the search.

### Who was supposed to make the change?

On the classical answer, the industrial working class, by virtue of its position and its numbers. The New Left kept the question and pluralised the answer: students, the colonised world, new salaried strata, women, and the excluded all appear as candidates, none crowned, each tested against the events of the decade.

The debate mattered because it was never merely academic. Strategy follows sociology: a movement that expects its agent to be the organised industrial class builds unions and parties of a certain kind; a movement that expects students builds campuses campaigns; a movement that expects the periphery builds solidarity organisations and waits for news. The New Left built, in sequence and sometimes at once, all of these, which is another way of describing its organisational incoherence. Its defenders answer that the incoherence was the price of honesty in a period when the honest answer was that nobody knew, and when pretending to know had been the older traditions' most expensive habit.

## The new working class debate

One candidacy among the new agents deserves separate treatment because it kept the argument inside the theory of class instead of abandoning it. In France, Serge Mallet's study of the advanced technicians of the automated industries proposed that the most modern stratum of wage labour, salaried, highly trained, and strategically placed at the controls of the newest plant, was developing demands that exceeded wages and reached for control over the enterprise itself: a new working class, whose grievances were about authority rather than income. Andre Gorz gave the thesis its strategic form in the middle sixties, arguing that reforms should be chosen for their power to enlarge the working class's capacity rather than merely to relieve its condition, reforms that could not be digested by the system without changing it. The British and American journals translated and argued over both writers eagerly, because the thesis offered what the catalogue of substitutes conspicuously lacked: an agent that was still a working class, continuous with the tradition's theory, yet fitted to the affluent, automated capitalism the founders had diagnosed.

### What was the new working class thesis?

It held that the agent of change had not vanished but mutated: that the technicians and salaried professionals of the most advanced industries, demanding control rather than merely consumption, could inherit the classical proletariat's role, reunited with the tradition by their position inside the newest production.

History marked the thesis as it marked the others. The new strata grew, prospered unevenly, and divided, their upper reaches absorbed into management and their demands for control surfacing only in moments of general crisis, most spectacularly in the French May of 1968, when the factories of the advanced industries joined the students in the largest general strike in European history and then settled for wages. The episode is the debate in miniature: the new working class acted, decisively, and then declined the role the theory had written for it. What survived was Gorz's criterion rather than Mallet's sociology, the idea that a reform be judged by whether it increases the capacity of those who win it, which passed into the general stock of the European left and outlived the class that was supposed to embody it.

## Anti-imperialism as connective tissue

One commitment held the dispersed coalition together when doctrine could not: opposition to empire and to the wars of the postwar empires. It was present at the founding, in the Suez autumn, and it ran through every phase afterward: the Algerian war for the French sympathisers who watched their republic torture in the name of civilisation; the Cuban revolution as proof that a small country could defy the hemisphere's hegemon; above all Vietnam, the decade-long crucible in which the American formation was forged and the British formation's later phase was recruited. Anti-imperialism did connective work because it satisfied every strand of the double rejection at once. It indicted the Western governments' violence and hypocrisy; it indicted, after 1968's second shock in Prague, the Soviet state's claim to lead an anti-imperialist camp; and it offered the new agents a theatre in which they were visibly acting on history rather than merely interpreting it.

The commitment had its own deformations, which the record should not hide. Solidarity could curdle into romanticism, the distant revolution idealised precisely because its costs were borne elsewhere, and the sins of liberation movements forgiven by a standard never applied at home. The founders' generation, which had learned scepticism from 1956, was generally better armoured against this than the successors who arrived with Vietnam. But the through-line is real and it matters for the grading of the movement's Marxism: anti-imperialism was the point at which the New Left's politics touched the global structure of capitalism directly, and its analyses of the world order, dependent development, and the metropolitan state's external face were often ahead of the academy that later professionalised them.

## The colonies write back

The anti-imperialism described above acquired, in the middle sixties, a harder organisational form, and the change tested the founders' inheritance severely. The Tricontinental conference at Havana in 1966 announced a solidarity of the liberation movements of three continents; the Cuban example, and its most famous emissary's call to create two, three, many Vietnams, proposed a strategy in which the periphery's armed struggles would overstretch and break the metropolitan power. In Britain the Vietnam Solidarity Campaign and the war crimes tribunal organised by Bertrand Russell carried the solidarity argument into the streets and the press; in the United States the movement's radicalising wing concluded that it lived inside the empire's headquarters and owed the empire's enemies not sympathy but sabotage of the war machine, a conclusion that ends, by a short road, at the factional catastrophe. The founders watched the elevation of the distant guerrilla into the universal subject with the scepticism their own history had taught them. They had seen a universal subject anointed before, in Moscow, and had buried the last one in 1956.

### Why did the distant revolution tempt the second formation?

Because it solved, in imagination, the problem the movement could not solve at home: the absence of an agent. A struggle visibly making history elsewhere could be adopted by proxy, its victories credited to the movement's account and its costs invoiced to others, which is why the founders, who had audited one such adoption, distrusted the currency.

The temptation's anatomy is worth the space because it explains the second formation's trajectory better than any story of individual folly. Each stage was a rational response to a real blockage: participation had not stopped the war; elections had not stopped the war; the working class of the metropolis had not stopped the war. The conclusion drawn by the most impatient, that only an escalation mirroring the enemy's would serve, followed from the premises of emergency, and the founders' counter-argument, that means determine ends and that an imported revolution is a contradiction, required exactly the patience the emergency had abolished. The argument about violence, taken up below, is this argument in its sharpest form.

## The current and the shop floor

For a formation accused of abandoning the working class, the New Left's actual relations with industrial militancy were close, complicated, and mostly conducted at one remove. The Britain of the nineteen sixties was entering its long season of unofficial strikes and shop stewards' organisation, and the founders watched it with a sympathy qualified by analysis: the stewards' movement embodied the agency and self-organisation they celebrated, yet its horizon was the wage bargain inside the existing order, which their whole doctrine taught them to regard as the trap rather than the exit. Kindred spirits built the bridges the founders theorised about: the Institute for Workers' Control gathered stewards, academics, and union officials around the demand for industrial democracy, the one programme that translated the current's master value, participation, into the language of the factory. In the United States the distance was greater and the guilt correspondingly sharper; the student formation's attempts to reach industrial workers, through the organising projects and later through anti-war work in the unions, met the hard fact that much of organised labour's leadership supported the war the students were trying to stop, and the famous confrontations between construction workers and demonstrators entered the period's iconography as the emblem of the alliance that never happened.

### Why did the current keep its distance from the shop floor?

It did not keep its distance from workers; it kept its distance from the official movement that claimed to speak for them, whose bureaucracies it regarded as part of the administered order. Its wager was that unofficial militancy and the demand for control expressed the class's real movement better than its own institutions did.

The honest summary is that the current understood the official labour movement better than it understood the labour process, and the asymmetry marks its Marxism to the end. Its members could anatomise a union bureaucracy or a Labour conference with surgical skill, because those were institutions of the kind they knew, made of meetings, journals, and resolutions. The point of production they knew largely as readers, and the political economy of the strike wave, the incomes policies, and the productivity bargains of the sixties was written by others, in other traditions, while the New Left's best minds were elsewhere. The liability column of the final grading begins here, in a formation whose founding subject was the working class and whose working members were, overwhelmingly, teachers of it.

## The universities as habitat

No institution did more to shape the current's fate than the expanding university, and no feature of the period is easier to misread in retrospect. The British and American systems both underwent a vast enlargement in the nineteen sixties, new universities, new disciplines, new multitudes of students drawn from classes that had never sent their children. The New Left was native to this expansion: its British founders staffed the adult education departments and then the new universities; its American vehicle was inconceivable without the mass campus; its second formation was the expansion's children in revolt against the institution that had gathered them, demanding, in the occupations of 1968 and after, that the university practise the democracy it taught. The free university and anti-university experiments of those years, shoestring institutions where movement intellectuals lectured without fees or credentials, were the habitat dreaming of itself purified: education as participation, the seminar as the prefiguration of the good society.

### Why were the universities the movement's natural habitat?

Because they were where its people were and where its method belonged. The expansion had concentrated the young, the literate, and the dissatisfied in institutions nominally devoted to critical inquiry, gave the movement meeting rooms, printing facilities, and a renewable constituency, and made the seminar its characteristic political form.

The habitat explains both the movement's intellectual quality and its political ceiling. A formation incubated in universities acquired their strengths, rigour, memory, and a suspicion of slogans, and their characteristic weakness, the tendency to mistake the clarification of a question for its settlement. It also acquired their demography: as higher education stratified, so did the movement's audience, and the language of the later New Left, formed in the seminar, travelled badly to the doorstep and the shop floor. When the objection of the following sections describes the movement's relocation into the universities as a fall, the record should be corrected in one respect: the movement had been born there, among the expansion's first beneficiaries, and the relocation was less a retreat than a homecoming, which does not refute the objection but complicates its geography.

## The Wilson governments and the education of hope

The British founders had never expected much of Labour governments, which was fortunate, because the governments of 1964 to 1970 conducted a systematic education of the entire left in the distance between office and power. The new administration arrived trailing the rhetoric of technological revolution and proceeded to defend the currency at the price of its programme, to support the American war in Vietnam short of troops and long on diplomatic cover, to legislate against the immigration of Commonwealth citizens whose cause the left had made its own, and to end the decade by proposing statutory curbs on the trade unions that its own movement rose to defeat. Each episode recruited for the New Left among the young, who drew the founders' original conclusion with increasing speed: that the parliamentary road, travelled by a party machine of that character, did not lead where its passengers had bought tickets for. The second formation's contempt for the official left was not an inheritance of doctrine but a finding of the decade, renewed at every congress and every by-election.

### What did the Wilson governments teach the current?

They taught that the second rejection had been, if anything, understated: that a Labour government could hold office for six years, command the state, and leave the structures of power, empire's reflexes, and capital's prerogatives essentially as it found them, educating a generation faster than any journal could.

Yet the record has another face, which fairness requires this guide to enter. The same governments liberalised the laws on censorship, homosexuality, abortion, and divorce, abolished hanging, founded the Open University, and expanded the higher education in which the current lived. The founders' heirs were among the beneficiaries of nearly all of it, and the reforms embodied exactly the politics of culture and everyday life the current had championed, delivered by the machine it despised. The paradox is not a debating point; it is the period's deepest lesson about where change actually came from in the postwar democracies, from a pincer of movement pressure and administrative liberalism that neither the movements nor the administrators fully controlled. The New Left's theory, which privileged the movement's side of the pincer, never quite digested the other side's contribution, and its accounts of the decade read, in consequence, angrier than its own biography warrants.

## The manifesto moment

Midway through the nineteen sixties, the surviving founders attempted a regroupment, and the attempt produced the British current's most ambitious single document. The May Day Manifesto, drafted by a group around Williams, Thompson, and Hall and issued as a Penguin special in 1968 after private circulation the year before, attempted to restate the founding analysis for a Britain governed by a Labour administration that was managing capitalism with diminishing conviction: the new structures of power, the communications industries, the education system, and the war economy were anatomised, and a politics of democratic control over all of them was proposed in the founding idiom, concrete, moral, and addressed to a public beyond the left itself. Kindred projects of the same years, the Institute for Workers' Control and its conferences on industrial democracy, the new journals of socialist economics, mark a road the current surveyed and did not take: a patient reconstruction of a broad socialist politics, institution by institution, in the founding temper.

The manifesto failed to found anything durable, and its failure is diagnostic. By 1968 the audience for patience had been conscripted by emergency, in Vietnam abroad and in the universities at home, where the second formation was assembling under slogans the manifesto's authors found reckless. The document survives as evidence that the first New Left's project was still alive and still argued at full stretch on the eve of its eclipse, and as a control case for the declension story: the eclipse was not caused by the exhaustion of the founders' ideas, which the manifesto shows in working order, but by the arrival of a generation for whom the founding quarrels were ancient history and the war was now.

### What did the May Day Manifesto attempt to rebuild?

It attempted to rebuild the founding coalition as a politics rather than a readership: to give the scattered currents of the British left a common analysis of the new structures of power and a programme of democratic control, in the first formation's idiom, at the moment the second formation was making that idiom inaudible.

## First and second: separating the generations

Much confusion dissolves once two formations sharing one name are kept apart. The first New Left, in the British usage this guide follows, is the formation of 1956 to the early nineteen sixties: ex-communist and university socialists, journals and clubs, disarmament marches, adult education classrooms, the founding books. Its temper was reconstructive and its timescale patient; it meant to remake a tradition. The second New Left is the explosion of the later nineteen sixties across the Western world: the mass student movement, the anti-war mobilisation at full scale, the street politics of 1968, the turn to revolutionary rhetoric and, in its fringes, to clandestine violence. Its temper was insurrectionary and its timescale urgent; it meant to make a revolution. The two formations overlap in personnel and vocabulary, and the second claimed the first as its ancestry, but their theories of change were different and often opposed. The first distrusted spontaneity and built institutions; the second distrusted institutions and built moments.

The American chronology runs on its own track and maps imperfectly onto the British pair. The founding of Students for a Democratic Society and the Port Huron Statement belong to the first formation's years and share its reconstructive temper, its faith that a patient politics of participation could rebuild democracy from its local roots. The organisation's later career, its growth into the mass anti-war movement and its disintegration in 1969, belongs to the second formation's pattern and shares its fever. A student who collapses these phases into one story of youth revolt will misunderstand every document in the archive, reading the Port Huron Statement's measured democratic prose as a prelude to the rhetoric of 1969 when it was, in intention, an alternative to everything that rhetoric became.

### Where did the first New Left end and the second begin?

In Britain the break runs through the early sixties: the founding editor's departure, the ebbing of the disarmament campaign, and the review's turn to continental system mark the close of the first formation. The second arrives with the student explosion and the war's escalation later in the decade, a different temper wearing the inherited name.

The distinction matters for grading the movement's Marxism. The first formation's Marxism was a scholar's reconstruction, textual, historical, and humanist, tested in seminar and archive. The second formation's Marxism was an activist's appropriation, eclectic, urgent, and increasingly Leninist in its organisational longings even as its culture grew more libertarian, tested in occupation and demonstration. Judgments that fit one fit badly on the other: the first was too patient for its moment, the second too impatient for its means. Between them they pose the question that organises the remainder of this guide: what did the whole current, in both its tempers, actually leave behind?

## The situationist detour

The second formation's style had a source the founders never touched: a small, ferocious avant-garde centred in Paris, the Situationist International, whose 1967 treatise on the society of the spectacle argued that modern capitalism ruled not by forbidding desire but by merchandising its image, and whose members had practised a politics of constructed situations, detourned advertisements, and scandal as method. In the Strasbourg affair of 1966, situationist-influenced students seized a student union's funds to print a pamphlet on the poverty of student life, and the pamphlet's contempt for the university, the parties, and the unions alike reads as the second formation's unconscious manifesto: total refusal, addressed to the young, in the idiom of the culture industry turned against itself. In May 1968 the walls of Paris carried situationist slogans, and for a season the detour looked like the main road.

### What did the situationists contribute?

They contributed a style and a suspicion: the style of the slogan, the happening, and the subverted image, and the suspicion that every organisation, including the movement's own, would harden into spectacle. The second formation absorbed both, and its allergy to its own institutions owes as much to Parisian avant-gardism as to democratic principle.

The founders' distance from the detour was temperamental and doctrinal at once. Men formed by the archive and the adult education class did not mistake the slogan for the analysis, and their suspicion that the spectacle's critics were performing for it has worn well. Yet the situationists had seen something the founders' framework needed: that the administered society reproduced itself as imagery, that the critique of political economy required a critique of the picture world, and that the young experienced their unfreedom most sharply not as poverty but as boredom. The second formation's cultural politics, at its best, is the founders' analysis rewritten under that pressure.

## The anti-apartheid thread

One solidarity outlasted every fashion of the period and deserves record as the British current's most continuous practical commitment. The movement to boycott and isolate the South African racial state was founded in London at the turn of the sixties, in the same milieu of exiles, church radicals, and new journals that formed the New Left, and it drew the founders' generation into three decades of committee work, pickets of supermarkets and sports grounds, and argument with successive British governments that preferred trade to justice. The campaign's methods were the first formation's methods exactly: moral witness, consumer pressure, the patient building of a common sense against the official one. Its eventual victory, the isolation and then the negotiated end of the apartheid state, arrived after the founders' story closes, but the thread runs unbroken from the founding years, and it stands in the record against the charge that the current's anti-imperialism was a matter of rhetoric at a distance. In this case the distance was a London high street, and the work was done there, weekly, for thirty years.

## The year at home

The British and American 1968s were lesser convulsions than the French, and their specific shapes explain the two formations' different endings. In Britain the year's emblematic events were the two great London demonstrations against the Vietnam war, in March and October, the occupations and sit-ins that spread through the new universities and art schools, and a celebrated trial of strength at the London School of Economics that had begun the year before over the appointment of a director from a white-minority regime. Nothing in Britain approached a general strike; the trade union movement's energies ran on their own track of industrial militancy, parallel to the students and rarely intersecting. The British second formation therefore remained a politics of minorities, brilliant, televisual, and structurally incapable of the French escalation, and its decline, when it came, was the slow dispersal already described rather than a collapse.

In the United States the year's convulsions were real and lethal. The assassinations of the spring, of the civil rights movement's leader and then of the presidential candidate who had belatedly taken up the anti-war cause, destroyed the electoral hope in a season; the police riot outside the Democratic convention in Chicago that summer displayed the state's answer to the movement on national television; and the Tet offensive had already convinced much of the country that the war was unwinnable while convincing the movement's radicals that only the system's violence stood between them and its transformation. The American second formation ended the year larger, angrier, and more divided than it began it, with its founding organisation carrying a membership it could not govern and a rhetoric it could not cash. The contrast with Britain is the comparison table's last lesson: the American movement was broken by the scale of its country's violence, at home and abroad, while the British movement was preserved, at the price of marginality, by its country's comparative calm.

### What separated the British and American experiences of the great year?

Scale and the state's reply. The American movement faced assassinations, a televised police riot, and a war its own families were conscripted into; the British faced policing of a milder order and a war watched on screens. Each formation's fate was set less by its ideas than by the temperature of its country.

## The Italian autumn

The second formation's most consequential encounter with the industrial working class happened in neither Britain nor America but in Italy, and it disciplines every generalisation in this guide. Italy possessed what the other cases lacked: a mass communist party of genuine working-class rootage, a recent tradition of factory militancy, and a home-grown Marxist current, the workerism of the early sixties journals, which had argued that the working class's struggles drove capitalist development rather than following it, an inversion of the orthodox sequence that the importing review would later carry into English. When the hot autumn of 1969 brought millions of Italian workers into strikes that overflowed the unions' control, demanding equality of increases and rejecting the organisation of work itself, the second formation's Italian wing stood at the intersection of the student movement and a real proletarian insurgency, the conjunction the British and Americans had only imagined. The decade that followed, of factory councils, autonomous assemblies, and a state response that slid toward emergency and terror from more than one direction, is the second formation's fullest historical test: the agent appeared, the institutions improvised themselves, and the outcome was still defeat, of a bitterness proportionate to the hopes.

### What did the Italian autumn add to the second formation?

It added the missing term. Where the British and American movements searched for a substitute agent, the Italian movement met the classical one in motion, outside its official organisations, and the meeting showed both that the search had been premature and that the agent's arrival settled none of the questions of programme and power by itself.

For the comparison this guide has maintained, Italy is the control case. It demonstrates that the Anglo-American formations' difficulties were not caused by the working class's absence, since Italy had its presence in abundance, nor soluble by theory's sophistication, since Italian theory was the period's most inventive. What defeated the Italian movement, the state's adaptation, the official party's hostility, the employers' restructuring, and the armed fringes' gift of pretexts to repression, is the same list that defeated the others, with the volume raised. The second formation's tragedy was not that it never found its subject. It is that finding the subject was the beginning of the problem, and the problem had no solution in the period's stock of ideas, the founders' included.

## The continental May

The second formation had its epicentre neither in London nor in New York but in Paris, and the events of May 1968 in France set the pattern that the British and American movements spent the following years imitating and digesting. A student revolt over the conditions of the mass university detonated, within weeks, a general strike of some ten million workers, the largest in European history, factory occupations across the country, and a crisis of the Gaullist state serious enough to send its president briefly out of the capital. The French Communist Party and the great union federation, far from leading the explosion, worked to contain and settle it, and the spectacle of the official left negotiating the return to work over the heads of the occupiers completed, for the watching generation abroad, the education that 1956 had begun for their elders. In West Germany the extraparliamentary opposition had already formed around a student federation expelled by its own social democratic party, and its most famous spokesman, shot and wounded that spring, had given the strategy of the long aftermath its enduring phrase: the long march through the institutions.

### What did the continental May teach the two formations?

It taught that the advanced societies could still convulse: that students could detonate what workers then enlarged, that the official left would act as the fire brigade of order, and that a general strike could win wages and lose the moment, all of which the British and American movements absorbed as both inspiration and warning.

For this guide's comparison the continental May performs a clarifying service. It shows the second formation as a genuinely international event with national scripts, in which the French script came nearest to the classical revolutionary scenario and still stopped at its threshold. The British and American formations, whose own 1968s were dramatic but never general, received Paris as a standard against which to measure their insufficiency, and the measurement fed the desperation of the following years. A movement that believes history offered it a moment and that it failed the moment will do desperate things with the belief. The factional infernos of 1969 and after are unintelligible without that borrowed standard burning in the background.

## The second shock

In August 1968 the armies of the Warsaw Pact invaded Czechoslovakia and ended the Prague Spring, the attempt by a communist government itself to build the socialism with a human face that the founders of 1956 had demanded from below. For the surviving first formation the event was the founding year repeated with the roles clarified past argument: the reformers this time were not dissidents but the party's own leadership, and the answer was the same. Whatever remained of the hope that the existing socialist states might evolve toward the founders' standard was extinguished, and the obituaries written that autumn have a finality the ones of 1956 lacked, because 1956 could still be read as the exposure of a deformation and 1968 had to be read as the revelation of a system.

### What did the invasion of Czechoslovakia settle?

It settled the argument the founders had been conducting since the founding year about whether the Soviet-type system could reform itself toward their standard. The reform's authors were the system's own rulers, and the system's answer was invasion, after which the double rejection required no further evidence and admitted no further appeal.

For the second formation the second shock cut the other way, and the divergence is one of the keys to the period. Where the founders saw confirmation, many of the young saw only the discrediting of one more set of elders, and the fringes drew the conclusion that all existing roads were traps and only the most absolute rupture would serve, a conclusion that led within two years to the factional inferno described in the decline section. A single event thus closed the tradition's old argument and opened its most destructive one. The founders, who had spent a decade teaching that events must be read whole, in their conjuncture, could only watch their own lesson applied by students who had read the conjuncture and chosen the abyss.

## What it produced: cultural studies

The most durable institutional product of the British New Left is an academic field. In 1964 the University of Birmingham established a Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies, with Hoggart as its founding director and Hall, who succeeded him at the decade's end, as the figure under whom it became internationally influential. The centre was the founding generation's research programme given an address: the study of culture as a whole way of life, of the media, subcultures, schooling, and the state, conducted with the methods of literary criticism, sociology, and history in deliberate mixture. Its working papers and its students colonised the humanities and social sciences over the following decades, and the field it named now exists on every continent, in departments whose occupants often do not know the genealogy of the chair they sit in.

Genealogy matters here because the descent was direct and personal, not a vague influence. The centre's founders were the journals' people; its founding questions were the first New Left's questions, about how consent is made in a media society and how ordinary people's cultures encode resistance and accommodation together; its characteristic method, the conjunctural analysis of a society's moving parts, was the founders' habit of mind formalised. The fuller treatment of the media theory that descends from this work belongs to the companion guide on [Marxist media theory explained](/2012/05/15/marxist-media-theory-explained/), which traces the line from the centre's studies of encoding and decoding to the present discipline. For this guide the point is evidential: when the older left's charge is entered, that the New Left produced nothing but careers, the existence of a world discipline founded in its rooms is the first exhibit for the defence, whatever one thinks of the discipline's later trajectory.

### What did cultural studies inherit from the movement?

It inherited the founding questions and the founding refusal together: the insistence that culture is ordinary and material, that audiences make meanings rather than merely receiving them, and that a society must be read whole, in its conjuncture, before any of its parts can be explained. The field is the first New Left's method with the politics filed as ancestry.

The inheritance had a price that the balance sheet below must record honestly. A field founded to serve a movement's self-understanding became, over time, a profession serving its own reproduction, and some of its practitioners would not recognise the founding politics as anything but a creation myth. Hall himself worried the point in print, distinguishing the moment of the movement from the moment of the discipline and refusing to pretend they were the same. The worry is the movement's epitaph and its vindication in a single gesture: only a formation that had taught its heirs to analyse how institutions domesticate insurgent ideas could have produced heirs so lucid about their own domestication.

## The moral economy

Among the concepts the founding generation contributed to the general stock, one has travelled farthest beyond its origin: the moral economy. Thompson coined the term in a celebrated essay of 1971 on the eighteenth-century food riot, arguing that the crowd which seized grain and set prices was not a spasm of hunger but the enforcement of a moral consensus, a popular economics of rights, just prices, and obligations, against the new political economy of the free market. The concept condensed the whole founding method into two words: ordinary people possessed an economics, coherent and arguable, which the historian's task was to reconstruct rather than to pity or to pathologise, and the conflict of the period was not between reason and riot but between two economies, one moral and one merely market.

### What did the idea of a moral economy assert?

It asserted that subordinate people judge economic arrangements by norms of fairness and subsistence, and act collectively on the judgment, so that a riot, a boycott, or a strike can be read as an argument. The concept gave the founding generation's respect for experience its most portable scholarly form.

The term's career since is a miniature of the current's diaspora. Anthropologists carried it to the peasantries of Southeast Asia, where it organised a famous account of rebellion; political scientists carried it to the study of welfare and protest; it is invoked in debates its coiner never addressed, about food prices, fuel costs, and the obligations of the rich in a crisis. Thompson himself returned to the concept late, reviewing its travels with a proprietor's mixture of pride and alarm. Few ideas minted in the journals of the first New Left have had so long a half-life, and none illustrates the founding wager better: that beginning from the experience of ordinary people, exactly described, produces concepts that outwork the systems built to explain them away.

## History from below

The second great scholarly product was a way of writing history. The founding generation had been historians before it was anything else, and the exodus of 1956 freed its scholarship from the last vestiges of party supervision at the moment when the social history of ordinary people was becoming possible as a discipline. Thompson's reconstruction of the English working class's self-making set the template: the crowd, the chapel, the illegal union, the threatening letter, all read as the acts of people reasoning about their condition with the concepts available to them. A generation of younger historians took the method outward: to the crowd in the French Revolution, to bandits and millenarians, to the enslaved and the colonised, to women whose absence from the record became itself a subject. The Past and Present journal, founded earlier by some of the same historians, and the History Workshop movement that Raphael Samuel built from the late nineteen sixties, gave the approach its institutions: conferences where trade unionists and academics argued as equals, local history written by its subjects, an entire democratisation of who counts as a maker of the past.

History from below changed what counts as a historical fact, and the change long outlived the politics that motivated it. It also embodied the first New Left's theory of agency in disciplinary form: if the past's ordinary people made themselves, against structure and often against doctrine, then the present's ordinary people were not the passive material of anyone's strategy, a proposition with obvious consequences for every vanguard that encountered it. Professional historians who share none of the founders' politics now work inside the evidential world the method created, which is perhaps the purest form of the movement's success: a victory so complete that the victors are no longer identified.

## The publishing infrastructure

Ideas need carriers, and the current built carriers of unusual durability. The merged review itself survived its founders, its schisms, and its century, becoming one of the longest-lived journals of the international left. Around it grew a publishing house, founded in 1970 as the review's book arm and renamed Verso in the middle of that decade, which carried the translation programme into permanent form: the continental classics, the new theory, the histories, in editions that stocked the world's seminar rooms. The Socialist Register, founded in 1964 by Ralph Miliband and John Saville as an annual of democratic socialist argument, gave the humanist wing its own durable venue when the review's pages turned elsewhere. In the United States the infrastructure was thinner and more fugitive, movement papers and pamphlets rather than institutions, with Monthly Review, an independent Marxist journal older than the New Left itself, providing the steadiest platform and the anti-war movement's literature otherwise scattered across mimeographs and small presses.

### How did translation become a political act?

In a culture whose Marxism had been formed by a handful of texts, deciding what to translate was deciding what could be thought. The review's editors chose the continent's strongest systems over its orthodoxies, and a generation's Marxism, humanist first and structural after, was in large part the shadow of those choices.

Step back and the infrastructure explains a mystery of the movement's afterlife: why its ideas remained available, citable, and teachable long after its organisations vanished. Movements that publish survive their defeats in a way movements that only march cannot, because defeat disperses people but not libraries. The founders, several of them professional custodians of texts, understood this in their bones. The New Left's bookshelf is its most complete monument, and the sections on the movement's decline should be read with the bookshelf in view: what declined was a politics; what persisted was an apparatus for keeping a politics thinkable.

## The diaspora of the disciplines

Cultural studies and social history were the flagship products, but the current's cadres seeded a whole archipelago of fields, and the inventory supports the grading better than any slogan. In sociology, the young scholars who gathered in the National Deviancy Conference from 1968 rebuilt the study of crime and control around the act of labelling, the process by which the powerful define the deviant, in direct descent from the founding suspicion of official categories. In education, the new sociology of the curriculum asked whose knowledge the school transmits, a Williams question in a new faculty. In geography, a radical school formed that read space itself as a social product, extending the founders' analysis of landscape and region. In literary studies, the cultural materialism that Williams named in his last decade gave a generation of critics their method and their quarrels. Even the study of sport, of television audiences, of popular music, fields that did not exist as scholarship before, carry the founding generation's fingerprints, the insistence that the ordinary culture of ordinary people is a worthy object of exact attention.

### What connects the fields the current seeded?

A single methodological inheritance: begin from experience as it is lived, treat the official categories as part of the problem, and read the whole conjuncture before explaining any part. The fields differ in everything else, which is why the inheritance is usually invisible to the heirs.

The diaspora's invisibility is the point on which the movement's defenders and its critics finally agree, from opposite valuations. The critics say the influence dissolved because it was never a politics; the defenders say it dissolved the way salt dissolves, by becoming the medium. A reader of this guide is equipped to judge: the founders set out to change what could be thought about culture, agency, and experience inside the socialist tradition, and the disciplines are where thoughts, once changed, are kept. The political balance sheet is drawn below; the intellectual one is not seriously in dispute.

## Journals as institutions

A reader who asks how a movement without a party held together for a decade will find the answer in its periodicals, which performed, in dispersed form, nearly every function a party performs in concentrated form. A journal recruited: the subscription list was the closest thing to a membership roll, and the convert's first act was to subscribe. A journal educated: its issues were the curriculum of a self-taught movement, sequenced by editors who knew what their readers had read last quarter. A journal disciplined, mildly and by prestige rather than by expulsion: to be answered in its pages was to be corrected before the only public that mattered. And a journal remembered: bound volumes carried the founding arguments forward into years when their authors had quarrelled, moved, or died, so that a newcomer in 1968 could be formed by a polemic of 1957 without knowing the names were no longer on speaking terms.

The comparison cuts both ways, and the founders felt the cut. What a journal cannot do is act: it cannot call a strike, hold a picket together in the rain, or negotiate. The New Left's recurring frustration, its sense of being an audience for its own analysis, is the frustration of a movement whose institutions were all built for the production of consciousness in a tradition that insisted consciousness was not the whole of politics. The second formation solved the frustration by building organisations of action, campus chapters and then factions, and destroyed itself with the solution. The first formation never solved it at all, and instead dissolved gradually into the educational system whose classrooms its journals had always resembled. Which failure is preferable is a genuine question in the theory of movements, and this guide does not pretend it has a comfortable answer.

### Why did two small journals matter more than a party?

Because the movement's real work was the manufacture of a new common sense, and print is the machine for that work. Parties command the already convinced; journals create the convinced, one reader at a time, and the two journals' merger built the room in which a generation argued itself into a new left.

## The feminist formation

The last of the movement's major products arrived as an internal reckoning. The women's liberation movement that emerged at the end of the nineteen sixties drew its early cadres, its meeting forms, and much of its theory from the New Left, and then turned the formation's own methods against the formation: the personal was audited as political, the movement's kitchens and typing pools were examined as evidence, and the great male theorists of liberation were asked why liberation stopped at their own front doors. The socialist feminism that resulted, represented by writers such as Sheila Rowbotham, whose historical work recovered women's resistance from the same archival depths the founding historians had plumbed, and Juliet Mitchell, whose long essay on women's condition appeared in the review in 1966 and attempted a structural account of women's situation in terms of production, reproduction, sexuality, and socialisation, was neither an offshoot nor an application of the New Left. It was its method, generalised past the point its founders had reached, and the guide to [Marxist feminism: the complete guide](/2016/09/01/marxist-feminism-complete-guide/) follows that generalisation into a tradition of its own.

### What did socialist feminism take from the New Left?

It took the method and refused the exemption. History from below became the recovery of women's past; the analysis of culture became the analysis of the household and the body; the demand for participation became the consciousness-raising group. What it declined to inherit was the assumption that some emancipations could wait their turn behind others.

For the grading of the movement's Marxism the feminist episode is decisive evidence in both directions. In the movement's favour: its categories proved fertile in the hands of those it had marginalised, which is the test of a living method. Against it: the marginalisation was real, documented, and defended at the time with the same impatience the founders had met from their own elders when they raised unfashionable questions. A tradition is judged by whether its self-criticism is one of its products. On that test the New Left passes, but the pass was earned by its critics inside the house, and the credit belongs to them.

## The Ruskin conference and the women's movement

The feminist formation had a founding scene of its own, and it belongs in this history because it replays the pattern of 1956 in miniature. In the spring of 1970 some six hundred women gathered at Ruskin College, Oxford, for the first national conference of the women's liberation movement in Britain, a conference that grew out of a history workshop on women, which is to say out of the founding generation's own institution, built by the younger scholars around Samuel to democratise the writing of the past. The conference's organisers had expected a scholarly meeting; what arrived was a movement. The workshops overflowed their syllabus into the streets of Oxford, the demands crystallised in the following years into the movement's first programme, and the scholars who had convened the meeting, Rowbotham among them, understood that the subject they had proposed to study had stood up and taken the conference over, exactly as the founders' theory of agency said subjects do.

### What happened at the Ruskin conference?

The study of women's history became the women's movement in public. A workshop convened by the history movement's heirs assembled the scattered local groups into a national presence, set the first demands in train, and demonstrated the founding method's fertility by turning it on the founders' own blind spot.

The American parallel ran through the student organisation's own caucuses, where the women of the movement, trained in its participatory practice and assigned its mimeograph machines, presented their analysis of the movement itself at its conventions and were answered, in the founding years, with a condescension that radicalised a generation more efficiently than any text. The debt and the indictment are both documented in the movement's own papers. What followed, the autonomous women's movement of the nineteen seventies with its journals, refuges, and theory, is the largest single proof that the New Left's methods were genuinely general: they worked best, in the end, in the hands of those the movement had taught and slighted in equal measure.

## Marcuse and the philosopher of the second formation

If the first formation's presiding texts were works of history, the second's was a work of philosophy: Herbert Marcuse's study of advanced industrial society, published in 1964, which argued that the system had learned to integrate its opposition by delivering goods, that needs themselves had become instruments of control, and that refusal survived mainly among those the integration had failed to reach. Marcuse, a veteran of the Frankfurt Institute whose career this series traces in the [Marcuse one dimensional man guide](/2015/08/15/marcuse-one-dimensional-man-guide/), became the student movement's most cited theorist on three continents, his name painted on banners in cities where his books were barely available. The association was partly his doing and partly an accident of timing: he alone among the senior Marxist philosophers told the young that their instincts were right, that the administered world's comforts were a form of domination, and that the refusal of the whole arrangement was a rational act.

The fit between philosopher and movement was nevertheless imperfect, and the imperfection instructs. Marcuse's theory was a philosophy of civilisation with a pessimism built into its foundations; the movement that adopted him wanted a strategy. He gave them a diagnosis of why revolution was blocked and a blessing for their refusal, but no account of how refusal becomes transformation, and when the movement's fringes drew the conclusion that only exemplary violence could rupture the integration, he refused to follow, criticising the turn even as governments and newspapers held him responsible for it. The episode belongs in this guide because it displays, at maximum magnification, the current's permanent structure: a theory of consciousness in search of a practice, and a practice in search of a theory, meeting gratefully and parting unreconciled.

## The founders on the Soviet Union

A word is owed to the question the founders argued about most and settled least: what the Soviet Union was. The inherited vocabularies were all occupied. The communists called it socialism building itself; the Trotskyists, a workers' state degenerated; the state capitalists, a capitalism with the plan as its boardroom; the cold warriors, totalitarianism, a category the founders refused because it fused their enemy with fascism and licensed the Western crusade. The first New Left's characteristic position was a refusal of the whole menu in favour of a judgment by fruits: whatever its class nature, a system that shot its striking workers and censored its historians had forfeited the name of socialism as the founders defined it, by the quality of the human relations it produced. The refusal gave their anti-communism its distinctive moral texture and its theoretical thinness, since a judgment by fruits declines to answer the structural question the menu existed to answer.

### What did the founders conclude about the Soviet Union?

That the definitional war mattered less than the verdict of experience: a society whose rulers feared its writers, jailed its dissidents, and invaded its neighbours' experiments had failed the test of socialism as the founders understood it, whatever label the science of society eventually assigned to its mode of production.

The thinness was not costless. Without a settled theory of the Soviet formation, the founders' heirs oscillated in the seventies between revulsion and a romantic anti-imperialism that excused in Havana or Beijing what it condemned in Moscow, applying the test of fruits selectively where the fruit was distant. The founders themselves, formed by 1956, were steadier: Thompson in particular held the line in both directions for forty years, against the apologists of the East and against the cold warriors who sought his signature for their crusades, refusing each camp the use of his name. It was the double rejection practised as a personal discipline long after it had ceased to be a movement's, and it is the clearest evidence that the founding gesture was a principle and not merely a mood of one season.

## The cultural cold war

The founders also had to reckon, midway through their story, with the discovery that the other side had been running a New Left of its own, or the simulacrum of one. In 1966 and 1967 the American press revealed that the intelligence services had financed, through conduits, a glittering archipelago of cultural Cold War institutions: congresses, magazines, and festivals that had championed the non-communist left and the modern arts as the free world's answer to socialist realism. Several of the founders' acquaintances had written for the subsidised journals in innocence; the journals' anti-communism, which had seemed a position, was also a payroll. The revelation cut in several directions at once. It vindicated the founders' suspicion that the official West's cultural freedom had a management. And it implicated, by proximity, everyone who had taken the era's cultural money or platforms, teaching the movement a permanent lesson in the archaeology of patronage that its own journals, financed by subscriptions and stubbornness, had been spared by their poverty.

The episode belongs in this history because it marks the moment the current's central category, the manufacture of consent, acquired documentary proof from the enemy's archives. The founders had argued that culture in the Cold War was a battlefield on which states fought by other means; the revelations showed the order of battle, the budgets, and the casualty lists of compromised reputations. Their own side's corresponding institutions, the subsidised fellowships and the directed research of the socialist bloc, were the mirror image, and the founders' journals said so. The double rejection had always implied that the cultural war had two general staffs. Now it had the receipts.

## The rivals for the inheritance

The New Left was not the only claimant to the vacancy of 1956, and its distinctness sharpens against its rivals. The Trotskyist organisations, themselves veterans of the argument against Stalinism, recruited from the same disillusioned generation and offered what the founders conspicuously withheld: a programme, a party in embryo, and an explanation of the Soviet phenomenon worked out in advance. Their critique of the New Left, formlessness, dilettantism, a politics of the mood, was the orthodox charge in a revolutionary accent, and their own subsequent history of splits, entries into the Labour Party, and miniature orthodoxies supplied the founders with their standing reply, that the apparatus reproduced the disease at smaller scale. The anarchist and pacifist milieux of the disarmament campaign pressed from the other side, finding the founders still too Marxist, too attached to the state they proposed to democratise, and the argument between the libertarians and the socialist humanists inside the marches was among the most fertile of the founding years.

### Who else claimed the inheritance of the break?

The revolutionary sects claimed it with a programme, the anarchists with a refusal of programmes altogether, and the Labour left with a promise to capture the old machine. The New Left's distinctness lay in declining all three securities: no finished doctrine, no rejection of organisation as such, and no faith in the machine's capture.

The rivalry matters beyond antiquarian completeness because it corrects a lazy assumption of the literature, that the New Left was the sixties left entire. It was, at every point, one contender among several for the allegiance of the radicalised, distinguished less by size than by the quality of its print and the reach of its eventual diaspora. Its rivals won many of the period's street arguments and some of its organisations. What the New Left won was the archive and the syllabus, the right to be the period's narrator, which is why its version of the story, including the version in this guide's sources, must be read with the discount its own method prescribes for all victors' histories.

## The other print: underground and movement papers

The second formation built print institutions of its own, and their difference from the founders' journals is the difference between the formations in material form. Where the review was bound, quarterly, and edited, the underground press was loose, weekly or improvised, and collective: papers produced in squats and communes, sold in the street, prosecuted for obscenity and conspiracy with a regularity that certified their importance to the authorities. In the United States the movement papers of the student organisation's last years and the underground weeklies of the big cities performed the same office. The underground press inverted the founders' model at every point: where the review sought authority through rigour, the papers sought solidarity through immediacy; where the review accumulated, the papers spent; where the review addressed posterity, the papers addressed Saturday.

### What did the underground press change?

It changed the reader into a producer. The movement paper was written by its audience in a sense the founders' review never attempted, with the predictable gains in energy and losses in accuracy, and it carried the second formation's culture, its music, its drugs, its sexual politics, into print forms the first formation would have found unrecognisable.

Posterity has been kinder to the review than to the papers, for the archival reason that bound volumes survive and newsprint does not, and the kindness distorts the record of the period's reading. Measured by circulation and by the formation of the young, the papers may have mattered more. The founders, connoisseurs of print, recognised the irony with grace: the institution they had built for the manufacture of a new common sense had been outflanked by institutions cruder, faster, and closer to the experience they had taught everyone to honour. The lesson generalises beyond the period. Every movement gets the media its theory of consciousness implies, and the two formations' different papers are their theories of consciousness made visible in ink.

## The older left's case

The criticisms must now be heard at the strength their makers gave them, beginning with the older left's. From the communist and orthodox Marxist side, the charge was desertion. At the moment when the movement's duty was to defend the existing socialist states and the industrial working class's organisations against a hostile world, the New Left had, on this account, dissolved every certainty that made defence possible: it had replaced the science of society with moralising, the party with a discussion circle, the proletariat with a shifting cast of students and marginals, and the critique of political economy with the criticism of advertisements. Worse, its anti-communism, whatever its intentions, objectively served the other camp: every essay on Hungary was ammunition for the cold warriors, every confession of the tradition's crimes a gift to those who wished the tradition buried rather than reformed. A movement that begins by agreeing with its enemies about its own side's record, the orthodox concluded, will end by agreeing with them about everything.

From the social democratic and Labour side the charge was irresponsibility. The New Left, on this account, was a revolt of the seminar against the committee room: clever young people who had never had to win a wage claim, staff a council, or hold a marginal seat, dismissing as betrayal the compromises by which actual improvements in actual lives are made. Its politics of moral witness and symbolic action substituted the pleasures of purity for the disciplines of power. Its cultural turn mistook the superstructure for the building. And its contempt for the parliamentary road ignored the arithmetic that road exists to solve: that in a democracy the unconverted majority has a vote for every vote of the converted, and a politics that cannot persuade them governs nothing. Labour's elder statesmen, who had built the welfare state the young founders took as their baseline, heard in the new current's ingratitude the sound of a generation that had never been hungry lecturing one that had.

### Why did the older left distrust the newcomer?

For reasons that were partly interest and partly doctrine. The newcomer threatened the older organisations' claim to monopolise the socialist inheritance, poached their young, and published their secrets. Doctrinally, it denied the two propositions each elder held sacred: for communists, the leading role of the party; for social democrats, the sufficiency of the parliamentary road.

Between the two charges there is a common core that gives them their force: the New Left, both elders agreed, was better at negation than construction, better at diagnosing the old world than at specifying the institutions of a new one or the road from here to there. The charge is the external form of the strongest objection, which the next sections state from inside the movement's own premises. But fairness requires the external form to stand first in its undiluted state, because the movement's replies, however brilliant, were replies to real weaknesses, and the weaknesses were visible to hostile eyes precisely because they were real.

## The New Left's answer

The movement's own case, stated with the same force, begins from the record. The older left's certainties, it argued, had been tested by history and had failed the test in the sight of everyone. The communist parties had defended the indefensible until their own leader confessed it, and had answered Hungary with apologetics; a tradition that requires its members to lie about the present will lie about the future, and its science is a liturgy. The social democratic parties had governed, and their governments had kept the bomb, the colonies' wars, and the inequalities of the boardroom intact beneath the welfare state's floor; a tradition whose summit is the competent administration of capitalism has quietly changed its subject. Against both records the founders set a simple claim: that socialism is a quality of human relations or it is a word, that it must therefore be judged by the experience of the people living it, in the factory and the kitchen and the colony and the classroom, and that any organisation which forbids that judgment has already ceased to be its vehicle. The double rejection was not desertion of the cause; it was the refusal to confuse the cause with its custodians.

On the disputed points the answers were specific. To the charge of abandoning the working class, the founders replied that they had abandoned a metaphysical portrait of that class, not the class itself, whose actual composition, culture, and consciousness they studied more closely than their critics did, and whose organisations they served in the unions and the adult education classes while the orthodox were drafting resolutions about it. To the charge of substituting culture for economics, they replied that the economy had moved into culture: that a capitalism which sold desires as industriously as goods could not be fought with a theory that stopped at the factory gate, and that the critic of advertisements was analysing production by other means. To the charge of serving the cold warriors, they replied with the ledger of 1956 itself: the truth about Hungary and the purges was not the property of any camp, and a left that suppressed it to deny its enemies a talking point had adopted its enemies' theory of truth.

The strongest strand in the movement's case, though, was generational and forward-looking. The postwar world, the founders argued, had changed the subjects of politics: mass secondary and higher education, the media society, the end of the European empires, the bomb. A socialism that could not speak to students as a material force, to the colonised as principals rather than auxiliaries, to women as more than a reserve army, and to citizens as inhabitants of a culture rather than only as sellers of labour power, would shrink into a heritage society. The New Left offered itself, immodestly but not absurdly, as the renovation the tradition required to survive its own century. Much of the renovation's detail failed; the diagnosis that renovation was required has been conceded, in practice, by every successor current that adopted the renovation's agenda while forgetting its name.

## The strongest objection: defeat converted into a career

There remains the objection that comes from inside the movement's own analysis, and it is the strongest because it uses the movement's favourite instrument, the sociology of intellectuals, against the movement itself. Stated at full strength, it runs as follows. Here was a current defined by rejecting both existing left traditions. Denied a working-class agent, it substituted students and marginal groups; denied a road to power, it substituted moral witness and cultural criticism; and when its political moment passed, it did not disappear but relocated, its personnel flowing into the expanding universities, its journals becoming academic quarterlies, its concepts becoming the small change of disciplines. The theory of Western Marxism, which describes how defeated revolution curdles into philosophy, then applies to the New Left with embarrassing precision: the movement is its own thesis, the clearest available case of political defeat converted, by way of the seminar room, into academic career. Its Marxism, on this account, was never a politics at all but the ideology of a rising professional stratum, the new salariat thinking about itself.

### What is the strongest charge against the New Left?

That its trajectory proves its critics right: that a current which began by refusing both a party and a programme ended by staffing university departments, so that its history reads as the conversion of political defeat into professional advancement, with theory as the conversion mechanism.

The movement's answer, and this guide's, begins by conceding the objection's factual skeleton. The relocation happened; the personnel did flow into the universities; the concepts did become disciplinary currency. The concession was, in fact, made first and most lucidly by the movement's own survivors, who analysed their trajectory with the same instruments they had turned on everyone else, which is either the refutation of the charge of bad faith or its most elegant confirmation. What the objection cannot digest is the residue: what the movement changed outside the universities while its people were supposedly only advancing within them. A field of historical writing was democratised. The culture of a society learned to read its media as politics. Movements for women's liberation and against colonial wars were staffed, trained, and partly theorised by the current's cadres, and the participants in those movements did not experience their gains as anyone's career move. The welfare of the objection's logic is that it must call all of that superstructure, which is precisely the error the movement was founded to correct.

The fair verdict holds both halves. The New Left failed at the thing the old lefts existed for, the conquest or transformation of state power, and it succeeded at things the old lefts had not known were things: the renovation of historical and cultural knowledge, the training of two generations of critics, the keeping of a socialist vocabulary alive and arguable through decades when both its custodians would have embalmed it. Whether the exchange was worth it depends on a judgment about what intellectual movements are for that this guide will state honestly as its own: in a period when the political roads were blocked by the failures of both traditions, keeping the questions open, and equipping strangers to ask them better, was not the worst available achievement, and it was achieved.

## The argument about violence

No account of the second formation is honest that avoids the question of violence, because the question divided it from the first formation absolutely and from its own founding documents nearly so. The first New Left had been formed in the disarmament campaign and carried a pacifist strain in its bloodstream; its violence was the violence it opposed, the bomb and the colonial war. The second formation inherited a world in which the colonised were fighting, and the Algerian war's great theorist, Frantz Fanon, whose book of 1961 arrived in English in the middle of the decade with a preface by Sartre, taught that the colonised subject was made in the act of violent refusal, that violence was not a tactic to be weighed but a cleansing force in the psychology of the oppressed. The argument detonated inside a movement whose American wing was simultaneously practising nonviolence in the South and being beaten for it. What the students took from Fanon was rarely his clinical care; it was the licence, transferred to societies nothing like Algeria, to treat escalation as authenticity and restraint as complicity.

### What did the argument about violence divide?

It divided the founders from the successors on the meaning of the founding itself. The elders held that a socialism made by violent means would bear the means in its institutions, as their own tradition's record proved; the young's fringes held that violence refused was violence consented to, and that empire's daily violence made the distinction a luxury.

The record's verdict is unsparing in both directions. The fringes' turn to clandestine violence in the early seventies achieved nothing it intended and much that its enemies intended, completing the public discrediting of the whole inheritance. Yet the founders' position had its own evasion, since the anti-colonial wars they supported were not won by witness, and the line between solidarity with a liberation movement's violence and endorsement of one's own was never drawn with the precision the founders' consciences required. The argument remains open in the tradition because both its answers are armed with the tradition's own history.

## The long march through the institutions

The German spokesman of the extraparliamentary opposition had proposed, before his shooting in 1968, a strategy for the aftermath he already foresaw: since the revolutionary moment was not at hand, the movement's cadres should undertake a long march through the institutions, entering the universities, the media, the professions, and the administration, and transforming them from within over a generation. The phrase became the second formation's most consequential bequest and the standing text for the strongest objection examined above. Read hostilely, it is the career charge signed by its defendant: the movement announced its intention to become the establishment. Read as its author meant it, it is the opposite: a refusal to cede the institutions to the enemy, a strategy of presence in the places where a society reproduces its common sense, precisely the terrain the first formation's cultural analysis had marked as decisive.

The half century's evidence supports a verdict between the readings. The marchers did transform parts of the institutions they entered: curricula, newsrooms, and professional norms carry the period's impress in every Western country, and the fields the movement founded are the proof at scale. The institutions also transformed the marchers, as institutions do, and the transformation ran in both directions at once in most individual lives, which is why the aggregate statistics of the objection, so many professors, so many editors, settle nothing by themselves. What can be said is that the long march was the only strategy of the second formation that was actually executed, and that its results, the renovation of the culture's commanding heights and the deradicalisation of the renovators, are the movement's final paradox in administrative form.

## The state takes notice

A movement's significance is sometimes certified by its enemies' filing systems. The American state's campaign against the second formation is now a matter of documentary record: an intelligence programme of surveillance, infiltration, and disruption directed at the movement's organisations, its newspapers repeatedly raided and its leaders prosecuted on charges that collapsed as often as they convicted, its black allies in the Panther organisation subjected to a violence of policing that ended, in Chicago in 1969, with a police raid in which a sleeping leader was shot dead. The British state's attention was cooler in method and kindred in intent: the underground press prosecuted, the Black activists of the Mangrove restaurant tried in 1970 and 1971 in a case that exposed, through the defendants' own conduct of their defence, the policing of the migrant communities the founders had written about a decade before, and the bombings attributed to a small anarchist groupuscule answered with the longest trials of the period. Historians debate the weight of repression among the causes of decline, and the debate is recorded in the next section; what the files settle is that the authorities, who were in a position to know, did not regard the movement as a seminar.

### What did the state's attention prove?

It proved that the administered society's tolerance had a perimeter and that the movement had found it. The surveillance files and the collapsed prosecutions are the period's unintended archive, and they complicate every account in which the second formation appears as a merely cultural episode that the real world indulged.

The point requires its balance. Repression explains the American organisation's end only in company with its internal collapse, which the state's agents exploited more often than they caused, and the British movement's dispersal owes almost nothing to it. The honest place of the files in this history is as evidence about the state the movement opposed: a state that preached the open society and kept, in its drawers, the instruments of a different one. The founders, who had learned that lesson from the other camp in 1956, were the least surprised people in either country.

## Why it declined

Decline has different dates in the two formations, and the difference itself is instructive. In Britain the first formation did not fall; it divided and dispersed through the middle nineteen sixties, the historians to their new discipline, the review to its system, the campaigners to a dozen causes, so gradually that no obituary was ever written. In the United States the organisation's end was an event: by 1969 Students for a Democratic Society, swollen beyond its founders' imagining by the war's escalation, tore itself apart in factional war between disciplined would-be vanguards and the remnants of the participatory faith, while the state, whose surveillance and prosecution of the movement are now documentary matters, pressed on the cracks. The fringes that survived the split carried its logic into clandestinity and, in a few notorious cases, into violence that disgraced the inheritance in the public mind for a generation.

Beneath the chronologies lie common solvents. Escalation was the first: a movement formed for patient reconstruction found itself, over Vietnam, conscripted into emergency, and emergency favours the most absolute voices. Generational turnover was the second: the campus base renewed itself every four years, carrying memory away with each graduation. The third was the success of the enemy's adaptation: the war ended, the draft ended, the postwar boom ended, and the conditions that had made the coalition cohere dissolved into the harder, poorer nineteen seventies, in which the questions changed from liberation to survival. The fourth solvent was internal and the most corrosive: the return of the repressed, as the second formation, in its desperation, re-imported the very orthodoxies the first had been founded to escape, the vanguard party, the catechism, the discipline, in miniature and therefore in caricature. The double rejection was reversed by the rejecters' grandchildren, and the wheel came full circle in a few short years.

### Why did the British and American declines differ in shape?

Because the carriers differed. A formation carried by journals and scholars could subside into institutions and persist as influence; a formation carried by a single mass organisation could be broken in a single season, and was. The mode of survival available to each had been decided by the mode of founding, decades of consequence packed into that first institutional choice.

It would falsify the record to end the decline story in dissolution. What dispersed did not vanish: it composted. The cadres of the sixties surface in the feminism and environmentalism of the seventies, in the community organising and solidarity campaigns of the eighties, in the seminar rooms where the canon the movement built is still taught. A movement's decline and a movement's influence are different variables, and the New Left is the standing modern example of their divergence.

## Was it actually socialist?

The definitional quarrel deserves a section of its own because it has consumed so much of the literature. Was the New Left socialist at all, or a liberal moralism in socialist dress? Was it Marxist, or a humanism that cited Marx the way an earlier generation cited scripture? The answers vary by formation and phase, and sorting them is more instructive than settling them. The British founders were socialists by formation and intention, several of them Marxists of deep textual learning, who understood their project as the tradition's reconstruction after the exposure of its Stalinist deformation. The American founders were, in their founding document, democrats before they were anything else, critical of corporate liberalism from premises a European social democrat would have recognised, and radicalised toward Marxist vocabularies only as the decade and the war educated them. The second formation everywhere draped itself in Marxism-Leninism's vocabulary while practising a politics its supposed teachers would mostly have disowned.

The confusion is compounded by a genuine ambiguity in the founding concept itself. A current defined by double rejection could shelter the reconstruction of Marxism and the exit from Marxism under one roof, and it did: some of its ornaments ended as liberals, some as conservatives of a peculiar bitterness, and some as the tradition's most faithful scholars. The label certifies, once again, an address in 1956 rather than a destination. What can be said without qualification is that the current's centre of gravity, in both formations and both decades, lay inside the broad socialist tradition: its members sought the transformation of capitalism, not its amelioration alone, and they sought it by the agency of the people subject to it. By that test the New Left was socialist, unevenly, argumentatively, and with a permanent minority report filed by its own members, which is to say, in the manner of every living socialist current that has ever existed.

### How does a movement's Marxism differ from a party's?

A party's Marxism is a doctrine of rule: settled, teachable, enforceable, and subordinated to the organisation's survival. A movement's Marxism is a doctrine of inquiry: provisional, plural, and answerable to events. The New Left's version belonged wholly to the second kind, which explains both its intellectual fertility and its political fragility.

## The inheritance in British politics

The British current's political afterlife ran, unexpectedly, through the Labour Party it had despaired of renovating. In the nineteen seventies the party's left, gathering around Tony Benn, assembled a programme whose elements read like the first formation's agenda transcribed into policy: industrial democracy and workers' control from the Institute's conferences, an alternative economic strategy from the socialist economists of the Register's circle, unilateralism's heirs in the revived disarmament campaign, and a rhetoric of participation that the decade's community politics had kept warm. Benn himself, a minister turned tribune, consciously presented the programme as the completion of the postwar settlement's betrayed promise, and the young activists who staffed his campaigns were, in large part, the second formation's veterans and the first formation's grandchildren. The episode matters because it tests the founding wager from the other side: here was the current's programme, carried by a mass party at last, with a charismatic advocate and a crisis to explain. It was defeated, inside the party and then in the country, and the defeat closed the British New Left's long political story in the early nineteen eighties, in the same years that the founding generation's greatest figure mounted his last campaign.

### What did the party's left inherit from the current?

It inherited the programme the current had kept in print through the wilderness years: industrial democracy, the alternative strategy, disarmament, and the participatory style, together with the cadres who carried them. The inheritance proved that the ideas had survived their movement; the sequel proved that survival and victory are different estates.

## The American afterlives

The American formation's dispersal was wider and stranger than its British counterpart's, because the country's institutions for absorbing dissent were larger and more various. Its cadres surface across the following half century in every quarter of American intellectual life: in the feminist and gay liberation movements that its survivors founded or staffed, in the law schools and history departments where its veterans wrote the period's history, in journalism, in the labour movement's reform caucuses, in the community organising tradition that carried the participatory method into the cities and eventually into national politics by routes this guide will not trace. The founding document itself acquired a second life as a classic, reprinted, annotated, and argued over at every anniversary, its measured sentences read with astonishment by readers who know the organisation only by its ending. The astonishment is the document's revenge: the statement of 1962 reads, against the record of 1969, less like the movement's seed than like its lost constitution, the road of patient democratic construction that the war foreclosed.

### What survived of the American formation?

Its method and its document survived better than its organisation: participatory practice passed into the successor movements and the organising professions, and the founding statement passed into the canon of American democratic writing, while the organisation that produced both is remembered chiefly for the manner of its ending.

## Exterminism and the founder's last campaign

The British story has a coda that belongs to the founders personally, and it closes the circle of 1956 in a way no novelist would risk. In 1980 Thompson, by then the tradition's most famous living historian, published an essay arguing that the arms race had acquired its own logic, exterminism, a system of weapons and counter-weapons reproducing itself independently of the politics that had built it, and he threw himself into the revived campaign against the new generation of nuclear missiles stationed in Europe. The European Nuclear Disarmament movement he helped to found proposed, in the founding idiom, a double refusal for the new decade: against the missiles of both blocs, for a Europe of the peoples beyond the frozen camps, in dialogue with the dissidents of the East, the heirs of 1956 and 1968 alike. For a few years the ageing founder of the first New Left was again the tribune of a mass movement, addressing crowds of a size the Aldermaston marches had never reached, and the movement he led was recognisably the founding coalition reassembled at a higher age: historians and clergy, students and trade unionists, the politics of moral witness against both establishments at once.

The coda's significance is not sentimental. It demonstrates that the founding formation's politics was a repeatable form, available whenever its conditions recurred, and that its central figure understood his own life in exactly the structural terms this guide has used. When the Cold War ended at the decade's close, the double rejection's occasion ended with it, and the founders' story became history in the strict sense, which is where this guide leaves it.

## The Dominion echoes

The double rejection was performed wherever the conditions recurred, and two cases from the settler dominions test this guide's claim that the form travels. In Canada, at the end of the nineteen sixties, a current inside the social democratic party drafted a manifesto for an independent socialist Canada, arguing that the country's economy had become a branch plant of the American one and that its left's custodians had accepted the arrangement; the Waffle, as the current was nicknamed, fused the anti-imperialism of the Vietnam years with an economic nationalism the British and American formations never needed, and was expelled from its party within three years, carrying its cadres into the universities and the unions. In Australia the same years produced a formation around the Sydney and Melbourne journals of the left, a generation of historians who rewrote the national past from below, and a brief, vivid season in which the whole repertoire, journals, teach-ins, moratorium marches against the Vietnam war, was performed in the antipodes with local materials.

### What do the Dominion cases add to the account?

They separate the form from its birthplaces. A generation presented with custodians it could not believe, an empire it declined to serve, and an expanding university to meet in produced the recognisable formation under other flags, which supports the claim that the New Left names a recurrent possibility and not a pair of national accidents.

The Dominion cases also refine the comparison table's central variable, the carrier. The Canadian formation attempted the one carrier the British and American formations avoided, a faction inside a mass party, and its fate, rapid expulsion once it threatened the machine's control, is the experiment the founders' institutional minimalism had predicted: the party digested or ejected every renovation that declined to become a machine itself. The Australian formation, carried by journals and campuses like the British, shared the British fate of the long diaspora, its historians and critics colonising the national culture for two generations. Carrier determines destiny with almost physical regularity across all four cases, and a theory of the New Left that began from its doctrines would miss the law entirely, which is the final justification for this guide's institutional way of telling the story.

## The question of succession

Every account of the New Left is eventually asked whether anything of the kind exists in the present, and the brief for this guide instructs that the question be answered structurally, without conscripting living movements into a genealogy they have not chosen. The structural answer begins by separating the form from the content. The form recurs whenever the double rejection recurs: whenever a generation finds the existing vehicles of the left discredited together, by complicity and by inadequacy respectively, the space the New Left occupied reopens, and something assembles in it wearing the period's clothes. The content does not recur, because the content was the property of a specific conjuncture: the postwar settlement, the Cold War's frozen camps, the first media society, the end of the European empires. A successor formation would face a different capitalism and different custodians, and its founders would be recognisable less by their doctrines than by their opening gesture: the refusal, performed in public, of both available inheritances at once.

The historiographical moral is the sharper one. Movements of this type are misread when they are treated as episodes, brilliant meteor showers after which the sky returns to normal. The record assembled in this guide suggests a better image: the New Left is what the socialist tradition does, again and again, when its institutions fail it, a standing possibility inside the tradition rather than an accident upon it. It happened in 1956 because 1956 made it necessary. The necessity, not the nostalgia, is the legacy, and it is why the founders' writings retain the power to startle readers who share none of their circumstances: the circumstances rhyme often enough.

One discipline follows for the reader of the record itself. The temptation, in studying a movement defined by refusal, is to grade it entirely by the standard of the things it refused, as if escaping two failures were the same as succeeding. The founders resist the temptation in their own best pages, where the question is never merely what was escaped but what was made: the books on the shelf, the fields in the universities, the movements staffed and the concepts still in circulation. By that standard the succession question answers itself in a limited form. Whatever later generations call themselves, the ones that repeat the founding discipline, suspicion of custodians, fidelity to experience, the patient building of institutions for argument, are the heirs in substance; the ones that repeat only the founding gestures are the heirs in costume. The distinction cannot be drawn from outside by a guide, and it does not need to be. It is drawn, in every period, by the work.

## The state arrives late

The liability of strategy and the liability of economics shared a root: the current had no theory of the state adequate to its own experience. It had watched states censor, invade, concede, and absorb for fifteen years, and its conceptual equipment for the spectacle remained the inheritance's handful of phrases about committees of the bourgeoisie. The deficiency was repaired, characteristically, from the current's own margins and in the form of a quarrel. In 1969 Ralph Miliband, a founder of the annual that carried the humanist wing, published a systematic anatomy of the capitalist state that argued from its personnel, its socialisation, and its institutional tilt; the review answered through Nicos Poulantzas, a Greek theorist formed in the Paris of structural Marxism, who objected that the state was not an instrument to be held but a structure to be analysed, its class character inscribed in its forms rather than in its officeholders' biographies. The exchange ran through the review's pages into the nineteen seventies and became the Anglophone Marxism of the state's founding debate.

### What did the great debate over the state settle?

It settled the question rather than the answer. After the exchange, no serious account could treat the state as a tool lying ready for whichever class picked it up; its forms, its relative autonomy, and its internal divisions entered the tradition's permanent agenda, which is the form theoretical progress takes in this field.

The debate's personnel complete this guide's argument about the formations. Miliband was the first New Left incarnate, the refugee scholar of the founding generation, writing the state's anatomy as an extension of socialist humanism. Poulantzas was the second formation's theory incarnate, systematic, continental, and formed by the very structuralism the founders had resisted. That the two could argue in one journal's pages, at the highest level, over the tradition's central missing concept, is the best evidence that the current, for all its fission, remained one conversation. The state theory arrived late, after the movements that needed it had dispersed, and the sequence repeated the current's rhythm exactly: the analysis perfected in the aftermath, the library enriched by the defeat.

## The last polemic

The current's internal wars ended, as they had begun, with a book by Thompson. By the late nineteen seventies the theoretical initiative in British Marxism had passed to the structural school the importing review had naturalised: a Marxism of systems and instances, hostile to humanism, experience, and the moral vocabulary, armed with a French master's authority and installed in the journals and film schools of the new theory. In 1978 Thompson answered with a polemic of a quarter of a million words whose target was that whole formation and whose method was the founding generation's entire arsenal, history, irony, moral judgment, and the archive, deployed at maximum strength. The book arraigned the structural school as the final form of the disease of 1956: an orthodoxy, complete with catechism, heresy hunts, and a custodianship of the texts, differing from the old Stalinism in its Parisian accent and its absence of a state, and guilty, in Thompson's charge, of the same original sin, the eviction of human agency from history in the name of science.

### What was the last polemic about?

It was the founding quarrel fought to the finish. Thompson's vast attack on the structural Marxism of the younger theorists defended experience, history, and moral agency against a doctrine he regarded as Stalinism's intellectual heir, and it divided the British left's intellectuals into camps that have never entirely reconciled.

The polemic's reception completed the period's symmetry. The younger theory, entrenched in the institutions, largely declined the engagement, answering with reviews rather than reckonings; the founders' generation recognised its own obituary being written by its greatest survivor; and the argument itself, humanism against anti-humanism, experience against structure, passed into the tradition's permanent stock, to be refought by every subsequent generation under new names. It is the same duel the series stages in its comparison of humanist and structuralist Marxism, and its irresolution is not a failure of the participants. The tension between the subject who makes history and the structures that make the subject is the tradition's engine, and the New Left's two tempers were its pistons.

## The economics that was never written

One liability deserves its own examination before the grading, because it is the oldest charge and in substance the truest. The New Left never produced a political economy to set beside its cultural theory. No founder wrote the current's Capital, or even its Wage Labour and Capital; the movement that announced the economy had moved into culture left the economy's own workings, crisis, accumulation, the falling and rising fortunes of profit, to the orthodoxies it despised or to the academic economics it ignored. The reasons were partly temperamental, since the founders were historians and critics, and partly doctrinal: having watched the economy function as the unanswerable trump in every orthodox argument, the basement that explained the building and excused the landlord, they were disposed to treat economic explanation itself as a suspect genre. The disposition was understandable and the cost was severe. When the postwar boom faltered at the end of the nineteen sixties and the questions of the seventies became brutally economic, inflation, profitability, the fiscal crisis of the state, the current's heirs had to borrow their economics from traditions the founding had bypassed, and the borrowing is visible in every subsequent attempt to complete the New Left's theory from outside.

### Why did the current's economics lag its cultural theory?

Because its founders' skills, its founding polemic, and its audience all pulled the other way: historians and critics, armed against an orthodoxy that had used economic law as a gag, writing for readers whose experience of capitalism was increasingly the experience of consumption, schooling, and media rather than of the mill.

The exceptions prove the rule by their marginality within the current's own canon. The importing review eventually carried serious political economy; the annual founded by the humanist wing published economists of distinction; the theory of the state that the seventies required was built substantially by a scholar formed in the current's margins. But these were acquisitions, not foundations, and they arrived after the movement that might have used them had dispersed. A grading of the New Left's Marxism must record the asymmetry plainly: the tradition's most complete renovation of the theory of consciousness was accomplished by a current that left the theory of crisis essentially where it found it, and the incompleteness is not a detail. It is the shape of the whole achievement, a head without the corresponding body of doctrine, brilliant in diagnosis and unarmed before the decades in which diagnosis was not the scarce commodity.

## The missing transition

One absence in the current's theory is so systematic that it must be named before the grading, because it organised all the others: the New Left never produced a theory of the transition, an account of how a society passes, by what steps and through what institutions, from the administered present to the self-governing future it described so vividly. The older traditions each possessed one, discredited but definite: the communists' seizure and plan, the social democrats' hundred reforms accumulating into a new order. The founders had rejected both scripts on the evidence, the first as a road to the command state and the second as a road to nowhere in particular, and they never replaced them. What they offered instead were criteria, participation, control, the enlargement of capacity, by which any step could be judged, and criteria are a compass rather than a map: they tell a movement whether it is walking in the right direction without telling it the way through the mountains.

### Why did the current never write its theory of the transition?

Because its founding evidence blocked both available scripts and its founding virtue forbade inventing a third by faith. A generation that had watched a transition produce the command state, and another produce the administered society, chose criteria over prophecy, knowing the price and judging the alternative's price higher.

The heirs felt the absence and attempted repairs. The most interesting came at the end of the nineteen seventies from within the British state itself, a pamphlet by public sector workers and movement intellectuals arguing that socialists employed by the state should work in and against it at once, using its resources against its logic, a strategy of dual position for a movement without a road. The pamphlet's honesty about the contradiction it inhabited made it a minor classic of the inheritance, and its fate repeated the pattern: admired, cited, and never generalised into the missing theory. The absence therefore stands in the grading below not as an oversight but as the current's deepest characteristic, the negative space around which its achievements arranged themselves. It knew what it was against with unmatched precision, what it was for with unusual vividness, and how to get from the one to the other not at all.

## Hegemony's English career

One concept above all carried the current's method into the decades after its dispersal, and its career shows the inheritance at work on new material. Hegemony, the Italian prisoner's name for the way a social order secures consent through the institutions of civil society rather than by coercion alone, reached the English-speaking left substantially through the channels this guide has described: the selections the founders published, the review's systematic importation, the Birmingham centre's working papers. In the hands of the centre's director during the nineteen eighties, the concept produced the period's most influential analysis of the new right's ascendancy, the reading of Thatcherism as an authoritarian populism that had won the common sense of the crisis before it won the state. Whatever a reader's verdict on that analysis, its form is the founding formation's signature at full maturity: begin from the conjuncture, read the culture as the terrain of consent, take the adversary's popular appeal seriously as evidence rather than dismissing it as deception.

### How did hegemony become the heirs' master concept?

Because it solved the founders' founding problem in a portable form: how an order rules a society whose members consent to it, and therefore where a counter-politics must work. Experience had named the problem in 1956; the imported concept gave the heirs the instrument the founders had improvised without.

The career carries the usual irony. A concept forged in a fascist prison to explain a revolutionary party's tasks became, in its English afterlife, the property of cultural analysis, invoked in seminars whose occupants might never read the notebooks entire. The founders would have recognised the process, since it is the process this guide has tracked throughout: a politics becoming a method, a method becoming a field. Hegemony's travels are the New Left's own trajectory in the biography of a single word.

## The Marxism graded

The opening of this guide promised a grading, and the evidence is now in. As a body of Marxist theory, the current's account shows clear assets and clear liabilities, and a grading that flinches from either column betrays the founders' own standards. In the asset column: a theory of culture and ideology adequate to the media age, where the inheritance offered only reflection; a recovery of agency and experience that repaired the tradition's most disabling deformation; a method of conjunctural analysis, the reading of a whole society's moving parts in their articulation, that remains the most transferable instrument the current forged; and a demonstration, in the founding books, that historical materialism could still produce masterpieces of empirical scholarship. In the liability column: a political economy that never advanced beyond gestures, leaving the current eloquent about consent and mute about crisis; a theory of the state assembled late and from borrowed parts; a strategy that oscillated between moral witness and borrowed vanguardism without settling the question of power; and an organisational theory whose founding preference for argument over discipline was never replaced by anything that could hold a majority together through a defeat.

The grade, if a single word is demanded, is that the New Left was a great renovator and a poor founder. It renewed the tradition's questions, its archive, and its methods beyond recognition, and it founded no durable vehicle for its politics, by a mixture of principle and incapacity that its members argued about for the rest of their lives. The renovation is why the series to which this guide belongs exists in its present shape: the map of Western Marxism, the recovery of the young Marx, the centrality of culture and ideology, the suspicion of custodial doctrine, are all, in their English-language transmission, substantially this current's work. A tradition is fortunate in its renovators even when it has reason to complain of them, and the complaint and the fortune should both be recorded in the same ink.

## The movement's self-history

The New Left wrote its own history early and contentiously, and the quarrels over that history are part of the evidence. The first accounts were memoirs of the break, written by participants for whom 1956 remained the axis of a life. The founders then became their own period's historians in a stricter sense: Hall's retrospective anatomy of the first formation, written three decades on, coined the distinction between first and second New Lefts that this guide adopts, and it did so as an argument, warning that the first formation's complexity was being flattened by both its detractors and its nostalgists. Thompson's late polemics and Saville's memoirs answer from the other wing. A second shelf, by scholars who arrived after, reconstructs the journals' economics, the clubs' geography, and the American organisation's internal life from archives the participants had kept or, in some bitter cases, destroyed.

### Who wrote the movement's history first?

Its participants did, unavoidably, and the early shelf is therefore evidence and advocacy at once. The memoir literature fixed the founding year and the double rejection as the story's spine before the archives opened, and later scholarship has spent as much effort correcting the founders' account of themselves as confirming it.

The self-history poses the reader a problem this guide has tried to solve by method rather than by verdict: nearly every primary narrator was a combatant in the quarrels being narrated. The remedy applied throughout has been triangulation, the journal against the memoir against the archive, and a standing discount for the gravitational pull of 1956 in every participant's telling. It is the same remedy the founders applied to the orthodoxies' histories of themselves, and applying it to them is the sincerest form of the respect they demanded for their subjects.

## For students and exam candidates

You will meet the New Left in survey courses as a colourful interlude, and the marks lie in refusing the colour and supplying the structure. Learn the founding as a sequence: the double shock of 1956, the two journals and their merger, the first editorship and the change of hands, the English quarrel, and the separate American founding with its statement of participatory democracy. Distinguish the first formation from the second whenever you write, since examiners treat the distinction as the sign of a prepared mind. The theoretical context sits in the companion accounts of [Marxist humanism explained](/2011/07/15/marxist-humanism-explained/), for the recovery of the young Marx on which the early current stood, and of the [Western Marxism: the complete guide](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/), for the tradition the current both joined and renovated. Build the two formations into a comparison table of your own, trigger, vehicle, demand, legacy, and keep the table current in a tool such as the [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), because the comparison, reproduced from memory, answers half the questions this topic sets.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This material teaches best as a problem in definition. Open with the double rejection and ask the seminar to specify, from the documents, what exactly was refused in each direction and what the refusals left standing; students who can reconstruct the negative definition stop asking whether the current had a doctrine and start asking the better question of what held it together. Set the founding texts against each other across the Atlantic: a chapter of the great work of English social history beside the Port Huron Statement, and let the difference between a historians' formation and an organisers' formation emerge from the prose itself. Follow the cultural studies descent through the companion guide to [Marxist media theory explained](/2012/05/15/marxist-media-theory-explained/), which carries the line from the Birmingham centre to the analysis of encoding and decoding. Close with the strongest objection, the conversion of defeat into career, and require the seminar to grade it against the evidence of what the movement built outside the universities. The exercise teaches the sociology of intellectuals by making students practise it on intellectuals they have just been studying.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three frontiers in this field remain genuinely open. The first is archival: the merged review's editorial records, the correspondence of the founding quarrel, and the papers of the disarmament campaign still contain unmeasured distances between the received story and the documents, particularly on the change of editorship and on how far the first formation's national network actually reached. The second is comparative: the British and American formations are usually studied by different scholars in different literatures, and a systematic comparison of their carriers, journals against organisations, with the consequences traced through their declines, is a book the field conspicuously lacks. The third is the later question, the structural recurrence of the double rejection, for which the companion account of [Marxism and social movements](/2022/04/01/marxism-and-social-movements/) supplies the series' framework and the [Western Marxism reading guide](/2016/03/15/western-marxism-reading-guide/) supplies the sources. Journalists writing about any movement that claims the inheritance should establish which formation, British or American, first or second, is actually being claimed, since the four combinations name four different politics and most public confusion on the subject is a conversation between them conducted as if they were one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the New Left?

A current of socialist thought and practice that took shape after the crises of 1956, defined in the first instance by refusal: it would not follow the communist parties after their centre confessed Stalin's crimes and crushed Hungary, and it would not accept the parliamentary labour parties' management of capitalism as socialism's final form. Around that refusal it built journals, campaigns, and a distinctive set of preoccupations: culture and everyday experience as political ground, the recovery of the young Marx against the catechism, the search for agents of change beyond the industrial working class, and an anti-imperialism directed at both Cold War camps. It existed in a British and an American formation, different in origin and legacy.

### Q: What triggered the New Left?

A single year supplied the trigger, and two blows landed within it. First the Soviet leadership's closed-session report of February 1956 admitted, from the summit of the system, the terror that communists abroad had spent decades denying. Then in the autumn Soviet forces suppressed the Hungarian rising, workers' councils and all, demonstrating that the admission would not be followed by reform from within. In Britain the same autumn brought the Suez fiasco, which exposed the governing consensus, Labour's included, as the custodian of a bankrupt imperial reflex. A generation drew the joint conclusion: both available left traditions were disqualified, and something new had to be founded in the cleared space.

### Q: How did the British and American New Left differ?

They differed first in what made them. The British current was a secession: historians and intellectuals broke with a communist party and a governing consensus in the same year, and built journals to house the argument. The American current was a foundation in a vacuum: with the older left destroyed by the anti-communist purges, students built an organisation from the civil rights movement's example upward. The carriers therefore differed, print against organisation, and so did the products: the British left a review, a publishing house, cultural studies, and a school of history; the Americans left a mass practice, a vocabulary of participation, and cadres dispersed through the successor movements. Their endings matched their beginnings, fission in one case, implosion in the other.

### Q: What is participatory democracy?

The American formation's central demand: that democracy be measured not by the periodic choice of rulers but by the share ordinary people hold in the decisions that shape their lives. Wherever consequences fall, in the firm, the university, the city, or the state, those who bear them acquire a standing right to deliberate and decide. The idea grew from the practice of the Southern civil rights movement, whose mass meetings made strategy in common, and it served the student organisation as internal constitution as well as public programme, with results that displayed both its moral force and its procedural costs: decisions by exhausting consensus, leadership disavowed while it accumulated informally.

### Q: What was the labour metaphysic criticism?

The phrase belongs to C. Wright Mills, who in an open letter of 1960 attacked the inherited assumption that the industrial working class remained, by its position alone, the appointed agent of socialist transformation. He called that assumption a metaphysic, a Victorian survival preserved as piety after the evidence had turned: in the advanced countries the class had been integrated into the postwar order, its organisations bureaucratised, its revolutionary vocation a hope rather than a finding. The criticism did not dismiss workers; it dismissed the waiting. If the appointed agent would not arrive, politics had to identify, by looking, who might actually move, and Mills pointed to the young intelligentsia and the colonised world.

### Q: What did the New Left actually produce?

More than its obituaries suggest. In Britain it founded cultural studies as an academic field, launched at Birmingham in 1964 and descended directly from the founding generation's questions; it created the modern discipline of history from below; it built a review and publishing house that carried continental Marxism into English for two generations; and it formed much of socialist feminism's founding cadre. In the United States it produced a mass movement practice, the vocabulary of participatory democracy, and a diaspora that staffed the feminist, environmental, and organising movements of the following decades. Everywhere it left an archive: the founding books and journals remain the period's most complete record of its own self-understanding.

### Q: Why did the New Left decline?

The two formations declined in their carriers' images. The British current, carried by journals and scholars, dispersed gradually through the middle sixties as its founding quarrel divided the historians from the review, its people emigrating into the disciplines they founded. The American organisation, swollen by the war's escalation beyond any structure its participatory constitution could govern, collapsed in factional war in 1969 under state pressure it had helped to provoke and could not withstand. Common solvents acted on both: emergency displaced patience, generational turnover dissolved memory, the war's end dissolved the coalition's glue, and the second formation, in desperation, re-imported the orthodoxies the first had been founded to escape.

### Q: Was the New Left actually socialist?

In intention and in centre of gravity, yes, though the label covers a spread. The British founders were socialists by formation, several of them Marxist scholars of the first rank, engaged in reconstructing their tradition after its Stalinist deformation. The American founders began as radical democrats, critical of corporate power from moral premises, and moved toward Marxist vocabularies as the decade radicalised them. The later, mass phase everywhere draped itself in a Marxism more rhetorical than studied. The current's unity was never a creed but a direction: against capitalism's organisation of life, for the self-government of the people subject to it, and against both of the older traditions that claimed to monopolise that cause.

### Q: What were the theses on English exceptionalism?

A connected set of arguments advanced in the merged review in the middle sixties by Perry Anderson and Tom Nairn. England, they held, had made its bourgeois revolution too early and incompletely: its rising class had fused with the landed aristocracy instead of defeating it, leaving the state, the culture, and eventually the labour movement in archaic forms, an empiricist intellect, a deferential working class, a socialism without theory. Britain's decline and its left's timidity were the long interest on that original incompletion. E. P. Thompson's celebrated reply defended the national tradition's radical resources against the model, and the quarrel between the theses and the reply organised the British current's subsequent history.

### Q: Is there a New Left today?

Answer the question by separating the form from the period's content. The content belonged to a vanished world: the postwar settlement, the two frozen camps, the first media society, the empires in liquidation. The form is a standing possibility inside the socialist tradition. Whenever the existing vehicles of the left are discredited together, one by complicity with power's abuses and the other by the poverty of its alternative, the space the New Left occupied reopens, and a generation assembles in it performing the same founding gesture: refusal of both inheritances, recovery of suppressed texts, and the building of journals and campaigns outside the custodians' control. Whether the occupants keep the old name matters less than whether the gesture recurs.

### Q: How does the double rejection separate the New Left from the old?

The old left, in both its branches, defined itself by custody: the communist parties as custodians of the revolution's science, the labour parties as custodians of the people's gradual advancement, each demanding loyalty to an institution as the price of belonging to the cause. The double rejection withdrew that loyalty from both institutions in the same season, on the evidence of the same year, and relocated the cause in the judgment of its adherents. What separates the New Left is therefore not a doctrine but a discipline of refusal: it would inherit the tradition's texts, questions, and hopes while declining to inherit its organisations' authority over them.

### Q: What should a student separate between the first and second New Lefts?

Separate the tempers, the carriers, and the theories of change. The first formation, from 1956 into the early sixties, was reconstructive: senior scholars and young graduates building journals, clubs, and a campaign, patient in timescale, humanist in theory, bent on remaking a tradition. The second, the explosion of the later sixties, was insurrectionary: a mass student movement carried by organisations and emergencies, eclectic and increasingly Leninist in its rhetoric, bent on making a revolution it could not define. They share personnel, vocabulary, and the founding refusal, which is why they share the name; they differ in nearly everything else, which is why examinations reward the distinction and punish the blur.

### Q: Why did a single year of crisis become the founding moment in the standard account?

Because the year's events performed, in public and in sequence, the argument no pamphlet could have made. A secret speech confessed the terror; an invasion proved the confession changed nothing structural; an imperial fiasco disgraced the alternative tradition in the same weeks. Contemporaries experienced the three as one detonation, dated their own biographies from it, and wrote the standard account from inside that experience. Later scholarship has complicated the edges, restoring earlier preparations and parallel movements abroad, without dislodging the year from the story's centre, because the participants' own sense of a before and an after is itself a historical fact of the first order.

### Q: How did the journals function as the movement's institutions?

In place of a party, the journals performed the party's cultural functions in dispersed form. They recruited, as a subscription replaced a membership card; they educated, sequencing a self-taught movement's reading issue by issue; they disciplined, mildly, by the prestige of being answered or ignored in their pages; and they remembered, carrying the founding arguments in bound volumes to readers who had never met their authors. What they could not do was act in the world at the speed of events, and the frustration of a movement whose institutions were all built for consciousness, in a tradition that insisted consciousness was not the whole of politics, runs through the entire history.

### Q: What separates a movement's Marxism from a party's Marxism?

The relation to doubt. A party's Marxism must be able to govern: it settles questions, trains cadres in the settlements, and treats revision as a security matter, since the doctrine is the organisation's title deed. A movement's Marxism governs nothing and can afford the opposite discipline: questions stay open, rival answers share the journal's pages, and the theory's authority rests on its explanatory performance rather than on any custody. The New Left's Marxism was of the second kind throughout, fertile because unsettled, fragile for the same reason, and its history is the standing demonstration that the two kinds, however often they marry, are not the same animal.
