A pendulum, a guitar string, and a shout across a field look like three unrelated physics problems, yet AP Physics 1 treats them as one family bound by a single habit of thought. Each system repeats, each carries energy without carrying matter very far, and each answers questions through relationships between a handful of variables rather than through isolated facts. Students who memorize the period formula, the wave speed formula, and the standing wave conditions as separate trivia routinely stall on questions that change one given and hide another. Students who learn how the variables lean on each other find that most questions in this territory collapse into one or two reasoning steps. That difference, between stored equations and understood links, is what this article builds.

## Why oscillation and wave questions reward relationship readers

Scoring patterns in this part of the course punish formula collectors with unusual consistency. A typical prompt describes a spring stretched a little further, a pendulum carried to a taller building, or a string tightened at the peg, and then asks what happens to a period, a frequency, or a pitch. Nothing in such a prompt invites a long computation. Everything invites a proportionality judgment: which variable sits under a square root, which sits outside it, and which does not appear at all. A candidate who has rehearsed only substitution reaches for numbers the prompt never gave, while a candidate who has rehearsed dependencies answers in seconds and banks time for the free-response section.

Graph questions sharpen the same contrast. Waves and oscillators arrive on the exam wearing graphs more often than any other costume: displacement against time, displacement against position, velocity against time for a swinging mass. Each graph offers some variables directly and hides others behind a slope, a spacing, or a quarter-cycle shift. Reading those graphs fluently is a trainable skill with a small set of moves, and this article returns to the moves repeatedly because the exam does.

The third reason the topic rewards relationship thinking is connective. Oscillation is where the course's earlier machinery, forces, energy, and circular motion, comes back in a new arrangement, and wave motion is where that machinery gets exported into a medium. A student who sees the pendulum as applied Newton's second law, the spring oscillator as applied energy conservation, and simple harmonic motion itself as the shadow of uniform circular motion owns the topic instead of renting it. The full map of the course, against which this territory sits, is laid out in [the AP Physics 1 complete guide](/2008/03/28/ap-physics-1-complete-guide/), and this article assumes that map while going far deeper on the swinging, vibrating, and sounding corner of it.

## Where waves, sound, and SHM sit inside AP Physics 1

Within the course framework, oscillation and mechanical waves form a late block that leans on nearly everything taught before it. The framework expects students to model periodic motion, to analyze mass-spring and pendulum systems, to reason about mechanical waves as energy transport, and to handle sound with its interference patterns and standing wave geometries. The multiple-choice section samples the block with graph reading, proportionality judgments, and short computations. The free-response section uses it for some of its favorite architectures: an experimental design built around a pendulum or a vibrating string, a graph-to-equation translation for an oscillator, and a qualitative-quantitative translation in which a standing wave pattern must be explained in words and then in numbers.

Two features of the block deserve emphasis before any formulas appear. First, the mathematics stays algebra-based throughout: square roots, reciprocals, ratios, and sine-shaped descriptions, with no calculus required and none rewarded. Second, the block is unusually honest about its boundaries. The Doppler effect appears as a conceptual shift in observed frequency rather than as a formula to grind, damping appears as a real-world complication rather than a modeling unit, and the pendulum's famous formula carries a small-angle condition that the exam expects students to respect. Knowing where the course stops is worth points by itself.

Preparation for this block pays best when it is built on the mechanics that feed it. The force analysis behind every restoring force, the energy bookkeeping behind every amplitude, and the circular motion behind the reference circle all live in [the forces, kinematics, and energy deep dive](/2008/03/23/ap-physics-1-forces-kinematics-energy/), and weak foundations there surface here as mysterious errors. Strengthen the base and this block becomes the most predictable scoring territory in the back half of the course.

## The InsightCrunch wave relationship table

One table organizes the whole topic. Each row names a variable or quantity, states the relationship that governs it, and identifies the graph or picture in which it can be read. Worked through row by row, the table doubles as a revision checklist: any row a student cannot explain in words is a row the exam can still use against them.

| Variable or quantity | Governing relationship | Where it is read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Period T | Time for one complete cycle, T = 1/f | Peak-to-peak spacing on a position-time graph |
| Frequency f | Cycles per second, f = 1/T, measured in hertz | Count of cycles inside a measured time interval |
| Angular frequency ω | ω = 2πf = 2π/T | Steepness of the matching sine description |
| Spring oscillator period | T = 2π√(m/k) | Rises with mass, falls with spring constant |
| Pendulum period | T = 2π√(L/g), small angles | Rises with length, falls with gravitational field |
| Maximum speed in SHM | v_max = ωA at equilibrium | Peak of the velocity-time graph |
| Maximum acceleration in SHM | a_max = ω²A at the extremes | Peak magnitude of the acceleration-time graph |
| Total energy of a spring oscillator | E = ½kA² | Set by amplitude alone, constant through the cycle |
| Wave speed | v = fλ | Slope-free product linking the two wave graphs |
| Wave speed on a string | v = √(tension/linear density) | Set by the medium, never by the source |
| Wavelength λ | Distance for one complete spatial cycle | Crest-to-crest spacing on a position snapshot |
| Standing wave on a string | L = nλ/2, harmonics at f_n = nv/(2L) | Node spacing equals half a wavelength |
| Standing wave in an open tube | L = nλ/2, all harmonics present | Antinodes at both open ends |
| Standing wave in a stopped tube | L equals an odd multiple of λ/4, odd harmonics only | Node at the closed end, antinode at the open end |

| Sound intensity level | Each +10 dB step multiplies intensity by ten | Logarithmic loudness scale for comparing sources |

Two habits make the table earn its place. First, every relationship should be read aloud in both directions: period grows with the square root of length, so quadrupling a pendulum's length doubles its period, and halving the period demands a quarter of the length. Second, every row should be tied to its picture, because the exam prefers to hand over the picture and ask for the row. A student who can move between formula, graph, and physical story in all directions has converted a memorization burden into a small network, and networks are what survive exam pressure.

## The restoring force: the one idea behind every oscillator

Simple harmonic motion begins with a single signature: a net force that points back toward equilibrium and grows in direct proportion to the displacement from it. Written compactly, the force obeys F = -kx in the spring case, where the negative sign carries the whole idea. Push the object right and the force points left; pull it left and the force points right; double the displacement and the force doubles. That proportionality is what makes the motion simple and harmonic rather than merely repetitive. Many systems swing or bounce in cycles, but only a restoring force proportional to displacement produces the clean sinusoidal rhythm the exam calls SHM.

Newton's second law converts the signature into a prediction about acceleration. Because acceleration equals net force divided by mass, the oscillator's acceleration is also proportional to displacement and oppositely directed, a = -(k/m)x for the spring. Read that statement slowly and several exam answers fall out of it. Acceleration is largest where displacement is largest, at the two extremes. Acceleration is zero where displacement is zero, at equilibrium. Acceleration always aims at the center of the swing, never at the extremes. Any graph or ranking question about an oscillator can be attacked by asking where the displacement is largest and letting the proportionality do the rest.

Proportionality also explains why the period takes the form it does. A stiffer spring pulls harder at every displacement, so the mass accelerates back more fiercely and completes its cycle sooner: period falls as k rises. A heavier mass responds more sluggishly to the same force, so the cycle stretches: period rises as m rises. Because acceleration involves the ratio k/m inside a cycle-time argument, the dependence lands on the square root of that ratio, T = 2π√(m/k). The formula stops being a fact to memorize once the causal story is in place, and the causal story is exactly what the brief for this topic asks students to build, because it dismantles the misconception that SHM is a pile of formulas. Formulas here are compressed reasoning, and the reasoning regenerates them on demand.

Equilibrium deserves a precise definition, because students routinely confuse it with rest. Equilibrium is the position where the net force is zero, which for a moving oscillator is the position it crosses at maximum speed, not a position where it lingers. A pendulum bob at the bottom of its arc is at equilibrium and moving fastest; a mass on a spring passing its natural-length point is at equilibrium and moving fastest. Questions that ask where speed peaks, where acceleration vanishes, or where kinetic energy tops out are all asking for equilibrium by three different names.

## The mass-spring system taken apart

The horizontal spring oscillator is the cleanest SHM system in the course: a mass on a low-friction surface, attached to a spring anchored at one end. Its period depends on exactly two properties, the mass and the spring constant, T = 2π√(m/k), and its frequency follows as the reciprocal. Amplitude does not enter the period at all. Stretch the system twice as far before release and the mass travels twice the distance each cycle at twice the maximum speed, finishing the longer trip in the same time. That amplitude independence is one of the most tested single facts in the topic, and it surprises students every year because everyday intuition expects a bigger swing to take longer.

Worked numbers fix the scale. Take a 0.50 kilogram mass on a spring with constant 200 newtons per meter. The angular frequency is ω = √(k/m) = √(200/0.50) = √400 = 20 radians per second, and the period is T = 2π/ω = 2π/20, about 0.31 seconds, giving a frequency near 3.2 hertz. Released from rest at an amplitude of 0.10 meters, the mass reaches a maximum speed of v_max = ωA = 20 × 0.10 = 2.0 meters per second as it crosses equilibrium. Each figure follows from the table in one step, which is the point: the exam's spring questions are chained one-step consequences for students who hold the relationships together.

Maximum acceleration lands at the extremes, where the spring force peaks. With the same numbers, a_max = ω²A = 400 × 0.10 = 40 meters per second squared, directed toward equilibrium at both ends of the travel. A useful cross-check runs through Newton's second law directly: at maximum stretch the spring exerts kA = 200 × 0.10 = 20 newtons on a 0.50 kilogram mass, and 20 newtons over 0.50 kilograms is again 40 meters per second squared. Two routes to one number is the signature of a healthy solution, and the habit of checking by a second route is worth cultivating for the whole exam.

### Why does quadrupling the mass only double the spring period?

Period grows with the square root of mass because a heavier oscillator both resists acceleration and stretches each phase of the cycle: T = 2π√(m/k) turns a factor of four in mass into a factor of two in period, while doubling the spring constant divides the period by √2, about 1.41.

Stiffness changes reward the same square-root reading. Halving the spring constant stretches the period by √2, and a spring four times as stiff halves the period. Exam writers exploit the gap between linear intuition and square-root reality relentlessly, offering doubled and halved distractors beside the correct √2 and 2 factors. The defense is to answer proportionality questions from the formula's shape rather than from gut feeling, and to state the dependence in words before touching any numbers: period follows root-mass and inverse root-stiffness.

## The pendulum taken apart

A simple pendulum pairs a compact mass with a light string or rod of length L, swinging through small angles. Gravity supplies the restoring effect here: displaced sideways, the bob's weight gains a component along the arc that pulls it back toward the bottom, and for small angles that component is proportional to the displacement along the arc. Proportionality is again the gatekeeper. With it, the motion is simple harmonic with period T = 2π√(L/g); without it, at large angles, the motion stays periodic but drifts away from the sinusoidal ideal and the formula loses accuracy.

Mass cancels out of the pendulum in a way that students should be able to narrate, not merely recite. A heavier bob feels a proportionally heavier pull from gravity, and it also carries proportionally more inertia, so the resulting acceleration is unchanged. The swing's timing belongs to the geometry and the gravitational field, never to the bob. Length and field strength are the only levers: quadruple the length and the period doubles; carry the same pendulum somewhere gravity is four times weaker and the period doubles again.

### Why does the pendulum's mass drop out of its period?

Gravity pulls a heavier bob harder in exact proportion to its extra inertia, so acceleration along the arc is unchanged and the period, T = 2π√(L/g), contains only length and the gravitational field. Mass appears on both sides of Newton's second law for the pendulum and cancels, leaving timing to geometry and gravity alone.

Small angles carry the approximation that makes the pendulum tractable. The restoring component involves the sine of the swing angle, and the sine behaves like the angle itself, measured in radians, only while the angle stays small, in practice below roughly ten to fifteen degrees for the accuracy the course assumes. Released from thirty or forty degrees, a real pendulum still swings in a steady rhythm, but its period runs slightly longer than 2π√(L/g) predicts, and exam questions that show a large release angle are often testing whether the student notices the condition attached to the formula. The honest statement is that the pendulum formula is a small-angle result, and amplitude independence holds only inside that small-angle neighborhood.

Concrete numbers anchor the scale here as well. A pendulum one meter long in a field of 10 meters per second squared has period T = 2π√(1.0/10) = 2π√0.1, about 2.0 seconds, swinging out and back once every two seconds at a frequency of 0.50 hertz. Halve the desired period to one second and the length must fall by a factor of four, to about 0.25 meters, because L = g(T/2π)² gives 10 × (1/2π)², about 0.25 meters. Clockmakers exploited exactly this relationship for centuries: a seconds pendulum, ticking once per second each way, sits near a quarter meter at half period and near one meter at full two-second period, and nudging the bob along the rod trims the rate. The exam's pendulum questions are the same physics in modern dress.

## Frequency and period: the reciprocal pair

No two quantities in the topic get swapped more often, or more expensively, than frequency and period. Period measures seconds per cycle; frequency measures cycles per second; the two are reciprocals, f = 1/T and T = 1/f, and their units, seconds and hertz, announce which is which. A 50 hertz oscillation has a period of 0.020 seconds. A pendulum with a two-second period swings at 0.50 hertz. The arithmetic is trivial, and the error pattern is anything but: students under time pressure read a graph's peak spacing as frequency, report 0.31 hertz for a system whose period is 0.31 seconds, or carry the wrong member of the pair into v = fλ and produce a wave speed off by a reciprocal.

Angular frequency completes the trio and appears wherever SHM is written as a function of time. Defined as ω = 2πf = 2π/T, it measures radians of cycle per second and converts the reciprocal pair into the language of the sine curve. The same oscillator can be described by T = 0.31 seconds, f = 3.2 hertz, or ω = 20 radians per second, and fluency means translating among the three without friction, because different exam parts prefer different members: graphs surrender period, formulas want angular frequency, and physical descriptions usually speak in frequency.

A reliable self-check guards the pair. Period and frequency must always multiply to one, so any solution reporting f = 4 hertz alongside T = 4 seconds indicts itself instantly. Building that reflex, glance at the pair and multiply, costs a second per problem and recovers more points across a season of practice than any single content review in this topic.

## Describing SHM: position, velocity, and acceleration through the cycle

An oscillator's story can be told as three curves marching together through the cycle. Position follows a sine-shaped curve between +A and -A. Velocity traces the same shape shifted by a quarter cycle: it peaks at equilibrium crossings, vanishes at the extremes, and points in the direction of travel. Acceleration traces the position curve flipped in sign: proportional to displacement and opposite to it at every instant, peaking where position peaks and vanishing where position vanishes. A student who can sketch any one of the three from another owns the descriptive core of SHM.

Anchor numbers give the curves scale. For the spring oscillator already examined, with A = 0.10 meters and ω = 20 radians per second, position swings between +0.10 and -0.10 meters, velocity between +2.0 and -2.0 meters per second, and acceleration between +40 and -40 meters per second squared. Notice the multipliers: each step from position to velocity to acceleration multiplies the peak by ω. That ladder, A, ωA, ω²A, is one of the most reusable patterns in the topic, and it explains why acceleration figures in SHM look startling next to the gentle displacements that produce them.

Phase language locates the starting point. Released from rest at maximum stretch, the position curve begins at its peak and follows a cosine shape. Pushed from equilibrium at maximum speed, position begins at zero and follows a sine shape. Both oscillators share period and amplitude; they differ only in phase, the offset that says where in the cycle the clock started. Multiple-choice questions that present two otherwise identical oscillators with different graphs are almost always interrogating phase, and the fastest reading is to check the value at time zero before comparing anything else.

### What do amplitude and phase change, and what do they leave untouched?

Amplitude sets the scale of the motion: the reach of the position curve, the peak speed ωA, the peak acceleration ω²A, and the total energy. Phase sets the starting point within the cycle. Neither touches the period, which the spring constant and mass, or the length and gravitational field, decide entirely on their own.

The quarter-cycle shifts deserve one more pass, because they generate a dependable family of graph traps. Velocity leads position by a quarter period: when position sits at +A, velocity is passing through zero on its way negative; when position crosses zero heading down, velocity sits at its negative peak. Acceleration is exactly out of step with position: maximum positive displacement pairs with maximum negative acceleration. Any answer claiming that velocity peaks at the extremes, or that acceleration peaks at equilibrium, contradicts the machinery and can be eliminated on sight.

## Energy inside an oscillation

Energy gives the second full account of SHM, and the exam expects students to run both accounts in parallel. For a spring oscillator, elastic potential energy is ½kx², largest at the extremes where x = ±A, and kinetic energy is largest at equilibrium where speed peaks. The total, E = ½kA², stays fixed in the idealized system because no non-conservative work intervenes. At any position between center and extreme, the kinetic energy is the difference KE = ½k(A² - x²), a relation that answers the common question of speed at a given displacement without touching the time functions at all.

Checking the worked spring numbers through energy confirms consistency. With k = 200 newtons per meter and A = 0.10 meters, total energy is ½ × 200 × 0.01 = 1.0 joule. At equilibrium, all of it is kinetic: ½mv² with m = 0.50 kilograms gives ½ × 0.50 × v² = 1.0, so v² = 4.0 and v = 2.0 meters per second, matching v_max = ωA exactly. At half amplitude, x = 0.05 meters, potential energy is ½ × 200 × 0.0025 = 0.25 joules, leaving 0.75 joules of kinetic energy and a speed of √3, about 1.7 meters per second. Notice the asymmetry worth remembering: at half the displacement, three quarters of the energy is already kinetic, because energy follows the square of displacement rather than the displacement itself.

Amplitude's grip on energy follows the same square law. Double the amplitude and the total energy quadruples, since E = ½kA². Halve the amplitude and the energy falls to a quarter. Questions that rank oscillators by energy, or that ask how a changed release point alters maximum speed, reduce to this one proportionality: v_max scales with A, energy with A², period with neither.

The pendulum runs the same bookkeeping with gravity holding the potential energy. Released from a raised position, the bob trades gravitational potential for kinetic energy on the way down, reaches maximum speed at the bottom where its height is lowest, and trades back on the way up. The total mechanical energy stays constant in the idealized treatment, and the speed at the bottom follows from the drop height through ½mv² = mgh. A bob released from rest 0.20 meters above its lowest point reaches √(2 × 10 × 0.20) = 2.0 meters per second at the bottom, with g taken as 10 meters per second squared. Mass cancels again, quietly reinforcing the topic's recurring theme: in pendulum physics, mass is the variable that looks important and is not.

Amplitude, by contrast, matters enormously to a pendulum's energy while leaving its small-angle period alone. Raise the release height and the bottom speed grows with the square root of height, the arc widens, and the energy grows in direct proportion to height, all while the clock of the swing keeps the same rate. Holding those two ledgers apart, timing on one side and energy on the other, prevents the most common pendulum error of all: assuming that a pendulum released from higher must swing faster in period as well as in speed.

## The vertical spring and the shifted equilibrium

Hanging the spring vertically changes the scenery without changing the physics. Gravity stretches the spring to a new equilibrium where the spring force balances the weight, at an extension of mg/k below the natural length. Released or disturbed from there, the mass oscillates about that stretched equilibrium with exactly the period the horizontal case predicts, T = 2π√(m/k). Gravity relocates the center of the oscillation; it does not retime it.

Energy accounting in the vertical case rewards care. If gravitational potential energy is tracked alongside elastic potential energy, the combined potential behaves exactly like a single spring potential measured from the shifted equilibrium, which is why speed calculations about that point work so cleanly. The exam's vertical-spring questions test precisely this comfort: identifying the true equilibrium as the hanging rest position, measuring displacement from it, and refusing to be distracted by the static stretch, which affects where the motion happens and nothing about how fast it cycles.

## The reference circle: SHM as the shadow of circular motion

One geometric picture unifies the topic's graphs and formulas. Imagine a point traveling around a circle at constant angular speed ω, and watch only the shadow it casts on one diameter as light shines across. The shadow slides back and forth along the diameter, lingering at the edges, hurrying through the middle: it executes simple harmonic motion with amplitude equal to the circle's radius and period equal to the rotation period. SHM is uniform circular motion viewed from the side.

The reference circle explains facts that otherwise sit as separate memoranda. Velocity of the shadow is the component of the point's tangential velocity along the diameter, maximal when the point crosses the diameter's perpendicular and zero at the ends, which is why the oscillator's speed peaks at equilibrium. Acceleration of the shadow is the diameter-component of the point's centripetal acceleration, always aimed at the center, which is why oscillator acceleration always aims at equilibrium and peaks at the extremes. Phase becomes literal geometry: the angle of the radius at time zero.

The picture also disciplines graph sketching under time pressure. Faced with an unfamiliar starting condition, a student can place the point on the circle, rotate mentally through a quarter turn, and read off which quantities peak where. Teachers have used the reference circle for generations precisely because it converts three memorized curves into one rotating radius, and the exam's hardest descriptive questions about oscillators yield to it faster than to any formula sheet.

## What a wave carries, and what it leaves behind

A mechanical wave is a traveling disturbance that transports energy through a medium while the medium's particles oscillate around their own equilibrium positions and stay, on average, where they started. A stadium crowd performing a wave sends the pattern around the arena while every spectator ends in the same seat. A pulse sent down a rope reaches the far wall and delivers its energy there, while each segment of rope finishes the event at home. That separation, pattern travels and matter oscillates, is the founding idea of the whole wave half of the topic, and exam questions probe it directly with floating corks, drifting buoys, and marked points on strings.

Frequency belongs to the source and wavelength emerges from the marriage of source and medium. An oscillator vibrating at frequency f launches successive crests one period apart in time; the medium carries each crest forward at speed v; the spatial spacing between crests is therefore λ = v/f, equivalently v = fλ. Change the source's frequency and the wavelength adjusts so the product holds. Change the medium and the speed changes, dragging the wavelength with it while the frequency, set by the source, stays put. That asymmetry, frequency follows the source and speed follows the medium, resolves a large family of boundary questions in which a wave passes from one string, one depth, or one temperature into another.

Amplitude in a wave plays the role it played in SHM: it measures the maximum displacement of the medium's particles and governs the energy the wave transports, which grows with the square of amplitude. Amplitude does not govern speed in the linear regime the course treats. Shouting louder does not make sound arrive sooner; plucking a string harder does not make the pulse reach the bridge sooner. Keeping amplitude in the energy ledger and out of the speed ledger is another of the topic's recurring separations.

## Transverse and longitudinal waves

Orientation separates the two great wave families. In a transverse wave, the medium's particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction the wave travels: a string's segments move up and down while the pulse runs horizontally, and the displacement creates visible crests and troughs. In a longitudinal wave, particles oscillate parallel to the travel direction, crowding into compressions and spreading into rarefactions, with no sideways excursion at all. Sound in air is the course's flagship longitudinal wave, and its compressions are regions of raised pressure that the ear ultimately detects.

Graphing a longitudinal wave demands a translation that trips many students. Because air particles displace along the travel axis, their displacement can still be plotted against position, producing a sine-shaped curve that looks transverse but must be read differently: where the displacement curve crosses zero heading down, particles crowd into a compression; where it crosses heading up, a rarefaction sits. Pressure graphs offer the alternative portrait, peaking at compressions and dipping at rarefactions. Exam items that show a sound wave drawn like a rope wave are testing whether the student treats the drawing as a representation with rules rather than as a photograph.

Both families obey the same bookkeeping. Each has frequency set by its source, speed set by its medium, wavelength fixed by their ratio, and energy scaled by amplitude squared. The families differ in geometry and in which media support them, not in the arithmetic that describes them, and confidence in that shared arithmetic is what the topic is really examining.

## Wave speed: the medium decides

The relation v = fλ describes every wave, but it is a description, not an explanation. Explanation lives in the medium. For a string, speed rises with tension and falls with linear mass density according to v = √(F_T/μ), where μ is mass per unit length. Tighten the string and pulses run faster; swap in a heavier string at the same tension and pulses slow. Quadrupling the tension doubles the speed, because the tension sits under a square root, another square-root dependence in a topic full of them. Guitarists exploit the tension law every time they tune: raising tension raises speed, which raises the standing wave frequencies the string can sing at, which the ear hears as pitch climbing.

Sound speed in air sits near 340 meters per second at ordinary room conditions, rising gently with temperature as the air molecules move faster and hand the disturbance along more quickly. Exam problems typically supply 340 or 343 meters per second, and the exact choice matters less than the structural point: a singer cannot change the speed of sound en route to a listener by singing a higher note. The higher note raises frequency and shrinks wavelength in exact compensation, at 440 hertz a wavelength of 340/440, about 0.77 meters, while at 220 hertz the wavelength doubles to about 1.55 meters and the speed stands unchanged.

### What actually sets the speed of a wave on a string?

Only the string itself decides: its tension and its mass per unit length, combined as v = √(F_T/μ). The hand that plucks sets frequency and amplitude, and the wavelength then adjusts to satisfy v = fλ. Faster vibration means shorter waves at the same speed, never a faster wave.

Boundary behavior follows from the medium principle. When a wave crosses into a medium where its speed differs, frequency survives the crossing untouched because the source keeps oscillating at its own rate and the boundary must match cycles one for one. Wavelength absorbs the whole change: a wave entering a slower medium compresses its wavelength in proportion to the speed drop. Questions staging a pulse that runs from a light string into a heavy joined string, or sound passing between air layers, are solved by protecting frequency, recomputing wavelength, and leaving the rest to the medium.

## Reading wave graphs without mixing the axes

Two graphs dominate the wave half of the exam, and confusing them is the single most reliable way to donate points. A displacement-versus-position graph is a snapshot: it freezes the whole medium at one instant, and the distance between neighboring crests on it is the wavelength, while the vertical reach is the amplitude. A displacement-versus-time graph watches one point of the medium: it shows that point's oscillation, and the time between neighboring crests on it is the period, from which frequency and, with wavelength, the wave speed follow.

Harmonic motion ties the two portraits together, because the point watched by the time graph is itself an oscillator executing SHM with the wave's frequency. The snapshot and the single-point trace therefore share amplitude, differ in what their horizontal axes measure, and together determine the wave completely. A reliable protocol reads the axes before the curve: name the horizontal quantity, name what one crest spacing means on that axis, and only then extract numbers. Students who skip the axis check routinely report a period where a wavelength lives, then feed the error into v = fλ and watch a straightforward problem collapse.

Direction of travel can also be read from a snapshot when a second instant or a marked particle's velocity is given. If a marked point on the string is moving upward as the snapshot was taken, the wave pattern must be sliding in whichever direction brings the neighboring shape into that point's position next. The technique is local and mechanical: shift the drawn pattern slightly left or right in the mind, see which shift makes the marked point move as stated, and choose that direction. Practicing that shift on a few examples converts an intimidating graph family into a routine one.

## Sound: a longitudinal wave the ear reports as pitch and loudness

Sound reaches the exam as the applied face of longitudinal waves. A vibrating source, a speaker cone, a reed, a vocal cord, pushes air into alternating compressions and rarefactions that propagate outward at the speed the air permits. The ear receives the pressure pattern and reports two headline qualities: pitch, tracking the frequency, and loudness, tracking the intensity carried by the wave. Human hearing spans roughly 20 hertz to 20,000 hertz, and exam problems live comfortably inside that band, in the few hundred hertz of voices and instruments.

Because sound is longitudinal, its diagrams and questions reward the translation skill built earlier. A pressure-versus-position graph peaks where compressions sit; a displacement-versus-position graph for the same wave crosses zero at those spots. Either representation yields the wavelength from one full spatial cycle, and pairing that wavelength with the given or computed speed yields frequency. Sound problems frequently chain exactly those two steps and then ask for something human: the note's octave relation, the time for an echo to return, or the comparison between two sources.

Echo reasoning deserves its own sentence because it appears so often in disguise. An echo is a round trip, so a cliff face that returns a shout after 1.2 seconds sits at 340 × 1.2 / 2, about 204 meters. Forgetting the division by two is the planted error in every such item, and the defense is to narrate the journey, out and back, before computing anything.

## Intensity, loudness, and the decibel pattern

Intensity measures the power a wave delivers per unit area, and it follows the amplitude-squared law that runs through the topic: double a sound wave's pressure amplitude and the intensity quadruples. Spreading matters as well; an idealized point source pours its power over an expanding sphere, so intensity falls with the square of distance. Doubling the distance from a small source quarters the intensity, a relationship exam items test with moved listeners and relocated speakers.

Loudness comparisons use the decibel scale, a logarithmic ruler built for exactly such wide-ranging ratios. The rule a Physics 1 student needs is the step rule: every increase of 10 decibels corresponds to ten times the intensity. Twenty decibels above a reference means one hundred times the intensity; thirty means one thousand times. Working backward, halving or doubling distance changes intensity by a factor of four or a quarter, never by a factor that the decibel scale reports as a round step, and questions often ask for the qualitative verdict, noticeably quieter, rather than a computed level. Treat the scale as a ratio translator and its questions stay gentle.

## Superposition: when waves share the same space

Two waves occupying the same medium at the same time add their displacements point by point, a rule called superposition. The medium does not choose between them or average them; it sums them. A crest meeting a crest builds a double-height crest for the instant they overlap, then each wave continues onward with its shape intact, as though the meeting never happened. A crest meeting an equal trough cancels to flatness at the overlap, again only for the instant of meeting. Pulses pass through each other; waves are not billiard balls.

Superposition scales from pulses to continuous tones and underlies everything in the interference sections that follow. It also governs a subtler exam favorite: two waves of slightly different frequency traveling together, whose sum swells and fades in the pattern named beats. The student who internalizes superposition as arithmetic, displacements adding with signs respected, finds the rest of the wave unit to be bookkeeping. The student who treats each wave as an object that can block or deflect the other invents collisions the physics does not contain.

## Interference and path difference

Sustained interference between two coherent sources, same frequency and a fixed phase relationship, produces a stable landscape of loud and quiet places. The geometry that decides each location is path difference: how much farther the wave from one source traveled than the wave from the other. Where the path difference equals a whole number of wavelengths, crests arrive with crests and troughs with troughs, giving constructive interference and a loud spot. Where the path difference equals a half-integer number of wavelengths, crest arrives with trough, giving destructive interference and a quiet spot.

Worked geometry makes the rule concrete. Two speakers driven in phase emit a tone with wavelength 2.0 meters. A listener positioned 7.0 meters from one speaker and 4.0 meters from the other sits at a path difference of 3.0 meters, which is 1.5 wavelengths, squarely in the destructive family: the spot is quiet. Move the listener until the difference becomes 4.0 meters, exactly two wavelengths, and the same tone arrives in step from both speakers: the spot is loud. Exam variants change the wavelength, offset the sources' phase, or ask for the smallest nonzero path difference producing silence, which is always half a wavelength.

Phase offsets at the sources shift the whole map by half a step. Drive the speakers in opposite phase and the loud and quiet conditions exchange places, because the arrival pattern inherits an extra half-cycle flip. Reflection can impose the same flip: a wave reflecting from a fixed end of a string inverts, while reflection from a free end does not, a fact that matters directly for the standing waves built next.

### How can two loudspeakers leave one seat in near silence?

Each speaker's wave reaches that seat, but the two travel distances differ by half a wavelength, so crests from one arrive locked against troughs from the other and the air's displacement sums near zero. Shift seats until the distances differ by a whole wavelength and the same pair sounds loud there.

## Standing waves on a string

Send a continuous wave along a string fixed at both ends and the reflected wave, inverted at each fixed end, interferes with the incoming wave everywhere at once. At most frequencies the result is a shifting mess. At special frequencies the interference locks into a standing wave: a pattern that vibrates in place, with nodes where the string never moves and antinodes where it swings through its widest arc. The pattern is superposition made visible, two traveling waves of equal amplitude and frequency crossing in opposite directions.

Geometry dictates the allowed wavelengths. Both ends must be nodes, since fixed ends cannot move, so the string length must hold a whole number of half wavelengths: L = nλ/2 for n = 1, 2, 3, and so on. Each allowed pattern is a harmonic, with frequencies f_n = nv/(2L), built on the fundamental f₁ = v/(2L). Adjacent nodes sit half a wavelength apart, λ/2, a spacing students can measure straight off a diagram, making the diagram-to-wavelength step one of the exam's favorite opening moves.

Numbers carry the idea cleanly. A string 1.2 meters long carries waves at 240 meters per second. Its fundamental frequency is f₁ = 240/(2 × 1.2) = 100 hertz, with wavelength 2.4 meters folded into the string as a single half-wave loop. The third harmonic vibrates at 300 hertz with wavelength 2L/3 = 0.80 meters, drawn as three loops with four nodes counting the endpoints. Tightening the string raises v and lifts every harmonic in proportion, while pressing a finger to shorten the vibrating length raises the fundamental by shrinking L: two different physical gestures, one shared formula.

Harmonic content explains why instruments playing the same note sound different, and exam qualitative items borrow that richness. The fundamental usually carries the largest amplitude and sets the perceived pitch, while the mixture of higher harmonics shapes timbre. A question that overlays two standing wave sketches and asks which string sounds higher is asking for f₁ comparisons through L and v; a question that shows the third harmonic and asks for the fundamental's wavelength is asking for the factor of three in the loop count.

## Standing waves in tubes: open ends and stopped ends

Air columns resonate by the same logic with different boundary conditions. At an open end of a tube, air moves freely and the standing wave places a displacement antinode; at a closed end, air cannot move and the pattern places a node. A tube open at both ends therefore mirrors the string: antinodes at both ends, L = nλ/2, all harmonics present, frequencies f_n = nv/(2L). A tube closed at one end must fit a node-to-antinode pattern, which consumes odd quarter wavelengths: L equals λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, and only odd harmonics sound, at f = v/(4L), 3v/(4L), 5v/(4L).

The asymmetry between the two tube families is a point factory. A stopped tube 0.85 meters long, with sound at 340 meters per second, resonates fundamentally at f = 340/(4 × 0.85) = 100 hertz, its wavelength of 3.4 meters extending four times the tube's physical length beyond the open end in the pattern's geometry. Its next resonance is the third harmonic at 300 hertz; there is no second harmonic, and answering 200 hertz to a next-resonance question is the standard planted error. An open tube of the same length starts at 340/(2 × 0.85) = 200 hertz and offers every multiple: 200, 400, 600. Side-by-side comparisons of equal-length tubes appear constantly, and the stopped tube always sounds the lower fundamental, at half the open tube's frequency, because it fits its wave in quarters rather than halves.

Reading tube diagrams requires the same axis discipline as wave graphs, plus one extra conversion. Diagrams draw displacement patterns, but pressure tells the complementary story: a displacement node is a pressure antinode, and vice versa. An item that asks where pressure variation is greatest inside a stopped tube points at the closed end, where air sloshes least and pressure swings most. Keeping the two portraits straight, displacement for motion and pressure for squeeze, prevents an entire species of error.

## Resonance: pushing at the natural frequency

Resonance is the dramatic face of standing waves and driven oscillation. Every oscillator and every air column owns natural frequencies at which it responds with disproportionate enthusiasm. Drive a system at one of those frequencies, even gently, and each push arrives in step with the motion already underway, feeding energy in cycle after cycle until amplitude grows large or losses balance the input. A child on a swing, pumped by small pushes timed to the swing's own period, is the canonical picture: timing, not strength, does the work.

The exam treats resonance qualitatively and through the standing wave conditions. A tube resonates for the wavelengths that fit its boundary conditions; a string resonates at its harmonics; a driven spring-mass system responds most strongly when driven near √(k/m)/(2π) in frequency terms. Questions typically ask which driving frequency produces the large response, or why a singer's note can threaten a glass while louder noise cannot: the note must match a natural frequency and sustain the match long enough for amplitude to build. Off-frequency driving, however powerful, produces only modest response, which is why resonance questions always reduce to a matching test between the driver's frequency and the system's own list.

## Beats: two close frequencies negotiating

Overlap two tones of slightly different frequencies and superposition writes a new pattern in time. The combined sound swells when the waves drift into step and fades when they drift out of step, cycling at the beat frequency f_beat = |f₁ - f₂|. Forks at 256 and 260 hertz produce four swells per second, one each time the pair slips by a full cycle relative to each other. Piano tuners live inside this arithmetic, listening for beats to slow and vanish as a string is drawn into unison with its reference.

Beat questions on the exam stay close to the definition: given two frequencies, report the absolute difference; given a beat rate and one frequency, offer the two candidates f ± f_beat and let context choose. The two-candidate structure is worth flagging, because a measured beat of 4 hertz against a known 256 hertz fork means the unknown fork is 252 or 260 hertz, and items delight in withholding the tie-breaker until a second measurement, such as beats quickening as a string is tightened, settles the sign. That two-step reasoning, absolute difference first and direction second, is the whole skill.

## The Doppler shift, treated at the level the exam treats it

When a sound source and an observer move relative to each other, the observed frequency shifts away from the emitted frequency. Approach compresses the effective wavelength ahead of a moving source, or carries the observer into crests more rapidly, and the observed pitch rises. Recession stretches the spacing, or lets crests arrive less often, and the observed pitch falls. AP Physics 1 examines the Doppler effect at this conceptual level: the direction of the shift, the reasoning from wavefront spacing, and the comparison between source motion and observer motion, rather than heavy formula computation.

Wavefront diagrams carry the reasoning. A stationary source emits concentric circles spaced one wavelength apart. Set the source moving rightward and the circles bunch ahead of it and spread behind it, each circle still centered where it was emitted. An observer in front samples the bunched spacing and hears a higher frequency; an observer behind samples the spread spacing and hears lower. Questions show such diagrams, or ambulance scenarios, and ask for direction and relative size of shifts. The disciplined answer names the mechanism, spacing changed between emission and reception, and avoids inventing symmetric claims the diagrams do not support, because source motion changes the wavelength in the medium while observer motion changes only the encounter rate, two routes to a similar shift that the course keeps conceptually distinct.

## The question types that test this topic on multiple choice

Multiple-choice items in this block cluster into recognizable families, and naming the family early shortens every solution. Proportionality items change one variable in a formula and ask for the factor in the answer, rewarding the square-root readings built throughout this article. Graph-reading items present one of the standard curves and ask for a variable the curve hides, rewarding the axis protocol. Ranking items line up several oscillators or tubes and ask for an ordering by period, frequency, or pitch, rewarding qualitative dependency checks over computation. Scenario items describe a gesture, tightening, shortening, moving closer, doubling, and ask for the qualitative consequence.

A family worth isolating is the two-graph item, which pairs a snapshot with a single-point trace, or a position curve with a velocity curve, and demands a quantity neither graph surrenders alone. Wave speed is the usual target: wavelength from the snapshot, period from the trace, product v = fλ assembled across the pair. Students who practice reading the two portraits as a matched set find these items generous; students who treat them as two separate problems rarely finish either.

Elimination habits specific to the block also pay. Any option that makes a pendulum's period depend on its bob's mass is dead on arrival. Any option that speeds a wave up by raising its frequency at a fixed medium is dead on arrival. Any option giving a stopped tube an even harmonic is dead on arrival. Stockpiling such instant kills, each one a corollary of the relationship table, converts hard items into quick ones and protects against the arithmetic slips that long computations invite. Broader multiple-choice craft across subjects is collected in [the multiple-choice strategies guide](/2006/10/27/ap-multiple-choice-strategies-all-subjects/), and it layers cleanly on top of the topic-specific kills catalogued here.

## The question types that test this topic on free response

Free-response questions in this block favor three architectures. Experimental design items hand students a pendulum, a string, or a tube and ask for a procedure to measure something, often the gravitational field from pendulum timing or an unknown frequency from standing wave geometry, complete with variables to control, quantities to graph, and the slope or intercept that yields the prize. Graph-translation items give an oscillator's position graph and demand its equation, its velocity graph, or the effect of a changed release. Qualitative-quantitative translation items describe a standing wave or interference scenario in words, require a justified prediction in prose, and then attach numbers to the same scenario.

The pendulum-g measurement deserves a worked sketch because it recurs so faithfully. Time many swings of a pendulum at several lengths, because timing thirty periods and dividing crushes reaction-time error. Compute T² for each length and plot T² against L: the relationship T = 2π√(L/g) squares into T² = (4π²/g)L, a line through the origin whose slope is 4π²/g, so g falls out as 4π² divided by the slope. Every element of that chain, the repeated timing, the linearization, the slope identification, is a scoring point wearing plain clothes, and the same linearization instinct serves spring and wave experiments throughout the block.

Justification quality separates scores on the prose parts. Stating that a tube's fundamental drops because the wavelength quadruples the length earns more than stating the formula alone; the rubric hunts for the causal connective tissue. Physics free-response strategy across all four exams, including how readers award such justification, is treated in depth in [the physics FRQ strategy guide](/2008/02/21/ap-physics-frq-strategy-all-exams/), and the habits there compound with this topic's relationship-first preparation.

## A worked approach: an oscillator read from its graph

Consider a constructed practice scenario in the exam's style. A position-time graph shows a mass on a spring oscillating between +0.20 and -0.20 meters, with successive peaks 0.50 seconds apart. Reading the graph takes three glances: amplitude is the reach, A = 0.20 meters; period is the peak spacing, T = 0.50 seconds; from those, frequency is f = 2.0 hertz and angular frequency is ω = 2π/T = 4π, about 12.6 radians per second. Maximum speed follows as v_max = ωA = 4π × 0.20 = 0.80π, about 2.5 meters per second. If the mass is 0.40 kilograms, the spring constant comes from ω² = k/m, giving k = mω² = 0.40 × 16π², about 63 newtons per meter. Total energy is ½kA² = ½ × 63 × 0.04, about 1.3 joules, confirmed independently by ½mv_max² = ½ × 0.40 × (0.80π)², which also lands near 1.3 joules. Two routes agreeing is the check the exam cannot see but the score can feel.

Notice the order of operations, because it generalizes. The graph surrenders A and T first; everything else is a consequence drawn from the relationship table in a fixed cascade: f from T, ω from f, maxima from ω and A, constants from ω and the given mass, energy from whichever pair is most convenient. Free-response parts march down exactly this cascade, each part consuming the previous part's harvest, which is why an early misread is so costly and why the first thirty seconds belong to careful extraction.

## A worked approach: a standing wave in a stopped tube

A second constructed scenario exercises the tube family. A tube closed at one end is 0.85 meters long and resonates first at 100 hertz with sound speed 340 meters per second, numbers already verified in the tube section. A follow-up asks for the tube's third resonance and for the wavelength there. Odd harmonics only: the resonances run 100, 300, 500 hertz, so the third resonance is 300 hertz, and its wavelength is λ = v/f = 340/300, about 1.13 meters, which the geometry confirms independently because the tube then holds three quarters of a wavelength, 3λ/4 = 0.85 meters, giving λ = 1.13 meters again. Agreement between the frequency route and the geometry route closes the problem with confidence.

A companion part might ask what changes if the tube is opened at both ends, same length. The boundary conditions flip to antinode-antinode, the fundamental moves to 200 hertz, and the full harmonic ladder switches on. Structuring an answer around boundary conditions first, pattern second, numbers last gives the reader every justification point the part contains, and it inoculates against the reflex error of reusing the stopped-tube numbers with the new geometry.

## Three error autopsies

Autopsy one: the reciprocal slip. A student reads a peak spacing of 0.50 seconds from a graph, labels it frequency, and computes a wave speed of 0.50 × 1.2 = 0.60 meters per second for a wave whose wavelength is 1.2 meters. The true frequency is 2.0 hertz and the true speed 2.4 meters per second. The error was not arithmetic; it was a naming failure at the moment of extraction, repaired by the discipline of writing units beside every extracted quantity, since 0.50 seconds refuses to be called cycles per second once its unit is on paper.

Autopsy two: the snapshot misread. A student takes a displacement-versus-position snapshot, measures the time-labeled spacing printed beside it incorrectly, or worse, measures peak spacing on a time graph and reports it as wavelength. Snapshot axes measure meters, trace axes measure seconds, and the artifact being measured, wavelength or period, belongs to the axis, not to the curve's shape. The two curves can look identical while meaning entirely different things, which is precisely why the exam pairs them.

Autopsy three: the even harmonic in a stopped tube. A student lists a 0.85 meter stopped tube's resonances as 100, 200, 300 hertz by analogy with strings. The node-antinode geometry permits only odd multiples of the quarter-wave fundamental, so the ladder is 100, 300, 500. The analogy to strings failed because the boundary conditions differ, and the repair is to derive ladders from boundary conditions each time rather than importing them across geometries.

## Traps that cost points in this topic

The traps in this block form a short, memorizable rogue's gallery. Frequency and period get swapped at extraction time. Amplitude gets promoted into a period variable it never influences. Pendulum mass gets treated as a timing variable when it cancels. Wave speed gets assigned to the source instead of the medium. A stopped tube gets awarded even harmonics. Sound's speed gets changed by a louder or higher source. Graphs get read by shape while their axes go unnamed. Each trap corresponds to a row of the relationship table read carelessly, and each has a named defense in the sections above.

One more trap deserves separate mention because it hides in otherwise strong work: the small-angle condition. A pendulum released from 45 degrees does not keep the period 2π√(L/g) promises, and a response that applies the formula without acknowledging the angle's size can lose both the computation and the justification. When a problem states a modest angle, name the approximation as satisfied; when it states a large one, flag the formula as approximate and expect a period slightly longer than the small-angle value. Examiners plant the angle in the givens precisely to see whether anyone is watching it.

## The two oscillators side by side

Placing the spring and the pendulum in direct comparison exposes the topic's deepest pattern: identical architecture, different levers. Each system has an equilibrium, a restoring effect proportional to displacement, an inertia that resists the restoring effect, and a period fixed by the ratio between them. For the spring, the restoring strength is k and the inertia is m, giving T = 2π√(m/k). For the pendulum, gravity supplies a restoring strength that scales with the bob's own weight while inertia scales identically, so mass cancels and the geometry steps in: T = 2π√(L/g), with length playing the role that mass played for the spring, the quantity in the numerator that stretches the cycle.

Energy tells a parallel comparison. Spring energy lives in elastic potential, set by ½kA². Pendulum energy lives in gravitational potential, set by the release height. Speed peaks at equilibrium in both, acceleration peaks at the extremes in both, and amplitude governs energy in both while governing period in neither. A student examined on an unfamiliar oscillator, a mass on a rubber band, a bobbing buoy treated as an oscillator, should reach for this template: find equilibrium, find what restores, find what resists, and the period's dependency list writes itself. Buoyancy-driven bobbing, for instance, restores through displaced water weight and resists through the object's mass, and even without a formula the template predicts which changes speed the bobbing and which do nothing.

Dependency questions become almost mechanical under the comparison. Which changes quicken a spring oscillator? Stiffer spring, lighter mass. Which changes quicken a pendulum? Shorter length, stronger field. Which changes affect neither period? Amplitude in both, mass in the pendulum, and in the spring the surface, the color, and every other distractor the item writer can dress up. The exam's ranking items, ordering four pendulums by period or four springs by frequency, are this comparison administered at speed.

## Limiting cases: checking formulas by their extremes

A formula that cannot survive its extremes should not be trusted in its middle, and extreme-case checking is a portable weapon this topic teaches well. Let the spring constant grow without bound: an infinitely stiff spring should snap back instantly, period collapsing toward zero, and T = 2π√(m/k) obeys, shrinking as k grows. Let the mass grow without bound and the period swells, as intuition demands. Send the pendulum's length toward zero and its period vanishes; carry it to a planet with a stronger field and the period shrinks by the root of the field ratio. Every dependency in the relationship table passes these interrogations, which is part of why the table can be trusted under pressure.

Exam answers can be audited the same way after solving. A computed pendulum period that lengthens when the problem shortens the string has failed a limiting check before any arithmetic is reviewed. A wave speed that changes when only the source frequency changed has failed one too. Building the reflex to ask what should grow, what should shrink, and what should stand still takes seconds and catches the sign and reciprocal errors that raw recomputation often repeats, since recomputation tends to follow the same mistaken path that produced the answer.

## When the swing is not simple: large angles, damping, and real springs

Ideal SHM is a model with edges, and the exam respects the edges. The pendulum formula assumes small angles; beyond them the restoring component follows the sine of the angle rather than the angle itself, the motion stays periodic but ceases to be sinusoidal, and the period creeps upward as amplitude grows. A response that acknowledges the approximation and its direction, large swings run slow, demonstrates the model awareness the course framework prizes.

Real oscillators also lose energy. Friction and air resistance drain amplitude over time in the behavior called damping: the swings shrink, often geometrically, while the period stays near the ideal value for the light damping of laboratory systems. A graph of a damped oscillator shows decaying peaks with steady spacing, and items built on such graphs test whether students read period from the spacing, which survives, rather than expecting constant amplitude, which does not. The idealized formulas remain the working tools; damping is the reality check that explains why a laboratory pendulum's fifth swing is smaller than its first while its timing barely changes.

Springs deviate too when pushed beyond their elastic comfort. Stretch far enough and the force stops growing in strict proportion to extension, the proportionality constant itself starts to wander, and the motion departs from SHM. The course confines itself to the proportional regime, and problems signal that confinement by describing ideal springs or light stretches. Recognizing the regime a problem lives in, and saying so in a justification, is a small habit that protects against overclaiming in prose answers.

## Measuring the spring constant two ways

Laboratory work in this block usually certifies the spring constant through two independent doors, and free-response items love to make students walk through both. The static door hangs known weights on a vertical spring and records the stretch: the spring force balances weight at each hanging equilibrium, so a graph of force against stretch is a line whose slope is k itself. The dynamic door sets the spring oscillating with several masses and times the periods: since T² = (4π²/k)m, a graph of T² against m is again a line, whose slope 4π²/k yields k from timing alone. Two slopes, two methods, one constant, and agreement between them is the experimentalist's version of the second-route check.

Each door guards a different error. Static measurement can be fooled by reading stretch from the wrong reference or by exceeding the spring's proportional range. Dynamic measurement can be fooled by sloppy timing, which is why the procedure times many cycles and divides, and by counting errors, which is why careful students count complete cycles aloud against a fiducial mark at equilibrium, where the mass moves fastest and its crossings are sharpest to judge. Notice the same fiducial logic serves the pendulum laboratory: time the bob as it sweeps the bottom, not as it hesitates at the turnarounds, because the bottom crossing is a crisp event and the turnaround is a smear.

## Waves at boundaries: reflection and what crosses over

Pulses meeting the end of a string demonstrate boundary rules in their simplest form. At a fixed end, where the string is tied down and cannot move, the reflected pulse returns inverted: a crest comes back as a trough. At a free end, idealized as a ring sliding on a pole, the pulse returns upright. Inversion on fixed-end reflection is not a decorative fact; it is the mechanism that guarantees a node at a fixed end when reflections superpose into standing waves, which is why the standing wave section could assert nodes at tied ends with confidence.

Where two strings of different densities join under the same tension, an arriving pulse splits its story: part reflects, part transmits into the second string at the new speed the second medium imposes. Frequency cannot change across the junction because the joined point must move with a single rhythm, so the transmitted wave's wavelength rescales to the new speed. Entering a heavier, slower string, the transmitted wavelength shortens; entering a lighter, faster string, it lengthens. The reflected pulse inverts when the wave runs into the heavier string and stays upright when it runs into the lighter one, mirroring the fixed and free end rules in softened form. Exam items in this family are answered entirely by protecting frequency, recomputing wavelength from the new speed, and applying the inversion rule by density comparison.

## Instruments as applied standing waves

Musical instruments are standing wave machines with good public relations, and the exam borrows their familiarity constantly. A guitar string fixed at nut and bridge vibrates in its harmonic ladder, with pitch set by the fundamental through length, tension, and density. Shorten the string at a fret and the fundamental climbs because L shrank. Tune at the peg and the fundamental climbs because tension raised the wave speed. Swap to a bass string and the fundamental drops because the heavier string slows the wave. Three everyday gestures, three distinct rows of the relationship table, one formula f₁ = v/(2L) with v = √(F_T/μ) nested inside it.

Wind instruments run the tube logic. A flute behaves as an open tube, both ends effectively open to the air, supporting all harmonics over a fundamental set by its length. A clarinet behaves as a stopped tube, its reed end closed, and the missing even harmonics give both its lower fundamental for its size and its hollow timbre. Organ pipes make the comparison visible in architecture: stopped pipes reach a given pitch at half the length an open pipe needs, a fact builders have exploited in cramped lofts for centuries. Exam items that place two pipes side by side, or a pipe beside a string, are testing whether the student assigns boundary conditions before reaching for numbers, which remains the standing wave skill in every costume.

Octaves give the quickest sanity check in the family. Doubling a frequency raises pitch by one octave, so any change that doubles a fundamental, halving a string's length or halving an open tube's length, sounds exactly one octave up. Halving a stopped tube's length doubles its fundamental too, since f = v/(4L), while its ladder still skips the even rungs. Reasoning through octaves first and harmonics second orders the work the way musicians hear it and the way items are usually keyed.

## Sound across media and the temperature footnote

Sound outruns its airborne reputation in denser media. In water it travels at roughly 1,500 meters per second, and in steel faster still, several thousand meters per second, because the medium's stiffness hands the disturbance along far more aggressively than its density holds it back. The exam rarely demands those figures from memory; it deploys them in given form to test the crossing rules. A 500 hertz tone generated underwater keeps its 500 hertz when the situation is analyzed in air on the other side of a boundary in a thought experiment, while its wavelength rescales from about 3 meters in water to about 0.68 meters in air, following the speeds. Frequency's loyalty to the source is the invariant that makes such problems one-step affairs.

Temperature nudges the airborne speed in a direction students can reason out without a formula. Warmer air molecules move faster themselves, collide more vigorously, and pass the compression along more quickly, so sound speed rises as air warms. An outdoor echo timed on a cold morning and a warm afternoon differs slightly for this reason. Problems that mention the temperature beside the speed are usually just supplying a plausible speed value, but qualitative items occasionally ask for the direction of the effect alone, and the molecular story answers it.

## The ear as the final instrument

Perception translates the physics with two logarithmic quirks that items occasionally probe. Pitch tracks frequency closely, with doubling heard as the octave step rather than as twice as high in any linear sense. Loudness tracks intensity logarithmically, which is the entire reason the decibel scale exists. Timbre, the quality separating a flute from a clarinet at the same pitch and loudness, is harmonic content made audible: the relative strengths of the harmonics riding on the shared fundamental. A question that shows two waves with equal frequency and amplitude but different shapes is pointing at timbre, and the correct reading assigns the difference to harmonic mixture, not to pitch or loudness.

Hearing's frequency window, roughly 20 hertz to 20,000 hertz for young ears, frames a few boundary items. Below the window sits infrasound, above it ultrasound, and both are ordinary waves obeying the same v = fλ bookkeeping with wavelengths the numbers supply. A 25,000 hertz wave in air carries a wavelength of 340/25,000, about 0.014 meters, a centimeter-scale wave that no human ear reports. The physics does not change at the window's edge; only the listener does.

## Graphing an oscillator's energy

Energy graphs complete the oscillator's portrait gallery. Plotted against time, kinetic and potential energy trade places twice per cycle on smooth humps, while total energy draws a flat line at ½kA² in the ideal system. Plotted against position, potential energy traces a parabola, ½kx², kinetic energy traces the same parabola inverted, and total energy runs flat across the top at the turning-point value. A mass at the extremes owns all potential and no kinetic; at equilibrium the ledger inverts; at intermediate positions the split follows the square law computed earlier, three quarters kinetic at half amplitude.

Laboratory versions of these graphs slope gently downward as damping taxes each cycle, and the flat total line sags into a slow decline. Questions built on energy graphs ask for turning points, equilibrium crossings, and amplitude readings from the total line's height, all extraction skills. A subtler item asks where speed is half its maximum, and the energy graph answers without time functions: half maximum speed means quarter maximum kinetic energy, so potential energy holds three quarters of the total, placing ½kx² at ¾ of ½kA², which gives x² = ¾A² and x at about 0.87A. Students surprised that the mass must travel so far out to slow by half have met the square law in its most instructive disguise.

## Harmonic ladders compared across three geometries

Three boundary geometries generate the three ladders this topic asks students to hold simultaneously, and a single comparative pass fixes them in memory better than three separate treatments. A string of length L fixed at both ends, and a tube of length L open at both ends, share the same ladder: wavelengths 2L, L, 2L/3, and frequencies f₁, 2f₁, 3f₁ with f₁ = v/(2L). A tube of length L stopped at one end climbs a sparser ladder: wavelengths 4L, 4L/3, 4L/5, and frequencies f₁, 3f₁, 5f₁ with f₁ = v/(4L). The stopped ladder's fundamental sits an octave below the open ladder's for the same length, and its rungs skip every second step.

Why the ladders take these shapes returns to boundary arithmetic. Node-node and antinode-antinode boundaries both fit half-wavelength units, so the count of half waves climbs one at a time and every integer harmonic survives. Node-antinode boundaries fit quarter-wavelength units, and fitting an even number of quarters would put the wrong condition at the far end, so only odd quarter counts fit and only odd harmonics survive. A student who derives the ladders from the boundary conditions, as urged throughout this article, can rebuild either one on a blank page; a student who memorized three lists owns nothing rebuildable.

The diagrams that accompany ladder questions encode the same information visually. Count the loops in a string sketch and the harmonic number stares back, since each loop is half a wavelength. Count quarter-wave units in a tube sketch the same way. When a problem shows a pattern and asks for a frequency ratio between two sketches of the same system, loop counting replaces formula work entirely: a three-loop pattern vibrates at three times the one-loop fundamental, full stop.

## Measuring wave speed with echoes and microphones

Two classical measurements of sound speed appear in laboratory items, and each hides a characteristic trap. The echo method times a clap's return from a distant wall and divides the round trip: distance to the wall equals v times the one-way time, with the measured interval halved. The two-microphone method places microphones a known separation apart along a sound's path and reads the time lag between the same pressure peak arriving at each; speed is separation divided by lag, no halving, because the journey is one-way. Confusing the two architectures, halving where no round trip exists or failing to halve where one does, is the planted error in both directions.

Resonance offers a third measurement route with a different flavor. Slide a tube's water level until a held tuning fork sings loudest above the column, and the air column's length at first resonance equals a quarter wavelength of the fork's tone: λ = 4L, so v = 4Lf with the fork's stamped frequency supplying f. Successive resonances as the level drops appear at three quarters, five quarters, marching down the stopped-tube ladder. An experiment item built on this apparatus tests the ladder, the extraction of wavelength from a length, and the product v = fλ in one integrated performance, which is why it recurs in laboratory-themed questions year after year.

## A worked approach: two speakers and a row of seats

Interference becomes concrete when a whole row of listeners is audited at once. Take two speakers 4.0 meters apart, driven in phase, emitting a tone of wavelength 1.0 meter into the space in front of them. Along the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the speakers, every point is equidistant from both, so the path difference is zero and the tone sounds loud all along that central line. Moving sideways, the path difference grows; the first quiet seat appears where the difference reaches half a wavelength, 0.50 meters, and loud and quiet zones alternate as the difference climbs through 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 meters, each whole wavelength restoring loudness and each half step restoring quiet.

Questions built on this stage ask variations of three things. They ask for the condition at a named point, which is path difference arithmetic. They ask what happens when the tone's frequency doubles, and the pattern compresses because every wavelength halves while the geometry stands still, so zones crowd toward the center line. They ask what changes if one speaker is wired with reversed polarity, and the entire map inverts, loud where quiet was, because the source phase flip adds half a wavelength to every path difference at once. Each variation interrogates the model rather than the arithmetic, which is the signature of this item family.

## Phase in depth: the starting line of the cycle

Phase rewards a second, slower visit because so many descriptive items turn on it. Two oscillators can share amplitude, period, and system, yet differ completely in their graphs, because their cycles began at different points when the clock started. A pendulum released from its left extreme and a pendulum pushed through its bottom at the same instant trace position curves offset by exactly a quarter period. Comparing them at any later moment demands tracking the offset, not the systems, since the systems are identical.

Graph items exploit phase through initial values. Read the position at time zero and the starting configuration announces itself: at +A means released from the right extreme; at zero and rising means pushed through equilibrium toward the right; at zero and falling means the mirror push. Velocity at time zero disambiguates the zero-crossing cases, which is why items supply a velocity curve or a stated direction of motion when the position curve alone cannot settle it. The habit to build is translation between verbal release descriptions and graph starting points in both directions, because items ask it in both directions.

Phase also disciplines the sine-versus-cosine choice in equation writing. A position function written as A cos(ωt) starts at maximum; written as A sin(ωt) it starts at zero rising. Neither is more correct; they are phase choices, and the free-response grading rewards whichever matches the described release, with the constants A and ω graded separately from the phase decision. Students who treat the choice as arbitrary lose the phase point silently; students who read the release condition earn it deliberately.

## The pendulum laboratory: what care looks like

Experimental items about pendulums grade the small decisions that separate a believable procedure from a lucky one. Length is measured to the center of the bob, not to the string's top or the bob's bottom, because the formula's L is the radius of the bob's arc. Release angles stay small so the small-angle formula being tested actually applies, and a careful procedure says so explicitly. Timing spans many complete cycles, started and stopped as the bob crosses the bottom marker, where its speed makes the crossing instant sharp. The count is verified by a second run, and the period is computed from total time divided by cycle count rather than from a single swing.

Analysis choices follow the same care. Plotting T² against L linearizes the relationship into a line through the origin, so the gravitational field follows from one slope, g = 4π²/slope, and any nonzero intercept confesses a systematic error, such as a consistent length offset from measuring to the wrong point on the bob. That interpretive step, reading an intercept as an error's fingerprint, is a favorite final part on pendulum experiment items, and it is answerable in one sentence once the model's prediction of a zero intercept is understood. Care in this laboratory is not fussiness; it is the scored content.

## A worked approach: ranking four pendulums without a calculator

Ranking items deserve a worked demonstration because their method differs from computation problems in kind. Four pendulums are described: the first has length 1.0 meters and a 0.20 kilogram bob; the second has length 0.25 meters and a 0.40 kilogram bob; the third has length 4.0 meters and a 0.10 kilogram bob; the fourth has length 1.0 meters and a 0.20 kilogram bob released from twice the angle of the first, still within small angles. Asked to rank the periods, a prepared student strikes the masses from consideration instantly, notes that the first and fourth share length and therefore share period exactly, amplitude notwithstanding, and reads the rest from root-length: the 4.0 meter pendulum doubles the 1.0 meter period, and the 0.25 meter pendulum halves it. The ranking falls out as third longest, then first and fourth tied, then second shortest, with no calculator touched and no formula evaluated.

The demonstration generalizes into a three-pass protocol. The first pass deletes variables the target quantity does not depend on, striking mass and amplitude from every pendulum question about period. The second pass reduces each survivor to its ratio against a chosen reference case, working in factors rather than values. The third pass applies the dependency's shape, square root here, to the factors and orders the results. Students who drill the protocol on springs, pendulums, strings, and tubes find that ranking items, which item writers design to consume time, become the fastest points in the section.

## From springs to sound: the course's hidden arc

Stepping back, the block's architecture reveals a deliberate arc worth naming at the end of study. The course begins the topic with one object obeying a restoring force, generalizes to a chain of coupled oscillators passing a disturbance along, and arrives at sound, where the oscillators are air parcels and the disturbance is pressure. Every stage reuses the previous stage's vocabulary: equilibrium, displacement, restoring effect, period, frequency, amplitude, energy. Even the standing wave is an oscillator wearing spatial clothes, its nodes and antinodes marking where the medium's local oscillation is forbidden or maximal.

That arc explains the topic's role in the course's final accounting. A student fluent here has demonstrated force analysis, energy conservation, circular motion, graph literacy, experimental design, and proportional reasoning inside a single block, which is precisely why the block anchors so many free-response questions and why preparation invested here pays interest across the whole score. The relationships are few, the costumes are many, and the exam is a costume parade. Learn the relationships and every costume becomes transparent.

## Driven oscillations: when an outside hand sets the tempo

Left alone, an oscillator swings at its natural frequency, the one its own parameters dictate. Driven steadily by an external periodic force, it eventually settles into swinging at the driver's frequency instead, whatever that frequency is, but the amplitude of the response depends dramatically on how the driver's frequency compares with the natural one. Drive far below or far above the natural frequency and the response stays modest. Drive at the natural frequency and the response swells to the largest amplitude the system's losses permit. That response curve, quiet at the wings and towering at the match, is resonance drawn as a graph, and qualitative items ask students to sketch or interpret it.

Timing explains the tower. At the natural frequency, each push from the driver arrives in step with the velocity the oscillator already has, so the driver does positive work every cycle and the amplitude grows until losses per cycle grow to match the input per cycle. Off the match, pushes arrive partly out of step, sometimes opposing the motion, and the average energy transfer starves. A parent pushing a swing demonstrates the whole curve bodily: pushes timed to the swing's own rhythm build a soaring arc from small efforts, while pushes at any other rhythm mostly jostle.

Laboratory signatures reinforce the concept. A set of pendulums of assorted lengths hung from a common driven bar responds selectively: the pendulum whose length matches the driver's period swings wide while its neighbors barely stir, because the bar drives all of them at one frequency and only the matched pendulum resonates. Items built on this classic demonstration ask which pendulum responds and why, and the answer requires only the length-period relationship matched against the driving period. Resonance, once again, is a matching test.

## The vibrating string experiment: measuring a speed two ways

A string driven by a vibrator at one end, passing over a pulley to a hanging mass, is the wave block's counterpart to the pendulum laboratory, and it scores the same virtues. The hanging mass sets the tension through its weight. The vibrator sets the frequency. The standing wave pattern that appears on the string displays its wavelength directly, since each loop measures half a wavelength. Wave speed then falls out twice: from v = fλ using the displayed pattern, and from v = √(F_T/μ) using the tension and the string's measured linear density. Agreement between the two values validates the model; disagreement sends students hunting for the mis-measured quantity.

Procedure items for this apparatus ask predictable things. To change the pattern from three loops to four at fixed frequency, the tension must drop so the speed falls and the wavelength shortens, or equivalently the hanging mass is reduced: four loops of λ/2 must fit the same length, so λ falls to 2L/4 from 2L/3, speed must fall by a factor of 3/4, and tension, sitting under the square root squared back into the relation v² = F_T/μ, must fall to (3/4)² = 9/16 of its former value. The factor work, wavelength ratio into speed ratio into tension ratio, is the experiment's characteristic computation, and it rewards students who chain ratios instead of solving for absolute values at every stage.

Variables worth controlling are equally predictable: the same string throughout, so μ stands still; the same vibrating length; a vibrator whose frequency is genuinely fixed rather than drifting with load. Identifying the linear density measurement, mass of a measured length of string divided by that length, completes the standard description. A student who can narrate this apparatus end to end, setup, measurement, chain of ratios, and cross-check, is prepared for nearly any experimental question the wave half can pose.

## Energy transport in waves: the amplitude-squared rule at work

Waves earn their place in physics by moving energy, and the amount moved obeys the square law that keeps recurring. For a wave on a string or a sound wave in air, the transported power scales with the square of the amplitude, other things equal. Double the amplitude of a speaker's cone motion and the acoustic power roughly quadruples, which is why modest increases in amplifier drive produce dramatic loudness gains long before the decibel arithmetic is consulted. Halve the amplitude and the power quarters, a asymmetry worth internalizing: amplitude changes are leveraged, never linear, in their energy consequences.

Spreading dilutes what amplitude built. A small source radiating uniformly pours constant power over spheres of growing area, so intensity falls as 1/r². A listener who backs from 2 meters to 6 meters from a speaker receives one ninth the intensity, since the distance tripled. Exam items pair the two laws in sequence: a source's amplitude change sets the power ratio, a listener's relocation sets the spreading ratio, and the received intensity multiplies the two verdicts. Keeping the laws in separate columns, source strength in one and geometry in the other, prevents the cross-contamination errors these paired items are built to provoke.

The same square law quietly governs the oscillator sections too, where total energy followed A² and maximum speed followed A. Spotting one exponent across springs, pendulums, strings, and sound is the kind of unification this article has pursued throughout: the topic is smaller than it looks, and its recurring numbers, the square root in periods and speeds, the square in energies, reciprocal in frequency and period, are few enough to hold in one hand.

## Pressure and displacement portraits of one sound wave

Sound's two portraits deserve a reconciliation pass, because items switch between them mid-question. Track a single compression traveling through air. At the compression's center, air parcels have converged maximally: pressure peaks there, while the displacement of the parcels at that exact center is zero, since parcels just left of center moved right and parcels just right of center moved left, meeting in the middle. A quarter wavelength away sits the rarefaction, pressure at its minimum, displacement again crossing zero in the opposite sense. Displacement reaches its own extremes halfway between compression and rarefaction, where parcels swing widest.

The practical consequence is a quarter-cycle offset between the pressure graph and the displacement graph of the same wave. Given one portrait, the other is the same sine shape shifted by a quarter wavelength, with pressure maxima standing where displacement crosses zero going down. Inside a stopped tube, the closed end is a displacement node and therefore a pressure antinode, the place of maximum pressure swing, which is why a microphone sealed at the closed end of a resonating tube registers the strongest signal while a tracer of air motion there registers nothing. Any item that mixes the words pressure and displacement in one stem is testing this reconciliation, and slowing down to name which portrait each clause describes is the reliable defense.

## A worked approach: the unknown fork and the slowing beats

Beats earn a full worked treatment because their two-step logic is so testable. A tuner sounds an unknown fork together with a trusted 256 hertz fork and hears four beats per second. The beat rate gives only a magnitude: the unknown fork sits at 252 hertz or at 260 hertz, and no amount of listening to the same pair resolves the fork's identity further. The resolution comes from perturbing the system in a known direction. The tuner loads the unknown fork's tine with a small lump of wax, which adds mass and must lower its frequency, and the beats slow to three per second. If the fork had been at 252 hertz, lowering it would carry it farther below 256 and the beats would have quickened toward five per second; instead the beats slowed, so the lowering fork was descending toward 256 from above, closing the gap. Its original frequency was 260 hertz, and the wax has drawn it to about 259 hertz.

Every element of that solution is a reusable move: absolute difference first, two candidates second, a perturbation with a known frequency direction third, and the beat trend as the verdict. Tightening a string raises its frequency; adding mass to a fork or lengthening a tube lowers a frequency; any such directional fact can serve as the tie-breaker. Items substitute strings, tubes, and sirens for forks, but the skeleton never changes, and students who rehearse the skeleton rather than the fork story answer the whole family.

## Pendulums across gravitational fields

Carrying one pendulum between worlds isolates the field's role in the period with unusual clarity. A pendulum with a two-second period in a field of 10 meters per second squared, length about one meter as computed earlier, is transported to a world where the field is 2.5 meters per second squared, one quarter as strong. The period scales with 1/√g, so quartering the field doubles the period to four seconds, while length, amplitude, and mass stand irrelevant as ever. On a world with field 40 meters per second squared, the same pendulum's period halves to one second. The bob's mass never enters the discussion, and an item that changes the bob between worlds is testing whether the student notices that nothing happened.

Altitude whispers the same effect at smaller volume. A pendulum clock taken up a mountain, where the field is slightly weaker, runs slightly slow, its period stretched by the root of the field ratio; taken down a mine, it gains. The effect is tiny for terrestrial altitude changes, and honest answers say so, but the direction is unambiguous and items ask for the direction. Pairing field reasoning with the length reasoning from the laboratory section gives students two independent pendulums levers and a clear rule for which lever a given scenario pulls: geometry questions pull length, location questions pull field, and distractor details pull neither.

A research-grade payoff hides in these comparisons: because the period measures g so cleanly, pendulum timing historically measured the field itself, mapping its variation across the Earth's surface and revealing the planet's slight equatorial bulge through the slower swing timed at the equator. The experimental design items built on pendulums ask for nothing more exotic than this classical measurement, dressed in school apparatus.

## The wave problem protocol: five questions in order

A compact protocol organizes every wave scenario the exam presents, and stating it plainly closes the body of this article before its verdict. The opening question asks what kind of wave or oscillator is on stage: spring, pendulum, traveling wave, standing wave on a string, standing wave in an open tube, standing wave in a stopped tube. The second question inventories the givens with units attached and names the target quantity. The third asks which relationships connect them, choosing rows from the relationship table rather than ransacking memory. The fourth asks what the boundary conditions or source conditions fix in advance, frequency from the source, speed from the medium, pattern from the boundaries. The fifth demands a second-route check, an energy confirmation, a limiting case, or a geometry recount, before the answer is allowed to stand.

Run against the scenarios in this article, the protocol reproduces every solution: the graph oscillator falls to questions one through three and the energy check; the stopped tube falls to question four's boundary discipline; the beats problem falls to question three's absolute difference and question five's perturbation check. Protocols feel slow in practice and fast in performance, because they replace the search for a starting point, which is where unpracticed students spend their minutes, with a sequence that always starts somewhere useful. Five questions, asked in order, are this topic's entire method.

## Two snapshots and a moving point: direction of travel, worked

Direction-of-travel items repay one slow, complete demonstration. A snapshot shows a wave train on a string with wavelength 0.80 meters, and a marked particle sits on the slope that rises toward a crest on its right and at that instant is moving downward. Which way does the wave travel? Slide the pattern mentally a small step to the right: the displacement at the particle's fixed position then comes from the pattern formerly on its left, which sits lower on that slope, so the particle's displacement falls, matching the stated downward motion. Slide the pattern left instead and the higher pattern near the crest arrives at the particle, lifting it, which contradicts the given motion. The wave travels rightward, and the particle, having just been passed by the crest, is descending toward the trough that follows.

The method is always the same three moves: freeze the pattern, slide it a small step in one candidate direction, and ask whether the marked point's displacement moves as the problem states. Reverse the stated motion and the direction answer reverses. Given the period as well, 0.20 seconds in a companion part, speed follows at once from v = λ/T = 0.80/0.20 = 4.0 meters per second, and the frequency is 5.0 hertz. Direction items pair naturally with speed computations this way, the qualitative verdict feeding nothing into the arithmetic but confidence, and the arithmetic returning a speed whose direction the first part already settled.

A subtler variant marks a particle at a crest or trough, where instantaneous velocity is zero, and asks about the motion an instant later or the direction from a second snapshot taken a quarter period on. At extremes the particle reverses, so the second snapshot's shift reveals travel direction by which side the pattern has leaned toward. Quarter-period spacing between snapshots is the writer's favorite interval precisely because it converts extremes into equilibrium crossings, making the shift impossible to misread for anyone who tracks one marked point through both frames.

## Why proportionality forces the sine shape

A fair question lingers under the whole topic: why sine? Many motions repeat without being sinusoidal, a bouncing ball's height and a blinking light among them, so what makes the proportional restoring force produce the smoothest curve in mathematics? The reference circle already contains the answer geometrically, since SHM was defined there as circular motion's shadow, and shadows of steady rotation are sinusoidal by construction. The force law explains why nature cooperates: acceleration proportional to negative displacement is exactly the acceleration the shadow of uniform rotation exhibits, so any system obeying the force law must trace the shadow's curve.

That necessity has a practical corollary students can use. Given any candidate description of an oscillator, check the acceleration's loyalty: if acceleration is always the negative of a constant times displacement, the motion is SHM with ω² equal to that constant, and every formula in this article applies with ω read off directly. If the acceleration follows any other rule, a square, a constant magnitude, a dependence on velocity, the motion may still oscillate, but the period formulas and the A, ωA, ω²A ladder do not apply, and answers built on them are counterfeit. The proportionality is not one fact among many in this topic; it is the admission ticket for all the others.

## Successive resonances: measuring a wavelength from two tube lengths

The stopped-tube resonance apparatus hides a measurement refinement that laboratory items reward. A tuning fork of known frequency is held over a tube whose air column length can be varied, and resonances appear at column lengths of λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4 as the column lengthens. The first resonance alone yields the wavelength only after multiplying the measured length by four, which multiplies any reading error by four as well. The spacing between successive resonances is more forgiving: consecutive lengths differ by exactly half a wavelength, since 3λ/4 minus λ/4 is λ/2, so the wavelength is twice the spacing between two loud positions, an extraction that cancels the small systematic offset caused by the antinode sitting slightly beyond the tube's open mouth.

That offset, called the end correction, is why the first resonance length is never exactly a quarter of the wavelength computed from v = fλ. Careful procedures acknowledge it; clever ones eliminate it by using the spacing method, in which the offset appears in both lengths and subtracts away. An experimental item that gives two resonance lengths and asks for the wavelength is testing the spacing method whether it names it or not. Given lengths of 0.21 meters and 0.63 meters for a 400 hertz fork, the spacing is 0.42 meters, the wavelength is 0.84 meters, and the implied sound speed is 400 × 0.84 = 336 meters per second, a plausible laboratory value that sanity-checks itself against the expected 340. A student who instead quadrupled the first length would report 0.84 meters as well in this tidy case, but with messier lengths the two methods diverge, and graders know which one the data was built for.

## Pulses meeting on a string: interference in its simplest theater

Continuous tones can make interference feel abstract, but two pulses on a long string stage the same physics in a form students can watch. Launch a crest-shaped pulse from the left and an identical crest from the right. As they cross, the string's displacement at the overlap is the sum, a crest of double height at the instant of perfect meeting, and then the pulses emerge on opposite sides with their original shapes restored, each having passed through the other without damage. Launch a crest against a trough of equal size and the string momentarily flattens to a straight line at the meeting, energy hidden briefly in the string's motion rather than its displacement, before both pulses reappear intact.

The pulse theater teaches three lessons continuous waves cannot teach as cleanly. First, superposition is local and temporary: the waves interact only where and while they overlap. Second, waves are not objects that collide; nothing rebounds, nothing is deflected, and the medium carries both patterns simultaneously without confusion. Third, the flat instant in the crest-trough meeting is not annihilation. The string at that moment is moving, its kinetic energy holding what its shape surrendered, which is why the pulses can reconstitute on the far side. Free-response items that show before-and-after pulse sketches and ask for the during sketch are testing exactly these three lessons, and the during sketch is always drawn by adding displacements point by point, never by guessing a collision outcome.

Extending the theater to a pulse train closes the loop back to standing waves. A steady stream of pulses reflecting between two fixed ends builds the standing pattern when the round-trip rhythm synchronizes with the source, and falls into disorder when it does not. Resonance on a string, seen this way, is synchronization between a driver and a round trip, the same matching logic that governed the swing, the tube, and the driven pendulum, encountered now in its fourth costume of the article.

## Reading a motion sensor: oscillation data as it actually arrives

School laboratories rarely hand students the clean curves of textbook diagrams. A motion sensor aimed at a cart on a spring, or at a hanging mass, returns a position-time trace with slightly uneven peaks, a gentle downward drift of the extremes as damping taxes the motion, and noise thick enough to hide sloppy reading. Experimental items built on such traces test extraction discipline under realistic conditions, and the moves differ subtly from the idealized graph protocol.

Period comes first, and it comes from counting. Rather than trusting one peak-to-peak interval, the careful reader spans as many complete cycles as the trace offers, measures from the first clean peak to the last, and divides by the number of intervals between them, which averages the reading error across the whole record. Peaks are preferred to zero crossings for this purpose when the trace drifts, because the equilibrium line itself may wander as the sensor or the spring settles, while peak spacing stays honest. Amplitude is read at the trace's start, from equilibrium to the first extreme, with the understanding that later extremes report a damped story. A question that asks for the amplitude at a later time is not being cruel; it is testing whether the reader noticed the envelope shrinking.

Velocity leaves its fingerprints on the position trace's slope. The steepest slopes, found at equilibrium crossings, give the maximum speed directly when the graph's scales are applied, and comparing that read-off value with ωA computed from the period and amplitude is the data version of the second-route check. Where the trace flattens at the extremes, velocity passes through zero exactly as the model demands, and a trace whose extremes are rounded rather than pointed is displaying the sensor's sampling, not new physics. Acceleration never appears on the sensor's own graph, but its shape can be predicted from the position trace by the sign-flip rule, and items occasionally ask for exactly that prediction, rewarding students who know the acceleration curve is the position curve inverted and rescaled by ω².

The drift itself is data, not dirt. A set of peak heights declining geometrically, each a fixed fraction of the one before, is the signature of light damping, and the period measured between the shrinking peaks remains the system's natural period to the accuracy the course requires. Reporting that constancy, period steady while amplitude decays, in an experimental conclusion demonstrates the precise model awareness the framework asks for: the idealization holds for timing even as it visibly fails for amplitude, and a mature analysis says both things in the same breath.

## A worked approach: one string, three changes

A final cascade shows the relationship table working at full speed. A string 0.80 meters long, fixed at both ends, carries waves at 160 meters per second, so its fundamental sounds at f₁ = 160/(2 × 0.80) = 100 hertz. The first change quadruples the tension. Speed follows the square root of tension, so the wave speed doubles to 320 meters per second, and the fundamental doubles with it to 200 hertz, exactly one octave up, while the wavelength of the fundamental, fixed by the string's length at 1.6 meters, never changes. Frequency rose because speed rose, and the geometry stood still: the source-medium division of labor in a single gesture.

The second change, applied instead to the original string, presses the vibrating length to half, 0.40 meters, at the original tension. Speed is a property of the string and its tension, so it stays 160 meters per second; the fundamental wavelength halves to 0.80 meters; the fundamental frequency doubles to 200 hertz by the other road. Two utterly different physical acts, tightening and shortening, arrive at the same pitch through different variables, and an exam that describes one while asking about the other is testing whether the student tracks which quantity each gesture owns.

The third change swaps the string for one of four times the linear density at the original tension and length. Speed falls by the square root of the density ratio, halving to 80 meters per second, and the fundamental drops to 50 hertz, an octave below the original. Combining changes multiplies their factors: the heavier string, tightened to four times the tension and pressed to half length, would sound at 50 × 2 × 2 = 200 hertz, each factor traceable to one gesture and one row of the table. Solutions narrated as factor chains like this are shorter, harder to break, and easier to check than solutions that recompute from scratch at every step, which is why this article has modeled them throughout.

## Where an oscillator spends its time

One consequence of the sinusoidal shape surprises nearly every student who meets it: an oscillator loiters near its extremes and hurries through its middle, so it spends most of its period in the outer reaches of its swing. Using the reference circle makes the fraction exact. Position exceeds half the amplitude, in one direction or the other, whenever the radius's angle lies within sixty degrees of either end of the diameter, which totals two thirds of the full turn. The oscillator therefore spends about two thirds of each period farther than A/2 from equilibrium, and only one third within the central band, even though it passes through that band at its greatest speed.

The loitering explains visual impressions that items occasionally probe. A pendulum photographed at random moments is most likely caught near a turnaround; a mass on a spring observed in snapshots clusters near its extremes in any honest sample. It also disciplines graph intuition: the position curve's flat-topped lingering near its peaks and its steep plunge through zero are the same fact drawn rather than counted. Students who expect the oscillator to spend its time evenly across its range misjudge averages, for instance by guessing the average speed over a cycle from the maximum speed with the wrong fraction. The correct average speed over a full cycle is 4A/T, the total distance of four amplitudes traveled in one period, which is 2/π, about 0.64, of the maximum speed ωA. That ratio, well under the naive guess of one half in the other direction, is the loitering expressed as a single number, and deriving it requires nothing beyond distance over time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How are waves tested on AP Physics 1?

Waves appear in both sections through a predictable family of tasks. Multiple-choice items emphasize graph reading from snapshots and single-point traces, proportionality judgments about speed, frequency, and wavelength, and boundary scenarios where a wave changes medium. Free-response parts build on standing waves, interference geometry, or an experimental measurement of wave speed on a string or in a resonating tube. Throughout, the arithmetic stays light: v = fλ, the string speed relation v = √(F_T/μ), and ratios carry nearly every computation. The heavier demand is conceptual translation, moving between a physical description, a graph, and the governing relationship without losing track of which quantity the source controls and which the medium controls.

### Q: What is simple harmonic motion on AP Physics 1?

Simple harmonic motion is the periodic motion produced by a restoring force proportional to displacement and directed toward equilibrium, F = -kx in the spring form. Its signatures are sinusoidal position, velocity, and acceleration curves, acceleration always proportional to displacement and opposite in sign, and a period set by the system's inertia and restoring strength rather than by amplitude. The course's two model systems are the mass on a spring, with T = 2π√(m/k), and the small-angle pendulum, with T = 2π√(L/g). Recognizing the proportionality in an unfamiliar system is the tested skill, because proportionality is what licenses every SHM formula that follows.

### Q: How is sound tested on AP Physics 1?

Sound is tested as the longitudinal wave application of the wave unit. Items ask for wavelength and frequency conversions at the supplied speed of sound, echo and timing reasoning with round trips halved, intensity and decibel comparisons using the ten-decibel step rule, and standing wave analysis of air columns in open and stopped tubes. Interference between two speakers, beat frequency between close tones, and the conceptual direction of the Doppler shift complete the usual inventory. Diagrams alternate between displacement and pressure portraits of the same wave, and part of the test is knowing which portrait a question is showing and where compressions sit in each.

### Q: How do you analyze a pendulum on AP Physics 1?

Analysis starts with the small-angle period, T = 2π√(L/g), and the dependency reading it encodes: length and gravitational field set the timing, while bob mass and small release amplitude do not. Energy supplies the second account, with gravitational potential converting to kinetic energy so the bob moves fastest at the bottom of the arc, where ½mv² = mgh for a release height h. Graph questions use the same quarter-cycle relationships as any oscillator. Experimental questions ask for length measured to the bob's center, many timed cycles, and a T²-versus-L graph whose slope yields g. Naming the small-angle condition protects the justification points attached to the formula.

### Q: What wave properties does AP Physics 1 test?

The tested properties form a short list: frequency set by the source, period as its reciprocal, wavelength as the spatial cycle length, amplitude as the maximum displacement of the medium, and speed set by the medium, tied together by v = fλ. Around those sit superposition, interference with its path difference conditions, standing wave patterns under boundary conditions, resonance at natural frequencies, beats between close frequencies, and the qualitative Doppler shift. Each property is tested both numerically and conceptually, and the conceptual forms, such as what happens to wavelength when a wave enters a slower medium at fixed frequency, are where preparation shows.

### Q: How is a mass-spring system tested on AP Physics 1?

Mass-spring items test the period T = 2π√(m/k) and its square-root dependencies, the peak values v_max = ωA and a_max = ω²A, and the energy account E = ½kA² with kinetic energy at a general position given by ½k(A² - x²). Graphs of position, velocity, and acceleration against time appear constantly, as do ranking items comparing springs, masses, and amplitudes. Vertical arrangements test whether students identify the hanging equilibrium as the oscillation center while leaving the period unchanged. Laboratory items measure k statically from force-versus-stretch slope or dynamically from the slope of T² against m, and strong answers narrate which method is in play.

### Q: How do you handle wave interference on AP Physics 1?

Start from superposition: displacements add point by point where waves overlap. For two coherent sources, compute the path difference from the point of interest to each source and compare it with the wavelength. A whole number of wavelengths means constructive interference and a loud or high point; a half-integer number means destructive interference and a quiet or flat point. Then check the sources' phase: driving the sources in opposite phase swaps the two conditions. Worked in that order, condition, path difference, phase audit, interference items reduce to arithmetic plus one judgment, and the judgment is the part students most often skip.

### Q: What is resonance on the AP Physics 1 exam?

Resonance is the large response a system gives when driven at one of its natural frequencies. The exam treats it through standing waves and driven oscillators: a tube resonates at the frequencies whose wavelengths fit its boundary conditions, a string at its harmonics, and a pendulum or spring-mass system when pushed in rhythm with its own period. Questions ask which driving frequency produces the strong response, or why a sustained pure tone can endanger a glass while louder mixed noise cannot. The governing insight is matching: energy accumulates only when each push arrives in step with the motion already present, so timing, not raw power, is the variable that matters.

### Q: How are standing waves tested on AP Physics 1?

Standing wave items present a string fixed at both ends or an air column in a tube and ask for allowed wavelengths, harmonic frequencies, or the pattern belonging to a named harmonic. Strings and open tubes fit half-wavelength units, L = nλ/2, with all harmonics present at f_n = nv/(2L). Stopped tubes fit odd quarter-wavelength units, sounding only odd harmonics from a fundamental of v/(4L). Diagrams are scored territory: node spacing equals half a wavelength, loops count the harmonic number, and displacement nodes coincide with pressure antinodes in tubes. Boundary conditions come first in every sound solution, because they select the ladder before any number is computed.

### Q: How do you relate frequency and period on AP Physics 1?

Frequency and period are reciprocals, f = 1/T and T = 1/f, with period in seconds per cycle and frequency in cycles per second, or hertz. A period of 0.50 seconds is a frequency of 2.0 hertz; a frequency of 50 hertz is a period of 0.020 seconds. Angular frequency joins them through ω = 2πf = 2π/T and is the form SHM formulas consume. The exam's recurring trap is extraction: peak spacing on a time graph is a period, cycle counts over an interval give frequency, and swapping the pair poisons every downstream computation. Writing units beside each extracted value is the standard defense.

### Q: How is wave speed calculated on AP Physics 1?

The universal relation is v = fλ: measure or derive any two of speed, frequency, and wavelength and the third follows. For a wave on a string, the medium supplies the speed directly through v = √(F_T/μ), tension over linear mass density, so tightening or lightening the string raises the speed while changing the plucking frequency does not. For sound, problems supply a speed near 340 meters per second in air. When a wave crosses into a new medium, frequency survives unchanged, the new medium sets the new speed, and wavelength rescales to match, which is the full method behind boundary calculations.

### Q: What links simple harmonic motion and circular motion on AP Physics 1?

Uniform circular motion casts an SHM shadow. A point moving steadily around a circle, projected onto one diameter, slides back and forth in simple harmonic motion with amplitude equal to the radius and the rotation's period. The shadow's velocity is the diameter-component of the point's tangential velocity, peaking at the center crossing, and its acceleration is the diameter-component of the centripetal acceleration, always aimed at the center and peaking at the extremes. This reference circle explains the quarter-cycle shifts among the position, velocity, and acceleration curves, gives phase a geometric meaning as the radius's starting angle, and lets students derive SHM facts instead of memorizing three separate graphs.

### Q: How do you read a wave graph on AP Physics 1?

Read the axes before the curve. On a displacement-versus-position snapshot, horizontal spacing between crests is the wavelength and vertical reach is the amplitude. On a displacement-versus-time trace of one point, horizontal spacing between crests is the period and the same vertical reach is the amplitude. Combining one of each yields the wave speed through v = λ/T or v = fλ. Direction of travel comes from sliding the snapshot slightly and checking whether a marked point's displacement moves as stated. The common failure is measuring the right spacing on the wrong axis, so naming each axis aloud is the professional habit the exam rewards.

### Q: How is the Doppler effect treated on AP Physics 1?

The Doppler effect is treated conceptually rather than computationally. Students should know that relative approach raises the observed frequency and relative recession lowers it, and should be able to reason from wavefront spacing: a moving source bunches the wavefronts ahead of itself and spreads them behind, so an observer in front samples a shorter effective wavelength while an observer behind samples a longer one. Observer motion changes the encounter rate with the fronts instead. Items use diagrams and everyday scenarios, ambulances and passing trains, and ask for the direction of the shift and comparisons between observers, not for multi-step formula work.

### Q: How do you connect energy to oscillations on AP Physics 1?

An ideal oscillator conserves total mechanical energy while trading it between kinetic and potential forms twice per cycle. For a spring, total energy is E = ½kA², all potential at the extremes, all kinetic at equilibrium, and split by the square law in between, with kinetic energy at displacement x equal to ½k(A² - x²). For a pendulum, gravitational potential and kinetic energy trade through the drop height, giving the bottom speed from ½mv² = mgh. Amplitude is the energy dial: doubling it quadruples the total energy and doubles the maximum speed, while leaving the period untouched. Energy reasoning also supplies the fastest second-route check on any computed maximum speed.

### Q: What determines the period of a pendulum and of a mass-spring system on AP Physics 1?

The pendulum's period, T = 2π√(L/g), is determined by string length and gravitational field strength alone; bob mass cancels because gravity's pull and the bob's inertia scale together, and amplitude matters only outside the small-angle regime. The mass-spring period, T = 2π√(m/k), is determined by the oscillating mass and the spring constant; amplitude does not enter at all in the ideal model. Both dependencies are square roots, so quadrupling the numerator variable doubles the period while quadrupling the denominator variable halves it. Nearly every period question on the exam reduces to identifying which of these four variables changed and applying the root.

### Q: How do you find the wavelength of a standing wave in a tube or on a string on AP Physics 1?

Let the boundary conditions count the wave for you. On a string fixed at both ends, the length holds a whole number of half wavelengths, so the wavelength of the nth harmonic is λ = 2L/n; adjacent nodes in a diagram are half a wavelength apart, which can be measured directly. An open tube follows the same rule. A stopped tube fits odd quarter wavelengths, so the fundamental wavelength is 4L and the nth odd harmonic has λ = 4L/(2n - 1) counting n = 1, 3, 5. With wavelength in hand, frequency follows from f = v/λ at the appropriate wave speed.

### Q: What are beats and how are they tested on AP Physics 1?

Beats are the periodic swelling and fading heard when two tones of slightly different frequency overlap. Superposition drifts the pair in and out of step, and the loudness pulses at the beat frequency, f_beat = |f₁ - f₂|, the absolute difference of the two frequencies. Forks at 256 and 260 hertz beat four times per second. Testing stays close to that definition, with one sophistication: a beat measurement alone leaves the unknown frequency ambiguous between f + f_beat and f - f_beat, so items add a perturbation with a known direction, tightening a string or loading a fork, and let the quickening or slowing of the beats settle the sign.

### Q: How do you read displacement, velocity, and acceleration graphs for an oscillating object on AP Physics 1?

The three graphs share amplitude information through the ladder A, ωA, ω²A and are shifted by quarter periods relative to one another. Velocity peaks where displacement crosses zero, because the object moves fastest through equilibrium, and velocity is zero at the extremes. Acceleration is the displacement graph flipped in sign, proportional to displacement and opposite to it at every instant, peaking at the extremes and vanishing at equilibrium. To identify an unlabeled curve, test the extremes: the curve at maximum where displacement is maximum and oppositely directed is acceleration; the curve at zero there is velocity. Phase offsets between graphs reveal the release condition, such as release from rest at an extreme.

### Q: What is the difference between transverse and longitudinal waves on AP Physics 1?

The difference is the direction of the medium's oscillation relative to the wave's travel. In a transverse wave, particles oscillate perpendicular to the travel direction, producing visible crests and troughs, as in a wave on a string. In a longitudinal wave, particles oscillate parallel to travel, producing compressions and rarefactions instead; sound in air is the standard example. Both families share the same bookkeeping, frequency from the source, speed from the medium, v = fλ, and energy growing with amplitude squared. The tested subtlety is graphical: a longitudinal wave plotted as displacement against position looks like a transverse wave but must be read through the compression pattern it encodes.
