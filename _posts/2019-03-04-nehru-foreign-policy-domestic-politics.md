Nehru's foreign policy puzzled foreign observers for a simple reason: they measured it against the resources of the state that produced it. The India Jawaharlal Nehru led from 15 August 1947 was a country where most adults could not read, where foodgrain imports were a recurring necessity, where Partition had displaced millions across Punjab and Bengal, and where the treasury inherited from the Raj was being spent on refugee relief, administrative integration of more than five hundred princely states, and the first of the Five-Year Plans. A government carrying that load might be expected to treat foreign affairs as a caretaker function, handled quietly while attention stayed at home. Nehru did the opposite. He kept the external affairs portfolio in his own hands for the entire seventeen years of his premiership, spoke on foreign questions in Parliament more often and at greater length than on any subject except planning, recognised the People's Republic of China within months of its proclamation, signed the Panchsheel agreement with Beijing on 29 April 1954, co-convened the Bandung Conference of April 1955, took a leading and risky position during the Suez crisis of 1956, and stood among the founders of the Non-Aligned Movement at Belgrade in September 1961. This article defends a single claim about that record. Nehru's foreign policy was the domestic-political device that no other policy domain could deliver; its substance was real, but its function was structurally primary.

## The Puzzle of a Poor Country's Expensive Diplomacy

The puzzle deepens once the costs are counted honestly. Foreign-policy positioning consumed the scarcest resource the Indian government possessed, which was the Prime Minister's own time and authority. Nehru's speeches collected in Independence and After, published in 1949 from his addresses of 1946 to 1949, already show a head of government spending remarkable energy on questions that touched no Indian voter's daily life: the future of Indonesia under Dutch attack, the Palestine question before the United Nations, the condition of Indians in South Africa, the architecture of Asian cooperation. The Asian Relations Conference, which met in New Delhi in March 1947 before independence had even arrived, drew delegates from across the continent to a capital that was simultaneously bracing for Partition. A purely instrumental reading of statecraft struggles with that allocation of effort. India in 1947 had no army capable of projecting power beyond its borders, no surplus capital to lend, and no trade leverage worth the name. What it had was a voice, and Nehru used the voice constantly.

The standard explanation, developed in the international-relations literature, treats the voice as the instrument of a weak state's security strategy. On that reading, non-alignment was a third-bloc posture: India refused membership of the Western and Soviet camps so that neither camp could command its votes, its territory, or its alignment in a future war, and so that both camps would court it with aid. Sumit Ganguly's Indian Foreign Policy: Retrospect and Prospect (2010) presents a version of this reading with great care, tracing Nehruvian foreign policy as a coherent response to the structural position of a new state in a bipolar order. The reading is not wrong about the international effects. India did extract assistance from both blocs, did preserve freedom of manoeuvre, and did convert moral standing into diplomatic influence disproportionate to its material power. Yet the strategic reading leaves the domestic allocation unexplained. A security posture does not require evening rallies. It does not require foreign policy to be argued, repeatedly and at length, before the Lok Sabha and before election crowds. It does not explain why the Congress party, rather than the foreign office alone, carried the doctrine as a badge of identity.

The missing variable is the party-political landscape inside India. Nehru governed through a dominant party that faced organised rivals on two flanks. On the right stood the Hindu Mahasabha and, from its founding on 21 October 1951, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh under Syama Prasad Mookerjee, both of which attacked non-alignment as weakness and leaned toward the Western camp as the natural partner against communism and Pakistan. On the left stood the Communist Party of India, which had adopted the insurrectionary Ranadive line at its Calcutta congress of 1948, denounced the Congress government as a collaborator of imperialism, and reset its programme only in 1951. Between those flanks, the Congress needed an ideological position that was unmistakably its own. Non-alignment supplied it. A doctrine that refused both Washington and Moscow allowed the Congress to present itself as the sole author of an independent Indian path, and to cast its rivals as parties that would mortgage that independence to a foreign capital. That is the sense in which foreign policy did domestic work that no other domain could do. Land reform divided the Congress internally. Industrial policy divided it. Language policy divided it. Foreign policy, alone among the great questions of the 1950s, united the party while dividing its opponents.

## Nehru's Foreign Policy Explained Through the Domestic Ledger

Read through the domestic ledger, the famous sequence of Nehruvian foreign-policy acts changes its meaning without changing its facts. The recognition of China, announced on 30 December 1949, was in diplomatic terms a judgment that the government in Beijing was a fact to be dealt with. In domestic terms it was a demonstration that the Congress government would take decisions about Asia without waiting for Western permission, at a moment when the Jana Sangh's predecessors were demanding alignment against the communist world. The Panchsheel agreement of 29 April 1954 was, in diplomatic terms, a trade and intercourse agreement concerning Tibet with a famous preamble. In domestic terms its five principles gave the Congress a vocabulary of principled coexistence that could be, and was, turned inward: a state that preached peaceful coexistence abroad could present its planned economy and its secular settlement as parts of one civilisational project at home. Bandung in April 1955 was, in diplomatic terms, a conference of twenty-nine Asian and African governments. In domestic terms it was the spectacle of an Indian Prime Minister treated as a peer by Zhou Enlai and a convenor by Sukarno and Nasser, reported back to Indian readers as proof that the Congress had made India count in the world. Each act paid a domestic dividend, and the dividends were collected by one party.

### Why did Nehru spend so much of his authority on foreign policy?

Nehru spent his authority on foreign policy because foreign policy returned more authority than it consumed, and returned it to the Congress as a party rather than to the state in the abstract. Each performance abroad certified the claim that only Congress leadership could represent India before the world, a claim no rival could match.

From the Asian Relations Conference of March 1947 to the Belgrade summit of September 1961, the certification repeated itself before ever larger audiences. Neither the Jana Sangh nor the Communists commanded a leader of comparable international standing, and neither could stage the spectacle of India consulted by two continents. Foreign policy was therefore an investment in the ruling party's scarcest asset, its claim to be the natural government of the country, and Nehru, who understood the arithmetic from his own rally crowds, invested deliberately and repeatedly.

The four dimensions of the domestic function organise the evidence. The first is ideological positioning: non-alignment marked out a third space in Indian politics that the right and the left could not occupy without ceasing to be themselves. The second is cover for state-led development: the anti-colonial, non-aligned identity dignified the planned economy adopted at Avadi in January 1955 and embodied in the Second Five-Year Plan of 1956, presenting state direction of investment as national reconstruction rather than bureaucratic control. The third is electoral mobilisation: across the general elections of 1952, 1957 and 1962, Nehru campaigned as a world statesman, and foreign policy gave his rallies a register of national pride that district-level promises could not supply. The fourth is party management: a shared external doctrine held together a Congress whose factions disagreed about land ceilings, linguistic states, and the pace of socialism, because disagreement stopped at the water's edge of the Nehruvian world view. The sections that follow test each dimension against dated evidence, then turn to the cases, Bandung, Suez and Tibet, where the domestic function is easiest to see, and finally to 1962, where the device broke in Nehru's hands and the breakage itself confirms the analysis.

## Dimension One: A Third Space Neither Right nor Left Could Occupy

The founding generation had already rehearsed the argument about India's place in the world before independence, and the rehearsal was triangular. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel's instincts were administrative and security-minded, B. R. Ambedkar warned repeatedly that foreign policy built on sentiment rather than interest would cost India dearly, and Nehru's universalism supplied the third point of the figure. The texture of that founding argument, traced in [Nehru's modernist universalism](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/), matters here because non-alignment inherited its domestic meaning from the triangle: it was the position of the Congress mainstream, defined against a Hindu right that wanted India anchored to the West and a communist left that, between the Calcutta congress of 1948 and the programme reset of 1951, treated the Congress government itself as an imperialist client. Once Patel died on 15 December 1950, the internal counterweight to Nehru's doctrine weakened, and the doctrine hardened into the party's public identity.

Consider what each rival would have had to surrender in order to steal the doctrine. The Jana Sangh, founded in October 1951, built its foreign-policy criticism on the claim that non-alignment was a failure of nerve: Mookerjee and his colleagues argued in Parliament and in the party press that India's safety required friendship with the democratic West and a harder line against China and Pakistan. For the Jana Sangh to adopt non-alignment it would have had to abandon the civilisational anti-communism that distinguished it from the Congress. The Communist Party faced the mirror-image problem. After 1951 its programme accepted parliamentary work, but its identity remained tied to the socialist camp and to the claim that the Congress was a bourgeois government managing India for foreign capital. For the Communists to adopt non-alignment they would have had to concede that the Congress, of all parties, had authored a genuinely independent foreign policy, which was the one concession their critique of the regime could not survive. Non-alignment was therefore not merely a policy the Congress held. It was a position engineered, whether by design or by happy accumulation, so that occupying it cost the opposition its reason to exist. Nehru's private correspondence shows that he understood the geometry. His letters to chief ministers, sent fortnightly through the 1950s, repeatedly frame foreign-policy questions for Congress provincial leaders as questions of the party's standing, instructing them in the doctrine as party doctrine and not merely as state policy.

The secular settlement at home reinforced the same geometry, and the reinforcement ran in both directions. A state that refused to define itself by religion in its domestic arrangements, the settlement argued over in [the secular settlement debated in the Constituent Assembly](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/), could plausibly refuse to join blocs defined by ideology abroad, and a state that stood apart from the blocs abroad could present its domestic impartiality as strength rather than indecision. Nehru made the connection explicitly in his parliamentary statements of the early 1950s, where the refusal of military alliances and the refusal of a state religion appear as expressions of a single habit of mind: India would judge every question on its merits and accept no external tutelage, whether from a church, a Comintern, or a Pentagon. The habit was genuinely held. It was also, in the strictest sense, a brand, and brands in politics are assets that compound. By the middle of the decade the Congress could campaign on the claim that a vote for its candidates was a vote for the India that Bandung applauded, while a vote for its rivals was a vote for tutelage to Washington or to Moscow. No economic statistic the government published in those years, in a decade of modest growth and persistent food imports, could carry that emotional charge. Foreign policy carried it.

## China Recognition in 1949 and 1950: An Early Decision with a Domestic Address

The recognition of the People's Republic of China is the cleanest early case because the decision was taken fast, against visible domestic dissent, and for reasons that Nehru explained on the record. Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic on 1 October 1949. India announced its recognition on 30 December 1949, among the first non-communist states to do so, and the two governments established relations in the spring of 1950, with K. M. Panikkar going to Beijing as ambassador. Patel, in his letter to Nehru of 7 November 1950, had already set down the contrary view in the most famous internal dissent of the period, warning that Chinese professions of friendship concealed expansionist intent and that India's northern frontier policy rested on assumptions Beijing did not share. The letter is primary evidence that recognition and its companion policies were contested inside the government itself, by the Deputy Prime Minister, on security grounds. Nehru heard the warning and continued. The strategic explanation for continuing is that a state does not gain safety by refusing to acknowledge its largest neighbour. The domestic explanation adds what the strategic one omits. Recognition dramatised independence. At the very moment when the Hindu right was portraying the Congress as soft and the Western powers were organising containment, the Congress government showed Indian voters a foreign policy that answered to no foreign capital, dealing with communist China as an equal while refusing communist doctrine at home. The pairing was the message: friendship with Beijing abroad, suppression of the Ranadive line's insurrection at home, and no contradiction between the two, because the author of both was a sovereign Indian judgment.

The Korean War, which began on 25 June 1950, tested the pairing within months and the test was passed in a way that deepened the domestic asset. India voted in the United Nations Security Council to condemn North Korea's attack, refused to send combat troops, contributed the 60th Parachute Field Ambulance as a medical unit, and criticised the crossing of the 38th parallel and the bombing near the Yalu as escalations that risked a wider war. Each element of that sequence can be read strategically, as an effort to localise a conflict. Read domestically, the sequence was a demonstration staged for three audiences at once. To the West it said that Indian non-alignment was not neutralism between right and wrong, since India had named the aggressor. To the communist world it said that India would not be enlisted. To Indian voters it said that their government occupied the moral centre of a world quarrelling between two armed camps, and that the centre was an Indian address. When the war ended in armistice in July 1953, India chaired the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission, with Major General K. S. Thimayya in command of the custodian force, and Indian diplomacy acquired the reputation of the honest broker. Reputations of that kind are convertible currency in domestic politics, and the Congress converted this one for a decade, pointing to Korea whenever the opposition called non-alignment an evasion. The word the party used was judgment. The doctrine taught voters to hear every foreign crisis as a fresh occasion on which the Congress exercised judgment and its rivals merely echoed a camp.

## Panchsheel, 29 April 1954: A Treaty Text That Spoke to Two Audiences

The Agreement on Trade and Intercourse between the Tibet Region of China and India, signed at Beijing on 29 April 1954, is remembered for its preamble, and the preamble is remembered because Nehru wanted it remembered. The five principles it announced, mutual respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty, mutual non-aggression, mutual non-interference in internal affairs, equality and mutual benefit, and peaceful coexistence, had been formulated during the negotiations that opened in Beijing in December 1953, when Zhou Enlai received the Indian delegation led by N. Raghavan. The agreement itself was a practical instrument: it regulated trade marts, pilgrim routes, and the withdrawal of Indian military escorts and postal services from Tibet, quietly surrendering the inherited British position there. As diplomacy, Panchsheel traded a legacy position for a Chinese assurance. As domestic politics, it did heavier work. The five principles were promptly detached from the treaty and circulated as a creed. They appeared in Congress resolutions, in school texts, in the Prime Minister's broadcasts, and in the joint statement Nehru and Zhou issued in New Delhi in June 1954, when the Chinese premier's visit drew crowds that the Congress organisation mobilised and the newspapers photographed. A doctrine with a name, a date, and five numbered tenets is a gift to a ruling party: it can be taught, repeated, and demanded of opponents. When the Jana Sangh attacked the government over Tibet, the Congress could answer from the creed. When the Communists attacked the government as a Western client, the creed answered again, since its first application had been to an agreement with Beijing.

The inward turn of Panchsheel deserves emphasis because it is the point at which foreign doctrine visibly becomes domestic ideology. Peaceful coexistence, in Nehru's parliamentary exposition of 1954 and 1955, was never only about states. He presented coexistence as the ethic of a plural society: communities, languages, and regions within India were also required to coexist without aggression or interference, under a state that kept the peace among them. The move connected the treaty to the Constitution's own foreign-policy directive, Article 51 in Part Four, which commits the state to promote international peace and security and honour its treaty obligations, a commitment whose place among the Directive Principles the founders had debated as part of the larger settlement traced in [the directive principles and fundamental rights settlement](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/). By that route a trade agreement about Tibet became an argument for the Congress's domestic order: the party that practised coexistence among nations claimed the natural right to administer coexistence among India's communities, and parties of the religious right or the revolutionary left could be painted as enemies of coexistence in both registers at once. Critics at the time noticed the sleight and named it. Acharya Kripalani and other opposition speakers in the Lok Sabha debates of 1954 charged that Panchsheel was a slogan raised to cover a surrender in Tibet. The charge had force on the merits, and its failure to damage the government is itself evidence for the domestic reading: the creed protected the policy that had produced it, because attacking Panchsheel meant attacking the vocabulary in which national pride had been taught to speak.

## Dimension Two: The Avadi-NAM Connection and Cover for the Planned Economy

The second dimension is the one with the clearest documentary trail, because the dates interlock. In January 1955 the Congress met at Avadi, near Madras, and adopted the resolution committing the party to a socialist pattern of society, the founding text examined in [the Avadi-NAM connection](/2019/02/05/avadi-resolution-socialist-pattern-1955/). Three months later Nehru stood at Bandung as a principal author of Afro-Asian solidarity. In 1956 the Second Five-Year Plan, built on P. C. Mahalanobis's model, assigned the commanding heights of heavy industry to the public sector, and the Industrial Policy Resolution of April 1956 gave the assignment legal form. The sequence is usually narrated as two stories, an economic story and a foreign story, that happened to share a decade. The domestic reading insists they are one story. A government asking its citizens to accept state direction of investment, licensing of private industry, and the deferral of consumption in favour of steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela, and Durgapur needed a legitimating language larger than economics. Anti-imperialism supplied it. Planning could be presented as the domestic front of the same struggle that non-alignment fought abroad: as foreign capital and foreign alliances were refused in the name of independence, so the commanding heights were reserved to the Indian state in the name of the same independence, and the citizen queuing for cloth or kerosene was enrolled as a participant in a world-historical emancipation. The steel towns received Soviet, German, and British assistance respectively, a fact Nehru used with relish, since aid accepted from both blocs without alliance was non-alignment made visible in blast furnaces.

### What did the Avadi resolution have to do with non-alignment?

Avadi and non-alignment were the inner and outer faces of a single claim: India would follow a path authored in India, owed to neither of the world's camps. The socialist pattern adopted in January 1955 needed moral cover against the charge that state control was mere bureaucracy, and non-aligned prestige supplied that cover continuously.

The prestige accumulated at Bandung in April 1955 and through the diplomacy of the decade was borrowed abroad and spent at home. When Congress speakers defended licensing, public-sector steel, and land ceilings, they defended them as the economics of independence, drawing on the authority non-alignment had earned to carry the planned economy's hardest demands.

The cover also worked as a shield against the two critiques that mattered. From the right, the Swatantra Party, founded in August 1959 by C. Rajagopalachari, attacked the licence-permit system as a negation of freedom; the Congress answer, that economic freedom in a post-colonial economy meant freedom for foreign and domestic monopolies to resume their old dominion, drew its plausibility from the anti-imperialist frame that foreign policy kept in daily repair. From the left, the Communist critique held that planning under a bourgeois government was a fraud; the Congress answer pointed abroad, where the same government defied both blocs, recognised China, and sheltered Asian nationalism, and asked why a client regime would do any of those things. Neither answer was conclusive as economics. Both were effective as politics, and their effectiveness depended on the foreign-policy record being continuously refreshed. This is why the rhythm of Nehruvian diplomacy, a summit, a declaration, a mediation, a state visit in almost every season, mattered to the economic programme: the programme consumed legitimacy at a high rate, and diplomacy was the mint that produced it. The broader settlement of the period, the equilibrium among the three founding tensions that held from the early 1950s to 1962, is the subject of [the Nehruvian-era equilibrium](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/), and foreign policy was the equilibrium's most renewable resource.

## Bandung, April 1955: The Statesman Abroad and the Dividend at Home

The Asian-African Conference that met at Bandung in Indonesia from 18 to 24 April 1955 brought together twenty-nine governments, and its Indian meaning was manufactured as carefully as its diplomacy. Nehru arrived as one of the conference's sponsors alongside Sukarno, Zhou Enlai, Gamal Abdel Nasser, and U Nu, and the Indian press corps that accompanied him reported the proceedings to a home audience in a register of national arrival. The conference's ten principles, adapted from Panchsheel, and its refusal to organise itself as a bloc aligned against either superpower, bore Nehru's drafting influence, exercised in part through Krishna Menon's tireless corridor work. The diplomatic substance was real: China was presented to the assembled governments as a state that could be lived with, the Afro-Asian group at the United Nations acquired coherence, and India confirmed its convening power. The domestic dividend was collected in a different currency. Photographs of Nehru instructing, mediating, and being sought out by the leaders of two continents ran in every Indian newspaper, and the Congress organisation, which understood spectacle, ensured that the returning Prime Minister was received as a conqueror in a war of recognition. In a country whose founding humiliation had been powerlessness before foreign rulers, the spectacle of the world consulting Delhi was a political fact of the first order, and it attached to the party that had produced the statesman.

Bandung also sharpened the doctrine's edge against the domestic opposition in a way that pure diplomacy would not have required. Nehru used his conference interventions to attack military alliances, naming the Baghdad Pact and SEATO as instruments that carried the Cold War into Asia, and he carried the same attack home, where the Jana Sangh's sympathy for Western alignment could be branded as sympathy for the pacts that Bandung had repudiated. Pakistan's membership of both pacts allowed the Congress to fuse the foreign and domestic arguments completely: alignment was what Pakistan had chosen, alignment had brought Pakistan foreign arms without security, and the party proposing alignment for India was proposing the Pakistani path. The argument was not fair in every particular, since the Jana Sangh's position had security reasoning of its own, but fairness is not the test of a party-political device. The test is occupancy. After Bandung, the ground of patriotic foreign policy was occupied so thoroughly by the non-aligned Congress that its rivals campaigned on other subjects whenever they could, conceding the field on which the ruling party was invincible. Historians of the conference, writing from the archives of several continents, stress its place in the making of the Third World as a political idea. Indian voters in 1955 experienced it more simply, as the week their Prime Minister chaired the world, and the Congress banked the experience as electoral capital that would be drawn upon in 1957, when it returned to power with 371 Lok Sabha seats.

## Suez, 1956: A Condemnation Argued in the Lok Sabha and Spent at Home

The Suez crisis supplied the hardest test of the doctrine before 1962, and the government's handling of it shows the domestic function operating under international risk. When Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal Company on 26 July 1956, India attempted mediation through the London conferences of August and September, with Krishna Menon shuttling between capitals carrying proposals for international oversight that respected Egyptian sovereignty. When Britain, France, and Israel invaded Egypt at the end of October 1956, India condemned the invasion in the strongest language Nehru used against any Western action in his tenure, and the Lok Sabha debates of December 1956, a mandatory source for this article, record the government defending its stance against opposition members who warned that India was squandering Western goodwill and endangering economic assistance. Nehru's defence in those debates is a document of the domestic reading. He argued the question as one of principle that India could not trade, insisting that a country which had won freedom from empire could not grade aggression by the flag of the aggressor, and he carried the House, including much of the opposition, because the anti-imperialist premise of the argument was one the whole political class had been educated in. The Jana Sangh's pro-Western leanings, serviceable in quiet years, became a liability in the Suez winter: to defend London and Paris in December 1956 was to defend an invasion of an Asian country by its former rulers, and the party's speakers kept their criticism procedural, attacking the government's diplomacy rather than its verdict.

### Why did the Suez stance pay the Congress at home while it cost India abroad?

The Suez stance paid at home because it let the Congress enact, before a domestic audience, the founding story of the nation itself: an Asian people resisting European empire, with India standing where its freedom struggle had taught it to stand. The costs abroad were real but diffuse, while the domestic return was concentrated and immediate.

Chilled goodwill in London and Washington could not be itemised for a voter; the ruling party's renewed title to the anti-colonial inheritance, asserted in the very season the socialist pattern needed defending in Parliament, could be felt in every constituency. Members who rose in the December 1956 debates to question the economic risk found themselves answering Nehru's question instead, whether India proposed to have a foreign policy of its own or a foreign policy rented from its creditors, and on that question the House and the country were his.

The companion crisis of that autumn, the Soviet suppression of the Hungarian uprising in November 1956, exposed the asymmetry that critics had always alleged, and the exposure is part of the evidence rather than an embarrassment to be managed. India declined to condemn the Soviet action in the terms it had used for Suez, abstaining and equivocating at the United Nations while Nehru expressed sorrow and spoke of the complexity of the Hungarian case. Opposition speakers, Kripalani prominent among them, charged the government with a double standard that emptied its Suez rhetoric of principle. The charge was accurate as far as it went, and the domestic reading explains why the government absorbed it so calmly. The anti-imperialist dividend depended on the identity of the victim and the aggressor as Indian audiences perceived them. Egypt invaded by Britain and France activated the founding story; Hungary crushed by the Soviet Union activated no comparable Indian memory, and the Communist Party, which might have been expected to pay for its defence of Moscow, was a regional force in 1956 rather than a national rival for power. The government therefore priced the two crises differently because its domestic market priced them differently. That is not a defence of the asymmetry as morality. It is a demonstration that the pricing mechanism was domestic, which is the claim this article exists to defend.

## Dimension Three: Foreign Policy on the Election Trail in 1952, 1957 and 1962

The electoral dimension is the easiest to underestimate because it left no single document, only a pattern repeated across three campaigns. In the first general election, held across the winter of 1951 to 1952 on the machinery described in [the first general election of 1951 to 1952](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/), Nehru campaigned as the election's central figure, travelling tens of thousands of miles and addressing crowds estimated in the tens of millions. His set speeches moved between the Constitution, the Five-Year Plan, and the world, and the movement was the message: the Congress asked to be judged as the government that had simultaneously founded a republic, launched its development, and seated it among the nations. No rival could stage that triptych. The Hindu Mahasabha and the young Jana Sangh offered a cultural programme and a critique of appeasement; the Communists offered a revolutionary programme their own 1951 reset had half withdrawn; the Socialists offered an economics. Only the Congress offered India itself, enlarged to world scale, and the result, 364 Lok Sabha seats from just under half the votes cast, installed the pattern for a decade. Foreign policy was not the largest subject in the campaign, but it was the campaign's frame of pride, the register in which a vote for the Congress became a vote for the nation's standing rather than for a ministry's performance.

The elections of 1957 and 1962 drew on the accumulated capital in different proportions. In 1957, campaigning after Bandung and Suez, Nehru could present the doctrine as vindicated performance: India courted by both blocs, aid flowing from Washington and Moscow alike, the Prime Minister received as an elder of the Afro-Asian world. The Congress improved its tally to 371 seats even as the Communists won Kerala, a defeat the party centre answered in part by pointing outward, treating the Kerala result as a provincial deviation within a national verdict delivered by a country proud of its government. In 1962 the dividend had thinned but not vanished. The border dispute with China was already public, the forward deployments in the Himalayas had begun, and the opposition attacked the government for complacency toward Beijing; yet the Congress still returned with 361 seats, and its campaign still traded on Nehru's world standing, by then as reassurance rather than triumph. The sequence teaches the precise value of the device. Foreign-policy stature could not win an election by itself, and in 1967, after the device had broken, the Congress would discover the limits of every other asset it owned. What stature did across three elections was to raise the threshold of failure: voters dissatisfied with prices, with licensing, with local Congress bosses forgave a government they were proud to be represented by abroad, and they forgave it in the currency of seats. Political scientists call this a valence advantage. The Congress of the 1950s simply called it Panditji, and built its campaigns around the man and the doctrine as a single exhibit.

## Dimension Four: The External Face That Held a Fractious Party Together

The Congress of the 1950s was less a party than a federation of provincial machines, ideological tendencies, and personal followings, held in one organisation by the memory of the freedom struggle and the fact of power. Its internal disagreements were continuous and sometimes bitter: over land ceilings, where the Nagpur resolution of January 1959 committed the party to cooperative farming and provoked a revolt of its propertied wing; over language, where the Hindi question divided its northern and southern units; over the pace and content of socialism, where a conservative tendency associated with figures such as Morarji Desai and S. K. Patil resisted the planning establishment. A party so composed needed at least one subject on which the whole of it could speak in a single voice without lying. Foreign policy was that subject. The Jana Sangh's right and the Communists' left defined the outer walls, and between them every Congress tendency, the socialist planner and the provincial conservative alike, could endorse non-alignment without abandoning any position it held on land or language. Nehru's doctrine functioned inside the party the way a national anthem functions inside a stadium: it did not settle any argument, but it periodically reminded the arguers that they belonged to one body, and it gave the leadership a text from which to preach unity whenever factional quarrels threatened to become public splits.

### How did foreign policy hold the Congress factions together?

Foreign policy held the Congress together because it was the one great subject on which the party's socialists, conservatives, regional bosses and Gandhians could all agree at no cost to their positions on the questions that divided them. The doctrine taxed no property, imposed no language and nationalised no factory, so unity behind it was always affordable.

A conservative chief minister could oppose land ceilings in his state assembly in the morning and praise non-alignment at a party rally in the evening. Nehru guarded that costlessness deliberately, presenting the doctrine in Parliament as national policy above party and inviting the whole House to own it, which made Congress dissent from it feel like dissent from the nation; the factions therefore fought their real battles over land and language while uniting, profitably and repeatedly, behind the external face.

The management function explains a feature of the record that the strategic reading finds puzzling, which is the doctrine's rigidity. A security posture should bend as threats change, yet Nehru defended non-alignment in nearly identical terms from 1947 to 1962, through the Korean War, the Chinese consolidation in Tibet, the founding of SEATO in 1954, and the border deterioration after 1959. The rigidity makes sense once the audience is correctly identified. A doctrine that exists to be agreed upon cannot be continuously renegotiated, because renegotiation would import the party's internal quarrels into the one room where they had been excluded. Menon's abrasive defence of the doctrine at the United Nations, and Nehru's refusal to discipline him despite repeated parliamentary storms, from the Krishna Menon controversy over Hungary to the defence debates of 1961, served the same end: the doctrine's custodians were protected because the doctrine itself was load-bearing. When the load finally shifted, after October 1962, the consequences ran exactly where this analysis predicts. The foreign-policy consensus shattered, and within months the party's internal management shattered with it, producing the resignations under the Kamaraj Plan of August 1963 examined in [the post-1962 organisational response](/2019/02/27/kamaraj-plan-1963-congress/), in which Nehru attempted to rebuild by personnel changes a unity that doctrine had previously supplied for free.

## Tibet, March 1959: Asylum, Conscience and the Constituency at Home

The decision to grant asylum to the Dalai Lama in March 1959 is the case where the domestic function overrode the foreign policy's own logic most visibly, and the brief for this article rightly lists it among the required cases. The Lhasa uprising of March 1959, the Dalai Lama's flight, and his arrival in India, where asylum was announced and he was received and eventually settled at Dharamsala, destroyed what remained of the Panchsheel relationship with Beijing. Nehru knew the price: his statements in the Lok Sabha in April and May 1959 show a Prime Minister measuring every sentence against Chinese susceptibilities, declining to let the Dalai Lama use Indian soil for political agitation, and refusing opposition demands for a more theatrical embrace. Yet asylum itself was never in question, and the reason was domestic in two registers at once. In the register of conscience, a government whose doctrine preached the protection of the weak could not return a religious refugee to his pursuers without forfeiting the moral identity on which the doctrine rested. In the register of constituency, the Hindu cultural sentiment that the Jana Sangh claimed to represent regarded Tibet's Buddhist hierarch as a figure within its own civilisational family, and a Congress that expelled him would have handed the Jana Sangh the single most emotive issue of the decade. Nehru therefore paid an international price he fully understood in order to protect a domestic identity he could not replace. The sequel confirms the reading. Each month of 1959, from the Longju clash of August to the Kongka Pass clash of October in which ten Indian police personnel were killed, raised the domestic cost of the China policy, and the opposition's cry that Panchsheel had been a delusion grew louder; yet the asylum decision itself was never the opposition's target, because on that decision the country, including its Hindu right, stood with the government. The device, even in decay, still sorted friends from enemies correctly.

## Belgrade, September 1961: The Movement Founded and the Dividend Reinvested

The summit that met at Belgrade from 1 to 6 September 1961, attended by twenty-five governments, converted non-alignment from an Indian posture into an institution with a name, the Non-Aligned Movement, and the conversion paid the last great domestic dividend of the Nehru period. Nehru attended as one of the five initiating leaders alongside Tito of Yugoslavia, Nasser of Egypt, Nkrumah of Ghana, and Sukarno of Indonesia, and his interventions pressed the assembled states to address the danger of war between the superpowers rather than to organise a third bloc on the model of the two they refused. The distinction mattered to him and he defended it against colleagues who wanted a harder anti-Western edge. For the domestic audience, however, the finer points dissolved into the single fact of the founding: a doctrine first announced in Nehru's broadcast of 7 September 1946, before independence, had become by 1961 the organising idea of a movement of nations, with its author seated among its founders. The Congress press made the genealogy explicit, tracing the line from 1946 through Bandung to Belgrade as the biography of an idea and of a party. C. Raja Mohan's Crossing the Rubicon (2003) reads the later transformation of Indian foreign policy against precisely this inheritance, treating the Nehruvian decades as the period that fixed the vocabulary Indian governments would afterwards have to argue with. The vocabulary was the asset. Even leaders who intended to depart from the doctrine would have to depart from it, in its terms, before its audience.

There is a temptation to treat Belgrade as the doctrine's triumph and therefore as evidence against the domestic reading, since triumphs of organisation belong to diplomacy. The temptation should be resisted on the evidence of Nehru's own priorities that autumn. He returned from Belgrade to a Parliament and a party absorbed by Goa, where Indian opinion across parties was demanding the end of Portuguese rule, and in December 1961 the government took Goa by force in Operation Vijay, to Western condemnation and to domestic acclamation so broad that the opposition competed to claim a share of it. The Goa action fits the domestic pattern exactly: a use of force that violated the letter of peaceful coexistence, undertaken when the domestic constituency for it had become irresistible, and defended by Nehru in the language of anti-colonial completion. The man who had preached Panchsheel to Zhou Enlai sent troops into Goa without a tremor of doctrinal embarrassment, because the doctrine's function was never to bind the government's hands. Its function was to express the nation's independence in a vocabulary the Congress owned, and Goa expressed it as completely as Bandung had.

## The Krishna Menon Correspondence: The Archive Where the Calculation Shows

Doctrines that do domestic work leave a particular kind of paper trail, and the under-cited primary source for this article supplies it. The correspondence between Nehru and V. K. Krishna Menon on United Nations positioning during 1956, preserved in the Krishna Menon Papers and the Nehru Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, records the two men calibrating India's stance through the Suez and Hungary crises with an eye that moves constantly between the General Assembly and the Lok Sabha. The letters and telegrams show Nehru instructing Menon on the phrasing of interventions, weighing how a sentence would read in New York against how it would be reported in Delhi, and treating parliamentary reaction in India as a term in the diplomatic equation rather than as background noise. Inder Malhotra's Dilemmas of the Indian Foreign Office (2010) describes the institutional side of this partnership, a foreign office in which the Prime Minister was his own foreign minister and Menon his instrument abroad; the correspondence adds the political side, documenting stance-taking as a jointly managed performance for two houses at once. Papers of this sensitivity survive because officials and families preserved them, and their modern counterparts survive in digital form for the same reason; the methods by which journalists protect confidential sources and their documents, described in the account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), belong to the same discipline of custody that keeps politically delicate archives intact for the historians who later test doctrines against them.

The correspondence matters to the adjudication because it is evidence of intention from inside the machine. Critics of the domestic reading sometimes concede the effects and deny the purpose, arguing that Nehru was a sincere internationalist whose domestic benefits were windfalls he neither sought nor noticed. The 1956 exchanges make that position hard to hold in its strong form. A Prime Minister who edits a delegate's sentences for their Delhi resonance, who calculates the order of a condemnation against the temper of his own Parliament, and who discusses Menon's parliamentary unpopularity as a cost to be managed rather than a surprise to be regretted, is a statesman for whom the domestic audience is constitutive of the foreign act. Sincerity is not in question: the same archive shows Nehru's genuine horror at the Suez invasion and his genuine belief that coexistence was a civilisational necessity. The domestic reading does not require Nehru to have been a cynic. It requires only that he was a politician, leading a party, in a democracy, and that the foreign policy he built was built to be carried by that party before the audience that kept it in power.

## The Doctrine Announced: The Broadcast of 7 September 1946

The domestic function was present at the doctrine's birth, before there was an Indian state to conduct a foreign policy at all. On 7 September 1946, five days after taking office as Vice-President of the interim government's Executive Council, Nehru broadcast to the nation the principles that would govern free India's external conduct: India would keep away from the power politics of rival blocs, would judge every international question on its merits, would work for the ending of colonial rule everywhere, and would seek friendship with all nations without enmity toward any. The occasion deserves a moment's attention. The broadcast was addressed not to foreign chancelleries, which had no reason to take instructions from an interim administration, but to Indians, and specifically to the politically awakened audience the Congress had spent three decades educating. It was a founding promise made in the domestic arena, in the same season in which the Constituent Assembly was being elected and the transfer of power negotiated, and it enrolled foreign policy in the founding settlement from the first hour. A doctrine announced to one's own people before one's state exists is, by the circumstances of its announcement, a domestic-political act, whatever else it later becomes.

The speeches of the following three years, collected in Independence and After (1949), show the promise being operationalised as party doctrine while the state was still being assembled. Nehru's address to the Constituent Assembly on the Objectives Resolution in December 1946, his statements on the Asian Relations Conference of March 1947, and his parliamentary expositions of 1948 and 1949 return obsessively to one construction: India's freedom would be incomplete, a mere change of management, if the freed state accepted tutelage in its external judgments. The construction did political work against specific opponents. Against the remnants of the princely order and the loyalist tradition it asserted that the new state's dignity was non-negotiable. Against the Hindu Mahasabha's argument that Pakistan's existence required India to seek Western protection, it asserted that protection was the old dependence under a new flag. Against the Communist Party, which in 1947 and 1948 was denouncing the transfer of power as a sham and the Congress leadership as imperialism's agents, it asserted that a government capable of defying both blocs in public could not plausibly be any bloc's agent. Every defiance was therefore double-entry bookkeeping: a position taken in the world, and a proof exhibited at home. When the full collection of the speeches appeared as India's Foreign Policy: Selected Speeches in 1961, with its coverage running from September 1946 to April 1961, Nehru presented the volume as the record of a consistent policy. It can equally be read as the collected sermons of a ruling party, the texts from which Congress workers learned what their government stood for in the world and, by reflection, what it stood for at home.

## Aid From Both Blocs: The Material Dividend and Its Domestic Spending

The strongest material fact in the strategic reading's favour is the aid record, and the domestic reading must absorb it rather than wave it away. Through the 1950s India received economic assistance from both camps on a scale no aligned state of its size could have matched: American development loans and, from 1954 onward, food assistance under Public Law 480; British participation in the Bhilai steel plant's consortium arrangements were matched by Soviet credits for Bhilai itself, German collaboration at Rourkela, and a flow of Soviet industrial equipment and rupee-payment trade that grew across the decade. The World Bank's Aid India Consortium, organised in 1958, institutionalised the Western side of the flow. Each rupee and dollar arrived without an alliance, and Nehru said so in Parliament, repeatedly, as proof that independence paid. That sentence, independence pays, is the hinge between the two readings. Strategically, the aid was the yield of autonomy. Domestically, the aid was spent twice: once in the plans, and once in the argument. The Congress could tell the voter that non-alignment was not an ornament but a revenue stream, that the steel plants rising in three states with three different foreign partners were the doctrine rendered in metal, and that the parties of alignment were proposing to halve the country's credit in the name of a security India had not needed to purchase. The Jana Sangh's warnings about China were answered with tonnage figures. The Communist claim that the plans served foreign capital was answered with the Soviet ambassador's presence at a Soviet-built plant. The doctrine's material success thus became a standing refutation of both flanks, renewed with every credit agreement signed in either capital.

The aid record also reveals the mechanism by which consequences began to discipline the function, the process the closing sections of this article describe. Food assistance created a dependence with its own politics: by the early 1960s the PL-480 pipeline was large enough that American displeasure, expressed during the Goa operation of December 1961 and again in the debates over India's Cuba and Vietnam positions, carried an implicit threat to the food supply of Indian cities. A doctrine maintained for domestic effect had produced an external dependency that had entered domestic calculations from the other side, as a reason for caution. Nehru's government absorbed the pressure without surrendering the doctrine, but the episode marks the point where the device began to generate its own constraints. The same is true of the Soviet relationship, whose warmth after 1955, symbolised by the Bulganin and Khrushchev visit of November and December 1955 and the crowds that received them in Delhi and Calcutta, gave Moscow a stake in Indian stability that paid India handsomely in the Security Council over Kashmir, where the Soviet veto of February 1957 blocked a resolution Delhi opposed. A veto is an international fact. It is also a domestic exhibit: the Congress could show its voters a great power spending its privilege in India's cause without India having spent its independence in return, and the exhibit was deployed in the election season of 1957 with precision. Function and consequence, in the aid and veto records alike, are not rival explanations. They are the two columns of one ledger, and the domestic column is where the entries were authorised.

## The Counter-Reading in Parliament: Kripalani, Mookerjee and the Communist Critique

A device is best understood by watching intelligent opponents fail to break it, and the parliamentary record of the 1950s preserves three sustained attempts. Acharya J. B. Kripalani, who had presided over the Congress in 1947 and left it to found the Kisan Mazdoor Praja Party, attacked from the Gandhian and institutional flank: non-alignment, he argued in debate after debate, was a policy of drift dressed as principle, which had left India friendless in the councils where its interests, above all Kashmir and the frontier, would be decided. Syama Prasad Mookerjee, until his death in June 1953, and the Jana Sangh speakers who followed him, attacked from the flank of civilisational security: the doctrine, they held, refused to name India's enemies, wasted goodwill in Washington, and sentimentalised a China that was digesting Tibet while Delhi recited coexistence. The Communist members attacked from the third side, holding in the early 1950s that the doctrine was a fiction concealing a steady tilt toward the Anglo-American camp, pointing to Commonwealth membership, retained British officers, and Western aid as the substance beneath the rhetoric. The three critiques were mutually contradictory. Kripalani's India was too isolated, Mookerjee's too exposed, the Communists' too Western. Each critique, however, made the same tactical error from the government's point of view, which was to contest the doctrine on foreign ground, where the Prime Minister's command of the material was unmatchable and where his answers could always rise from the particular charge to the general affirmation that the House was reluctant to vote against. Nehru's parliamentary performances on foreign affairs, long, professorial, and studded with the history of three continents, were themselves part of the device: they staged the leader's mastery as the nation's, and reduced his critics to parishioners correcting a text they had not written.

The opposition's difficulty was structural, and it is worth stating as a rule of the period. To defeat the doctrine politically, a rival needed an alternative that could be held by a plausible government of India, and neither flank possessed one. Alignment with the West, after Suez and after Pakistan's pacts had failed to deliver it security in either of its wars of the period, carried the taint of tutelage. Alignment with the socialist camp required excusing Hungary and would have required, after 1959, excusing China. The centre ground the doctrine occupied was therefore not merely popular; it was the only ground on which a government could stand, and the opposition's own spokesmen periodically conceded as much by endorsing non-alignment in principle while attacking its execution, a concession that left the Congress owning the principle and merely haggling over the practice. Party-political devices rarely achieve so complete a closure. The closure held for fifteen years, and its eventual breaking required not a better argument but an army: the Chinese divisions that crossed the Himalayan frontier in October 1962 did what Kripalani, Mookerjee, and the Communist benches together had been unable to do, which was to make the doctrine's central promise, that judgment could substitute for alignment in keeping India safe, appear false before the whole electorate at once.

## Kashmir at the United Nations, January 1948: The First Lesson in Both Directions

The first great foreign-policy act of the Indian state was a referral that its author came to regard as a lesson, and the lesson's two halves map exactly onto this article's two readings. On 1 January 1948 the Indian government, with Nehru's approval and against the instincts of officials who wanted a purely military solution, carried the Kashmir question to the Security Council under the Charter's provisions for pacific settlement, complaining of Pakistan's assistance to the raiders who had entered the state in October 1947 and asking the Council to restrain it. The international consequences are the familiar disappointment of the textbooks: the Council internationalised the dispute, linked India's complaint to a plebiscite machinery, and converted a question Delhi regarded as settled by the Maharaja's accession of 26 October 1947 into a standing agenda item. The domestic consequences ran the other way, and they were not trivial. By going to the United Nations as a complainant confident in law, the Congress government taught its first electorate that the Indian state would conduct itself as a law-abiding member of the society of nations even in a war it believed to be defensive, and it did so while the Constituent Assembly was drafting a constitution whose Article 51 would soon enshrine the promotion of international peace as a directive of state policy. The referral was the doctrine performed before the doctrine had a name, and the performance's audience, as with the broadcast of September 1946, sat in two galleries: the Council chamber in New York, and the Indian public learning what kind of state it possessed.

The sequel taught the harder half of the lesson, which Nehru absorbed into the doctrine's maturity without ever surrendering the doctrine. When the Council's handling of Kashmir convinced him that great-power politics, in that instance the Anglo-American tilt toward Pakistan, would govern outcomes regardless of the merits, he said so in private correspondence and in public hints, and the disillusion entered the foreign policy as a permanent suspicion of collective machinery dominated by the blocs. Domestically, the suspicion was as useful as the faith had been. A government disappointed by the United Nations could present its subsequent refusals, of alliances, of mediation on terms it disliked, of foreign bases and foreign judges, as the wisdom of experience rather than the vanity of doctrine, and could present its critics, who in the Jana Sangh demanded a harder military line and in the Communist press demanded solidarity with the socialist camp's Kashmir position, as innocents who had not learned what the government had learned at such cost. The Kashmir referral thus supplied the doctrine with its founding parable: India had trusted the world's institutions, the world's institutions had been captured by the blocs, and India would henceforth trust its own judgment. Parables of that completeness are rare in politics, and the Congress drew on this one for fifteen years, in debates on Goa, on Tibet, on the frontier, whenever a deputy's question implied that some external authority might judge India's conduct better than India.

## The Commonwealth Decision, April 1949: Remaining Without Belonging

The London Declaration of April 1949, by which India became a republic while remaining a member of the Commonwealth and accepting the Crown as the symbol of the association's free unity, is the most underrated exhibit in the domestic case, because it shows the doctrine's method applied to a question with no military or economic urgency at all. The Constituent Assembly was committed to a republic; the British connection was detested by the left as a survival of empire and suspected by the right as a dilution of sovereignty; and a clean severance was available at no material price, as Burma had demonstrated by leaving in 1948. Nehru chose instead to negotiate continued membership on republican terms, and he carried the decision through the Assembly's ratification debates of May 1949 against critics who called the formula a constitutional absurdity. The international reading of the choice is straightforward: the Commonwealth connection preserved trade preferences, the sterling balances relationship, a channel to London, and a bridge to the old dominions whose goodwill India courted. The domestic reading notices what the choice performed. A government that could remain inside the British association as an equal, having rewritten its terms, demonstrated that independence meant the freedom to keep what was useful without the taint of subordination, which was precisely the claim non-alignment made against the world. Commonwealth membership became the doctrine's proof of concept in miniature: association without alignment, cooperation without command, presence without belonging.

The performance had a further, quieter audience in the Congress itself. The party contained a strong republican and anti-imperialist sentiment that might have forced a rupture for honour's sake, and it contained Vallabhbhai Patel's pragmatic tendency, which valued the connection's administrative and commercial uses. Nehru's formula gave each tendency a sentence it could repeat. The left of the party could say that India had dictated terms to the Commonwealth for the first time in the association's history, converting an empire's club into a partnership of republics and kingdoms. The pragmatists could say that nothing useful had been thrown away. When a single decision can be narrated in two registers to two factions without either narration being false, a Prime Minister has found the governing style of the decade, and the style's name, in foreign policy, was non-alignment. The method reached its fullest expression in the middle 1950s, when India drew Soviet credits and American food, condemned Britain at Suez and kept the Commonwealth connection intact, sheltered the Dalai Lama and courted Zhou Enlai's memory of Panchsheel, all without experiencing the combinations as contradictions. They were contradictions only under the logic of the blocs. Under the logic of the domestic device they were the same act repeated: the sovereign judgment of the Indian state, exhibited to the Indian electorate, in forms each constituency could applaud.

## Krishna Menon at the United Nations: The Lightning Rod of the Doctrine

No account of the domestic function is complete without the figure through whom so much of it was discharged, and whose career measures its costs. V. K. Krishna Menon, High Commissioner in London from 1947, leader of the Indian delegation to the United Nations through the 1950s, and Defence Minister from April 1957, was the doctrine's second voice, sharper, more abrasive, and more expendable than its author's. At the General Assembly he defended India's positions on Korea, on disarmament, on the representation of China, and through the long crises of 1956 with a forensic aggression that made him, for Western delegations and newspapers, the personification of Indian self-righteousness. The domestic function of that personification was precise. Menon absorbed, into his own reputation, the resentment that Nehru's doctrine generated abroad and much of the criticism it generated at home, leaving the Prime Minister free to occupy the register of reason and conciliation above the fray. When Menon's marathon defence of India's Kashmir position held the Security Council in January and February 1957 for hours at a stretch, the performance was reported in India as a national epic and attacked in the opposition press as a vanity; both reports served the centre, the first by feeding the pride the doctrine traded in, the second by giving the doctrine's critics a man to hate instead of an idea to answer. Nehru's protection of Menon against parliamentary demands for his removal, sustained across a decade of controversies, is inexplicable as personnel management and entirely explicable as device management: the lightning rod is not dismissed for attracting lightning.

The end of the partnership confirms the analysis by inversion. After the war of 1962 the country required a sacrifice proportioned to the doctrine's collapse, and the sacrifice was Menon, removed from the Defence Ministry on 7 November 1962 under pressure from within the Congress parliamentary party itself, while Nehru, who resisted the removal and mourned it, survived in office for another eighteen months. The party that had united behind the doctrine united behind the disposal of its custodian, and the disposal was itself a piece of party management performed in the doctrine's ruins: by giving the electorate and the parliamentary party a named author for the military unpreparedness, the Congress centre hoped to separate the failure from the leader and the leader from the failure. The hope was only partly fulfilled, and the separation has been disputed by historians ever since, with Neville Maxwell's indictment of the forward policy and Srinath Raghavan's archival reconstruction assigning responsibility in different measures to the Prime Minister, his Defence Minister, and the intelligence and army leaderships. What is not disputed is the sequence of the sacrifice and what it reveals: the doctrine and its custodian were party assets with party prices, held while they appreciated and written down when they crashed, in the accounting of a political organisation rather than a foreign office.

## Geneva and Indo-China, 1954: The Mediator's Chair as Domestic Furniture

The Geneva Conference of 1954 on Korea and Indo-China gave India a role that the great powers had not assigned it and that illustrates, in a single episode, how diplomatic standing was manufactured and then spent at home. India was not a belligerent in Indo-China and not a member of the conference's inner circle, yet through Menon's presence in Geneva and the shuttle diplomacy of that spring, India emerged with the chairmanship of the International Commission for Supervision and Control in Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia, with Canada and Poland as its partners on the ground. The commission's thankless work through the later 1950s, policing a settlement that the signatories themselves undermined, brought India few tangible gains; its reports were ignored by the parties and its personnel exposed to the frustrations of a partition hardening into war. Judged as strategy, the episode purchased influence at the price of entanglement. Judged domestically, it purchased something Nehru's government valued more highly in 1954, a certificate. The power that had mediated in Korea was asked to supervise in Indo-China; the pattern itself became the argument. India was becoming, in the Congress's telling, the state the world summoned when armed camps required an honest intermediary, and each summons, reported faithfully by the Indian press, refreshed the electorate's sense that its government possessed a standing no predecessor regime and no rival party could claim. The certificate was framed and hung in the party's hall: it decorated the Avadi season, the Bandung spring, and the argument, made endlessly by Congress speakers, that a vote for the opposition was a vote to dismiss the world's mediator in favour of men the world had never heard of.

There is a further layer that the archives make visible and that honest treatment requires. The mediator's role was not always a performance. India's chairmanship in Indo-China reflected a genuine governmental belief, articulated in Nehru's statements of 1954, that the alternative to supervised coexistence in Asia was an expanding war drawing in China and the United States, on India's own periphery. The domestic reading has never needed to deny such beliefs, and it does not deny them here. It observes, instead, which beliefs were selected for theatrical investment. India held views on many questions in the 1950s, on Kashmir, on Portuguese Goa before 1961, on the racial order in Africa, and it invested its prestige selectively, in the questions whose mediation dramatised Indian centrality. Goa, where no mediation was possible and the domestic constituency was aroused, was handled by patience and then by force. Indo-China, where the constituency was indifferent and the drama was free, received the full apparatus of Indian good offices. Selection is the fingerprint of function. A state conducting strategy alone invests where its interests are engaged; a party conducting a device invests where the audience is watching, and the Nehru government's investments follow the audience with a fidelity that the interest map alone cannot explain. The International Commission's files, the Geneva correspondence, and the parliamentary debates of 1954 together constitute one of the fullest exhibits in the case, and they point, like the Suez debates and the Menon correspondence, to the same address in New Delhi where foreign acts were drafted with a domestic reader looking over the author's shoulder.

## Disarmament Advocacy: The Sermon That Cost Nothing and Paid Steadily

The last exhibit is the cheapest the doctrine ever produced and among the most durable. From the early 1950s Nehru made the abolition of nuclear weapons and the ending of atmospheric tests a signature Indian cause, proposing in April 1954, weeks before Panchsheel, a standstill on nuclear explosions while the powers negotiated, and returning to the theme at the United Nations and in Parliament until the Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963 gave the advocacy a partial monument. The cause engaged no Indian military interest directly, committed no resources, and risked no frontier. It was, in the vocabulary of strategy, nearly free. Its domestic yield was nonetheless steady and large, because it placed the Congress government at the head of the one procession in world politics that marched unambiguously toward peace, and it let every Congress speaker, down to the district level, claim membership of the party that spoke for mankind's survival against the bomb-makers of both blocs. The Jana Sangh, which wanted an Indian bomb as the answer to China and said so with increasing openness after 1962, could be painted before 1962 as the party of the arms race; the Communists, defending Soviet tests, could be painted as the party that excused its own camp's fallout. The centre, once again, was occupied by a position that cost its holder nothing and its opponents their patriotic credentials.

The disarmament record also supplies the cleanest available test of the sincerity objection, because here the two readings predict different private behaviour. If the advocacy were mere theatre for home consumption, the private record should show indifference to its substance; if it were pure conviction, the private record should show no attention to its presentation. The record shows both, in the proportions the domestic reading predicts for a device sincerely believed. Nehru's private letters reveal a genuine and recurrent dread of nuclear war, expressed to correspondents with no audience to impress, and the same archive shows the timing and phrasing of his public interventions adjusted to parliamentary calendars and party needs, the April 1954 standstill proposal being launched into a domestic season in which the government wished to crown the Panchsheel moment with a universal cause. Conviction supplied the content; function selected the moment and the megaphone. That combination, visible in the correspondence on Suez, visible in the handling of Tibet, visible in the election campaigns, is the article's whole thesis in miniature. Nehru's foreign policy was not a fraud practised on India's electorate. It was a body of genuinely held positions, assembled, sequenced, and amplified by a political intelligence that knew exactly what each position earned at home, and maintained as the ruling party's central asset until the Himalayan war of 1962 called in debts that no vocabulary could pay.

## The Fortnightly Letters: Doctrine Distributed as Party Instruction

The most direct evidence that foreign policy was administered as a domestic-political instrument is also the least dramatic, a file of letters. From the first years of independence until 1964, Nehru sent fortnightly letters to the chief ministers of the states, surveys of the national and international scene running to many thousands of words each, in which foreign questions routinely received the fullest treatment of any subject. The letters were official correspondence, but their function was pedagogical and, in the broad sense, organisational: they taught the Congress's provincial leadership, and through them the party's district machinery, how the centre wished the world to be described. A chief minister in Madras or Bombay who had read his fortnightly letter knew how to answer a Jana Sangh speaker on the Commonwealth, a Communist speaker on Korea, or a local editor on Tibet, in the authorised vocabulary. No comparable instrument existed for land reform, where the centre's letters pleaded and the states evaded, or for the language question, where the centre's own position shifted. Foreign policy was the subject on which the instruction was confident, continuous, and obeyed, because it was the subject on which the centre's authority was least contested and its material most abundant. The letters therefore constitute the device's distribution network, the channel by which a doctrine drafted in the Prime Minister's secretariat reached the last Congress office in the last district town, and their survival in published volumes allows the historian to watch the distribution working, season by season, from the China recognition to the Belgrade founding.

The content of the letters repays the domestic reading in a second way, through what they worry about. Nehru's recurring anxieties in the correspondence are not, in the main, the anxieties of a strategist: they are the anxieties of a party leader watching opinion. He returns repeatedly to the danger that India's stand will be misunderstood by its own public, to the misreporting of Menon's speeches in the Indian press, to the morale of the Congress organisation after foreign setbacks, and to the impressions carried back by foreign visitors whose praise or condescension would be quoted in Indian newspapers. The China letters of 1959 and 1960, in which he explains the frontier deterioration to the chief ministers with a candour the public statements lacked, show the network being used for damage management, preparing the party's provincial leadership for a failure whose political price the author already feared. A distribution network used for instruction in triumph and for inoculation in failure is a party asset by any definition, and its existence through the whole period, at the centre of the Prime Minister's working routine, settles the question of whether the domestic function was incidental to the foreign policy or built into its administration. It was built in. The foreign policy of the Nehru years came with its own ministry of home explanation, and the minister was the Prime Minister.

## Adjudication: The IR Reading Against the Domestic Reading

The disagreement this article must settle can be stated fairly. The international-relations reading, represented by Ganguly's Retrospect and Prospect (2010) and by a generation of textbook treatments, holds that non-alignment was a strategy addressed to the bipolar order: a weak state maximising autonomy, extracting resources from both camps, and converting normative standing into influence. The domestic-political reading defended here holds that the strategy's primary function was internal: it positioned the Congress ideologically, covered the planned economy, mobilised electorates, and managed the ruling party's factions, and that these functions explain features of the record the strategic reading cannot. The evidence that discriminates between the readings is of three kinds. First, allocation: a strategy addressed abroad does not explain why the doctrine was preached at election rallies and in fortnightly letters to chief ministers, nor why its custodian was a party leader rather than a professional foreign office. Second, rigidity: a strategy should have been revised as the Himalayan threat grew after 1959, yet the doctrine was defended unchanged until the war revised it by force, because revision would have reopened the internal quarrels the doctrine existed to suspend. Third, the archive: the 1956 Nehru-Menon correspondence shows the domestic audience inside the drafting of foreign acts themselves. On all three tests the domestic reading accounts for observations its rival leaves as residue, and the adjudication therefore goes to the domestic reading, with the standing qualification that the IR substance was genuine and its consequences real.

## 1962: When the Device Broke in Its Maker's Hands

The war of October and November 1962 is the control case that proves the framework, because the collapse ran in the order the domestic reading predicts. The Chinese attack destroyed the foreign policy's credibility first: Panchsheel's co-author invaded, the creed's five principles were quoted back at the government in Parliament as an indictment, and Nehru's broadcast of 20 November 1962, with its admission that India had been living in an artificial atmosphere of its own creation, conceded the doctrinal bankruptcy in the author's own voice. The party consequences followed within the year. Krishna Menon, the doctrine's custodian, was forced from the Defence Ministry in November 1962 and from the cabinet entirely soon after. The first no-confidence motion of Nehru's tenure was moved in August 1963. The Kamaraj Plan's mass resignations of the same month attempted a reconstruction of authority that doctrine no longer performed automatically. The full military and political narrative belongs to [the post-1962 foreign-policy reckoning](/2019/02/16/1962-china-war-decline-nehru/), and the point needed here is the sequence: an external failure became an internal crisis of the ruling party with a speed and completeness that would be inexplicable if the foreign policy had been merely a strategy conducted abroad. Assets that are load-bearing announce themselves when the load shifts. Between October 1962 and Nehru's death on 27 May 1964, the Congress began the long transition from a movement held together by a world view to a machine held together by power, and every later crisis of the party, in 1967, in 1969, and beyond, ran along the crack that opened when the device broke.

The complication the brief requires must finally be faced in its strongest form. Some consequences of the foreign policy were international through and through, and no domestic reading may confiscate them. The aid relationships with both blocs after 1956, American food assistance under PL-480 alongside Soviet industrial credits, were negotiated facts with their own weight, and they constrained later Indian choices as surely as any alliance. The diplomatic mobilisation of 1962, when Washington and London airlifted military supplies to a non-aligned India within days of Nehru's letters to President Kennedy of November 1962, showed the doctrine bending under a consequence it had itself made possible, since only a state uncommitted to either camp could be armed by one camp against the other's partner without a treaty crisis. The domestic reading survives these facts because it distinguishes function from consequence. The function of the doctrine, the work it was built and maintained to do, was domestic-political, and the intentions, allocations, and rigidities of the record prove it. The consequences escaped the function, accumulated in the international system, and in time constrained the function itself, until in 1962 a consequence arrived that the function could not survive. A device is not refuted by the world it operates in. It is measured by the work it did while it worked, and by that measure Nehru's foreign policy, the device that positioned a party, covered an economy, mobilised an electorate, and managed a federation of factions for fifteen years, did more domestic work than any other instrument the Congress ever held.

## The Device Measured: What Fifteen Years of Foreign Policy Purchased at Home

The framework can be given a final accounting, in the currency the device itself used. Between the broadcast of September 1946 and the Belgrade summit of September 1961, the Congress fought and won three general elections, carried a socialist-pattern resolution and two Five-Year Plans through a Parliament and a party thick with doubters, survived the linguistic agitation that redrew the internal map in 1956, absorbed the dismissal crisis of Kerala in 1959, and kept its factions inside one organisation through quarrels over land, language, and ideology that would multiply after 1964 into splits. Against that record, foreign policy's contribution was not ornamental. It was the one asset that appreciated through every one of those episodes, because every episode ended with the Prime Minister returning from a world stage on which his party's authorship of India had been re-certified. Governments usually consume legitimacy. The Nehru government manufactured it abroad at industrial scale, and the manufacturing is visible in the simplest comparison the period affords: the opposition parties contested the same elections with the same grievances about prices and administration, and lost three times to a ruling party whose foreign standing kept converting dissatisfaction into forgiveness.

The accounting has a methodological consequence for how the period should be written, and it is worth stating as the article's last word on the scholarly quarrel. Histories of Indian foreign policy organised by theatre, China policy, Pakistan policy, superpower policy, reproduce the foreign office's own filing system and miss the institution that consumed most of the product. Histories of the Congress organised by faction and election, in turn, treat foreign policy as a backdrop of prestige, a paragraph of atmosphere before the real business of organisation begins. The record assembled here, from Patel's dissenting letter of 7 November 1950 to the fortnightly letters to chief ministers, from the December 1956 debates to the correspondence that drafted their sequel in New York, shows the two archives describing one machine. The foreign policy was the Congress's ideology in its most finished form, the planned economy's protective colouring, the election campaign's frame of pride, and the party constitution's unwritten article of unity. When the machine broke on the Himalayan frontier in the autumn of 1962, it was not an ornament that fell. It was a load-bearing wall, and the structure it had held up, the Nehruvian settlement itself, began the subsidence that the rest of the 1960s would measure. That is why the domestic reading, defended against the strategic reading on the evidence of allocation, rigidity, and archive, deserves to stand as the primary account of Nehru's foreign policy: not because the world beyond India's borders was unreal, but because the purposes for which that world was engaged were set, priced, and finally paid for at home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was Nehru's foreign policy?

Nehru's foreign policy was the doctrine of non-alignment, announced in his broadcast of 7 September 1946 and practised from independence in 1947 until his death in 1964. India refused membership of both the Western and Soviet blocs, judged every international question on its merits, opposed colonial rule everywhere, and sought aid and friendship from all camps without accepting any camp's discipline. Its monuments are the recognition of China in 1949 and 1950, the Panchsheel agreement of 29 April 1954, the Bandung Conference of April 1955, the Suez stance of 1956, and the founding of the Non-Aligned Movement at Belgrade in 1961. This article's claim is that the doctrine also worked as a domestic-political device for the Congress, positioning the party in a third space its rivals could not occupy.

### Q: What is non-alignment?

Non-alignment is the refusal of a state to join either of the military and political blocs that divided the world during the Cold War, combined with an active claim to judge international questions independently. Nehru distinguished it sharply from neutrality: a neutral state abstains, while a non-aligned state participates, condemns aggression, sends mediators, and votes at the United Nations according to its own assessment. India condemned North Korea's attack in June 1950 yet refused combat troops, condemned the Anglo-French-Israeli invasion of Egypt in 1956, and accepted aid from both Washington and Moscow without signing an alliance with either. The doctrine was announced in Nehru's broadcast of 7 September 1946, institutionalised at Belgrade in September 1961, and served domestically to mark the Congress as the author of an independent Indian path.

### Q: What are the Panchsheel principles?

The Panchsheel principles are the five tenets of peaceful coexistence set out in the preamble to the Agreement on Trade and Intercourse between the Tibet Region of China and India, signed at Beijing on 29 April 1954. They are mutual respect for each other's territorial integrity and sovereignty, mutual non-aggression, mutual non-interference in each other's internal affairs, equality and mutual benefit, and peaceful coexistence. The principles had been formulated in the talks that opened in Beijing in December 1953, and Nehru and Zhou Enlai reaffirmed them in their joint statement at New Delhi in June 1954. Within India the five principles circulated beyond diplomacy as a creed of coexistence among communities and regions, which is why this article treats Panchsheel as a domestic-political instrument as well as a treaty text.

### Q: Why did Nehru recognise China in 1950?

Nehru recognised the People's Republic of China because he held that a government commanding the Chinese mainland was a fact no Asian policy could ignore, and that recognition cost nothing while purchasing a working relationship with India's largest neighbour. India announced recognition on 30 December 1949, among the first non-communist states to act, and relations were established in 1950 with K. M. Panikkar as ambassador. Sardar Patel's letter of 7 November 1950 recorded the internal dissent, warning that Chinese assurances concealed expansionist intent. Nehru heard the warning and continued, judging that refusal would neither weaken Beijing nor strengthen India. Domestically, recognition dramatised independence: the Congress government dealt with communist China as an equal while suppressing the communist insurrection at home, proving that its foreign judgments answered to no foreign capital.

### Q: What was the Bandung Conference?

The Bandung Conference was the Asian-African Conference held at Bandung in Indonesia from 18 to 24 April 1955, attended by twenty-nine governments representing most of the decolonising world. Its sponsors included Sukarno of Indonesia, Zhou Enlai of China, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, and U Nu of Burma alongside Nehru, and it adopted ten principles of coexistence adapted from Panchsheel while refusing to organise itself as a bloc against either superpower. India exercised drafting influence through Nehru's interventions and Krishna Menon's corridor diplomacy. The conference confirmed India's convening power and prepared the ground for the Non-Aligned Movement founded at Belgrade in 1961. For Indian domestic politics, Bandung was a spectacle of arrival: the Indian press carried images of Nehru treated as a peer by Asia's and Africa's leaders, and the Congress converted that recognition into electoral capital.

### Q: How did NAM benefit India domestically?

The Non-Aligned Movement, founded at Belgrade in September 1961 with Nehru among its five initiators, benefited India domestically by certifying the ruling party's central claim about itself. The Congress had argued since 1946 that only its leadership could represent India in the world as an independent author of policy; the movement's existence, with the Indian Prime Minister seated among its founders, appeared to prove the claim before the whole electorate. The benefit flowed through four channels examined in this article: ideological positioning against the Jana Sangh's pro-Western leanings and the Communists' socialist-camp loyalty, moral cover for the planned economy adopted at Avadi in 1955, electoral mobilisation across the general elections of 1952, 1957 and 1962, and a unifying doctrine for a Congress divided over land, language and the pace of socialism.

### Q: Did Nehru's foreign policy fail in 1962?

The war of October and November 1962 destroyed the credibility of the policy's central promise, although the policy itself was not formally abandoned. China, co-author of Panchsheel, attacked across the Himalayan frontier, Indian forces were defeated in both sectors, and Nehru's broadcast of 20 November 1962 admitted that India had been living in an artificial atmosphere of its own creation. Krishna Menon was removed from the Defence Ministry on 7 November 1962, the first no-confidence motion of Nehru's tenure followed in August 1963, and the Congress's internal unity, which the doctrine had underwritten, began the breakdown traced in the Kamaraj Plan resignations. What failed in 1962 was the doctrine's claim that judgment could substitute for military preparation and alignment in keeping India safe; the domestic-political device built on that claim failed with it.

### Q: What was the Panchsheel Agreement of 29 April 1954?

The Panchsheel Agreement was the Agreement on Trade and Intercourse between the Tibet Region of China and India, signed at Beijing on 29 April 1954 by N. Raghavan for India and Zhang Hanfu for China. Its operative clauses regulated trade marts and agencies, the routes and passes open to traders and pilgrims, and the withdrawal of the Indian military escorts and postal and telegraph services that India had inherited from the British position in Tibet. Its famous preamble announced the five principles of peaceful coexistence. The agreement ran for eight years and was not renewed after the deterioration of relations that followed 1959. In Indian domestic debate the agreement became a Congress emblem of principled diplomacy, attacked by the Jana Sangh as a surrender of the Tibetan buffer and defended by the government as the charter of a new Asian order.

### Q: What did India do during the Suez crisis of 1956?

During the Suez crisis India attempted mediation first and condemnation second. After Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal Company on 26 July 1956, Krishna Menon carried Indian proposals through the London conferences of August and September 1956, seeking international oversight of the canal that respected Egyptian sovereignty. When Britain, France and Israel invaded Egypt at the end of October 1956, India condemned the invasion at the United Nations and in Parliament in the strongest terms Nehru used against any Western action, and the Lok Sabha debates of December 1956 record the government defending the stance against members who feared for Western economic assistance. India also contributed to the diplomatic pressure that produced the invaders' withdrawal by March 1957. Domestically the stance renewed the Congress's anti-colonial credentials in the season the socialist pattern required defending.

### Q: Why did India grant asylum to the Dalai Lama in 1959?

India granted asylum to the Dalai Lama in March 1959 because refusal was impossible on the doctrine's own terms and costly on the constituency's terms. After the Lhasa uprising of March 1959 the Dalai Lama fled Tibet and reached Indian territory at the end of the month; a government preaching the protection of the weak and peaceful coexistence could not return a religious refugee to his pursuers. The Hindu cultural constituency, whose allegiance the Jana Sangh claimed, regarded the Tibetan hierarch as a figure of its own civilisational family, and expulsion would have handed the opposition the decade's most emotive issue. Nehru therefore granted asylum while limiting its political use, telling the Lok Sabha in April and May 1959 that the Dalai Lama would not conduct political agitation from Indian soil. The decision destroyed the Panchsheel relationship with Beijing, a price the government paid knowingly.

### Q: What happened at the Belgrade summit of 1961?

The Belgrade summit, held from 1 to 6 September 1961 with twenty-five participating governments, founded the Non-Aligned Movement as an institution. Nehru attended as one of the five initiating leaders, alongside Tito of Yugoslavia, Nasser of Egypt, Nkrumah of Ghana and Sukarno of Indonesia, and pressed the conference to address the danger of a superpower war rather than to constitute itself as a third bloc on the model of the two it refused. The declaration adopted at Belgrade called for disarmament, the ending of colonial rule, and the reform of international economic relations. For India the summit converted a national posture into a movement with a name, and the Congress presented the genealogy from the broadcast of September 1946 through Bandung to Belgrade as the biography of its own doctrine triumphant. The triumph was the doctrine's final dividend before the war of 1962.

### Q: What did the Nehru and Krishna Menon correspondence of 1956 reveal about United Nations positioning?

The correspondence between Nehru and V. K. Krishna Menon on United Nations positioning during 1956, preserved in the Krishna Menon Papers and the Nehru Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, reveals the domestic audience inside the drafting of foreign acts. Through the Suez and Hungary crises Nehru edited Menon's interventions with an eye to how sentences would be reported in Delhi, weighed the order of condemnations against the temper of the Lok Sabha, and treated Menon's parliamentary unpopularity as a cost to be managed. Inder Malhotra described the institutional partnership in Dilemmas of the Indian Foreign Office (2010); the correspondence adds the political calculation. For this article the letters are primary evidence that the domestic-political function of the foreign policy was intended by its authors and not a windfall they failed to notice.

### Q: What is Sumit Ganguly's reading of Nehru's foreign policy?

Sumit Ganguly, in Indian Foreign Policy: Retrospect and Prospect (2010), reads Nehru's foreign policy primarily through international-relations theory, as a coherent strategy by which a weak new state in a bipolar order maximised its autonomy. On that reading non-alignment was a third-bloc posture that kept India out of the superpower alliances, extracted assistance from both camps, and converted normative standing into influence beyond India's material weight. Ganguly treats the doctrine's principles as genuinely held and its failures, above all the unpreparedness exposed in 1962, as failures of implementation and military policy rather than of the framework itself. This article engages that reading respectfully and rules against its completeness: the strategic account cannot explain the doctrine's preaching at election rallies, its rigidity in the face of a growing Himalayan threat, or the domestic calculations visible in the 1956 correspondence.

### Q: What did C. Raja Mohan argue in Crossing the Rubicon in 2003?

In Crossing the Rubicon: The Shaping of India's New Foreign Policy (2003), C. Raja Mohan argued that Indian foreign policy after the Cold War was escaping the Nehruvian framework that had governed it for half a century, moving toward pragmatic engagement with the United States, an assertive Asian policy, and the open pursuit of great-power status. The book treats the Nehruvian decades as the period that fixed the vocabulary of Indian statecraft: non-alignment, strategic autonomy, and suspicion of alliances were the inheritance every later government had to argue with, whether it kept the words or discarded them. For this article the significance of Raja Mohan's account is its testimony to the doctrine's durability as a political language. A device that later reformers must define themselves against, decades after its author's death, had plainly done deeper work than the management of any single crisis.

### Q: How did foreign policy feature in the general elections of 1952, 1957 and 1962?

Foreign policy featured in the Congress campaigns of 1952, 1957 and 1962 as the frame of national pride within which other promises were made. In 1952 Nehru campaigned as the founder-statesman who had seated India among the nations, and the Congress won 364 Lok Sabha seats. In 1957, after Bandung and Suez, the campaign presented non-alignment as vindicated performance, with aid flowing from both blocs, and the tally rose to 371 seats even as the Communists won Kerala. In 1962 the border dispute with China had already damaged the doctrine, yet the Congress still traded on Nehru's world standing as reassurance and returned with 361 seats. The pattern across the three campaigns is a valence advantage: voters dissatisfied with prices or local Congress bosses forgave a government whose foreign standing made them proud, and the forgiveness was paid in seats.

### Q: What was India's position in the Korean War?

India's position in the Korean War combined condemnation of aggression with refusal of combat. When North Korea attacked the South in June 1950, India voted in the Security Council to name the aggressor and support resistance, declined to send fighting troops, contributed the 60th Parachute Field Ambulance as a medical unit, and criticised the crossing of the 38th parallel as an escalation risking a wider war with China. After the armistice of July 1953 India chaired the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission, with Major General K. S. Thimayya commanding the custodian force that held prisoners pending their choice of destination. The sequence became a standing Congress exhibit: non-alignment, the party argued, was not neutrality between right and wrong but judgment exercised in public, and Korea was the proof that the judgment could command the trust of both camps.

### Q: How did India respond to the Hungarian uprising of 1956?

India responded to the Soviet suppression of the Hungarian uprising in November 1956 with equivocation that its own Suez rhetoric made conspicuous. In the same weeks that Nehru condemned the Anglo-French-Israeli invasion of Egypt in the language of principle, his government declined to condemn the Soviet action in comparable terms, abstaining at the United Nations and speaking of sorrow and complexity. Opposition members led by Acharya Kripalani charged a double standard, and the charge was accurate as far as it went. The government absorbed it calmly because the domestic pricing of the two crises differed: Egypt invaded by former colonial powers activated the founding anti-imperial story for Indian audiences, while Hungary activated no comparable memory, and the Communist Party that defended Moscow was a regional rather than a national rival in 1956. The asymmetry remains the standard exhibit in critiques of Nehruvian moralism.

### Q: What was the Asian Relations Conference of March 1947?

The Asian Relations Conference met in New Delhi in March 1947, convened under the Indian Council of World Affairs with Nehru as the interim government's leading figure and host, and drew delegates from across Asia months before Indian independence. Delegations and observers from more than two dozen Asian countries and territories discussed the continent's common problems, and the conference founded the Asian Relations Organisation as a permanent body. Nehru's addresses to the conference presented Asia's awakening as the central fact of the coming world order and India as its natural meeting ground. The conference matters to this article because it predates the state whose foreign policy it rehearsed: before India possessed an army, a foreign office of its own, or a constitution, its future Prime Minister was already staging the convening role that Bandung and Belgrade would later institutionalise, before a domestic audience being taught to expect it.

### Q: Why did India take the Kashmir question to the United Nations in January 1948?

India took the Kashmir question to the United Nations on 1 January 1948 because Nehru's government believed its legal case was unanswerable and wished to display the new state's law-abiding character. Tribal raiders backed from Pakistan had entered Kashmir in October 1947; the Maharaja acceded to India on 26 October 1947; and the Indian complaint asked the Security Council to restrain Pakistan's assistance to the invasion. The Council instead internationalised the dispute and linked it to plebiscite machinery, an outcome Nehru came to regard as a lesson in great-power politics. Domestically, however, the referral performed the doctrine before the doctrine had a name: a state confident in law, seeking judgment rather than conquest, before the Constituent Assembly had even written Article 51's commitment to international peace into the Constitution's Directive Principles. The disappointment that followed entered the doctrine as a permanent suspicion of bloc-dominated machinery.

### Q: How did the Jana Sangh and the Communists attack non-alignment?

The Jana Sangh and the Communist Party attacked non-alignment from opposite directions, and the contradiction between their attacks was the doctrine's protection. The Jana Sangh, founded on 21 October 1951 under Syama Prasad Mookerjee, held that non-alignment was a failure of nerve which refused to name India's enemies, wasted Western goodwill, and sentimentalised a China digesting Tibet; its remedy leaned toward friendship with the democratic West. The Communist Party, after the programme reset of 1951, held the opposite: that non-alignment was a fiction concealing a tilt toward the Anglo-American camp, pointing to Commonwealth membership and Western aid as the substance beneath the rhetoric. Each attack required the voter to believe India stood in a camp, one camp too distant and the other too close, and the Congress answered both from the occupied centre: the government that recognised Beijing, condemned London over Suez, and took aid from Moscow and Washington alike could not plausibly be any capital's client.
