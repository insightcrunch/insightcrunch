Every schoolteacher in India holds a qualification whose legal worth is decided by a statute most teachers never read. The National Council for Teacher Education Act, 1993, the NCTE Act, determines which institutions may train teachers, which courses a recognised institution may run, what qualifications a person must hold to be recruited as a teacher, and what follows when any link in that chain is missing. A degree earned in good faith, after years of study and fees paid, can turn out to be legally worthless for the only purpose it was obtained, employment as a teacher, because the institution that awarded it lacked a permission the holder never knew to ask about. The Act is therefore best understood not as an education measure in the abstract but as a validity machine: it takes an enormous, decentralised training sector and sorts its output into qualifications the state will honour and qualifications it will not.

![The NCTE Act 1993 and teacher education in India, showing how recognition of training institutions decides whether a teaching qualification is valid for employment - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-07.webp)

The statute also carries a contradiction at its centre, and the contradiction is the reason this pillar exists. The recognition regime was built to discipline a private training sector that had expanded without planning or standards, and the regulated private sector, by and large, submits to it: private colleges apply, are inspected, are refused, appeal, and sometimes close. The most conspicuous breach of the regime, when it came to light, was not by a private college at all. Institutions funded by the Central Government, by state governments and by Union territory administrations had admitted students to teacher education courses for years without the recognition the Act required, and the qualifications those students earned were invalid by the statute's own terms. Parliament's response, in 2019, was not enforcement against its own institutions. It was a retrospective amendment that declared the institutions recognised all along. The state broke its own recognition rule and then legalised the breach, and any honest account of this Act has to hold both halves of that sentence together: the students caught in the breach were blameless and deserved rescue, and a regime whose violations are cured by legislation has announced the price of violating it.

Teacher education is the hardest of the professional educations to regulate well, and the reasons are structural, which is why the Act's failures deserve analysis rather than mere lament. In medicine or engineering, the consumer of the training is also, roughly, its judge: the patient and the employer can observe outcomes, and a market signal of some quality reaches the institution. In school teaching the consumer is a child of six or eleven, who cannot judge the training of the person at the blackboard and whose parents, in the government school system that employs most teachers, exercise no choice at all. The purchaser of the training, the state as employer, recruits by eligibility rules and seniority rather than by observed classroom quality, so the signal that might discipline a training college, that its graduates are preferred or avoided, barely exists. And the student teacher, the institution's immediate customer, is buying eligibility at least as much as education, and will rationally prefer the cheaper, shorter, less demanding route to the same certificate wherever the rules allow one. Every actor in the chain is responding sensibly to the incentives in front of them, and the sum of their sense is a system that produces credentials. A regulator dropped into that chain has to manufacture, by norms and inspection, the quality pressure that no market supplies, and has to do it against the customer's own preference for ease. That is the task Parliament set the Council in 1993, and it is the standard against which this pillar judges the result: not whether the Council was virtuous, but whether the machine it ran could ever have substituted for the missing signal, and what happened when it could not.

## What existed before the Act, and why advice was not enough

The Council the Act created was not new. A National Council for Teacher Education had existed since 1973, set up by a resolution of the Government of India as a national expert body to advise the Central and state governments on teacher education, with its secretariat housed in the Department of Teacher Education of the National Council of Educational Research and Training. For two decades it advised. The Statement of Objects and Reasons appended to the 1993 Bill records what two decades of advice achieved: the Council's status and role were purely advisory, and mainly for that reason it had very little impact on the standards of training institutions or on their unplanned growth. Advice could be, and was, ignored by the state governments and universities whose decisions actually governed the sector, and the body issuing it had no power to inspect, to refuse, to withdraw, or to insist.

The sector it failed to govern was changing underneath it. Teacher training in the decades after independence had been a modest, largely public enterprise, conducted in government training institutes, district institutes and university departments of education. From the nineteen eighties onward, and at gathering speed in the nineteen nineties, private unaided institutions entered in large numbers, particularly in a handful of states where demand for trained teachers, or more precisely demand for the credential that made a person appointable as a teacher, outran the public supply. Many of these institutions were genuine educational ventures. A substantial number were credential businesses: minimal land, minimal faculty, library and laboratory facilities that existed on paper, and a product whose value lay entirely in the eligibility it conferred. The National Policy on Education of 1986, and the Programme of Action prepared under it, drew the conclusion the advisory structure could not implement: the Council would be given statutory status, with the resources and capability to accredit institutions and to phase out the substandard. The Bill followed, and the Act received the assent of the President on 29 December 1993 as Act 73 of 1993.

The intellectual groundwork for a statutory council had been laid over three decades, and its failure to produce one is part of the explanation for the sector the Act inherited. The Education Commission of 1964 to 1966, chaired by D. S. Kothari, had treated teacher education as the weakest link in the educational chain and urged its integration with the universities and a decisive raising of its status and duration. The National Policy on Education of 1968 repeated the emphasis without machinery. The National Commission on Teachers of 1983 to 1985, chaired by D. P. Chattopadhyaya, examined the condition of school teachers and their preparation and recommended professionalisation of training, longer and better structured programmes, and an institutional order capable of enforcing standards. The National Policy on Education of 1986 moved from recommendation to construction: its Programme of Action launched the centrally sponsored restructuring of teacher education, under which District Institutes of Education and Training were established at the district level for elementary teacher preparation and in-service support, selected secondary training colleges were upgraded into Colleges of Teacher Education and Institutes of Advanced Study in Education, and the advisory Council was promised statutory form. The district institutes are worth pausing on, because they would return to the story a generation later: the public training infrastructure the 1986 settlement built, the DIETs in every district and their block and cluster support arms, is precisely the class of government institution that the 2019 amendment would retrospectively validate for having run courses without the recognition the 1993 Act required. The state built its own training system under one policy and licensed it under another, and for twenty years the two never met.

The universities, meanwhile, occupied an ambiguous middle ground that the Act would have to discipline. Secondary teacher training had grown up inside the university system, as departments and affiliated colleges offering the Bachelor of Education under university ordinances, while elementary training sat with state governments and their boards. Affiliation by a university and approval by a state education department were the only currencies of legitimacy either stream had known, and both were granted by bodies with interests of their own: universities gained affiliated colleges and examination fees, and state governments gained trained-teacher output figures, with neither institutionally charged to ask whether the training was any good. A statutory council with no funding relationship to the institutions and no examination income was designed to be the first actor in the system whose only interest was the standard. That design intention, and the system's three-decade experience of actors with other interests, is the context in which the Council's later failures should be read: the Act created a disinterested regulator for an interested system, and the interested system did not retire.

### When did the NCTE become a statutory body?

The Council existed as an advisory body from 1973 and acquired statutory personality under the National Council for Teacher Education Act, 1993, Act 73 of 1993, which received assent on 29 December 1993. The Act was brought into force on 1 July 1995, and the statutory Council was constituted on 17 August 1995, when the recognition machinery began to operate.

The Bill had been introduced in Parliament in 1992, and its passage through the two Houses during 1993 was not among the contested legislative events of that crowded year, for the proposition that a national council should license teacher training commanded a consensus that the subject's later history would strain. The consensus is visible in the Statement of Objects and Reasons, which reads as a bill of indictment against nobody: an advisory body that could not act, an unplanned growth nobody had authorised, a policy commitment from 1986 awaiting its instrument. What the parliamentary record of such a passage rarely supplies is adversarial testing of the design's weak points, and the weak points were present in the text from the start: an inspection function without a funded inspectorate, a planning mandate without a forecasting apparatus, and consequences that would fall on students for the defaults of institutions. A more contested passage might have hardened the design against its first two decades. The consensus passage it received is itself evidence of the era's assumption, that creating the regulator was the reform, and that the regulator would somehow acquire in operation the capacities the statute had not expressly built.

The gap between enactment in 1993 and commencement on 1 July 1995 matters, because the Act's operative date, the appointed day, is the hinge on which the validity of a generation of qualifications turns. Courses run before the appointed day belong to the older legal world, in which recognition was a matter for state governments and affiliating universities. Courses run after it belong to the Act. The deferred commencement was administrative necessity: a Council had to be appointed, four regional committees constituted, regulations framed, and an application window opened for the thousands of institutions already operating, before a prohibition could fairly be switched on. But the delay also meant that the Act spent its first eighteen months as a promise, and institutions planning expansion in that interval planned against a deadline they could see approaching.

The long title states the object with unusual completeness: an Act to provide for the establishment of the National Council for Teacher Education with a view to achieving planned and coordinated development of the teacher education system throughout the country, the regulation and proper maintenance of norms and standards in the teacher education system, and matters connected with those purposes. Three distinct ambitions are packed into that sentence. Planned and coordinated development is a planning ambition, concerned with how many teachers the country needs, where, and at which levels of schooling. Regulation and maintenance of norms and standards is a licensing ambition, concerned with the floor below which no training may fall. The third, the open-ended tail, is where the Act's later growth, into prescribing who may be recruited as a teacher at all, would find its room. Education, including teacher education, sits in the Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule since the forty-second constitutional amendment moved it there from the State List, so a central statute of this kind operates in a field the states also occupy, and the friction that competence question generates between the Council, state governments and universities runs through the whole life of the Act and is treated in the companion analysis of [education and the Concurrent List](/2015/05/01/education-concurrent-list-federalism/).

## The architecture of the Act

The Act is short by the standards of regulatory statutes, running to thirty-four sections in six chapters, and its weight is distributed in a pattern worth noticing. The first chapter holds the short title, extent and commencement, and the definitions, several of which were substantially enlarged in 2011 and repay attention. The second constitutes the Council, the Executive Committee and the regional committees. The third states the Council's functions and its power over the qualifications of school teachers. The fourth, sections 14 to 18, is the recognition chapter, and it is the part of the Act that produces litigation, livelihoods and headlines. The fifth deals with finance, accounts and audit, and the sixth with the miscellaneous apparatus of a statutory body: delegation, protection for action taken in good faith, directions by the Central Government, supersession of the Council, rule-making by the Central Government, regulation-making by the Council, and the laying of rules and regulations before Parliament.

The definitions in section 2 do quiet but decisive work. An institution is an institution which offers courses or training in teacher education. A recognised institution is one recognised by a regional committee under section 14, which means recognition is a status conferred by a named authority under a named provision, not a general reputation for legitimacy and not the same thing as affiliation by a university or approval by a state government, two confusions that recur through the case law. Teacher education itself is defined by reference to programmes of education, research or training of persons for equipping them to teach at pre-primary, primary, secondary and senior secondary stages in schools, and in non-formal education, part-time education, adult education and distance correspondence courses, a breadth that brings the elementary diploma, the secondary bachelor's degree in education, the master's degree, physical education and the open-learning variants inside one statutory perimeter. The 2011 amendment inserted a definition of school covering any recognised school imparting pre-primary, primary, upper primary, secondary or senior secondary education, or a college imparting senior secondary education, whether established and controlled by government, aided, or wholly unaided, together with a definition of local authority; those definitions were the foundation on which the Council's power over the qualifications of serving school teachers was then built.

Two features of the design explain most of what followed. The first is that the Act regulates entry and continuation by permission, but the permission is held at one remove from the classroom: the Council regulates institutions, the institutions produce teachers, and the state as employer recruits them, so a failure at the first step surfaces years later as a defect in a person's eligibility at the third. The second is that almost everything substantive, the norms of land and building, the qualifications of teacher educators, the duration and structure of each programme, the intake permitted, lives not in the Act but in regulations the Council makes under section 32, subject to rules the Central Government makes under section 31. Parliament fixed the skeleton and the sanctions; the flesh is delegated legislation, which is why the great regulatory events of the Act's life, the regulations of 2014 above all, are instruments most citizens never see cited, and why the courts, when they intervene, are usually construing a regulation's validity against the Act rather than the Act against the Constitution.

## The programmes inside the perimeter, and what lies outside it

Because recognition attaches to courses, the practical map of the Act is a map of programmes, and a reader who holds it can locate any qualification dispute. For the elementary stage, classes one to eight, the qualifying training is the two-year Diploma in Elementary Education, the successor of the older basic training certificates, entered after senior secondary school, and the four-year Bachelor of Elementary Education, an integrated undergraduate degree combining liberal study with professional preparation, first offered by the University of Delhi in 1994 and long confined to a small number of institutions. For the secondary stage the qualifying degree is the Bachelor of Education, historically of one year after a subject degree, of two years since the regulations of 2014, and available in integrated four-year form as the B.A. B.Ed. and B.Sc. B.Ed., run notably by the Regional Institutes of Education of the National Council of Educational Research and Training and, after the pilot of 2023, as the Integrated Teacher Education Programme. Above the bachelor's sit the Master of Education, the qualifying degree for teacher educators and for senior secondary teaching routes in combination with subject mastery, and the physical education sequence of the B.P.Ed. and M.P.Ed. Around these stand the pre-primary and early childhood diplomas, the diploma in education for the elementary stage's earlier nomenclature, part-time and distance variants of the bachelor's degree for serving teachers, and research degrees in education, which belong principally to the universities. Each programme has its own appendix of norms in the regulations, its own sanctioned intake logic, and its own recognition orders, so that an institution may be recognised for one programme on the same campus where another runs unrecognised, a fact pattern behind a significant share of validity litigation.

Equally instructive is what the perimeter excludes. The Act does not govern the qualifications of university and college teachers, which belong to the University Grants Commission and its eligibility test for lectureship; a person may be fully qualified to teach undergraduates and wholly unqualified, in this Act's terms, to teach class nine. It does not govern the training of teachers for institutions outside the school definition it carries, nor the vast informal market of coaching, whose instructors need no qualification the law recognises because the law does not reach them. It does not conduct examinations or award anything: the degrees and diplomas are awarded by universities and boards to students of recognised institutions, which is why the Act's sanctions operate by disconnecting institutions from those awarding bodies rather than by annulling awards directly. And it does not regulate the teacher in service, whose conditions, discipline and promotion are matters for the recruiting government and its service rules, except at the single point where section 12A and the eligibility test reach into the staffroom. The perimeter, in short, is the pipeline of preparation for school teaching, from admission to a training course to the certificate that makes its holder recruitable, and the Act's authority is at its strongest at the pipeline's entrance and its most contested at the exit, where its qualifications discipline meets the service rights of teachers already employed.

One seam in the perimeter generates persistent confusion and belongs on the map. The training of special education teachers, for children with disabilities, is regulated not by this Council but by the Rehabilitation Council of India under its own statute of 1992, which maintains the register of rehabilitation professionals and approves the diploma and degree programmes in special education, including the B.Ed. in special education. A school recruiting a special educator therefore looks to a different regulator's recognition, and institutions offering both general and special education programmes answer to both Councils for different courses on the same campus. The seam is defensible in origin, special education grew out of the rehabilitation rather than the schooling establishment, and untidy in operation, since inclusive education policy pushes the two streams together in the classroom faster than the two regulators have merged in the statute book. Candidates and recruiting bodies stumble over it regularly, and the validity table in this article should be read, for special education qualifications, with the Rehabilitation Council substituted for the Council throughout.

## The Council: composition, functions and the qualifications power

Section 3 constitutes the Council as a body corporate with perpetual succession, headquartered at Delhi, and assembles it from the constituencies whose cooperation the design assumes. The Central Government appoints the Chairperson, the Vice-Chairperson and the Member-Secretary. Around them sit ex officio members drawn from the Union education administration and the national educational bodies, thirteen persons possessing experience and knowledge in the field of education or teaching, nine members representing the states and Union territories by rotation, three members of Parliament, of whom two are from the House of the People and one from the Council of States, and three members appointed from among teachers of primary and secondary schools and teachers of recognised institutions. An Executive Committee under section 7 conducts the Council's business between its meetings. The structure is representative rather than technocratic: the states whose institutions will be regulated, the Parliament that enacted the statute, the profession being regulated and the academic bodies adjacent to it all hold seats, which gives the Council legitimacy across the system and also means its deliberations carry the system's internal disagreements into the room. The institutional structure invites comparison with the other central institution statutes, each of which distributes seats, expertise and governmental control differently, and the comparison is drawn in [the analysis of the central institution Acts](/2015/03/15/central-institution-acts-india-comparison/).

The Council's place among regulators is distinctive, and the distinction explains a recurring confusion. The University Grants Commission, whose statute is examined in [the pillar on the UGC Act](/2015/02/15/ugc-act-1956-explained/), sets and maintains standards in university education generally and funds universities; the Council sets and maintains standards for one professional stream wherever it is taught, including inside universities, and funds nothing. A university department of education therefore answers to two masters whose jurisdictions overlap but do not coincide: the university grants affiliation and awards the degree under its own Act, and the Council grants recognition under this Act, and neither body's approval substitutes for the other's. Teacher education is also the only school-adjacent profession whose training regulator sits at the centre with licensing power over institutions in every state, a centralisation the Concurrent List permits and the states have periodically resented, most sharply when a regional committee refuses or withdraws recognition from institutions a state government has encouraged.

Section 12 enumerates the functions, and the enumeration is best read as four groups. The first group is knowledge: the Council undertakes surveys and studies relating to teacher education and publishes the results, examines and reviews the implementation of its own norms, and undertakes research and innovation and their dissemination. The second is coordination: it coordinates and monitors teacher education across the country, makes recommendations to the Central and state governments, universities, the University Grants Commission and recognised institutions on plans and programmes, and lays down guidelines for the promotion of pre-service and in-service training. The third is standard-setting: it lays down norms and standards for teacher education courses, including minimum qualifications for teacher educators, guidance on curricula and methods, norms for minimum qualifications of persons to be recruited as teachers, and norms for the facilities, staffing pattern and admission criteria of institutions. The fourth is prevention: it is charged to take all necessary steps to prevent the commercialisation of teacher education, a function stated as a duty rather than a power and honoured, as the later record shows, very unevenly. Functions conferred by the Central Government on any other matter entrusted to it complete the list.

### What does section 12 of the NCTE Act empower the Council to do?

Section 12 charges the Council to survey and study teacher education, to coordinate and monitor its development across the country, to recommend plans to governments and universities, to lay down norms and standards for courses, teacher educators and institutions, to promote pre-service and in-service training, and to prevent the commercialisation of teacher education.

The most consequential function in the list is the one Parliament added eighteen years later. As enacted, the Act was chiefly about institutions: who trains teachers and to what standard. The National Council for Teacher Education (Amendment) Act, 2011, Act 18 of 2011, brought into force on 1 June 2012, carried the Act across the threshold of the school. It inserted the definitions of school and local authority described above, amended section 12, and inserted section 12A, which empowers the Council, for the purpose of maintaining standards of education in schools, to determine by regulations the qualifications of persons for being recruited as teachers in any pre-primary, primary, upper primary, secondary, senior secondary or intermediate school or college, established, run, aided or recognised by the Central Government, a state government, a local or other authority. The power is a recruitment-qualification power: the Council does not appoint teachers and does not regulate their service, but no appointing authority may lawfully recruit a person who lacks the qualifications the Council determines, and the state recruitment rules must conform.

Section 12A carries two provisos that show Parliament legislating with the serving teacher expressly in mind, and they matter to the eligibility test dispute examined later in this article. The first proviso protects continuance: nothing in the section shall adversely affect the continuance of any person recruited in a school or college under the rules in force immediately before the commencement of the 2011 amendment, solely on the ground of non-fulfilment of such qualifications as the Council may specify. The second proviso qualifies the protection: the minimum qualifications referred to in the first proviso shall be acquired within the period specified in this Act or under the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009. Read together, the provisos strike a deliberate bargain. A serving teacher's job is not forfeited the day the Council raises the standard; the standard nevertheless applies to that teacher, through acquisition within a statutory window rather than through exemption. That bargain, struck in 2011 for the qualifications generally, is the statutory frame inside which the courts would later have to place the eligibility test, and it forecloses the simplest version of the reliance argument, because Parliament had already said, in this Act, that continuance is protected and acquisition is required.

The Council's accountability frame completes the institutional picture, and it is conventionally centralised. The Council is funded through grants from the Central Government and its own receipts, maintains a fund under section 23, prepares an annual report and audited accounts that are laid before Parliament, and works under two reserve powers the Centre holds over it: the power under section 29 to give the Council directions on questions of policy, whose characterisation as policy is made final by the government's own decision, and the power under section 30 to supersede the Council for a period not exceeding six months where it has persistently defaulted in complying with directions or in performing its functions, after giving it a reasonable opportunity to show cause. The supersession power has not been the instrument of the Act's failures; the Council's shortcomings were never of the kind that a six-month supersession would cure, and the Centre's direction power operates in the background as the reason the Ministry, rather than the Council, announced and carried the great corrective measures of the record, the 2019 validation and the eligibility certificate's lifetime validity among them. A reader mapping where power actually sits should note the pattern: the Council licenses, inspects and adjudicates in the ordinary course, and the political centre intervenes, by amendment or direction, at the moments when the ordinary course produces an outcome the system cannot absorb. That division, regulator for the routine and legislature for the crisis, is the Act's real constitution, and the 2019 amendment is unintelligible without it.

It is worth pausing on what the 2011 amendment did not do, because it is frequently misdescribed, including in summaries that attribute to it the retrospective recognition of institutions. The 2011 amendment granted no recognition, deemed no institution recognised, and validated no qualification. Its whole subject was the qualifications of persons for recruitment as school teachers, together with the definitional apparatus that power needed. Retrospective recognition arrived only in 2019, by a separate amendment with a separate rationale, and conflating the two amendments scrambles the record of both: the first extended the Council's reach from the training institution to the school gate, on stated and prospective terms with a protected transition; the second reached backwards to cure a compliance failure that had already happened. The distinction is not pedantry. It is the difference between a regulator acquiring a new power and a legislature pardoning a breach of the old one.

## The regulations: where the Act's substance actually lives

Because the Act delegates its substance, the history of its regulations is a history of the Act itself, and the generations of the recognition norms mark the system's changing theory of control. The first regulations of the statutory era concentrated on entry: land, building, staff and the documents by which they were proved. Successive remakings, including the regulations of 2005 under which much of the recognition boom was processed, the norms of 2009, and the comprehensive remaking of 2014, shifted the emphasis in stages: from documents to inspection, from inspection to curriculum and duration, and in 2014 to the lengthening of programmes, the strengthening of school internship, performance appraisal reporting and accreditation. Each generation also answered the litigation of the one before. Judgments that exposed admissions made before unconditional recognition produced tighter sequencing rules; judgments on state interference produced clearer statements of the Council's primacy; judgments on the calendar produced the notified windows and cut-offs that structure the cycle. The regulations are laid before Parliament under section 33, as are the Central Government's rules, but parliamentary scrutiny of instruments of this volume is formal rather than real, and the effective legislature of teacher education is the Council in committee, amending appendices of norms that determine whether a college in a district town may open its doors. That is the standard pattern of Indian regulatory statutes, and this pillar notes it without pretending the pattern is a scandal; the scandal, where there is one, lies not in delegation but in what the delegate did with the power in the boom years, when the norms on paper and the institutions on the ground diverged beyond the inspection system's capacity to notice.

A further body of delegated law matters to the qualifications side. The minimum qualifications for teacher appointment were laid down by notification under the school education statute and revised as the programme map changed, most consequentially in alignment with the regulations of 2014, and the eligibility test guidelines are likewise subordinate instruments, issued in 2011 and amended as the certificate's validity and the papers' structure evolved. The practical consequence for the reader is that almost any precise question, what intake, what faculty strength, what qualifying marks, what internship length, is answered in an instrument the Council can amend without returning to Parliament, which is why this article states the durable structure and the landmark instruments and directs the reader, for the figure of the moment, to the notification in force. A pillar that hard-coded the numbers would be wrong within one amendment cycle; a pillar that explains where the numbers live, and which of them the courts have treated as structural, stays right.

The Council's academic instruments deserve a word beside its legal ones, because a part of its influence has run through persuasion rather than permission. The National Curriculum Framework for Teacher Education of 2009, prepared by the Council in the wake of the National Curriculum Framework for school education of 2005, restated the aims of teacher preparation around the reflective practitioner: a teacher formed by engagement with the child's context, by study of the disciplines she would teach, and by prolonged, supervised immersion in real classrooms, rather than by a syllabus of methods delivered in lecture. The framework bound nobody, and its career illustrates the two halves of the Council's personality. As an academic body descended from the advisory council of 1973, it could articulate the profession's best understanding of itself with real authority, and the framework's language runs through the curriculum documents of the two-year programmes that followed. As a regulator, it could not make the median college teach in the framework's spirit, because nothing in the recognition machinery measures spirit, and the distance between the framework's classroom and the inspected institution's classroom is the distance this article has been measuring throughout. The integrated programme is a further attempt to close that distance by changing the institution rather than exhorting it: place the preparation inside a university, lengthen it, staff it from the disciplines, and the framework's teacher becomes a plausible product rather than an aspiration.

## The recognition machinery: sections 14 to 18

Chapter IV is the operative heart of the Act, and its five sections form a single connected mechanism that a reader should hold as a whole. Recognition is the licence to exist as a teacher education institution. Permission is the licence to expand. Affiliation and examination by the university system are switched on and off by recognition. Contravention carries consequences graded from discontinuation of the course to the invalidity of the qualification itself. And an appeal lies from the regional committee that decides to the Council in Delhi. The machinery is deliberately front-loaded: nearly all of its force is exerted before a course begins or at the moment an institution strays, because the statute's drafters understood that a training defect discovered after a cohort graduates cannot be repaired by punishing the institution, only visited on the students, and the law's recurring embarrassment is that it keeps being discovered exactly then.

Section 14 governs recognition of institutions offering or intending to offer a course or training in teacher education. An institution applies to the regional committee concerned in the form and manner determined by regulations. The committee obtains such particulars as it considers necessary, satisfies itself, in the terms of clause (a) of sub-section (3), that the institution has adequate financial resources, accommodation, library, qualified staff, laboratory and other features to give a proper start to the course, and, where satisfied, passes an order granting recognition, subject to the fulfilment of such conditions as may be determined by regulations; where not satisfied, it refuses by a reasoned order. The examining body then acts on the order: every examining body shall, on receipt of the recognition order, grant affiliation where recognition has been granted and cancel affiliation where it has been refused. Institutions already offering courses immediately before the appointed day were given a transitional entitlement: they could continue the course for six months if they applied for recognition within that period, and until the disposal of the application, a provision that converted the entire existing sector into applicants at a stroke and spared it from overnight illegality.

### What does NCTE recognition actually authorise an institution to do?

Recognition under section 14 authorises the institution to conduct the named teacher education course, for the intake and duration the order specifies, and obliges the examining body to affiliate it for that course. It is course-specific and conditional, not a general licence to run any programme, and it endures only while the norms and conditions are met.

The committee's decision under section 14 is an administrative order of a particular kind, and its incidents shape the litigation. The order granting recognition is communicated to the institution and to the examining body, and it speaks from the session it specifies; an order refusing recognition must record reasons, and the obligation to reason is the hook on which judicial review hangs, since an unreasoned refusal, or one resting on a ground outside the Act and the regulations, cannot survive the writ court. The satisfaction the committee must reach is likewise bounded: clause (a) of sub-section (3) directs it to the institution's financial resources, accommodation, library, qualified staff, laboratory and other features necessary for the proper conduct of the course, and a refusal built on considerations foreign to that list, on the state's teacher-surplus policy, for instance, after Sant Dnyaneshwar, travels beyond the section. Recognition once granted is not a one-time event in the institution's favour but a status held on continuing condition, and the order itself is the institution's title deed, the document its students will one day need to see, which is why its custody, publication and verification recur through this article as a practical refrain.

Section 15 governs the recognised institution that wants more. A recognised institution intending to start a new course or training in teacher education, or to increase its intake in an existing course, may not do so on the strength of its existing recognition; it must obtain the permission of the regional committee, applying the same discipline of particulars, satisfaction and reasoned order. The distinction between the two sections is elementary and persistently blurred in practice and in print. Recognition attaches to the institution for a course; permission attaches to each new course or expansion of intake. An institution lawfully running a recognised bachelor's programme that launches a diploma, or doubles its seats, without permission is in breach in respect of the new activity even though its original recognition stands untouched, and the students of the unauthorised course are exposed exactly as if the institution had never been recognised at all.

### What is the difference between recognition and permission under the NCTE Act?

Recognition, under section 14, is the initial grant that admits an institution to conduct a named teacher education course at all. Permission, under section 15, is the further grant a recognised institution needs before starting a new course or increasing intake. A course run without the applicable grant leaves its students holding an invalid qualification.

Section 16 completes the circuit from the other side. Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, no examining body shall, on or after the appointed day, grant affiliation to any institution, or hold an examination for a course of training conducted by an institution, unless the institution has obtained recognition under section 14 or permission under section 15. The non obstante clause is the Act's assertion of supremacy within its field, and it is aimed squarely at universities and boards: affiliation granted in defiance of it does not cure the institution, and examinations held for an unrecognised course produce results the law refuses to treat as qualifications. Section 13 supplies the inspection power that feeds the machinery: for ascertaining whether recognised institutions are functioning in accordance with the Act, the Council may cause inspection of any such institution to be made by such persons as it may direct, and the institution must furnish the information the visitors require. Inspection is the machinery's sensory system, and its weaknesses, visiting teams assembled for a day, reports of uneven quality, long intervals between visits to institutions spread across a subcontinent, are the point at which the paper regime and the physical regime most often part company.

## Recognition in operation: calendar, conditions and the prospective rule

Between the text of sections 14 and 15 and an institution's lived experience of them stands the recognition calendar, and the calendar has generated as much litigation as the merits. Recognition runs in annual cycles keyed to the academic session: applications are invited for a coming session within notified windows, processed through scrutiny of documents, inspection by a visiting team, a stage at which the committee communicates its intent and the institution completes formalities including faculty selection through the affiliating university's process, and a formal recognition order specifying the programme, the intake and the session from which recognition runs. The Supreme Court's insistence, in Maa Vaishno Devi Mahila Mahavidyalaya, on treating the schedule as binding on every actor, the applicant, the committee, the state and the university alike, converted the calendar from administrative guidance into law: an institution that misses its window, or whose recognition order arrives after admissions close, cannot recover the lost session by pleading the delay of another authority. The doctrine is severe and deliberately so, because the alternative, admissions first and recognition later, inverts the Act.

The conditions attached to recognition reach into the institution's physical and academic substance. The norms in the regulations prescribe the land and built-up area for each programme and intake, the lecture rooms, library holdings and laboratory facilities for the relevant stage, the number and qualifications of teacher educators, including the requirement that the head hold the prescribed professional qualifications, the staff pattern for supporting personnel, financial provisions including endowment and reserve funds, and the curriculum framework the programme must follow. Recognition is granted subject to fulfilment of conditions determined by regulations, and the institution's obligation is continuing: it must maintain the norms for as long as recognition lasts, submit to inspection, and file the performance information the regulations require. The regulations of 2014 added two disciplines aimed at that continuity, a requirement of periodic performance appraisal reporting by every recognised institution and a push toward accreditation of institutions by an accrediting agency, both designed to replace the system's historic pattern, in which an institution was examined intensively once, at entry, and scarcely at all thereafter. How far either discipline has bitten is contested; the reporting obligation at least gave the Council a documentary basis on which, in a sector-wide information exercise in the middle of the twenty tens, it called upon institutions to furnish their records, and recognition was withdrawn from institutions that failed to establish their bona fides, an episode that closed a stratum of paper institutions the entry process had admitted.

One further rule of operation deserves statement because it explains why the 2019 amendment had to be legislative at all. Recognition granted under section 14 operates prospectively. An order obtained in a given year validates the course from the session the order specifies forward; it does not reach back to cure sessions already run without recognition, and the courts have consistently refused to treat a later grant as retrospective absolution for admissions made before it. The rule follows from the structure of the Act: recognition is a prior licence, and a licensing system in which the licence can be acquired after the activity would reward exactly the conduct it exists to prevent. But the rule's consequence, when a default by a government institution came to light after cohorts had graduated, was that no administrative act could save the students; only Parliament could deem the recognition to have existed. The prospective rule and the retrospective amendment are the two ends of the same principle, and the Act's history is unintelligible without both: the courts held the prospective line against private applicants for two decades, and when the defaulter proved to be the state itself, the line was moved by statute, for the state's institutions only.

## The regional committees and the consequences of default

The decisions under sections 14, 15 and 17 are not taken in Delhi. Section 20 requires the Council to establish regional committees, and four were constituted, for the Eastern, Western, Northern and Southern regions, with offices at Bhubaneswar, Bhopal, Jaipur and Bangalore respectively, each exercising the Council's recognition functions for the states within its region. The regional committee is thus the working sovereign of the Act: it receives the application, causes the visiting team to inspect, grants or refuses recognition, permits or refuses a new course, and withdraws recognition when an institution falls away. The Council at the centre frames the norms in regulations, hears appeals, and may, under section 21, terminate a regional committee and reconstitute it, a power that acknowledges, in the design itself, that the decentralised tier might itself fail or be captured. In practice the four committees have not worn the same face. Recognition booms in particular states in particular years, and refusals and withdrawals concentrated in others, track the committees' differing tempers as much as the institutions' differing quality, and litigants have learned to plead the regional committee's record as part of their case.

Section 17 states the consequences of contravention, and it is drawn in two movements. Where a regional committee, on its own motion or on a representation received, is satisfied after inquiry, and after giving the institution a reasonable opportunity of being heard, that a recognised institution has contravened the Act, the rules, the regulations or any condition of recognition, it may withdraw recognition by a reasoned order, effective from the end of the academic session next following the date of communication of the order. The deferred effect is deliberate and humane: a course in mid-stream is not extinguished overnight, and the institution must discontinue the course only when the session boundary arrives, with affiliation falling away in step. The second movement is the one that gives the section its teeth and its victims. A qualification in teacher education obtained from an institution whose recognition is withdrawn, or from an institution that was never recognised, after the relevant date, shall not be treated as a valid qualification for the purposes of employment under the Central Government, any state government or a university, or in any school, college or other educational body aided by the government. Section 17A, inserted later, closes the admission door from the students' side: no institution shall admit any student to a course or training in teacher education unless it has first obtained recognition or permission, so that enrolment itself, in an unrecognised course, is a prohibited act and not merely a risky one.

### Is NCTE recognition permanent once it is granted?

No. Recognition is a continuing status earned under conditions, and section 17 empowers the regional committee to withdraw it, after inquiry and a hearing, where the institution contravenes the Act, the rules, the regulations or the conditions of its recognition. Withdrawal takes effect from the end of the next academic session, and affiliation falls with it.

### What happens to students when recognition is withdrawn?

The withdrawal itself is deferred to the end of the next academic session precisely so a course in progress is not extinguished mid-stream, and courts have repeatedly protected students already admitted while upholding the withdrawal against the institution. But a qualification obtained after the course lost recognition is not valid for employment, which is where the loss ultimately lands.

The four committees do not carry equal loads, and the inequality is written into the sector's geography. The northern region contains the states in which the private boom ran longest and largest, and its committee has historically processed a volume of applications, inspections, appeals and consequent litigation out of proportion to the other three, while the eastern committee's region includes states where training capacity is thin and the regulatory problem is scarcity rather than surplus. A single national norm thus lands on four different regulatory realities, and the committees' reputations among institutions, for strictness, for speed and for susceptibility to pressure, diverge accordingly. The Act's answer to divergence is the appellate tier and the Council's power to frame uniform regulations, and the answer is incomplete in a way practitioners will recognise: uniformity of the written norm coexists with variety in its administration, and forum knowledge, which committee, which year's temper, which inspecting team's habits, becomes a professional asset for the consultants who shepherd applications through. A licensing system in which such knowledge has market value has already conceded something about itself.

Against the regional committee's orders under sections 14, 15 and 17, section 18 provides an appeal to the Council by any person aggrieved, to be preferred within sixty days of the order, with a power in the Council to entertain a later appeal for sufficient cause. The Council's appellate committee in Delhi is therefore a second forum of fact as well as law, and the volume of appeals it carries is a map of the recognition system's friction: refusals for deficiencies in land, building, faculty and finance dominate, alongside complaints of procedural irregularity against visiting teams and committees. Beyond the appeal lie the constitutional writ jurisdictions of the High Courts and the Supreme Court, and it is there, rather than in the statutory forum, that the large questions of the Act have been settled. The appellate structure embodies the Act's recurring tension in miniature: a specialist forum designed for speed and expertise, sitting beneath generalist courts that institutions reach for because the specialist forum's own consistency is in doubt.

For the individual reader, the machinery reduces to a single practical question, asked by lakhs of teachers and applicants every recruitment season: is this qualification valid? The answer is never found in the degree certificate alone. It is assembled from the institution's recognition at the time, the course's permission, any validating amendment that covers the case, and the holder's own position under the eligibility test discipline. The general position set out in the table that follows is legal information assembled from the Act, and it is not a substitute for advice on an individual qualification dispute, where the dates of admission, recognition and withdrawal decide everything. Three working rules govern the use of the table. Dates are everything: a recognition regime keyed to academic sessions makes the year of admission and the year of completion the first facts to fix, before any argument about equity is heard. Documents outrank assurances: the recognition order, the affiliation letter and the notification entry are the case, and a prospectus or a verbal assurance from the institution is not evidence of any of them. And the holder's remedy, where the qualification fails, almost never lies against the recruiting authority that refused it, which acted on the statute, but against the institution whose default produced the failure, or within the statutory forums the Act provides, which is why the table's final column sends each case in a different direction.

## The three-cornered contest: Council, state and university

No account of the Act is complete without the contest it institutionalised, because the recognition an institution needs is the product of three authorities whose incentives do not align. The Council wants standards and answers for the national floor. The state government wants teacher supply, controls the land and building permissions without which no institution can physically exist, runs the largest training institutions itself, and recruits the teachers the system produces. The university wants affiliated colleges, examination candidates and academic jurisdiction, and its statutes and ordinances were written for a world in which affiliation was its own sovereign act. The Act ranks them: recognition by the Council is first in time and supreme in effect, the examining body must follow the recognition order under sections 14 and 16, and state policy must yield where it conflicts with the Council's norms. But ranking is not the same as harmony, and the litigation record is a chronicle of each corner testing the ranking.

The state's test came first and produced the leading case. Maharashtra, confronting a surplus of trained teachers and a mushrooming of colleges, adopted a policy of refusing new teacher education institutions, and the dispute reached the Supreme Court in Sant Dnyaneshwar Shikshan Shastra Mahavidyalaya. The Court's answer defined the field: the Act occupies the ground of standards in teacher education, the Council's role is supreme within it, and a state policy, however well-intentioned its supply logic, cannot be deployed to frustrate the statutory scheme under which qualified institutions are entitled to recognition on the Council's norms. The university's test came through affiliation: universities delayed affiliation after recognition, imposed conditions of their own, or sought to examine students of institutions whose recognition was disputed, and the courts read section 16 as it is written, affiliation follows recognition and cannot precede it, be withheld on grounds the Act does not recognise, or survive its withdrawal. The third corner's test is quieter and continuous: states, as employers, have repeatedly sought to shape eligibility through recruitment rules, preferring their own training certificates or their own candidates, and in Basic Education Board, Uttar Pradesh v. Upendra Rai the Supreme Court held that a qualification recognised within the NCTE framework could not be refused by the state in favour of its own preferred certificate, the qualifications regime being national in design. Read together, the three lines of authority convert the Act from a licensing statute into a settlement of institutional power, and they explain its durability: every actor who might have routed around it has been told by the Court that the road is closed.

The settlement has a price, and the price is accountability diffusion. When a student discovers that her qualification is invalid, each authority can point at another: the state blames the Council's recognition, the university blames the institution's misrepresentation, the Council blames the institution's default and the state's failure to verify at recruitment. The Act assigns the defect to the institution and its consequences to the student, and the three-cornered structure means no single authority owns the student's loss. It is this diffusion, more than any single default, that made a legislative rescue necessary in 2019 and that will make another one necessary if the verification of recognition at the point of admission and recruitment is not made systematic. The technology for that verification is unglamorous and exists: a complete, public, course-wise and year-wise register of recognition, consulted by universities before affiliation, by institutions before admission and by recruiting bodies before appointment. The Council publishes recognition orders and lists of recognised institutions, and recruiting authorities do verify, but the register's completeness across the Act's whole history, including the pre-digital first decade, is imperfect, and disputes about sessions run in the nineteen nineties still turn on paper orders in regional committee files. A validity regime is only as good as its archive, and the archive is the Act's least reformed component.

## The enforcement design: consequences without crimes

A reader looking for the Act's penal clauses will not find them, and the absence is one of its most instructive features. The Act creates no offence of running an unrecognised institution, prescribes no fine and no imprisonment for admitting students without recognition, and gives neither the Council nor the regional committees a power to levy a monetary penalty. Its entire enforcement architecture is civil and relational, and it works by disconnection. The unrecognised institution is disconnected from the examining body, which section 16 forbids to affiliate it or examine its students. The defaulting recognised institution is disconnected from its recognition, which section 17 withdraws, and from admissions, which section 17A bars. The graduate of either is disconnected from public employment, because section 17 declares the qualification invalid for employment under government and in aided institutions. The state enforces the Act, in other words, by refusing to deal with the defaulter at the three points where the defaulter needs the state: affiliation, examinations and recruitment. Conduct that crosses into fraud, a forged recognition order, a fabricated inspection report, is left to the general criminal law, and several prosecutions of that character exist in the system's history, but the Act itself treats even flagrant non-compliance as a licensing matter.

This design has a rationale and a cost, and both should be stated plainly. The rationale is that teacher education is a field of permissions, and a permission system policed by invalidity is self-executing in a way a prosecution system is not: no inspector needs to raid a college and no magistrate needs to convict anyone for the sanction to bite, because the sanction operates automatically at recruitment, where the invalid qualification is simply refused. The cost is that the sanction falls with full force on the one party the system says it protects. An institution that runs an unrecognised course collects its fees in the present and faces, at worst, a refusal of future recognition; the student pays in the future, when the qualification fails at a recruitment counter years after the money changed hands, and at that distance the institution's promoters may be unreachable and its corpus dissolved. Deterrence theory would say the Act punishes the victim because the victim is the only reachable party, and the courts' protective doctrines for students admitted in good faith, like the 2019 amendment itself, are best understood as patches laid over that structural defect. The alternative designs were available and were not chosen: a penal provision against the promoters personally, a compensation mechanism for students of defaulting institutions, or a public register maintained in real time so that no student could enrol in ignorance. The register has arrived in incomplete form through the Council's publication of recognition orders, and the others remain unbuilt, which is why the validity question at the heart of this article continues to be asked by individuals rather than answered by the system.

A postscript to the enforcement design concerns the counterfeit end of the market, where the Act's logic is inverted. Periodically, recruitment verification drives by state boards and recruiting commissions have uncovered certificates that are not merely invalid but fabricated: degrees of institutions that never existed, recognition letters forged outright, and eligibility certificates that no testing body issued. The Act has little to say to this stratum, because forgery is not a licensing failure but a crime, and the cases travel under the general penal law of cheating and forgery rather than under anything in these thirty-four sections. The stratum matters to the article's argument for one reason: it exists because the genuine article, a valid qualification, is valuable and its validity is laborious to verify, and every weakness in the register and archive discussed earlier is a subsidy to the forger. The system's long-run answer is the same unglamorous one, a complete and queryable record of recognition and results, and the persistence of the counterfeit market is a rough index of how far that answer still has to travel.

## Teacher qualifications and the eligibility test

The Act's second life begins where its first ends: with the person the institution produced. The bridge is section 23 of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, under which a person possessing the minimum qualifications laid down by an academic authority authorised by the Central Government is eligible for appointment as a teacher, and the full treatment of that provision, including the extension Parliament granted serving teachers in 2017, belongs to [the pillar on the Right to Education Act](/2015/01/01/right-to-education-act-2009-guide/). The Central Government designated the National Council for Teacher Education as that academic authority by notification issued on 25 August 2010, and the Council, exercising both that designation and the power Parliament would confirm in section 12A of this Act, laid down the minimum qualifications for appointment. The qualifications pair the academic and training credentials for each stage of schooling with a condition that transformed Indian teacher recruitment: a pass in the teacher eligibility test, to be conducted by the appropriate government in accordance with the Council's guidelines.

The qualifications themselves form a ladder keyed to the stages of schooling, and a reader should be able to climb it. For the pre-primary and primary stages the ladder's first rung is senior secondary schooling with the two-year elementary diploma, joined, for classes one to five, by the eligibility test's first paper. For the upper primary stage, classes six to eight, the qualification is a bachelor's degree with either the elementary diploma or the Bachelor of Education, joined by the test's second paper. For the secondary stage the ladder requires a bachelor's or postgraduate degree in the school subject with the Bachelor of Education, and for the senior secondary stage a postgraduate degree in the subject with the Bachelor of Education, the test discipline of the elementary statute not extending to those stages. Physical education and the special and inclusive education streams have their own prescribed sequences under the regulations and the notifications. The ladder's logic is cumulative: each stage demands the full academic credential of the level the teacher will face, the professional preparation designed for the age group to be taught, and, where the elementary statute reaches, the screened eligibility that the test certifies. Devesh Sharma, examined below, is best understood as the judiciary policing the ladder's architecture against an administrative shortcut that would have let a higher rung's degree substitute for a lower rung's, on the ground that the rungs are different in kind and not merely in height.

The ladder has one rung built retrospectively, and its construction shows the qualifications system operating at its largest scale. When the 2017 amendment to the school education statute gave untrained serving teachers a final window to acquire the prescribed qualifications, closing on 31 March 2019, the delivery vehicle was an elementary diploma run through open and distance learning by the National Institute of Open Schooling, designed for teachers already standing in classrooms, with study materials, contact programmes and school-based activities substituting for the full-time attendance the ordinary diploma demands. The exercise was the largest single in-service training operation in the system's history, and it exposed the standing tension in the Council's mode rules: the Verma Commission had confined distance education to the continuing development of serving teachers precisely because a first qualification needs supervised practice, and the diploma for the untrained serving teacher sits exactly on that line, a first professional qualification earned by a person whose practice is their own classroom. The system resolved the tension pragmatically, as it had to, since the alternative was the dismissal or disqualification of a significant fraction of the elementary workforce, and the episode belongs in this article because it marks the point at which the qualifications regime stopped being a gate at entry and became, for one cohort, a programme of mass remediation run backwards through the pipeline the Act had built forwards.

The guidelines for conducting the test were issued by the Council in February 2011. They construct a screen rather than a ranking: two papers, one for teachers of classes one to five and one for classes six to eight, testing child pedagogy and the school subjects, with a candidate required to secure sixty per cent to be declared qualified, and eligibility certificates issued to those who clear it. The Central Board of Secondary Education conducted the first central test in 2011, and state tests followed under the same framework. Two design choices deserve emphasis. The test certifies eligibility, not merit: it admits the holder to consideration for appointment and confers no right to a post, a distinction the courts have had to repeat to disappointed candidates. And the pass is a floor laid over the training qualification, not a substitute for it: a candidate with the test but without the prescribed training qualification is as ineligible as one with the training and without the test. The qualifications notifications have themselves been litigated, and one episode fixes the boundary of the Council's power better than any general statement. By a notification of 28 June 2018, the Council recognised the Bachelor of Education as a qualification for appointment as a primary school teacher, subject to the appointee completing a six-month bridge course in elementary education within two years of appointment. The change responded to a real supply problem, graduate teachers were available while elementary diploma holders were scarce in several states, and B.Ed. holders pressed for access to the much larger primary cadre. In Devesh Sharma v. Union of India, decided in August 2023, the Supreme Court quashed the notification. The Court's reasoning went to the design of the qualifications structure: the elementary diploma is the qualification designed and prescribed for the primary stage, its curriculum addressed to the pedagogy of young children, and the Council could not, by notification, treat the secondary-stage degree as interchangeable with it, in a judgment that also drew on the right of the child to be taught by a teacher trained for the child's stage. The episode is a useful counterweight to the usual criticism that the Council's qualifications power is unreviewable: the power is broad, it is exercised by delegated instruments, and it is bounded both by the structure of the Act's own programme map and by judicial review that has shown itself willing to strike the instrument down.

The test's own operation repays a paragraph, because its results are persistently misread as a merit list. The central test and the state tests are criterion screens: the candidate either reaches the qualifying standard or does not, and the certificate records eligibility without rank. In most cycles, on the published results, only a minority of candidates who sit the test qualify, a pass proportion that has made the test itself controversial, with candidates and their associations attributing the rate to paper difficulty and pedagogy sections pitched at a level the training system does not teach to, and the Council and the testing bodies attributing it to the gap between the training the sector delivers and the standard the certificate is meant to certify. Both readings point at the same underlying fact, which the Verma Commission had already supplied: a screen set at the level of competent beginning teaching will fail large numbers of the products of a training system whose median institution does not teach to that level, and the quarrel about the test is, at one remove, the quarrel about the colleges. Attempts are not rationed by the guidelines, and a candidate may sit repeatedly, which converts the test for many into an examination of endurance across cycles as vacancies open and close in their states.

A teacher preparing for a verification or recruitment drive assembles a small dossier, the training certificate, the recognition status of the institution for the years of study, the eligibility test certificate and the appointment papers, and where any element survives only as a slide deck circulated by the institution, [a browser-based presentation viewer for opening such files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) keeps the dossier readable on any machine.

The scale against which this discipline operates is recorded in the school education statute's own history and is worth importing for proportion. When the Ministry of Human Resource Development assessed the serving elementary workforce in 2017, it counted about 66.41 lakh teachers at the elementary level, of whom about eleven lakh remained untrained against the prescribed standard, a figure the companion pillar on the Right to Education Act examines in its place. The eligibility test sits on top of that workforce problem rather than inside it: the untrained teacher's deficit is the training qualification, which the test cannot supply, while the trained teacher's exposure is the test itself. Keeping the two deficits distinct is essential to reading the litigation that follows, because the courts were asked, in case after case, to convert the second deficit into the first kind, a matter of a standard announced after appointment, and the answer they ultimately gave turned on Parliament having announced, in both statutes, that acquisition after appointment was the design.

One administrative feature of the test has shaped its politics and should not pass unnoticed. The test is conducted by the appropriate government: the central test serves the schools of the Central Government and the Union territories and candidates who seek them, and each state conducts its own test for its own recruitment, under the Council's guidelines but on its own calendar and with its own application of the qualifying standard and relaxations. A pass in one state's test is therefore, in the ordinary case, a key cut for that state's lock, portable only where a recruiting state chooses to accept another's certificate, while the central certificate travels to the central schools. The result is a national standard administered as a federation of tests, with candidates in states that hold the test infrequently waiting years for a sitting that candidates elsewhere take annually, an inequality of access to eligibility that the guidelines' uniform floor does nothing to correct and that no court has been asked, in terms, to cure.

The certificate's own validity tells a small story of the system's self-correction. The guidelines fixed the eligibility certificate's life at seven years, which meant a qualified candidate who found no vacancy within the period faced the test again. In 2021 the Ministry of Education extended the validity of the eligibility certificate to the holder's lifetime, with retrospective effect from 2011, so that certificates issued from the first tests onward stand for life. The change removed a genuine hardship, the expiry of a demonstrated competence that had not decayed in any way the test itself measured, and it stands as one of the few episodes in the Act's record in which a rule was relaxed because its rigour served no purpose, rather than because compliance had failed.

## The eligibility test in the courts

Whether the test binds only fresh appointees, or also teachers appointed before the school education statute existed, and whether it conditions promotion as well as entry, produced a decade and a half of litigation in the High Courts, which did not speak with one voice. Several High Courts reasoned from the continuance protection in section 12A of this Act and from the structure of the 2009 Act that the qualifications discipline was prospective: it governed recruitment after the appointed dates, and serving teachers' rights were governed by the service rules under which they were appointed, subject to the training qualifications Parliament had expressly extended deadlines for. Other courts reasoned from the child's right to quality education that a minimum standard which stops at the school gate, and never reaches the teachers already inside, protects the newest cohort of teachers and none of the children taught by the rest. The reliance argument was pressed with real force: a teacher appointed in nineteen ninety-eight under rules that demanded no such test, who had taught for twenty-five years, faced a condition of continuance invented long after appointment, on pain of losing the service itself. The quality argument answered that the standard is not a punishment but a floor, that the 2011 amendment to this Act had already announced that serving teachers must acquire the minimum qualifications within the statutory period, and that elementary education is a fundamental right of the child under Article 21A of the Constitution, against which a teacher's expectation of an unchanging qualification regime carries limited weight.

The promotion limb of the dispute had its own history and deserves separate notice, because it divided even courts that agreed about fresh appointment. A teacher appointed lawfully decades earlier, promoted through grades on the service rules of the time, confronted a rule that the next grade required a test that had not existed at entry; several High Courts treated promotion as a matter internal to the service, governed by the rules under which the teacher was recruited, and struck the condition down as applied to the pre-statute cohort, while others upheld it on the footing that no teacher has a vested right to be promoted unqualified against a standard the law sets for the post. The pattern of decision was therefore not a simple split between strict and lenient benches. It was a disagreement about which legal relationship the test governs: the relationship between the teacher and the employer, fixed at appointment and protected by the continuance logic of section 12A, or the relationship between the teacher and the child, governed by Article 21A and indifferent to when the teacher entered service. The first framing protects reliance; the second protects the classroom; and the statutes, read whole, contain materials for both, which is why the question survived fifteen years of litigation and why its resolution, when it came, had to be a choice between the framings rather than a discovery within them.

The Supreme Court resolved the core of the dispute in Anjuman Ishaat-e-Taleem Trust v. State of Maharashtra, decided on 1 September 2025 by a bench of Justices Dipankar Datta and Manmohan. The Court held that a pass in the teacher eligibility test is a mandatory minimum qualification under section 23 of the school education statute, for appointment and for promotion alike, rooted in the constitutional guarantee of quality education. For teachers in service who were appointed before that statute and have more than five years of service remaining, the Court allowed two years from the judgment to qualify, failing which the teacher would have to quit service or be compulsorily retired with terminal benefits where the qualifying service had been rendered. For teachers with less than five years to superannuation, the Court, exercising its power under Article 142 of the Constitution, permitted continuance to retirement without the test, while withholding promotion from any teacher who had not qualified. Review petitions by states, teachers' associations and individual teachers were dismissed by the same bench on 29 May 2026, the Court holding that section 23 had always contemplated the acquisition of the prescribed qualifications by in-service teachers, and extending the compliance deadline, again under Article 142, to 31 August 2028 as a final date. One question the 2025 bench deliberately did not settle: doubting the correctness of the Constitution Bench decision of 2014 in Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust, which had held the school education statute inapplicable to minority institutions altogether, it referred the applicability of that statute to minority institutions to a larger bench, so the position of teachers in minority institutions under the test discipline stands governed by the 2014 exemption unless and until the reference is answered. The administrative aftermath of the two judgments is part of their meaning and should be recorded. The obligation the Court created is not self-executing: state governments must hold or recognise test cycles at a frequency that gives the affected cohort a real opportunity to qualify within the window, must identify the teachers in each category the judgments create, those within five years of superannuation, those beyond it, those who have already qualified and those who have not, and must carry the terminal-benefit consequences for teachers who fail with the qualifying service rendered, with deficient cases open to departmental consideration on representation, as the judgment itself provides. The review judgment's extension of the deadline to 31 August 2028, expressly as a final date, converts the timetable into the operative fact of the subject for the next recruitment and promotion cycles, and any account of the test's applicability that omits the extension misstates the position as completely as one that omits the mandate. Teachers' associations that sought review and failed have announced the political continuation of the argument, in legislatures and in negotiation with state governments, and the reliance case will be pressed there; but as law, the question the brief for this article flagged as developing has developed, and the position stated here is the position the courts have left standing.

That is the careful statement the subject requires: mandatory for appointment, for promotion and, on the timetable given, for continuance in non-minority schools; the minority-institution question referred and open.

## The courts on recognition: strictness with a protected student

The recognition chapter has its own jurisprudence, older than the test litigation and pointing, at first sight, the other way: toward uncompromising strictness against institutions, moderated only at the point where students would pay. In State of Maharashtra v. Sant Dnyaneshwar Shikshan Shastra Mahavidyalaya, decided in 2006, the Supreme Court held that the Act is a complete code on the recognition of teacher education institutions, that the state government's policy or a university's inclination cannot dilute the norms the Council prescribes, and that recognition by the Council is the precondition on which affiliation and the conduct of courses depend, not a formality to be regularised after the event. In Adarsh Shiksha Mahavidyalaya v. Subhash Rahangdale, decided in 2012, the Court insisted on strict fulfilment of the conditions in sections 14 and 15 read with the regulations, held that no institution may admit students unless it has obtained unconditional recognition and affiliation, and refused to direct that students of unrecognised institutions be permitted to appear in examinations merely because interim orders had let them sit provisionally, a refusal that fixed the rule that judicial sympathy in an interim order creates no equity at the final hearing. In Maa Vaishno Devi Mahila Mahavidyalaya v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in 2013, the Court enforced the recognition calendar itself, declining to extend the statutory and regulatory timelines for institutions that had missed them and treating the schedule, rather than any individual hardship, as the discipline the system runs on.

Beneath the leading cases runs a broad river of writ litigation in the High Courts, and its remedial pattern is worth recording because it is how most institutions actually experience the Act. The commonest petitions are not challenges to the Act but complaints about its administration: recognition refused without adequate reasons, inspection reports withheld from the institution they condemn, applications undecided across a full admission cycle, conditions imposed that the regulations do not contain. The courts' response in this stratum is procedural discipline rather than substantive rescue: orders are quashed for want of reasoning and remanded for fresh decision within a stated period, committees are directed to decide by a date, and institutions are held, in return, strictly to their own defaults of document and deadline. The pattern has a systemic effect that neither side advertises. It makes the regional committees more careful reasoners than they would otherwise be, and it makes the process slower and more lawyered than a licensing system for thousands of small colleges can comfortably bear, so that the appellate and writ tiers together operate as a second, shadow recognition process running months behind the first. When the Verma Commission recommended strengthening the Council's legal and appellate structures, it was this shadow process it had in view: a regulator perpetually in court is a regulator whose standards are being set, case by case, in a forum it does not control.

Within that strictness runs one consistent exception, and it is the doctrine the 2019 Parliament would later borrow. Where recognition is withdrawn or refused, the courts have distinguished sharply between the institution, which takes the full consequence, and the student already admitted in reliance on the institution's apparent authority, whose completed or substantially completed course the courts have repeatedly protected, by permitting examinations, directing declaration of results, or reading the withdrawal as prospective in its effect on that cohort. The student's protection in the case law is not an entitlement to be trained badly; it is an application of the principle that the consequences of a regulatory breach should fall on the breaching party, and that a student cannot inspect a regional committee's file before enrolling. The tension between the two lines, no recognition without strict compliance, and no forfeiture for the innocent student when compliance fails, is the precise tension the retrospective validation amendment had to resolve, and it resolved it by the student's route, at the level of the whole affected class rather than case by case.

A word on how the default was discovered is necessary, because it conditions how much confidence the rest of the record deserves. The unrecognised government courses did not surface through the Council's inspection system, which is the channel the Act built for the purpose. They surfaced through the qualifications' failure at the point of use, when recruiting authorities and universities, applying the validity rule in individual cases, began refusing the products of named government institutions, and the affected students and teachers carried the refusals upward until the pattern was undeniable and undeniable at scale. A regulatory system learns of its largest failure from its victims rather than from its instruments when the instruments were never pointed at the powerful, and the discovery route is therefore evidence in itself, independent of the amendment that followed. It also explains the shape of the cure: because the cases arrived as a class of stranded qualifications rather than as an inspection finding against identified institutions, the remedy was framed for the class, by notification and deeming, rather than through the institution-by-institution discipline of sections 14 to 17, which would have taken years the stranded cohorts did not have.

## The retrospective validations: Parliament cures its own default

The discovery, when it surfaced, had a quality of bureaucratic surrealism. Institutions funded by the Central Government, by state governments and by Union territory administrations, including universities and government training institutions of long standing, had been admitting students to teacher education courses, in some cases for years, without the recognition section 14 requires or the permission section 15 requires. The reasons were administrative rather than mercenary: a course continued under a predecessor arrangement, a new programme began on a vice-chancellor's or a director's approval in the belief that a government institution stood outside the licensing net, an application was never filed because no one in the secretariat owned the task. The legal consequence did not vary with the innocence of the cause. Under section 17, the qualifications of the students who had passed through those courses were not valid qualifications for employment as teachers, and the defect emerged at the worst possible moment, at recruitment, when trained candidates produced certificates the recruiting state was bound to reject, in some cases after appointment, and in the case of serving teachers recruited earlier, under a cloud over qualifications already acted upon.

The National Council for Teacher Education (Amendment) Act, 2019, Act 2 of 2019, carried by a Bill first introduced in Parliament in 2017 and passed by both Houses before its notification on 11 January 2019, is the cure, and its mechanics deserve precision because they define exactly how far the cure extends. The amendment was deemed to have come into force on 17 August 1995, the date of the statutory Council's constitution, so its validation reaches back across the entire life of the recognition regime. It inserted a further proviso into section 14(1) under which institutions specified by the Central Government by notification in the Official Gazette, which are funded by the Central Government or a state government or a Union territory administration, which offered a course or training in teacher education on or after the appointed day up to the academic year 2017-2018, and which fulfil the conditions specified in clause (a) of sub-section (3) of section 14, shall be deemed to have been recognised by the regional committee. A parallel proviso inserted into section 15(1) deems the permission for such courses to have been granted. The Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded the government's case in terms of the students: the courses had been conducted without formal recognition, the qualifications of the students stood invalidated through no fault of theirs, and regularisation was required in their interest. The specification followed by notification on 12 May 2020, when the Ministry of Education issued the Gazette notifications naming the beneficiary institutions; the Ministry recorded that twenty-three institutions of the Central and state governments benefited, involving about thirteen thousand students and about seventeen thousand in-service teachers, whose qualifications stood validated.

The courts have since applied the deeming provisos in the spirit their text announces. Where an institution specified in the notification met the statutory conditions, benches of the High Courts treated the deemed recognition as complete in itself, not as an invitation to re-examine the institution's original default, and the Gauhati High Court held that the deemed recognition, once the conditions are satisfied, operates without a limitation or time bar of the kind the Council had sought to import into the provision. That judicial reception matters to the amendment's meaning. A validating provision is a deliberate legislative reversal of the legal past, and courts construe it, as they construed this one, to give the reversal the full effect the legislature stated, leaving the argument about whether the reversal was wise to the political forum where it belongs. The contrast with the prospective operation of ordinary recognition, maintained strictly in the same years against private institutions, is therefore not judicial inconsistency. It is the courts applying two different legal instruments, an administrative licence and a legislative deeming, each according to its nature, and reserving their strictness for the instrument whose integrity depends on it.

The limits of the device are as instructive as its reach. It is confined to publicly funded institutions; a private institution that ran the identical unrecognised course receives nothing from it and remains subject to refusal, withdrawal and the invalidity of its students' qualifications. It is confined in time, closing with the academic year 2017-2018, and the Ministry stated expressly that the amendment regularises the past and does not leave the door open for institutions to run unrecognised courses in future and seek ex post facto regularisation. And it is conditional: the institution must fulfil the substantive conditions of section 14(3)(a), so the deeming validates institutions that could have been recognised had they applied, rather than institutions whose facilities and staffing could never have passed. The 2019 amendment is therefore not a blanket amnesty. It is a targeted legislative judgment that a defined class of public defaulters, whose students were innocent and whose institutions met the physical standard, should be treated as if the paperwork had been done.

The case for that judgment, at its strongest, is the case the courts themselves had been making one student at a time. The students enrolled at government institutions on the faith of the state's own conduct; the state that ran the institution, the state that affiliated it, and the state that recruited its graduates were arms of the same sovereign that had omitted the recognition application, and a legal order that visited the omission on the student alone would punish the only party without responsibility. The numbers affected, tens of thousands of students and serving teachers on the Ministry's own account, made case-by-case litigation a remedy in name only. The opposing case, at its strongest, is structural, and it was voiced in the parliamentary consideration of the Bill and in the commentary that followed: a recognition regime is a deterrent structure, and a legislature that twice intervenes to validate default, first by the protected transitions scattered through the Act's history and decisively in 2019, teaches every regulated institution the lesson that the consequence of non-compliance, if the non-compliance is wide enough or public enough, is a validating amendment. The asymmetry compounds the lesson, because the validation was available only to the state's own institutions, while private institutions guilty of the same omission, sometimes for the same administrative reasons, continued to face withdrawal and the ruin of their students. Both cases are true, and the article's verdict has to hold them in that order: the validation was justified for the students it rescued, and it was nevertheless a confession. A regulator whose licensing net, after twenty-four years, had failed to catch the government's own training institutions had not been regulating the sector's most important providers at all, and the amendment is the documentary proof, enacted by Parliament itself, that the recognition regime's authority ran against the private sector with a rigour it never applied to the state.

## The quality record and the Verma Commission

Behind the validity dramas stands the question the Act exists to answer: whether statutory regulation raised the quality of teacher education. The honest answer, on the record of the system's own inquiries, is that for most of the Act's life it did not, because the regulator's growth in jurisdiction was outpaced by the sector's growth in size and by the commercial logic that made a teacher education college an attractive investment: low capital cost against other professional education, a captive demand created by eligibility rules, and fees decoupled from any visible measure of training quality. Recognition, designed as a quality floor, operated in boom years as a volume business, with regional committees processing applications at a pace inconsistent with serious inspection, and the courts' strictness in cases like Adarsh Shiksha was provoked by precisely the discovery, repeated across states, of institutions recognised on paper facilities, faculty who existed in affidavits, and libraries assembled for the visiting team's day.

The system's own verdict was delivered by the High-Powered Commission on Teacher Education constituted by the Supreme Court in 2011 under the chairmanship of Justice J. S. Verma, a former Chief Justice of India, which reported in August 2012 under the title Vision of Teacher Education in India: Quality and Regulatory Perspective. The Commission had been appointed against the background of the recognition litigation, and it treated the regulatory failure as a quality failure: the great majority of teacher education provision was in private unaided hands, much of it of poor quality, and the one-year duration of the principal programmes was too short to form a teacher. Its recommendations ran together into a redesign. Teacher education should be located within the higher education system and in multidisciplinary institutional environments rather than in standalone commercial colleges; the duration of the bachelor's and master's programmes in education should be doubled; the first professional degree or diploma should be offered only in the face-to-face mode, with distance education confined to the continuing professional development of serving teachers; entry to programmes should be screened by a transparent test of aptitude and readiness; public investment in institutional capacity should rise substantially, with model institutions to set the standard; and the Council itself should be rebuilt with specialised academic and accreditation structures equal to the inspection and quality-assurance task the Act had assigned it. The Commission also recommended longer, integrated programmes of teacher preparation, of the kind the system would take another decade to launch.

One stratum of the quality problem deserves separate statement because it reproduces itself. Teacher educators are produced by the Master of Education and the research degrees, and the master's programmes sit in the same institutional ecology as the bachelor's: a faculty member for a college of education must hold the prescribed postgraduate and professional qualifications, and in the boom years the demand for teacher educators outran the supply of credible ones, so that the colleges least able to teach employed the teachers least equipped to teach teaching. Inspectors found the same names on the faculty rolls of several institutions, qualifications from the weakest end of the system certifying the next generation of its instructors, and a research culture so thin that the discipline of education struggled for standing inside the universities that housed it. The Verma Commission's answer was structural, to pull teacher education into multidisciplinary universities where education faculty would work alongside the subject disciplines their students must teach, and to lengthen preparation so that teaching itself became an object of study rather than a technique appended to a degree. The integrated programme is that answer in institutional form. Its success will be decided less by its curriculum documents than by whether a generation of teacher educators emerges for whom the education of teachers is a first-choice academic career, a change no regulation can order into being but which every regulation can either invite or repel.

The Council's own reorganisation after the report followed the Commission's institutional advice in form: specialised academic work on curriculum and norms was separated from the assessment and accreditation function, a legal apparatus serviced the appellate load, and the Council began to speak the language of accreditation grades rather than bare recognition. Institutional redesigns of this kind are easy to announce and hard to make real, and the test of this one is the same test the Commission set the whole system: whether the quality of the median institution moved. On that test the evidence available within this article's discipline is indirect but not empty. The duration reform changed what every new teacher studies and for how long; the appraisal and information exercises culled institutions that existed chiefly on paper; and the integrated programme, if it grows from pilot to norm, changes where teachers are formed. Each is a movement in the direction the Commission pointed, and none is the transformation it described, which would have required the closure or conversion of a large part of the standalone sector, a step no government has yet been willing to take against the combined resistance of promoters, students mid-course and the legislators who represent both.

The implementation record is the familiar pattern of this series, of diagnosis outpacing remedy by years. The Council's regulations of 2014, the National Council for Teacher Education (Recognition Norms and Procedure) Regulations, 2014, notified on 28 November 2014, gave effect to the duration recommendation: from the academic session 2015-2016 the Bachelor of Education and the Master of Education became two-year programmes, the elementary diploma settled at two years, school internship lengthened, and the norms for faculty, facilities and curriculum frameworks were recast. The four-year integrated programme the Commission had gestured toward arrived with the National Education Policy of 2020, which set the direction that the minimum qualification for teaching would become the four-year integrated degree, and the Integrated Teacher Education Programme was launched in pilot mode for the academic year 2023-2024 in fifty-seven teacher education institutions, as the Ministry of Education announced, offering dual-major degrees combining a subject bachelor's with education, with admission through a national common entrance test conducted by the National Testing Agency. A candidate entering the integrated programme after school completes in four years what the older route, a subject degree followed by a two-year education degree, takes five to do, and studies the two in combination rather than in sequence. The reform, measured from the Commission's report of August 2012 to the pilot classrooms of 2023, took eleven years to arrive, which is the Verma Commission's truest legacy: it was right early, and the system moved at the speed the system moves.

## The institution's side of the counter: compliance as a cost structure

The brief of the regulated institution deserves its strongest statement too, because the Act's burdens are real and its procedures have not always deserved the obedience they demand. A promoters' society entering teacher education must assemble land of the prescribed extent in the prescribed form of title, construct to norm, endow funds it cannot touch, recruit faculty holding qualifications that are scarce in its district at salaries its fee income may not support, and then submit to a process whose stages, scrutiny, inspection, the committee's consideration, the affiliating university's parallel process, run on timelines the institution does not control and which, if missed by any party, cost it an entire academic year under the calendar doctrine. Legitimate grievances recur through the appellate record: inspection teams that arrive unannounced in form and perfunctory in substance, deficiencies recorded that the institution disputes on the facts, regional committees whose treatment of comparable applications is said, with documentary support, to differ, and recognition orders delayed past the admission season so that a granted recognition is worth a year less than it should be. The Council's appellate tier exists for these cases and does correct errors, but an institution that must litigate to obtain a lawful order has already paid the tax the system was designed to spare it.

The honest institution's deepest complaint, though, is not procedural. It is that the regime's rigour is unevenly distributed in fact, whatever the text says: the college that builds to norm competes for students against the college that rented its library for inspection day, and where the second college's recognition survives, the first college's investment is a competitive disadvantage. Regulatory economics has a name for the equilibrium: when enforcement is episodic, compliance is a cost borne by the honest and evaded by the rest, and the market selects for the evasive. That is the mechanism by which a licensing regime can coexist with a quality collapse, and it is the mechanism the Verma Commission's accreditation and restructuring proposals were aimed at breaking, by making quality visible and continuous rather than inspected and episodic. The 2019 amendment enters this analysis from the side: whatever its justification for the students, it confirmed to every private promoter watching that the most connected class of defaulter in the system is forgiven by statute, and a regime struggling to persuade the private sector that compliance is compulsory could ill afford the demonstration.

## The planning failure: surplus, shortage and the redesign on the table

The first limb of the Act's long title, planned and coordinated development, is the limb that has failed most completely, and its failure explains the sector's geography. A planning regulator would have matched the supply of training places to the projected demand for teachers, stage by stage and state by state, and refused recognition where the pipeline was already full. The Council instead operated, for most of its life, as a standards body processing applications as they came, with the result that training capacity concentrated where land was cheap and demand for credentials was high, not where teacher vacancies were. Several states acquired a large surplus of trained graduates chasing a small annual intake of government posts, with the predictable secondary effects of credential inflation, unemployed trained youth as a political constituency, and pressure on eligibility rules of exactly the kind the 2018 primary notification attempted to relieve; other regions, including much of the north-east, the Verma Commission noted, faced acute shortages of teachers and of training capacity alike. Periodic correctives, seasons in which the Council declined fresh applications from oversupplied states or programmes, treated the symptom without building the forecasting machinery the long title promised, and the exercise of estimating teacher demand remained with the state governments that also operated the largest training institutions, a division of labour in which the planner and a principal producer were the same party.

It is against that record that the redesign proposed by the National Education Policy of 2020 should be read. The policy proposes to recast the governance of higher education around a single overarching commission with distinct verticals for regulation, accreditation, funding and standards, and within that architecture to reconstitute the Council as the professional standard-setting body for teacher education: the setter of the professional standards and curriculum frameworks for the occupation, rather than the licensor of its colleges. The proposal has an evident logic. The licensing function is the one that has consumed the Council and compromised it; the standards function, exercised through the qualifications power, the eligibility test framework and the integrated programme design, is the one at which it has been most consequential, and the Verma Commission's vision of teacher education located inside multidisciplinary universities implies a world in which university accreditation carries much of the quality assurance that institutional licensing attempts. It also has an evident risk, which the Act's history teaches: functions transferred on paper have a way of persisting in fact, the recognition litigation would not vanish with the letterhead, and a standards body without an inspection arm may discover, as the advisory Council discovered before 1993, that standards without a gatekeeper are advice. The redesign, like the commencement of the Act itself, is a promise whose value depends on the machinery built to keep it.

## An honest assessment, and the verdict

What works in this Act should be recorded before what fails. The recognition architecture is coherent and, on paper, complete: a single licensing authority for institutions, course-level permission, an affiliation switch thrown by the same decision, inspection, a graded consequence ladder and a specialist appeal, all in five sections. The qualifications power added in 2011, and the eligibility test built on the 2010 designation, gave India for the first time a national floor under the occupation of school teaching, and after the Supreme Court's judgment of September 2025 that floor extends, on a stated timetable, to the serving profession and to promotion, which is more than most regulatory statutes achieve in a generation. The Act also generated, in the validity jurisprudence, a working doctrine of student protection that other regulatory fields would benefit from copying.

The Act also has to be read as one half of a couple, because its modern significance is unintelligible alone. The school education statute of 2009 created the demand side: a justiciable right to be taught, by a teacher holding prescribed qualifications, in a school meeting prescribed norms. This Act supplies the qualifications authority that statute designates, the training pipeline that produces the qualified, and the licensing floor beneath the pipeline. The couple's history shows a consistent division of labour in failure and repair. When the standard outran the system's capacity, it was the legislature that moved, extending the training deadline for serving teachers in 2017 to the 31 March 2019 date the companion pillar records, and validating the government's own unrecognised institutions in the amendment of the same period. When the question was whether the standard binds those already inside, it was the judiciary that moved, in 2025 and again on review in 2026, holding the line the legislature had drawn and softening only its timetable. A reader who wants to know whether an Indian statutory standard is real should watch that alternation: standards in this field are set by Parliament, postponed by Parliament, and finally enforced, if they are enforced, by courts applying the text Parliament declined to repeal. The NCTE Act has passed through all three stages, which is more than can be said for most of the statutes in this series, and its qualifications discipline operates as law in action in a sense its recognition discipline, against the state's own institutions, never was.

One duty in section 12 deserves a closing note because its non-performance is so complete: the duty to prevent the commercialisation of teacher education. The Act states the duty and supplies no instrument for it. It does not fix fees, cap charges, audit the finances of trusts that run colleges beyond the recognition conditions, or touch the capitation practices that the general law on unaided professional institutions addresses through other channels, including the fee committees the states constituted under the Supreme Court's framework for unaided institutions. A duty without an instrument is a sentiment, and the sentiment's presence in the statute has arguably done harm, by allowing every actor to claim the Act's authority against commercialisation while the commercial logic of the sector ran on untouched by anything in its thirty-four sections. The commercialisation the Verma Commission documented was not a deviation from the system's incentives; it was the incentives, and the Act that named the vice in its functions chapter never built the vice a remedy. Future amendment drafting in this field should take the lesson generally: a function clause is not a power, and a Parliament that wishes a thing prevented must say by whom, by what means, and at whose cost.

What does not work is equally plain. A licensing regime whose inspection arm cannot keep pace with the sector licenses the paper institution and misses the physical one, and the proliferation of substandard colleges under a statutory regulator is the central fact of the Act's first two decades, documented by the regulator's own commission. The recognition decisions of the regional committees have been inconsistent enough to sustain a permanent appellate and writ docket, and opacity in those decisions is a standing grievance among institutions and applicants alike. The deepest failure, though, is the one the series thesis names: this is not a statute that slept uncommenced, or rules that were never made. It is a statute in full force whose own proprietor, the state, was among its defaulters, and whose default was cured not by the regulator's enforcement but by Parliament's eraser. A teacher education system takes its real standards from what happens to those who ignore them. On the evidence of the 2019 amendment, what happens, if the defaulter is the government itself, is validation.

Two comparative observations sharpen the assessment. The first concerns the sanction. The Act's choice of invalidity over penalty has one underappreciated virtue: it cannot be bought. A fine for running an unrecognised course would be priced into fees within a season, as fines are in every regulated trade, while a qualification that fails at recruitment cannot be discounted by anyone, which is why the system's real discipline, where it has operated, has operated through students and parents learning to ask for the recognition order before paying. The market signal the sector lacked was, in the end, manufactured by the sanction itself, in the crude form of fear, and the steady spread of recognition verification into admission practice is the Act working as designed, a generation late. The second observation concerns the state's double position. Every regulatory statute in this series assigns the state the roles of rule-maker and enforcer; this one also made it the largest regulated party, the operator of the district institutes and government colleges whose compliance the 2019 amendment had to deem. Regulatory systems survive their subjects' defaults; the question is whether they survive their sovereign's. This one did, at the price of a public confession in statutory form, and the confession, quoted in every subsequent argument about the regime's fairness, is a permanent part of the Act's meaning.

What repair would look like can be stated from the article's own evidence, and it is mostly unglamorous. The register comes first: a complete, public, course-wise and session-wise record of every recognition and permission order since 1995, digitised from the regional committees' files, against which a student, a university or a recruiting board can verify a qualification in minutes, would do more for validity than any new offence, because it would move the discovery of default from the recruitment counter to the admission desk, where it belongs. Inspection comes second: a standing, professional inspectorate, or an accreditation system with consequences that bite between inspections, sized to the sector's actual population of institutions rather than to the establishment the Council happens to have. Third, a sanction that reaches the defaulter: a provision visiting the promoters of an institution that admits students without recognition with personal consequences, and a compensation route for the students it took fees from, so that the Act's cost ledger stops balancing itself on the innocent. Fourth, symmetry: the government institution brought inside the same calendar, the same inspection and the same published orders as the private one, in the same season, so that the next default by the state is discovered by the regulator rather than confessed by a ministry. None of this requires a new statute. All of it requires the state to fund and staff the machine it built in 1993, which is the sentence with which every honest account of this Act must end. The machine's design has been vindicated too often, by the courts that enforced it and by the Commission that rebuilt its academic programme, for its failures to be laid at the blueprint's door; they belong to the decades in which the blueprint was treated as the building.

The verdict follows the evidence. The NCTE Act built the indispensable skeleton of a national teacher education system and endowed it, by 2012, with every power the subject requires: licensing of institutions, norms of quality, command of recruitment qualifications and a national eligibility screen. Its failure has never been a failure of design. It has been a failure of the regulator's capacity and the state's example, and until inspection is real, recognition decisions are consistent and public institutions are held to the standard in the same season it is applied to private ones, the Act will continue to be most famous for the two occasions on which its own Parliament certified that the standard had not been met. The cluster's companion pieces take the story forward: the school education statute that made the Council's qualifications decisive for appointment is treated in its own pillar, the federal structure within which the whole settlement sits in the federalism analysis, and the study treatment gathers the cluster for examination purposes in [the education laws exam guide](/2015/05/15/education-laws-exam-guide/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the National Council for Teacher Education regulate?

The Council regulates the teacher education system: the institutions that train teachers, the courses they may run, the norms of land, building, faculty and curriculum those courses must meet, and, since the amendment of 2011, the qualifications of persons recruited as teachers in schools from the pre-primary to the senior secondary stage. Its regulation works through recognition of institutions under section 14, permission for new courses under section 15, inspection under section 13, and withdrawal of recognition under section 17. It does not appoint teachers, run schools, or award degrees; universities affiliate and examine, governments recruit, and the Council licenses the training on which both depend.

### Q: How does a teacher training institution get recognition?

The institution applies to the regional committee for its region in the form the regulations prescribe. The committee calls for particulars, causes the institution to be inspected by a visiting team, and must satisfy itself that the institution has adequate financial resources, accommodation, library, qualified staff, laboratory facilities and the other features needed to run the course properly. If satisfied, it grants recognition by order, subject to conditions in the regulations, and the examining body then grants affiliation. If not satisfied, it refuses by a reasoned order. Recognition is course-specific, and an appeal against refusal lies to the Council under section 18 within sixty days.

### Q: What is the Teacher Eligibility Test and who must pass it?

The Teacher Eligibility Test is a qualifying screen laid down within the minimum qualifications for appointment as a school teacher. The Central Government designated the Council as the academic authority under the Right to Education Act in August 2010, and the Council's guidelines of February 2011 set the framework: separate papers for the primary and upper primary stages, a pass mark of sixty per cent, and an eligibility certificate for those who clear it. Every candidate for appointment as a teacher in classes one to eight must hold the prescribed training qualification and the test pass. After the Supreme Court's judgment of September 2025, the pass is also a condition for promotion and, on a stated timetable, for continuance in service.

### Q: Why did Parliament grant retrospective recognition to teacher training institutions?

Because institutions funded by the Central Government, state governments and Union territory administrations had run teacher education courses without the recognition the Act requires, and the students who passed through them held qualifications that were invalid for employment through no fault of their own. The Amendment Act of 2019, deemed in force from 17 August 1995, inserted provisos into sections 14 and 15 deeming such institutions recognised and their courses permitted, for courses offered up to the academic year 2017-2018, provided the institutions met the substantive conditions of section 14. The Ministry of Education recorded twenty-three beneficiary institutions, about thirteen thousand students and about seventeen thousand in-service teachers.

### Q: What did the Justice Verma Commission say about teacher education?

The High-Powered Commission on Teacher Education, chaired by Justice J. S. Verma and reporting to the Supreme Court in August 2012 in Vision of Teacher Education in India: Quality and Regulatory Perspective, found that teacher education had expanded massively in private unaided hands with poor quality control, and that a one-year programme was too short to form a teacher. It recommended locating teacher education inside higher education and multidisciplinary institutions, doubling the duration of the bachelor's and master's programmes, confining the first professional qualification to the face-to-face mode, screening entry by a transparent test, raising public investment, and rebuilding the Council with specialised academic and accreditation structures.

### Q: What is the four year integrated teacher education programme?

The Integrated Teacher Education Programme is a four-year dual-major degree that combines a subject bachelor's degree, in arts, science or commerce, with the professional education degree, entered directly after school and preparing teachers for the stages of the restructured school system. It answers the Verma Commission's call for longer, integrated preparation located in multidisciplinary institutions, and reflects the National Education Policy of 2020, which set the direction that the four-year integrated degree become the minimum qualification for teaching. The Council launched the programme in pilot mode for the academic year 2023-2024 in fifty-seven teacher education institutions, with admission through a national common entrance test conducted by the National Testing Agency.

### Q: Can recognition of a teacher training college be withdrawn?

Yes. Under section 17, where a regional committee is satisfied, after inquiry and after giving the institution a reasonable opportunity of being heard, that a recognised institution has contravened the Act, the rules, the regulations or any condition of its recognition, it may withdraw recognition by a reasoned order. Withdrawal takes effect from the end of the academic session next following the communication of the order, so a course in progress is not extinguished mid-stream, and affiliation falls away in step. The institution may appeal to the Council under section 18. The gravest consequence falls on qualifications: one obtained from an institution after recognition ceased to hold is not valid for employment.

### Q: Why are so many teacher training colleges criticised?

Because a large part of the sector grew as a credential business rather than as education. Colleges were established with minimal land, faculty who existed mainly on paper, libraries and laboratories assembled for inspection day, and fees charged for a qualification whose entire value lay in the eligibility it conferred for teacher recruitment. Recognition, designed as a quality floor, was granted at volumes and speeds inconsistent with serious inspection, a failure the Verma Commission documented in 2012 and the Supreme Court's strictness in the recognition cases answered. The criticism is not of teacher education as such but of a licensing regime that for two decades certified the premises instead of the training.

### Q: What is the difference between NCTE recognition and university affiliation?

They are separate approvals from separate authorities, and neither substitutes for the other. Recognition is the Council's licence under section 14 of the NCTE Act, granted by the regional committee, permitting the institution to conduct the named teacher education course. Affiliation is the examining body's act of attaching the institution to itself so that its students may sit the university's examinations and receive its degree. Section 16 ties them in sequence: no examining body may grant affiliation or hold an examination for a course unless the institution has first obtained recognition or permission. A college affiliated but unrecognised produces degrees the law refuses to treat as valid qualifications for teacher employment.

### Q: What qualifications does a person need to become a school teacher in India?

The person must possess the minimum qualifications laid down by the academic authority the Central Government designates under the Right to Education Act, which is the Council, and confirmed for school recruitment by section 12A of the NCTE Act. In substance the qualifications combine the academic credential for the stage, the prescribed teacher training qualification for that stage, the elementary diploma for the primary stage, the Bachelor of Education for the upper primary and secondary stages and the Master's route for senior secondary, and, for classes one to eight, a pass in the Teacher Eligibility Test conducted under the Council's guidelines. Appointment rules of the recruiting government must conform to that floor and cannot lawfully dilute it.

### Q: What happens if a student joins an institution that has no NCTE recognition?

The student carries the loss. Section 17A bars an institution from admitting any student to a teacher education course unless it has first obtained recognition or permission, and section 17 provides that a qualification obtained from an unrecognised institution is not a valid qualification for employment under the government or in aided institutions. The courts have refused to let students of unrecognised institutions sit examinations on the strength of interim orders, following Adarsh Shiksha Mahavidyalaya. The protection the case law gives is to students caught by a withdrawal after admission in good faith, not to enrolment in an institution that never held recognition, which is why verifying the recognition order for the exact course and years matters before admission.

### Q: How can an institution appeal against a refusal or withdrawal of recognition?

Section 18 gives any person aggrieved by an order of a regional committee under sections 14, 15 or 17 an appeal to the Council, to be preferred within sixty days of the order. The Council may entertain an appeal after that period if satisfied that sufficient cause prevented a timely filing. The appeal is heard by the Council's appellate machinery in Delhi, which can confirm, modify or set aside the committee's order, and the institution may place fresh material on inspection findings, faculty, land and finance before it. If the appeal fails, the remaining remedy is the writ jurisdiction of the High Court, where the courts test the decision for legality and procedural fairness rather than re-inspecting the institution.

### Q: What did the NCTE Amendment Act of 2011 change?

The Amendment Act of 2011, Act 18 of 2011, in force from 1 June 2012, carried the Act from the training institution into the school. It inserted definitions of school and local authority covering government, aided and unaided schools from pre-primary to senior secondary, and inserted section 12A, empowering the Council to determine by regulations the qualifications of persons for recruitment as teachers in such schools. Its provisos protected the continuance of teachers already recruited under the earlier rules while requiring them to acquire the new minimum qualifications within the period specified in the Act or under the Right to Education Act. It granted no recognition to any institution; retrospective recognition came only in 2019.

### Q: Who sits on the National Council for Teacher Education?

Section 3 builds the Council from the constituencies of the system it regulates. The Central Government appoints the Chairperson, the Vice-Chairperson and the Member-Secretary. Ex officio members come from the Union education administration and the national educational bodies. Thirteen members are persons with experience and knowledge in education or teaching, nine represent the states and Union territories by rotation, three are members of Parliament, two from the House of the People and one from the Council of States, and three are appointed from among teachers of primary and secondary schools and teachers of recognised institutions. An Executive Committee conducts the Council's business between meetings. The design is representative, so the states whose institutions are regulated hold seats inside the regulator.

### Q: What do the four regional committees of the NCTE do?

The regional committees are the Council's working tier under section 20, established for the Eastern, Western, Northern and Southern regions, with offices at Bhubaneswar, Bhopal, Jaipur and Bangalore. Within their regions they perform the functions that decide individual cases: receiving applications for recognition under section 14, arranging inspection of institutions, granting or refusing recognition, granting permission for new courses and increased intake under section 15, and withdrawing recognition under section 17 after inquiry and hearing. The Council at the centre frames the norms they apply, hears appeals from their orders under section 18, and may terminate and reconstitute a committee under section 21. Most institutions meet the Act through their regional committee, not through Delhi.

### Q: When did the B.Ed become a two year programme and why?

From the academic session 2015-2016, under the Council's Recognition Norms and Procedure Regulations of 2014, notified on 28 November 2014. The change implemented the Verma Commission's central academic recommendation of 2012: a one-year programme could not carry the subject study, pedagogy, school internship and reflective practice that competent teaching requires, and had degenerated in much of the sector into an examination exercise. The two-year structure lengthened school internship, spread the curriculum across four semesters, and applied to the Master of Education as well, which also moved from one year to two. The Bachelor of Elementary Education had always been a four-year programme and the elementary diploma was settled at two years by the same regulations.

### Q: Can a teaching qualification earned through distance education be valid?

It depends on the programme and the holder's status. The Verma Commission recommended, and the Council's regulations reflect, that the first professional qualification in teacher education should be earned in the face-to-face mode, because classroom craft cannot be formed without supervised school practice. Distance and open learning routes are recognised for the continuing professional development of serving teachers, and a two-year Bachelor of Education through open and distance learning is permitted for in-service teachers who meet the prescribed experience and posting conditions, conducted under the Council's norms with study centres and practice requirements. A distance-mode first qualification obtained outside the programmes the regulations recognise does not become valid by being labelled a degree; the recognition of the specific programme is what decides.

### Q: Does the NCTE Act apply to minority institutions?

The recognition regime of the NCTE Act applies to teacher education institutions generally, including those established and administered by minorities, because recognition is a standards measure governing the validity of qualifications, and the Supreme Court's recognition jurisprudence has applied the Act across institutional types. A distinct question concerns the Right to Education Act, whose qualification and eligibility test discipline the Council's designation feeds: the Constitution Bench in Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust held in 2014 that the school education statute does not apply to minority institutions, and the bench in Anjuman Ishaat-e-Taleem Trust in 2025 doubted that holding and referred the question to a larger bench. Unless that reference changes the position, the eligibility test discipline does not run in minority institutions, while recognition of their training institutions under this Act continues.

### Q: How does the NCTE inspect teacher education institutions?

Section 13 empowers the Council, for ascertaining whether recognised institutions are functioning in accordance with the Act, to cause an inspection of any such institution by such persons as it may direct. In operation, inspections are conducted by visiting teams drawn from teacher education academics, which examine the land and building, the library and laboratories, the faculty in position against the approved staff pattern, the financial position and the conduct of the programme, and report to the regional committee. Inspection reports feed the decisions to grant, continue or withdraw recognition, and institutions are required to furnish the information the team seeks. The system's acknowledged weakness is capacity: the volume of institutions against the supply of credible visiting teams has kept inspection episodic rather than continuous.

### Q: Is the Teacher Eligibility Test certificate valid for life?

It is, following the change of 2021. The Council's original guidelines fixed the eligibility certificate's validity at seven years from the result, so a candidate who found no vacancy in that period faced the test again. In 2021 the Ministry of Education extended the validity of the eligibility certificate to the holder's lifetime, with retrospective effect from 2011, so certificates issued from the first tests onward remain valid for life. The change removed an expiry that measured nothing about the holder's continuing competence, since the test certifies a threshold of eligibility for appointment rather than a licence that lapses. State tests conducted under the same framework followed the same extension for their certificates.
