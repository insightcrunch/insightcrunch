On the evening of May 1, 1945, Grand Admiral Karl Dönitz stood before his staff at Plön, in the far north of Germany, and announced that he had become head of the German state, the man who would have to negotiate Germany's surrender. Adolf Hitler had shot himself in the Führerbunker the previous afternoon, an event reconstructed in this series' account of [Hitler's suicide on April 30](/2006/01/02/hitler-suicide-april-30-1945-fuhrerbunker/), and Hitler's final testament had named Dönitz, a naval officer with no party leadership experience, as Reich president and supreme commander of the armed forces. The territory Dönitz claimed to govern was a shrinking scatter of pockets: Norway, Denmark, the Netherlands, parts of Czechoslovakia and Austria, and fragments of Germany itself. Some five million Germans were still in uniform, though most were disorganized and many were simply trying to walk westward. Every Allied front was advancing, and no German resistance remained that could be called organized. Dönitz had inherited a catastrophe, and his first decision as head of state was to ask whether he could bargain his way out of a portion of it.

The German surrender that followed over the next seven days was the most compressed high-stakes negotiation of the European war, and it tested the Allied coalition at the moment of its greatest temptation to divide. The decisions made between May 1 and May 8 would determine not only how the fighting stopped but on what terms the victors would govern the peace, and every party to the negotiation understood that the form of the surrender would shape the postwar order as decisively as any battle.

The question that consumed the first week of May 1945 was whether Germany could surrender to some of its enemies while continuing to fight the others. Dönitz wanted exactly that: a surrender to the Western Allies that would let German soldiers and civilians flee westward while a rear guard held off the Red Army. It was a rational plan from the perspective of the men and women it was meant to save, and it was completely unacceptable to the coalition Germany had been fighting. Eisenhower, backed by an unconditional surrender policy that the Allies had maintained since Casablanca, insisted on surrender on all fronts simultaneously, and he threatened to close the Western Front to German refugees if Dönitz refused. What followed was a partial surrender to Montgomery on May 4, the refusal of a separate peace at Reims, the signing of unconditional surrender at 2:41 in the morning on May 7, a Soviet demand for a second ceremony, and a final signing in Berlin at 11:45 p.m. on May 8, three quarters of an hour after the ceasefire had taken effect.

This article reconstructs the decision sequence from May 1 through May 8, 1945, that produced the German unconditional surrender, and it holds the house thesis at maximum intensity: the surrender is the decisive demonstration of Allied committee architecture operating at its highest level of wartime coordination. Anglo-American-Soviet agreement on unconditional surrender, reached at Casablanca and maintained through Tehran and Yalta, held firm against Dönitz's explicit attempt to exploit the differences between the coalition partners. Eisenhower's refusal of a separate surrender preserved the coalition. The dual signing compromise, Reims plus Berlin, accommodated Soviet prestige while keeping the legal instrument intact. On the Axis side, no equivalent political transition existed, because the command architecture that had sustained the war effort died with Hitler; Dönitz could negotiate an end to hostilities precisely because there was no longer anything left to coordinate.

## The Succession: A Naval Officer Inherits a Ruin

Hitler's final testament, dictated in the bunker on April 29 and confirmed as operative after his suicide on April 30, was an extraordinary document. It expelled Hermann Göring and Heinrich Himmler from the party, both of whom had made separate peace approaches in the war's final weeks, and it named Dönitz as Reich president and commander-in-chief of the armed forces. Joseph Goebbels was named Reich chancellor, but Goebbels killed himself on May 1, leaving Dönitz the sole holder of executive authority. The choice of Dönitz has been endlessly debated. Beevor, in Berlin: The Downfall 1945, treats it as Hitler's final punishment of the party leadership that had failed him, elevating a man whose loyalty to the regime had never wavered and whose naval command had fought to the end without internal dissent. Steinert, in Capitulation 1945: The Story of the Dönitz Regime, argues the selection reflected Dönitz's standing as the one senior commander Hitler still trusted after the bunker purges, combined with the practical fact that the navy was the only service still functioning as an institution.

Dönitz's first public act as head of state was a radio broadcast on the evening of May 1, and its contents reveal the political strategy he would pursue for the next week. The broadcast announced Hitler's death, describing it as a hero's death in battle at the head of his troops, a fabrication that preserved the Führer myth even as the regime collapsed. It proclaimed Dönitz's succession and called on the German people and armed forces to continue the struggle, with particular emphasis on the fight against Bolshevism. The broadcast's true audience was not the German public, which could see the reality of defeat, but the Western Allies: Dönitz was positioning himself as the anti-communist German leader with whom the West might do business. The strategy failed, as the Reims negotiations demonstrated, but the broadcast is essential evidence of Dönitz's intentions. He did not present himself as the liquidator of a lost war but as the continuator of a struggle whose direction he proposed to change.

Whatever the motive, the legal basis of Dönitz's authority was a dead dictator's will, proclaimed over a state that no longer existed in any administrative sense. Dönitz established his government at Flensburg, near the Danish border, with a rump cabinet that included Count Schwerin von Krosigk as the leading minister and Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel and General Alfred Jodl for the armed forces high command. The "government" controlled no functioning ministries, collected no taxes, and exercised no civil authority beyond the territory its remaining troops physically occupied. Its one real asset was the Wehrmacht's surviving chain of command, which was still intact enough to order five million men to lay down their arms. That asset made the Flensburg government useful to the Allies as an instrument of surrender, and it made it dangerous as a potential instrument of continued resistance. Everything Dönitz did in his three weeks of nominal power was shaped by that tension.

The succession question that historians have contested, and that Kershaw's 2011 The End: Hitler's Germany, 1944-1945 examines strictly as post-publication research apparatus rather than as a source for these events, concerns how seriously the Allies took Dönitz's legal standing. The Western Allies never recognized the Flensburg government as the legal government of Germany, but they negotiated with its envoys, accepted its military surrender, and allowed it to continue issuing orders to German forces for three weeks after the fighting stopped. That practical arrangement was the precondition for an orderly end to hostilities, and it created the legal ambiguity that the complication section of this article addresses in full.

## Hitler's Final Testament: Anatomy of a Succession

The document that made Dönitz head of state deserves close attention, because its contents shaped everything that followed. Dictated in the Führerbunker on April 29, 1945, and divided into a political testament and a personal will, it was Hitler's last attempt to control events beyond his own death. The political testament expelled Göring and Himmler from the party and from all their offices, on the grounds that their unauthorized peace feelers constituted treason. It appointed Dönitz as Reich president and supreme commander of the armed forces, Goebbels as Reich chancellor, and named a full cabinet of party loyalists to the remaining ministerial posts. The personal will dealt with Hitler's marriage to Eva Braun, his suicide, and the disposition of his property. Three copies were dispatched from the bunker by couriers; at least one reached Dönitz.

The testament's choice of Dönitz has generated several competing explanations, and the honest historical position is that more than one of them is true. The punitive explanation, emphasized by Beevor, holds that Hitler was settling accounts: Göring had requested the succession on April 23 and been arrested for it, Himmler had negotiated with Count Bernadotte of the Swedish Red Cross, and both men had demonstrated the disloyalty Hitler had always feared from his paladins. Elevating Dönitz, a man without a party power base, was a final humiliation of the party hierarchy. The practical explanation, developed by Steinert, holds that Dönitz was simply the last senior commander Hitler trusted, the head of the only service still functioning, and the officer physically positioned in the north where the remaining German territory lay. The ideological explanation notes that Dönitz had never wavered in his National Socialist commitment, had enforced ideological discipline in the navy, and represented the regime's values more purely than the compromised party bosses.

What the testament did not do is equally significant. It made no provision for negotiating with the Allies, because Hitler to the end rejected any outcome short of victory or destruction. It made no acknowledgment that the war was lost, instead blaming the German people's failure and the betrayal of the leadership for the catastrophe. It offered no political program for the successor beyond continued resistance. Dönitz thus inherited an office whose founding document commanded him to fight on, and his first substantive decision was to disobey that command by seeking surrender. The irony is sharp: the testament's authority was the only basis for Dönitz's power, and the exercise of that power immediately contradicted the testament's intent. This contradiction is the root of the legitimacy problem addressed in the complication section: Dönitz ruled by Hitler's will and against Hitler's will simultaneously.

The couriers who carried the testament out of Berlin deserve a brief note, because their missions illustrate the regime's final communications. One copy went by air, one by land, through a city already encircled by Soviet forces. That any copy reached its destination is remarkable; that the succession it ordered was accepted without challenge by the remaining German command structure is a measure of how completely the Wehrmacht's obedience had been conditioned to formal authority, even authority derived from a suicide's dictated will. No general questioned whether a bunker testament constituted a valid constitutional instrument. The chain of command accepted Dönitz because the chain of command accepted orders, and that acceptance was the precondition for everything that followed in May.

## The Strategic Position: Five Million Men and Nowhere to Go

Dönitz's situation on May 1 must be understood in concrete military terms. Germany still held Norway, Denmark, the Netherlands, the Channel Islands, parts of Czechoslovakia, pockets of Austria, the Courland peninsula in Latvia, and scattered positions across northern Germany. Roughly five million Germans were still in uniform. These figures were misleading in the way all late-war German strength figures were misleading. The formations in Norway and Denmark were intact but cut off. The forces in Czechoslovakia were being ground down by the Soviet advance. The troops in northern Germany were a mixture of exhausted combat units, naval personnel pressed into infantry roles, Hitler Youth detachments, and old men of the Volkssturm. None of them could mount a coordinated defense, and all of them were being pressed by Allied forces that had no intention of stopping.

The decisive political fact was the direction of flight. German soldiers and civilians were moving west in vast numbers, desperate to surrender to the Americans and British rather than to the Soviets. The reasons were not abstract. The Red Army's advance through eastern Germany had been accompanied by mass reprisals against German civilians, and every German soldier who had served on the Eastern Front knew what captivity under the Soviets meant. Dönitz's strategic calculation was shaped entirely by this reality. His objective was not to save the Reich, which was gone, but to save as many Germans as possible from Soviet captivity by moving them westward behind a continued Eastern Front defense while ceasing hostilities in the west. It was a humane objective pursued through a strategic impossibility, because it required the Western Allies to become, in effect, accomplices in evading the eastern half of the coalition that had defeated Germany.

The Allied position, by contrast, was unified on the one point that mattered. Since the Casablanca conference in January 1943, the Anglo-American position on Germany had been unconditional surrender, and the Soviet Union had concurred in the principle through every subsequent conference. No separate peace was possible, and the Allies had structured their entire endgame around that commitment. Eisenhower's headquarters understood that any concession to Dönitz on the eastern question would fracture the coalition at the moment of victory and hand Stalin a legitimate grievance that could poison the postwar settlement. The firmness that followed was not cruelty toward fleeing Germans; it was the maintenance of a coalition commitment that had been the foundation of Allied strategy for two years.

## The German Pockets: Norway to Courland

The five million Germans still in uniform on May 1, 1945, were distributed across a map that bore little resemblance to the Reich of 1942. Understanding where they stood is essential to understanding what Dönitz could and could not order them to do. The largest intact formation was in Norway, where some 350,000 German troops remained under General Franz Böhme. Norway had been a strategic backwater for most of the war, garrisoned heavily against an Allied invasion that never came, and its garrison was well supplied, well organized, and completely cut off. It could neither affect the fighting in Germany nor be supplied from Germany. Its surrender, when it came, was a matter of transmitting orders, not of military decision.

Denmark held a smaller but significant garrison, and the Netherlands held German forces that had been bypassed in the Allied advance. The Channel Islands, those oddly persistent outposts, still flew the swastika and would do so until the surrender's implementation reached them. In the east, the Courland pocket in Latvia held the remnants of Army Group North, some 200,000 men trapped against the Baltic since the previous autumn, supplied fitfully by sea and incapable of breaking out. In Czechoslovakia, Army Group Center under Field Marshal Ferdinand Schörner still fielded substantial forces, perhaps 600,000 men, and Schörner was among the most fanatical of the remaining German commanders, issuing draconian orders against any sign of capitulation. In Austria and southern Germany, scattered army and Waffen-SS formations were dissolving into the Alpine terrain. In northern Germany itself, the forces facing Montgomery and the Americans were a heterogeneous mass of Wehrmacht remnants, naval infantry, Volkssturm militia, and Hitler Youth.

This distribution dictated the tempo of the surrender's implementation. The forces in Norway and Denmark could be ordered to stand down by radio and would obey. The Courland pocket required naval coordination. Schörner's Army Group Center was the dangerous case: a large, still-cohesive force under a commander ideologically committed to continued resistance, positioned in Czechoslovakia where Soviet forces were advancing. Schörner had spent the final weeks issuing orders that threatened execution for any sign of defeatism, and there was genuine doubt in Flensburg whether he would obey a surrender he regarded as betrayal. The OKW directives that followed the Reims signing had to reach Schörner and be obeyed by him, and the compliance was secured by a combination of Dönitz's direct radio orders, Keitel's personal intervention through the chain of command, and the manifest hopelessness of the position once the ceasefire hour passed. Scattered fighting in Czechoslovakia nevertheless continued for several days after May 8, as local commanders who had not received the orders, or who chose to disregard them, fought on in the confusion. The Prague uprising and its suppression overlapped with the surrender's implementation, creating a final spasm of violence that the ceasefire was meant to prevent but could not entirely contain. By the second week of May, even Schörner's command had dissolved into the general capitulation. The surrender's legal simultaneity was thus an achievement of command and communication, not a mere formality: five million men in a dozen disconnected pockets had to receive, believe, and obey the same order within roughly forty-eight hours.

The civilian dimension of the pockets compounded the military problem. Millions of German civilians were in flight from the east, clogging the roads of northern Germany, Denmark, and Czechoslovakia. The naval evacuation from the eastern Baltic, Operation Hannibal, had moved vast numbers of civilians and wounded soldiers westward by sea through the spring, and Dönitz, as a naval officer, regarded the continuation of these evacuations as a sacred obligation. His orders in the first days of May repeatedly emphasized that the navy's priority was the rescue of eastern refugees, and U-boat commanders received instructions that reflected this humanitarian mission layered atop a lost war. The tension between the military requirement to surrender and the humanitarian impulse to keep evacuating was one Dönitz never fully resolved; the ceasefire necessarily ended the organized evacuations, and the refugees still in the east when the order came were left to their fate.

## Lüneburg Heath: The Partial Surrender of May 4

Dönitz's first move was directed at Field Marshal Montgomery, whose 21st Army Group held the northern German front. On May 3, Dönitz sent Admiral Hans-Georg von Friedeburg, the commander-in-chief of the Kriegsmarine and his most trusted naval subordinate, to Montgomery's headquarters on Lüneburg Heath with a proposal. Friedeburg offered the surrender of all German forces in the Netherlands, Denmark, and northwest Germany to Montgomery alone. The proposal was carefully limited. It covered the forces facing the British and Canadians, and it left open the question of the Eastern Front, where Dönitz still hoped to maintain a fighting withdrawal.

Montgomery accepted the partial surrender, and the instrument was signed on May 4, taking effect the following morning. Approximately one million German troops laid down their arms under its terms. The acceptance was militarily sensible for the British: it ended fighting on Montgomery's front immediately and brought a million prisoners into custody without further casualties. But it was politically bounded. Montgomery's acceptance was explicitly a local, tactical surrender, not a general capitulation, and it did not commit the Western Allies to anything beyond their own front. Ziemke's operational analysis in Battle for Berlin emphasizes that the Lüneburg Heath surrender was possible precisely because it was framed as a battlefield instrument, which let Montgomery act within his military authority without touching the political question of Germany's overall surrender.

The significance of Lüneburg Heath lies in what it established as precedent. Friedeburg had demonstrated that Dönitz would deal, and Montgomery had demonstrated that the Western Allies would accept surrender only on terms that did not prejudice the wider settlement. Eisenhower, watching from Reims, drew the immediate conclusion that the partial-surrender channel had to be closed before it became a pattern. He directed that Friedeburg be sent onward to Supreme Headquarters Allied Expeditionary Force at Reims for broader discussions, and he made clear that the next negotiation would not be about one front. The stage was set for the confrontation that would define the surrender.

## The Radio War: Dönitz's Orders and the Eastern Flight

Between May 1 and May 7, Dönitz used the surviving German military communications network to pursue two objectives simultaneously: negotiating with the West and sustaining the eastward shield for as long as possible. His radio orders to the eastern commanders in this week are among the most revealing documents of the surrender period, because they show a head of state trying to manage a controlled collapse. The orders instructed eastern formations to hold their positions against the Soviets while facilitating the westward movement of troops and civilians, to maintain discipline during the retreat, and to prepare for the possibility of a general surrender whose timing was not yet fixed. Steinert's study of the Flensburg regime draws on these transmissions to reconstruct Dönitz's state of mind: a commander who understood the war was lost but believed he retained a duty to shape the manner of its ending.

The communications themselves were a diminishing asset. The German military radio network, though degraded, remained functional enough for Flensburg to reach the major commands, and the Allies, who were monitoring German traffic, could follow Dönitz's intentions in near real time. This created an asymmetry that favored the negotiators at Reims: Bedell Smith knew, from signals intelligence, the substance of Dönitz's instructions to Jodl before Jodl presented them. The four-day delay request did not arrive as a surprise; it arrived as a known quantity, already analyzed and already rejected in the Allied councils. The radio war was thus being fought on two levels: Dönitz transmitting orders to his subordinates, and the Allies reading those orders as they decided how to respond to his envoy.

The eastern flight that these orders sustained was one of the great population movements of the war's end. German soldiers in Czechoslovakia, Austria, and eastern Germany abandoned their positions in organized columns and marched west, often with civilian refugees intermixed in the same columns. Soviet forces, advancing rapidly, attempted to cut off these movements, and where they succeeded, the result was mass captivity. Where the Germans reached American or British lines first, the result was surrender to the West. The days between May 5 and May 8 saw the heaviest of this movement, as the imminence of the general surrender created a final window. Commanders who delayed their own surrender by hours could save thousands of their men from Soviet captivity, and many did exactly that, exploiting the raggedness of the ceasefire's implementation. This was the reality behind the fourth body question addressed later in this article: the westward movement was German initiative operating in the gaps of the surrender, not Allied policy.

Dönitz's final radio order of the war, the ceasefire directive transmitted after the Reims signing, is worth noting for its tone. It was brief, military, and devoid of political content: all forces were to cease hostilities at the appointed hour, remain in place, and await further orders. The man who had spent a week trying to discriminate among his enemies ended by issuing an order that discriminated among none of them. The radio war concluded with the instrument it had been resisting.

## Reims: The Refusal of a Separate Peace

Friedeburg arrived at Reims, Eisenhower's SHAEF headquarters, on May 5. Eisenhower refused to receive him personally. This was a deliberate decision, not an oversight. By keeping the German admiral at the staff level, Eisenhower denied Dönitz's envoy the political dignity of a commander-to-commander negotiation and signaled that what was on offer was a military instrument, not a diplomatic settlement. The discussions were conducted by Eisenhower's chief of staff, Lieutenant General Walter Bedell Smith, a choice that communicated the Allied position with bureaucratic finality: the terms were not being negotiated, they were being dictated.

Friedeburg's initial proposals at Reims were the same partial arrangements he had brought to Lüneburg Heath, extended to cover the Western Front more broadly. Bedell Smith rejected them. Eisenhower's position, communicated through his chief of staff, was that Germany would surrender unconditionally, on all fronts, simultaneously, or not at all. The Western Allies would not accept the surrender of western forces while German troops continued fighting in the east, because that arrangement would make the Anglo-Americans complicit in Dönitz's strategy of evading Soviet captivity for as many Germans as possible.

Dönitz, receiving Friedeburg's reports by radio, understood that a higher-ranking envoy was needed. On May 6 he dispatched Colonel General Alfred Jodl, the chief of operations of the OKW and the officer who had directed German military operations throughout the war, to Reims with authority to negotiate a full surrender. Jodl's arrival raised the stakes. He was the most senior German operational officer available, and his presence signaled that Dönitz was prepared to deal seriously. The contrast between the two German envoys is worth noting: Friedeburg was a naval officer operating outside his service's domain, while Jodl was the army operations chief whose signature would carry weight with every German commander who received the resulting orders. Dönitz's choice of Jodl for the final negotiation was an acknowledgment that the surrender required the army's institutional assent, not merely the head of state's authorization.

But Jodl's instructions still contained the core of the partial-surrender strategy: he was to seek a delay, reported in the sources as approximately four days, before the surrender took effect on the Eastern Front, to allow the maximum number of German troops and civilians to move westward. Eisenhower rejected the delay through Bedell Smith, and he added the threat that gave the rejection its force: if Dönitz did not sign a full surrender, Eisenhower would order the Western Front closed to German refugees and surrendering troops, leaving them to the Soviets.

The threat worked because it struck at the entire purpose of Dönitz's strategy. Dönitz had been trying to save Germans from Soviet captivity; Eisenhower was now telling him that continued resistance would deliver more Germans into Soviet hands, not fewer. Jodl requested authorization from Dönitz by radio on the night of May 6-7. Dönitz, recognizing that he had no leverage left, authorized the signature. The negotiation that had begun as an attempt to divide the coalition ended with the coalition's unity reaffirmed by the very man who had tried to break it.

## The Signing at Reims: 2:41 a.m., May 7

The unconditional surrender document was signed at Reims in the early hours of May 7, 1945, at approximately 2:41 a.m. Jodl signed for the German armed forces. Bedell Smith signed for the Western Allies. Major General Ivan Susloparov, the Soviet liaison officer at SHAEF, signed for the Soviet Union. The document ordered all German forces to cease hostilities at 11:01 p.m. Central European Time on May 8, a precise minute chosen to give the order time to propagate through the surviving German command structure.

The Reims signing was, in Eisenhower's judgment, legally complete. It was an unconditional surrender of all German armed forces, signed by an officer with Dönitz's authorization, accepted by the supreme Allied commander in the west and countersigned by the Soviet representative present. Eisenhower cabled the Combined Chiefs of Staff that the mission was accomplished. For a few hours, it appeared that the European war had ended with a single document signed in a schoolhouse in France.

The physical setting of the signing contributed to its later controversy. SHAEF headquarters at Reims occupied a schoolhouse, the Collège Moderne et Technique, and the signing took place in the war room, a cramped space dominated by maps of the completed campaigns. The contrast with the ceremonial spaces in which wars had traditionally been ended was stark, and the Soviets would later cite the modesty of the venue as evidence of the proceeding's inadequacy. But the modesty was itself a statement: this was a military headquarters receiving a military capitulation, not a palace hosting a peace conference. The unconditional surrender doctrine required no palace, because there was no peace to negotiate.

The timing of the announcement created a brief information crisis. News of the signing leaked before the agreed embargo, with some outlets reporting the surrender on May 7. Eisenhower's headquarters had planned a coordinated announcement for May 8, after the ceasefire arrangements were confirmed and the Soviet position clarified. The premature reports forced the Allies' hand and contributed to the atmosphere of confusion in which Stalin's objection arrived. The leak is a minor episode, but it illustrates the difficulty of managing information across a coalition even in victory: the Western press, the Soviet censors, and the military headquarters operated on different timelines, and the surrender's public meaning began to diverge before its legal meaning was settled.

But the signature of Susloparov contained a defect that Stalin would not overlook. Susloparov had signed without specific authorization from Moscow. He was a liaison officer, not a plenipotentiary, and he had committed the Soviet Union to an instrument negotiated entirely within a Western headquarters, in a ceremony that gave the Soviet Union the status of a countersigning witness rather than a principal victor. For a war that had cost the Soviet Union some 27 million dead, this was procedurally and symbolically inadequate. Ryan's The Last Battle, the earliest of the major English-language accounts, captures the Soviet fury at learning of the signing from Allied communiques rather than from their own chain of command. Stalin's objection was immediate, and it was not negotiable in substance, though its form could be managed.

## The Stalin-Eisenhower Exchange: Managing the Objection

Stalin's objection to the Reims signing was communicated through the channels the coalition had developed over four years of wartime diplomacy, and the exchange that followed is a model of how the Allied committee architecture managed a prestige crisis without breaking. Stalin's message to Eisenhower was blunt: the Reims signing was unacceptable as the final act of the European war, the Soviet Union required a second ceremony in Berlin under Soviet auspices, and the German signatories had to include the service chiefs. The message did not question the substance of the surrender terms, which Moscow found satisfactory. It questioned the form, and in the Soviet view the form was the substance: a war that had cost 27 million Soviet lives could not be concluded as an adjunct to a Western headquarters proceeding.

Eisenhower's reply accepted the demand for a second ceremony while protecting the legal integrity of the first. His position, worked out with Washington and London before it was communicated to Moscow, had three elements. First, the Reims signing was legally operative and would not be repudiated; the ceasefire orders already propagating through German units derived their authority from it. Second, a second ceremony in Berlin would be held, with Zhukov presiding and Tedder representing the Western Allies, to satisfy the Soviet requirement for a capitulation received in the enemy capital. Third, the Berlin document would be framed as a ratification and elaboration of the Reims instrument, not as a superseding act. This formulation gave Stalin everything he had demanded in substance while giving Eisenhower everything he required in law.

The exchange illuminates the difference between the Allied coalition and the Axis command structure at the level of crisis management. A command system resolves a prestige dispute by the fiat of the superior: one ceremony, one authority, no appeal. The Allied system resolved it by negotiation between nominal equals, each of whom had to be satisfied that the outcome served both the common cause and the national interest. The process was slower and more cumbersome than a fiat would have been. It also produced an outcome that both sides could live with, which a fiat imposed by either side would not have done. Had Eisenhower simply refused a second ceremony, Stalin would have had a genuine grievance to carry into the postwar settlement. Had Eisenhower repudiated Reims, the legal basis of the ceasefire would have been thrown into doubt. The dual-signing compromise avoided both failures, and it did so through the ordinary machinery of Allied consultation: messages between heads of government, coordination through the Combined Chiefs of Staff, and the personal relationship between the theater commanders.

The role of the Western governments in this exchange should not be understated. Churchill and Truman both backed Eisenhower's formulation, and the speed with which London and Washington endorsed the dual-signing approach reflected the maturity of the coalition's decision-making apparatus. There was no inter-Allied crisis, no public disagreement, no leak to the press suggesting division. The contrast with the Axis side, where Göring's and Himmler's separate peace feelers in April had been treated as treason and had contributed to the regime's final disintegration, is instructive. The Allies could absorb a prestige dispute because they had institutions for doing so. The Germans could not absorb a succession dispute because their institutions were identical with one man.

## The Soviet Objection and the Berlin Ceremony

Stalin's demands were threefold. First, the signing had to take place in Berlin, the enemy capital, not in a French schoolhouse. Second, it had to be conducted under Soviet auspices, with a Soviet commander presiding. Third, the German signatories had to be the heads of the armed services themselves, not merely the OKW operations chief. These were demands of prestige and procedure, but they were not frivolous. The Soviet Union had borne the overwhelming share of the land war against Germany, and the ceremony of surrender was the public accounting of that sacrifice. A surrender signed in the west, by a staff officer, witnessed by a Soviet liaison, did not constitute that accounting.

Eisenhower agreed to a second ceremony while maintaining that the Reims signing was legally operative. This was the dual-signing compromise, and it required careful handling. The Western Allies could not repudiate Reims without implying that the first surrender had been defective, which would have raised questions about the ceasefire orders already propagating through German units. The Soviets could not accept Reims as the final word without the ceremonial recognition their sacrifice demanded. The solution was to treat Berlin as a ratification and elaboration: the surrender would be signed again, in the Soviet capital of conquest, with full ceremony, while the legal effect dated from the Reims instrument and the May 8 ceasefire hour.

The Berlin ceremony took place at the Soviet military engineering school in the Karlshorst district of Berlin at 11:45 p.m. on May 8, shortly after the ceasefire had taken effect. Marshal Georgy Zhukov presided. For Germany, Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel signed as chief of the OKW, Admiral Friedeburg signed for the Kriegsmarine, and Colonel General Hans-Jürgen Stumpff signed for the Luftwaffe. For the Allies, Zhukov signed for the Soviet Union and Air Chief Marshal Arthur Tedder, Eisenhower's deputy, signed for the Western Allies. The ceremony was witnessed by General Jean de Lattre de Tassigny for France and General Carl Spaatz for the United States. Keitel's signature, delivered with the theatrical stiffness that the newsreels preserved, was the image of the German military's formal submission. The presence of all three German service chiefs satisfied Stalin's demand for completeness, and the location satisfied the demand for symbolism.

The legal status of the two signings has been the subject of the minor scholarly disagreement the brief identifies. The consensus position, which Beevor's account supports, is that the Reims instrument was the operative legal surrender and the Berlin ceremony was a confirmatory ratification demanded by Soviet prestige. No German unit was ever in doubt about which orders governed its conduct after May 8, and no Allied authority ever treated the Berlin document as superseding Reims in law. The dual signing was politics conducted through procedure, and it worked precisely because both sides understood the distinction between legal effect and ceremonial adequacy.

## The French Dimension: The Fourth Occupant

The Berlin ceremony's inclusion of General Jean de Lattre de Tassigny as a witness reflected a political reality that the surrender documents themselves barely acknowledged: France was to be the fourth occupying power in Germany, despite having played no part in the Casablanca formulation and a subordinate part in the final campaigns. The French presence at Karlshorst was the ceremonial ratification of decisions made at Yalta, where the Big Three had agreed to carve a French zone out of the originally planned Anglo-American areas. De Lattre's signature as witness, alongside Spaatz for the United States, gave France a visible place in the war's ending act that its military contribution to the final offensives had not fully earned but that its political rehabilitation required.

The French dimension mattered for the surrender's implementation because the French zone would be administered differently from the others. French occupation policy in its zone was initially the harshest of the Western Allies, driven by the experience of occupation and by de Gaulle's determination that France would extract both security and reparations from German territory. The prisoners who fell into French hands faced longer detention and harder labor than those held by the Americans and British. This variation among the Western zones is a reminder that the "Western Allies" were themselves a coalition with internal differences, and that the committee architecture operated at multiple levels simultaneously: the grand coalition of the Big Three, the Anglo-American partnership within it, and the French association alongside.

De Lattre's presence also carried a symbolic weight specific to the French experience. France had been the first great power to fall to Germany in 1940, and its restoration to the victors' table at Berlin closed a circle that began with the armistice at Compiègne. The symmetry was not lost on the participants: Germany's surrender was received in the Soviet-conquered capital, but France's witness signature asserted that the victory belonged to the coalition of the invaded as well as to the coalition of the invaders' conquerors. The fourth occupant's place at the ceremony was the political complement to the military fact of the surrender, and it foreshadowed the four-power administration of Germany that Potsdam would formalize.

## VE Day and Victory Day: The Calendar of Victory

The ceasefire at 11:01 p.m. Central European Time on May 8 and the Berlin signing at 11:45 p.m. the same evening produced a calendar ambiguity that has endured into the commemorative life of the war. In London, Paris, and New York, May 8 became Victory in Europe Day. The crowds that filled the Mall before Buckingham Palace, the celebrations in Times Square, and the victory parade in Paris all belonged to May 8. In Moscow, the Berlin signing at 11:45 p.m. Central European Time was already May 9 by Moscow time, and the Soviet Union celebrated Victory Day on May 9. The distinction was not a dispute about when the war ended; both sides agreed the formal ceasefire took effect at the same moment, though scattered fighting continued in places such as Czechoslovakia for several days afterward. It was a consequence of time zones applied to a signing that straddled midnight, and it has been preserved in the separate commemorative traditions that followed.

The celebrations themselves were genuine expressions of relief on a scale the twentieth century has rarely seen. In Britain, the King and Queen appeared on the Buckingham Palace balcony with Churchill, and the crowds that had endured six years of war sang in the streets until dawn. The balcony appearances, repeated through the day, became the defining image of the British victory: the constitutional monarchy and the parliamentary leadership presenting themselves together to a people whose endurance had made the victory possible. In the United States, the news arrived in the morning papers and the celebrations unfolded across the day, from the factory floors where workers downed tools to the ticker-tape canyons of New York. In Moscow, the celebration was the most massive of all, proportionate to a sacrifice of some 27 million dead, with crowds filling Red Square in a demonstration of collective endurance that the Soviet state choreographed and the Soviet people genuinely felt. In Paris, the parade down the Champs-Élysées marked the restoration of a capital that had lived under occupation for four years, and the French celebration carried a particular intensity as the public reclamation of national honor.

The joy was real, and it was shadowed everywhere by the knowledge of the cost and by the awareness, already present in May 1945, that the coalition celebrating together would not remain united for long. In London, the Foreign Office was already drafting memoranda about Soviet intentions in Eastern Europe. In Washington, the planners turning to the Pacific were calculating the cost of the invasion of Japan. In Moscow, the celebration coexisted with the consolidation of Soviet control over the liberated territories. The unity of VE Day was the unity of a completed task, not the unity of a shared future, and the participants understood the difference even as they sang.

The May 8 versus May 9 distinction deserves precise handling because it is frequently misunderstood. It does not reflect a disagreement about the surrender's validity, and it does not indicate that the Soviet Union considered the war to have ended a day later in any operational sense. The formal ceasefire bound Soviet commands at the same hour as everyone else, though scattered fighting continued in places such as Czechoslovakia for several days afterward. The difference is purely calendrical, a function of the two-hour difference between Central European Time and Moscow time applied to a ceremony that began three quarters of an hour before midnight. Contemporary Russian Victory Day commemorations on May 9 and Western VE Day commemorations on May 8 are both correct as to the moment; they differ only in the clock against which the moment is measured.

## The Scale of Surrender: Five Million Prisoners

The surrender of May 7-8 did not instantly demobilize five million men. It initiated a process that unfolded over the following weeks, as German units in Norway, Denmark, Czechoslovakia, Austria, and the remaining pockets laid down their arms in accordance with the OKW directives that followed the signings. The final accounting of prisoners was approximately as follows: some 2.5 million German military personnel fell into Soviet custody, about 2 million into American custody, and about 1 million into British custody, with smaller numbers held by the French and other Allied forces. These figures are approximate, as the chaos of the final weeks makes precision impossible, but their proportions are well established in the scholarship.

The fate of these prisoners varied enormously, and the variation was the direct consequence of the strategic question Dönitz had tried to resolve in his favor. German prisoners in Western custody were generally treated in accordance with the Geneva Convention. The great majority were released by the end of 1945, and the Western prisoner population declined rapidly through 1946. German prisoners in Soviet custody faced catastrophically worse conditions. Approximately one million German prisoners died in Soviet captivity, from starvation, disease, exposure, and overwork in the labor camps to which they were sent. The Soviet treatment of German prisoners was itself shaped by the German treatment of Soviet prisoners earlier in the war, in which millions had died, but the scale of the German losses in Soviet hands remains one of the great unreckoned costs of the war's end.

Dönitz's strategy of moving Germans westward had, in the event, partially succeeded despite Eisenhower's refusal of a separate peace. The four-day delay Jodl had sought was denied, but the surrender's implementation still allowed substantial westward movement in the days before and after May 8, as German commanders on the Eastern Front used every available hour to march their men toward American and British lines. Millions of German soldiers did reach Western captivity who would otherwise have fallen into Soviet hands. This was not the result of Allied policy; it was the result of German initiative operating within the shrinking window before the ceasefire. The moral accounting of this outcome is complex. The Western Allies did not facilitate the evasion of Soviet captivity as a matter of policy, but neither did they prevent it where German units managed it on their own.

## The Prisoner Archipelago: Camps East and West

The five million prisoners of May 1945 entered two profoundly different systems of captivity, and the difference was the material consequence of everything Dönitz had tried and failed to negotiate. In the west, the Americans and British established a vast network of temporary enclosures, the Rheinwiesenlager along the Rhine being the most notorious, where surrendered German soldiers were held in open fields under minimal shelter. Conditions in these camps in the first weeks were harsh: overcrowding, inadequate food, exposure to the elements. The harshness was substantially a function of scale rather than policy; the Western Allies had not planned for the surrender of millions of men in a matter of days, and the logistics of feeding and housing them lagged behind the military fact of their captivity. But the Western system was oriented toward processing and release. Screening for war crimes suspects, separation of Waffen-SS personnel, and the gradual discharge of the rank and file proceeded through the summer and autumn, and by the end of 1945 the great majority of Western-held prisoners had gone home.

In the east, the Soviet system was oriented toward labor and retribution. German prisoners were transported deep into the Soviet Union, to camps spread across Russia, Siberia, and Central Asia, where they were set to work in mines, forests, and reconstruction projects. Rations were minimal, medical care was rudimentary, and the mortality rate was catastrophic. The approximately one million deaths in Soviet captivity were not, for the most part, the result of deliberate extermination policy; they were the result of a system that treated prisoners as a disposable labor force in a country that could barely feed its own population. But the distinction between deliberate killing and lethal indifference mattered little to the men who died. The Soviet prisoner experience lasted years beyond the Western one: while the Americans and British had released nearly all their prisoners by 1947, the Soviet Union held German prisoners into the 1950s, with the last repatriations occurring after Stalin's death.

The screening processes in both systems deserve attention because they were the administrative expression of the surrender's political logic. In the west, the Allies separated prisoners into categories: ordinary Wehrmacht soldiers for prompt release, Waffen-SS members for extended detention, and suspected war criminals for transfer to the judicial system that would culminate at Nuremberg. This triage was imperfect and sometimes arbitrary, but it reflected a coherent theory of responsibility. In the east, the Soviet screening was cruder and more politicized, with officers and specialists selected for extended detention and intelligence exploitation. The two systems embodied the two halves of the coalition's approach to the defeated enemy: the Western emphasis on rapid demobilization and the restoration of civilian life, and the Soviet emphasis on extracting the maximum material and punitive value from victory.

The prisoner archipelago also illuminates the limits of what the surrender could accomplish. The Reims and Berlin documents ended hostilities between organized forces, but they could not resolve the humanitarian catastrophe of the war's end: the millions of displaced persons, the starving cities, the destroyed infrastructure, the epidemics. The prisoners were the most organized portion of a vastly larger population in motion, and their processing, however imperfect, was the most successful element of the Allies' immediate postwar administration. The camps, east and west, were the physical form of unconditional surrender applied to five million individuals: disarmed, categorized, and held at the victors' disposition.

## Flensburg: The Arrest of May 23

The Dönitz government continued its nominal operations after the surrender, issuing the OKW directives that implemented the ceasefire and managing the administrative details of a collapsing military. For three weeks, the Allies tolerated this arrangement because it served the practical purpose of transmitting surrender orders through a chain of command the German forces still recognized. But the tolerance had a limit, and the limit was political. A government headed by Hitler's designated successor, staffed by men who had administered the Nazi war effort to its end, could not be permitted to continue as a going concern once the military necessity for its existence had passed.

On May 23, 1945, Allied authorities arrested the Flensburg government. Dönitz, Jodl, Keitel, and the remaining ministers were taken into custody, and the rump administration was dissolved. The arrest was conducted without ceremony and without resistance. The men who had negotiated the surrender of Germany were now prisoners of the coalition they had negotiated with, and several of them would stand trial at Nuremberg within the year. Dönitz himself was tried and sentenced to ten years imprisonment for crimes against peace and war crimes related to the U-boat campaign, a verdict that reflected the Allies' judgment that his role in sustaining the war effort outweighed his role in ending it.

The legal consequence of the May 23 arrests is worth stating precisely. The Flensburg government ceased to exist as any kind of authority on that date, but the surrender it had signed remained legally operative. The surrender was an act of the German armed forces, authorized by the head of state at the time of signing, and its validity did not depend on the continued existence of the authority that had ordered it. This is the standard position in the scholarship, and it is the reason the brief describes the surrender as remaining legally operative after approximately May 23. The Allies had negotiated with Dönitz for the limited purpose of ending hostilities, and they arrested him for the broader purpose of ending the regime. The two acts were consistent because they served different ends.

## The Strategic Consequence: The European War Ends

VE Day formally ended the European war, but it did not end the Second World War. The Pacific War continued, with the Okinawa campaign still in progress through June and the planning for the invasion of Japan underway. The European surrender's strategic consequence was therefore twofold: it closed one theater completely, and it freed the Allied coalition to concentrate on the other. The [Potsdam Conference](/2005/12/22/potsdam-conference-july-august-1945/), scheduled for July and August 1945, would formalize the territorial and political arrangements for Germany that the surrender had made possible: the four occupation zones, the division of Berlin, the principles of denazification and demilitarization, and the framework for reparations. The atomic bomb decision and the subsequent Japanese surrender followed in August and September.

The legal framework of the surrender had been established long before May 1945. The unconditional surrender principle, announced at [Casablanca in January 1943](/2006/05/03/casablanca-conference-january-1943-unconditional-surrender/) and maintained through Tehran, Yalta, and every subsequent Allied conference, was the foundation on which the May negotiations rested. Eisenhower did not improvise the refusal of a separate peace; he applied a policy that the coalition had settled two years earlier and had never revised. The four-power occupation of Germany, the division of Berlin, and the subordination of all German authority to Allied control were the direct implementation of agreements the Big Three had already made. The surrender was the execution of a plan, not the invention of one.

The Nuremberg trials, which began in November 1945, were the judicial consequence of the surrender's political logic. Unconditional surrender meant that no German authority survived to negotiate the terms of justice, and the Allies established an international tribunal to try the surviving leadership for crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. The [indictment of October 1945](/2004/11/15/nuremberg-indictment-october-1945/), addressed in detail elsewhere in this series, named the organizations and individuals of the Nazi regime, and the trials that ran through October 1946 established the legal precedent that aggressive war was a punishable crime. Dönitz's presence in the dock, the man who had signed the surrender in Germany's name, was the final expression of the principle that ending the war and judging its authors were parts of a single Allied project.

### Why did Eisenhower refuse any separate surrender to the Western Allies?

Eisenhower refused because a separate surrender would have fractured the coalition at the moment of victory. The Casablanca unconditional surrender policy bound all three Allies to a peace, and accepting western-only terms would have made the Anglo-Americans complicit in helping Germans evade Soviet captivity. Stalin would have had a grievance, and the postwar settlement would have begun in bad faith.

The military logic reinforced the political logic. A western surrender with continued eastern fighting would have required Allied forces to manage German troop movements toward their own lines, effectively cooperating with the enemy against an ally. The refusal was therefore both a political necessity and an operational one. It preserved the coalition's unity and it denied Dönitz the leverage he sought. The threat to close the Western Front to refugees made the cost of continued resistance concrete: every day of delay would deliver more Germans to the Soviets, not fewer.

### What made the Reims signing legally sufficient despite Stalin's objections?

The Reims instrument met every requirement of a military capitulation. Jodl signed with Dönitz's radio authorization, acting as plenipotentiary for the German armed forces. Bedell Smith signed for the Allied supreme commander, and Susloparov countersigned for the Soviet Union. The document set a single ceasefire hour, and the OKW directives that followed gave it operational effect on every front.

Stalin's objections were to the ceremony's prestige and procedure, not to its legal form: the location was a Western headquarters, the Soviet signatory lacked Moscow's specific authorization, and the German signatory was a staff officer rather than a service chief. None of these defects affected the instrument's validity as a surrender order. Eisenhower's position, that Reims was operative and Berlin confirmatory, has been sustained by the subsequent scholarship. No German commander ever questioned which orders governed after May 8.

### Why did Stalin insist on a second ceremony in Berlin?

Stalin insisted because the Soviet Union had paid the overwhelming price of the land war and demanded a ceremony proportionate to that sacrifice. Twenty-seven million Soviet dead made a signing in a French schoolhouse, countersigned by an unauthorized liaison, an inadequate public accounting. The location and the signatories had to reflect who had broken German power in the east.

The demands were non-negotiable in substance, though Eisenhower successfully managed their form through the dual-signing compromise: Reims remained legally operative, and Berlin served as the ceremonial ratification. The location mattered: Berlin was the enemy capital, taken by Soviet arms at enormous cost, and the surrender of Germany had to be received there to register the reality of who had broken German power in the east. The German signatories mattered: Stalin wanted the heads of the services, Keitel, Friedeburg, and Stumpff, not merely the operations chief. The presiding authority mattered: Zhukov, the conqueror of Berlin, had to receive the surrender rather than witness a Western staff officer doing so. These were demands of prestige, but prestige in this context was the political form of a historical fact.

### How did millions of Germans still reach Western captivity despite the refusal?

They reached Western captivity through German initiative in the chaotic window around the surrender, not through Allied policy. Eastern Front commanders marched westward at maximum speed before and after May 8, seeking American and British lines. The surrender was simultaneous in law but ragged in practice. Front-line Allied commanders kept accepting surrenders as they always had.

German commanders on the Eastern Front understood the stakes of Soviet captivity as clearly as Dönitz did, and in the days before and after May 8 they used every available hour to move their men toward the west. Eisenhower's threat to close the Western Front was directed at Dönitz's negotiating position, not implemented as a standing order to turn back surrendering troops. The result was that Dönitz's objective was partially achieved by means he had not negotiated. The moral complexity is real: the Western Allies did not facilitate evasion as policy, but the structure of the surrender allowed it as fact.

### What was the Dönitz government's legal status between May 8 and May 23?

The Flensburg government existed in a deliberate legal limbo. The Allies never recognized it as Germany's government, but they used it to transmit surrender orders to the remaining German forces. Its authority came from Hitler's testament and from the fact that German troops still obeyed its orders. That was enough to end hostilities and not enough for any broader purpose.

The Allies maintained the arrangement for three weeks because dissolving the command structure before the surrender was fully implemented would have risked uncoordinated resistance and the breakdown of prisoner processing. Once the military purpose was served, the political purpose asserted itself: the government of Hitler's successor could not continue, and on May 23 it was arrested and dissolved. The surrender it had signed survived its dissolution because a capitulation, once executed and implemented, does not depend on the survival of the authority that ordered it.

## The Unconditional Surrender Doctrine: From Casablanca to Reims

The refusal that defined the Reims negotiations was not invented in May 1945. It was the application of a doctrine announced at Casablanca in January 1943, when Roosevelt and Churchill declared that the Allies would accept nothing less than the unconditional surrender of Germany, Italy, and Japan. The doctrine had been controversial from its inception. Critics argued then and later that it prolonged the war by denying the German resistance any basis for negotiating Hitler's removal, a debate this series addresses in its account of the July 1944 plot. Defenders argued that only an unconditional formula could prevent a repetition of 1918, when the German army's acceptance of an armistice on negotiated terms had allowed the myth of an undefeated army stabbed in the back. The doctrine survived its critics because it served the coalition's deepest need: a public, irrevocable commitment by each ally that none would make a separate peace.

The doctrine's operation across the subsequent conferences gave it the force of settled law within the alliance. At Tehran in late 1943, the Big Three reaffirmed the principle while coordinating the military operations that would enforce it. At Yalta in February 1945, the principle was translated into the specific machinery of occupation: the four zones, the division of Berlin, the Allied Control Council. By the time Dönitz inherited the German state, unconditional surrender was not a negotiating position but a constitutional fact of the coalition, embedded in two years of agreements, staff planning, and public commitment. Eisenhower had no authority to set it aside even if he had wished to, which is one reason the Reims negotiation took the form it did: Bedell Smith was not authorized to bargain, only to receive Germany's acceptance.

The doctrine's wisdom was tested precisely once, in the first week of May 1945, and it passed the test. Dönitz offered the Allies a genuine temptation: a quicker end to fighting in the west, a million prisoners without further casualties, and the humanitarian cover of rescuing Germans from Soviet retribution. A coalition without the Casablanca commitment might well have accepted, and the separate peace would have begun the postwar era with a fundamental breach of trust between Moscow and the Western capitals. The commitment device worked as commitment devices are meant to work: it removed the tempting option from the table before the temptation arrived, so that Eisenhower's refusal required no deliberation about whether a separate peace might be advantageous. The advantage was irrelevant. The pledge had been given, and the pledge held.

This is the sense in which the German surrender vindicates the much-criticized Casablanca formula. The formula's costs, in prolonging the war by denying negotiated exits, are real and belong in any honest accounting. But its benefit, in guaranteeing that the war ended as a coalition victory rather than as a scramble of separate bargains, was demonstrated in the week when it mattered most. Dönitz tested the coalition's unity at the moment of maximum strain, and the unity held because it had been built into the alliance's foundation two years before the test arrived.

## The House Thesis Thread: Coalition Unity as a Weapon

The German surrender is the house thesis at maximum intensity because it is the case in which Allied committee architecture faced its most direct Axis challenge and did not bend. Dönitz's entire strategy in the first week of May was an attempt to exploit the seams of the coalition: to surrender in the west while fighting in the east, to move Germans out of Soviet reach under the cover of a Western capitulation, and to preserve some remnant of German military organization as a bargaining asset. Every element of that strategy depended on the Western Allies agreeing to treat the Soviet Union as a separate party to a separate war. The committee architecture of the alliance, built through Casablanca, Tehran, Yalta, and two years of combined staff work, had been designed precisely to prevent this. The unconditional surrender formula was not a slogan; it was a commitment device, a public pledge by each ally to the others that none would make a separate peace. When Dönitz tested the pledge, Eisenhower enforced it.

The enforcement took the form of decisions at multiple levels, which is the characteristic signature of committee architecture under stress. The policy level was fixed: unconditional surrender on all fronts, settled at Casablanca and never revised. The theater level applied it: Eisenhower's refusal to receive Friedeburg personally, Bedell Smith's rejection of partial terms, the threat to close the Western Front. The coalition level managed the Soviet dimension: the acceptance of Stalin's demand for a Berlin ceremony while insisting on Reims as legally operative, the careful diplomacy that gave Moscow its prestige without conceding the instrument. No single commander improvised this response. It emerged from an institutional framework that had anticipated the problem of a German surrender for two years and had built the procedures, the policies, and the personal relationships to handle it.

The Axis side of the comparison is stark. The German command architecture, which had sustained the war effort through Hitler's personal authority, could not produce an organized political transition because it had no political institutions apart from Hitler. Dönitz inherited a chain of command and nothing else: no cabinet with real ministries, no diplomatic service capable of negotiating a settlement, no political party that could survive its leader. He could order the army to stop fighting, because the army still obeyed orders, but he could not organize a continued war effort, a negotiated peace, or a postwar German state, because the architecture that might have done those things had been identical with Hitler's person. The surrender was therefore the mirror image of the July 20 plot's failure: where the plotters had been unable to seize a state that was identical with Hitler, Dönitz was unable to preserve a state that had died with him. Command architecture produced total war and then produced nothing.

The dual signing deserves emphasis as the committee architecture's characteristic solution to a prestige problem. A command system would have resolved Stalin's objection by fiat or by force: one ceremony, one authority, no negotiation. The Allied system resolved it by addition rather than subtraction. Reims stood as the legal instrument. Berlin stood as the ceremonial ratification. Both signings were real, both were necessary, and neither diminished the other. The compromise required Eisenhower to accept a second ceremony he considered legally redundant and Stalin to accept a legal instrument he considered ceremonially inadequate, and both men accepted because the coalition mattered more than the form. That is committee architecture at its most functional: not the absence of disagreement, but the institutional capacity to absorb disagreement without breaking.

## The Complication: The Legitimacy of Karl Dönitz

The Dönitz government's three weeks of existence present the genuine complication this article must address honestly. Dönitz was simultaneously the legally operative authority for surrender purposes and a substantially illegitimate continuation of the Nazi regime. The Allies negotiated with him to end the war and then arrested him as a war criminal, and both acts were justified, but their coexistence requires explanation.

The case for Dönitz's operational legitimacy is straightforward. He held Hitler's testamentary designation, which was the only succession instrument available. The German armed forces recognized his authority, which made him the only person capable of ordering a general surrender. The Allies needed a German authority to sign the capitulation and to transmit ceasefire orders, and Dönitz was the only candidate. Without his authorization, the surrender would have had to be extracted unit by unit, prolonging the fighting and the chaos. In this limited sense, the Flensburg government was legitimate: it could do the one thing that needed doing, and it did it.

The case against his broader legitimacy is equally straightforward. His authority derived from a dictator's will, proclaimed over a state whose institutions had collapsed. His government included men who had administered aggressive war and its attendant crimes to the final day. His strategic objective in the surrender negotiations was not the welfare of Germany as a political community but the evasion of Soviet retribution for as many Germans as possible, a goal that was understandable in human terms and indefensible as a basis for discriminating among the victors. And his own record, which the Nuremberg tribunal judged as warranting ten years imprisonment, included the planning of aggressive war and the conduct of unrestricted submarine warfare. The man who signed the surrender was not a neutral technician of capitulation; he was a participant in the regime whose crimes made the surrender necessary.

The Allies' handling of this duality was pragmatic rather than principled, and the pragmatism deserves scrutiny. They used Dönitz's authority where it served the ending of hostilities and discarded it where it threatened the political settlement. This was not hypocrisy but triage: the first imperative was to stop the killing, and the only instrument available for that purpose was the chain of command Dönitz headed. Once the killing stopped, the second imperative asserted itself, which was to deny the Nazi regime any postwar political existence, even in the attenuated form of a rump government at Flensburg. The arrest of May 23 was the Allies' answer to the question of what the Dönitz government had been: a useful fiction for surrender purposes, and nothing more.

The honest verdict on Dönitz is therefore mixed in a way that resists simplification. He facilitated an orderly surrender that saved lives, including German lives, and he did so under the severe constraint of having inherited a lost war with no good options. He also served a criminal regime to its end, pursued a discriminatory surrender strategy that the Allies rightly rejected, and was justly convicted for his part in the war's planning and conduct. The Flensburg interlude does not rehabilitate him, and his conviction does not erase the utility of his surrender. Both facts stand, and the history of May 1945 requires both.

## Verdict

The German surrender of May 1945 was the most successful Allied committee decision of the European war. The unconditional surrender policy, maintained without deviation from Casablanca through the final week, denied Dönitz the separate peace he sought and preserved the coalition at the moment of maximum temptation to divide it. Eisenhower's conduct of the Reims negotiations was the operational expression of that policy: firm on the principle of simultaneity, flexible on the mechanics of implementation, and ruthless in the use of leverage when Dönitz hesitated. The dual signing with Berlin accommodated Soviet prestige without compromising the legal instrument, and the three-week management of the Flensburg government balanced the military need for a functioning German chain of command against the political need to extinguish the Nazi regime completely.

The verdict on Dönitz is the verdict the Nuremberg tribunal rendered, with the qualification that the surrender itself must be credited to his account. He ended the European war in an orderly fashion when disorder was the available alternative, and he did so after his strategy of a discriminatory surrender had been defeated. That the Allies negotiated with him and then imprisoned him is not a contradiction but a measure of the two different things he was: the last head of a criminal regime and the only available instrument of its capitulation.

For the house thesis, the surrender stands as the limiting case in the favorable direction. Committee architecture did not merely outperform command architecture in this instance; it confronted command architecture's final political maneuver and defeated it by refusing to engage on its terms. Dönitz asked the Allies to choose between their unity and their convenience. They chose unity, and the European war ended as a coalition victory rather than as three separate peaces. The cost of that choice was borne by the Germans whom Dönitz had hoped to save from Soviet captivity, and the moral weight of that cost belongs in the accounting. But the alternative, a fractured peace that rewarded the attempt to divide the victors, would have poisoned the settlement that followed. The Allies chose correctly, and the choice held.

The surrender also clarifies the relationship between the house thesis and the moral evaluation of the war's end. Committee architecture produced the better strategic outcome, but it did not produce a morally clean one. The million German dead in Soviet captivity, the chaos of the prisoner camps, the ragged implementation that left some units fighting after the ceasefire: these were consequences of the coalition's victory, not failures of its decision-making, but they were consequences for which the victors bore responsibility. The thesis claims that committee architecture makes better decisions than command architecture, not that it makes perfect ones or painless ones. The German surrender supports the claim in its strong form while illustrating its limits: the best available decision still carried a terrible human cost, and honesty requires that the cost be stated alongside the achievement.

## Study

The study of the German surrender repays attention to the documentary record, which is unusually complete for a negotiation conducted under such pressure. The Reims surrender document itself is short, a military instrument rather than a treaty, and its brevity is instructive: unconditional surrender required no elaborate terms because the Allies had settled the political questions at Casablanca, Tehran, and Yalta long before May 1945. The Berlin document is longer and more ceremonial, reflecting its different purpose. Reading the two together shows the distinction between legal effect and political form that governed the dual signing.

For the military historian, the essential primary sources are the SHAEF records of the Reims negotiations, Bedell Smith's papers, Jodl's authorization traffic with Flensburg, and Zhukov's records of the Berlin ceremony. Steinert's Capitulation 1945 remains the indispensable study of the Flensburg government, drawing on German naval and governmental records that illuminate Dönitz's calculations from the inside. Beevor's Berlin: The Downfall 1945 provides the comprehensive narrative of the war's end in Europe, with the surrender negotiations set against the fall of the capital. Ryan's The Last Battle, though the earliest of the major accounts, retains value for its interviews with participants conducted when memories were fresh. Ziemke's Battle for Berlin supplies the operational dimension that the diplomatic narratives sometimes slight.

For the student of coalition warfare, the surrender negotiations are a case study in the management of allied disagreement under the pressure of victory. The relevant comparison is with the First World War's armistice, where the absence of an unconditional surrender formula allowed the German army to propagate the myth that it had been undefeated in the field. The Casablanca formula was designed to prevent a repetition, and the May 1945 negotiations show the design working under stress. The Soviet dimension repays particular study: Stalin's objection to Reims and the Berlin compromise illustrate how coalition maintenance required continuous attention to the prestige economy of the alliance, not merely to its military operations.

For the student of the war's end as a human event, the surrender's aftermath repays study through the records of the prisoner processing system, the displaced persons camps, and the military government detachments that administered occupied Germany in the summer of 1945. These records show the surrender not as a single moment but as a months-long process of converting military victory into political order, with all the friction and improvisation that such a conversion entails.

Students of military medicine and the human cost of war's end may consult the battlefield medicine companion resource at [WWII Battlefield Medicine](https://reportmedic.org/tools/wwii-battlefield-medicine.html), which documents casualty treatment, prisoner-of-war medical conditions, and the medical dimensions of mass surrender as they applied across the war's final campaigns.

Kershaw's The End: Hitler's Germany, 1944-1945, published after this article's date, is cited here strictly as research apparatus for its synthesis of the German collapse's final phase, not as a source for the events of May 1945 themselves, which are documented in the contemporary and near-contemporary sources above.

**Allied force distribution, early May 1945 (map description):** In the north, Montgomery's 21st Army Group held the line from the Netherlands through northwest Germany to the Baltic, facing the German forces that surrendered at Lüneburg Heath. In the center, Bradley's 12th Army Group occupied central Germany from the Rhine to Czechoslovakia. In the south, Devers's 6th Army Group held southern Germany and Austria. In the east, Soviet fronts under Zhukov and Konev held Berlin, eastern Germany, and the line through Czechoslovakia to the Baltic, with the Courland pocket isolated in Latvia. Norway and Denmark remained under German occupation until the surrender's implementation. The Potsdam Conference of July-August would formalize this de facto distribution into four occupation zones, with Berlin divided among the four powers despite lying deep within the Soviet zone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why did Hitler name Dönitz as his successor rather than a general?

Hitler's choice of Dönitz reflected the bunker politics of April 1945 more than any constitutional logic. Göring and Himmler, the two most plausible party successors, had both made separate peace approaches and were expelled from the party in the testament itself. The army's senior field marshals were either dead, disgraced, or distrusted after the July 1944 plot and the subsequent purges. Dönitz was the one senior commander whose loyalty had never wavered and whose service, the navy, was still functioning as an institution. Beevor's account treats the selection as Hitler's final rebuke to the party leadership that had failed him, elevating a technocrat of total war over the politicians. Steinert adds the practical consideration that Dönitz commanded the only force still capable of organized action in the north, where the last German-held territory lay. The testament named Goebbels as chancellor, but Goebbels killed himself on May 1, leaving Dönitz the sole executive authority by default as well as by designation.

### Where was the Flensburg government located and who staffed it?

The Dönitz government established itself at Flensburg, near the Danish border in Schleswig-Holstein, operating from naval facilities in the area. Dönitz himself had been at Plön, near Kiel, when he received confirmation of Hitler's death on May 1, and the government coalesced around the naval command infrastructure of the north. The rump cabinet was headed in practice by Count Schwerin von Krosigk, the former finance minister, who served as the leading minister. The military side was represented by Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel as chief of the OKW and General Alfred Jodl as chief of operations. Admiral Hans-Georg von Friedeburg commanded the navy and served as Dönitz's principal diplomatic envoy. The government controlled no functioning civilian ministries, collected no revenue, and exercised authority only where German troops still stood. Its ministries were titles without departments, and its writ ran exactly as far as the Wehrmacht's surviving chain of command, which was the one asset that made it useful to the Allies as an instrument of surrender.

### What did Dönitz hope to gain by surrendering only to the Western Allies?

Dönitz's objective was to save as many Germans as possible from Soviet captivity. German soldiers and civilians were fleeing westward in vast numbers, driven by the well-founded fear of Soviet reprisals and the known fate of prisoners in Soviet hands. Dönitz proposed to cease hostilities in the west while maintaining a fighting withdrawal in the east, using the continued Eastern Front defense as a shield behind which troops and civilians could move toward American and British lines. The plan was rational from the perspective of the people it was meant to save: Western captivity meant survival and relatively prompt release, while Soviet captivity meant years of forced labor and a substantial risk of death. But the plan required the Western Allies to become accomplices in evading the eastern half of the coalition, which would have fractured the alliance at the moment of victory. Eisenhower understood this perfectly, which is why the partial surrender was refused and the threat to close the Western Front was deployed.

### How did Montgomery handle Friedeburg's partial surrender offer?

Montgomery accepted Friedeburg's offer as a local tactical surrender and nothing more. The instrument signed at Lüneburg Heath on May 4 covered German forces in the Netherlands, Denmark, and northwest Germany, approximately one million troops, and took effect the following morning. Montgomery acted within his authority as an army group commander: ending the fighting on his front immediately, without further British or Canadian casualties, was a straightforward military good. He did not treat the surrender as a political settlement or as a precedent for a general capitulation, and he forwarded Friedeburg to Reims for the broader negotiation that Eisenhower insisted upon. Ziemke's operational analysis emphasizes that the Lüneburg Heath surrender worked precisely because it was framed as a battlefield instrument. The acceptance demonstrated that the Western Allies would take surrenders on proper terms while refusing to let tactical instruments become political wedges, a distinction Eisenhower then enforced at the theater level.

### Why did Eisenhower refuse to receive Friedeburg at Reims personally?

Eisenhower's refusal was a deliberate act of diplomatic staging. By keeping Friedeburg at the staff level, Eisenhower denied Dönitz's envoy the dignity of a commander-to-commander negotiation and signaled that Germany was not being offered a diplomatic settlement but a military dictation. A personal reception would have implied that the terms were negotiable between principals; a staff-level discussion communicated that the terms were fixed and the German role was to accept them. The choice of Bedell Smith as the Allied negotiator reinforced the message: the chief of staff was the officer who transmitted the commander's decisions, not the officer who made them. This staging also served the coalition dimension. A personal meeting between Eisenhower and a German admiral, conducted without Soviet participation at the principal level, would have given Stalin grounds to suspect a separate arrangement. Keeping the negotiation at staff level, with Susloparov present as the Soviet liaison, maintained the appearance and the reality of Allied unity throughout the proceedings.

### What was the four-day delay Jodl requested at Reims?

Jodl's instructions from Dönitz included a request for approximately four days' delay before the surrender would take effect on the Eastern Front. The purpose was transparent: four more days of continued eastern fighting would allow the maximum number of German troops and civilians to move westward into American and British captivity before the ceasefire froze everyone in place. The request was the last surviving element of Dönitz's partial-surrender strategy, translated from the language of separate fronts into the language of staggered timing. Eisenhower rejected it through Bedell Smith, and the rejection was accompanied by the threat that gave it force: if Dönitz did not sign a full and simultaneous surrender, Eisenhower would order the Western Front closed to German refugees and surrendering troops. The logic of the threat was to turn Dönitz's own objective against him. Continued resistance would not save more Germans from the Soviets; it would deliver more of them into Soviet hands by closing the escape route westward.

### Why did Eisenhower threaten to close the Western Front to German refugees?

The threat was the decisive lever of the Reims negotiation because it struck at the entire purpose of Dönitz's strategy. Dönitz had been trying to preserve a westward escape route for German soldiers and civilians fleeing the Soviets. Eisenhower told him, in effect, that the escape route was an Allied concession, not a German right, and that it would be withdrawn if Germany did not surrender on all fronts simultaneously. The threat was credible because Eisenhower had the authority to issue such an order and the forces to enforce it. It was also carefully calibrated: it did not require any inhumane action in the present, but promised a catastrophic consequence in the near future, which is the structure of an effective coercive threat. Jodl and Dönitz understood immediately that continued resistance would achieve the opposite of its intended purpose. The four-day delay was abandoned, the authorization to sign was transmitted by radio on the night of May 6-7, and the unconditional surrender was signed at 2:41 the next morning.

### What did the Reims surrender document actually order German forces to do?

The Reims document was a short military instrument, not a treaty. It ordered all German armed forces, wherever located, to cease hostilities at 11:01 p.m. Central European Time on May 8, 1945. It required German commanders to issue the necessary orders to their subordinates, to remain in their positions pending further Allied instructions, and to surrender their arms and equipment. It prohibited the scuttling of ships, the destruction of equipment, and any continued resistance. The OKW directives that followed the signing translated these terms into specific orders for each theater, and the Flensburg government's surviving chain of command was the transmission mechanism. The document's brevity reflected the fact that the political questions had been settled long before: unconditional surrender, four-power occupation, and the complete subordination of German authority had been agreed at Casablanca, Tehran, and Yalta. Reims added nothing to the political settlement; it executed the military act that the settlement required.

### Who was Ivan Susloparov and why was his signature a problem?

Major General Ivan Susloparov was the Soviet liaison officer attached to SHAEF at Reims, the Red Army's representative at Eisenhower's headquarters. He signed the Reims surrender document for the Soviet Union in the early hours of May 7. The problem was that he signed without specific authorization from Moscow. As a liaison officer, Susloparov was empowered to observe and to communicate, not to commit the Soviet Union to instruments of this magnitude. He appears to have signed on his own judgment that the document's terms were acceptable and that refusing to sign would have been worse, but he immediately cabled Moscow to report what he had done, and Stalin's reaction was furious. The unauthorized signature became the procedural core of the Soviet objection to Reims: the Soviet Union had been committed to the war's ending document by an officer without plenipotentiary authority, in a Western headquarters, in a ceremony that treated the Soviet representative as a countersigning witness. The defect was procedural rather than substantive, but in the prestige economy of the coalition, procedure was substance.

### Why did Stalin demand a second signing in Berlin?

Stalin's demand rested on three deficiencies he identified in the Reims ceremony. First, the location: the surrender of Germany had to be received in Berlin, the enemy capital taken by Soviet arms, not in a French schoolhouse. Second, the auspices: the ceremony had to be conducted under Soviet authority, with a Soviet commander presiding, not as an adjunct to a Western headquarters proceeding. Third, the signatories: Germany had to be represented by the heads of its armed services, Keitel, Friedeburg, and Stumpff, not merely by the OKW operations chief. Behind these procedural demands lay the historical fact that the Soviet Union had borne the overwhelming share of the land war against Germany at a cost of some 27 million dead. A surrender signed in the west, by a staff officer, witnessed by an unauthorized liaison, did not constitute a public accounting proportionate to that sacrifice. The demands were non-negotiable in substance, though Eisenhower successfully managed their form through the dual-signing compromise: Reims remained legally operative, and Berlin served as the ceremonial ratification.

### What time was the ceasefire scheduled and why that precise minute?

The ceasefire was set for 11:01 p.m. Central European Time on May 8, 1945. The precision of the minute, one minute past eleven, was a practical choice rather than a symbolic one. The surrender was signed at 2:41 a.m. on May 7, and the German command needed time to transmit the ceasefire order through its surviving communications to every front: Norway, Denmark, Czechoslovakia, Austria, the Courland pocket, and the scattered positions in Germany itself. A round hour on May 8 would have been logistically tight; the additional day and the odd minute gave the OKW transmission system the margin it needed and created an unambiguous timestamp that could not be confused with any other order. The one-minute-past-the-hour formulation also served a legal purpose: it fixed a single moment at which hostilities ceased for all forces simultaneously, eliminating any argument by a local commander that his unit had not yet received the order. The Berlin signing at 11:45 p.m. on May 8 thus took place three quarters of an hour after the ceasefire had already taken effect.

### Who signed for each service at the Karlshorst ceremony?

At the Berlin ceremony in the Karlshorst district on the night of May 8, Germany was represented by the heads of its three armed services, satisfying Stalin's demand for completeness. Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel signed as chief of the OKW, representing the army and the armed forces as a whole. Admiral Hans-Georg von Friedeburg signed for the Kriegsmarine, the same officer who had negotiated at Lüneburg Heath and Reims, now appearing in his service capacity. Colonel General Hans-Jürgen Stumpff signed for the Luftwaffe. For the Allies, Marshal Georgy Zhukov signed for the Soviet Union and presided over the ceremony. Air Chief Marshal Arthur Tedder, Eisenhower's deputy, signed for the Western Allies. The ceremony was witnessed by General Jean de Lattre de Tassigny for France and General Carl Spaatz for the United States. Keitel's stiff, theatrical delivery of his signature was captured in the newsreels and became the visual emblem of German military submission. The presence of all three service chiefs, in the Soviet-conquered capital, under Zhukov's presidency, gave the ceremony the completeness and the symbolism that Reims had lacked.

### What role did Marshal Zhukov play in the Berlin signing?

Zhukov presided over the Berlin ceremony as the Soviet Union's signatory and as the conqueror of the city in which it took place. His role was both substantive and symbolic. Substantively, he signed the surrender document for the Soviet Union with full plenipotentiary authority from Moscow, curing the authorization defect of Susloparov's Reims signature. Symbolically, his presidency of the ceremony embodied the Soviet claim that the eastern victory had been the decisive one: the marshal who had directed the defense of Moscow, the battle of Stalingrad, the Kursk counteroffensive, and the final assault on Berlin now received Germany's formal submission in the ruins of its capital. The choreography of the evening placed Zhukov at the center, with Tedder signing for the Western Allies in the secondary position, a reversal of the Reims arrangement that was precisely the point. For the Soviet domestic audience, the images of Zhukov presiding over Keitel's submission were the visual confirmation that the Soviet Union had been the war's principal victor in Europe, a narrative the ceremony was designed to cement.

### Why do the Western Allies celebrate VE Day on May 8 and Russia on May 9?

The difference is calendrical, not substantive. The Berlin signing took place at 11:45 p.m. Central European Time on May 8, which was already May 9 by Moscow time, two hours ahead. The Western Allies dated their commemoration from the local time of the ceasefire and the signing as experienced in London, Paris, and Washington: May 8. The Soviet Union dated its commemoration from Moscow time: May 9. Both commemorations refer to the same moment, and neither side has ever disputed the other's dating as a matter of historical fact. The formal ceasefire bound all commands at the same hour; scattered fighting continued in places such as Czechoslovakia for several days afterward, but no additional day of organized operations occurred. The separate traditions have endured because commemoration follows the national clock, and the national clocks differed. Contemporary Russian Victory Day observances on May 9 and Western VE Day observances on May 8 are therefore both correct. The distinction is frequently misunderstood as reflecting a disagreement about the surrender's validity, but it reflects nothing more than the two-hour difference between Central European Time and Moscow time applied to a ceremony that straddled midnight.

### How many German soldiers surrendered and where were they held?

Approximately five million German military personnel were still in uniform in May 1945, and the great majority surrendered in the weeks following the May 7-8 signings as the OKW ceasefire directives reached each theater. The approximate distribution of prisoners was some 2.5 million in Soviet custody, about 2 million in American custody, and about 1 million in British custody, with smaller numbers held by the French and other Allied forces. These figures are necessarily approximate given the chaos of the final weeks, but their proportions are well established. The prisoners were held in a vast archipelago of camps: the Soviet camps stretched across the USSR, the American and British camps concentrated in Germany and France. Processing five million men, feeding them, screening them for war crimes suspects, and managing their eventual release was one of the largest logistical operations of the immediate postwar period, and it continued for years in the Soviet case long after the Western prisoners had gone home.

### What happened to the German troops who surrendered in the east?

German prisoners in Soviet custody faced catastrophically worse conditions than those in Western hands, which was precisely the outcome Dönitz had tried to prevent. Approximately one million German prisoners died in Soviet captivity from starvation, disease, exposure, and overwork in the labor camps to which they were sent. The survivors were held for years; the last German prisoners were not repatriated until the mid-1950s. The Soviet treatment of German prisoners was shaped in part by the German treatment of Soviet prisoners earlier in the war, in which millions had died in German camps, and the cycle of atrocity and reprisal is one of the Eastern Front's bleakest legacies. By contrast, German prisoners in American and British custody were generally treated in accordance with the Geneva Convention, and the great majority were released by the end of 1945. The disparity vindicated, in the narrow sense, Dönitz's assessment of what Soviet captivity meant. It does not vindicate his attempt to secure a discriminatory surrender, which the Allies rightly refused as incompatible with coalition unity.

### Why did the Allies arrest the Dönitz government on May 23?

The Allies arrested the Flensburg government because its military utility had expired and its political continuation was intolerable. For three weeks after the surrender, the Allies had tolerated the rump administration as the instrument through which ceasefire orders reached the remaining German forces. Once the surrender was fully implemented and the prisoner processing underway, that justification lapsed. What remained was a government headed by Hitler's designated successor, staffed by men who had administered the Nazi war effort to its end, claiming a continuity of German state authority that the Allies had every reason to extinguish. Unconditional surrender meant that no German authority survived the capitulation, and the continued existence of even a nominal German government contradicted the political settlement. The arrests were conducted on May 23 without resistance: Dönitz, Jodl, Keitel, and the remaining ministers were taken into custody and the administration was dissolved. Several of the arrested men would stand trial at Nuremberg within the year.

### What crimes was Dönitz convicted of at Nuremberg?

Dönitz was tried at Nuremberg and sentenced to ten years imprisonment. The tribunal convicted him on counts relating to crimes against peace, specifically the planning and waging of aggressive war, and war crimes connected with the conduct of the U-boat campaign, including the issuance of orders affecting the treatment of survivors of torpedoed ships. He was acquitted on the count of crimes against humanity. The sentence reflected the tribunal's judgment that his role in sustaining the war effort, as commander of the navy and then as head of state, outweighed the mitigating consideration of his role in ending it. The ten-year term was among the lighter sentences imposed on the major defendants, reflecting the tribunal's assessment that Dönitz was primarily a military professional rather than an architect of Nazi ideology, but it was an unambiguous criminal conviction. He served the full term and was released in 1956. The verdict stands as the Allies' final judgment on the duality of his May 1945 role: useful as an instrument of surrender, culpable as a leader of the regime.

### Which signing, Reims or Berlin, is legally the operative surrender?

The Reims signing of May 7 is the legally operative surrender, and the Berlin ceremony of May 8 was a confirmatory ratification. This is the consensus position in the scholarship, supported by Beevor's account and the documentary record. The Reims instrument was signed by Jodl with Dönitz's explicit authorization, accepted by Bedell Smith for the supreme Allied commander, and countersigned by the Soviet liaison present. It ordered all German forces to cease hostilities at a specified hour, and the OKW directives that implemented it gave it operational effect on every front. No German commander questioned its authority, and no Allied authority treated the Berlin document as superseding it in law. The Berlin ceremony cured the procedural defects Stalin had identified, the unauthorized Soviet signature, the Western location, the absence of the service chiefs, but it did not create a new legal act. Eisenhower's formulation at the time, that Reims was operative and Berlin confirmatory, has been sustained by subsequent historical judgment. The dual signing was politics conducted through procedure, and the procedure was sound.

### How did the Casablanca unconditional surrender policy shape the May negotiations?

The Casablanca formula, announced in January 1943, was the foundation on which the entire May 1945 negotiation rested. By committing the Allies publicly to accept nothing less than Germany's unconditional surrender, it created a commitment device that bound each ally to the others: none could make a separate peace without breaking a public pledge. Dönitz's strategy in May was an attempt to circumvent exactly this commitment, and Eisenhower's refusal was the application of a policy settled two years earlier, not an improvisation. The formula also shaped the form of the surrender documents, which were short military instruments rather than negotiated treaties, because the political terms had already been fixed at Casablanca, Tehran, and Yalta. Without the Casablanca commitment, the Western Allies might well have been tempted by Dönitz's offer; the short-term military convenience of a western surrender was real. The policy existed to remove that temptation, and in May 1945 it worked as designed. The surrender was the execution of a plan made in 1943, and its success vindicated the foresight of the commitment.
