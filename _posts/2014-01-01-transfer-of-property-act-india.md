A buyer of land in India usually passes through a long corridor of documents before owning anything: a conversation, a receipt for earnest money, an agreement to sell, perhaps possession of the plot or the flat, and at the end of the corridor, if the transaction is done properly, a registered deed of conveyance. The Transfer of Property Act, 1882 is the statute that assigns legal meaning to every step in that corridor, and its central message is uncomfortable for the buyer who stops halfway. Payment is not ownership. Possession is not ownership. Even an agreement in writing, signed and stamped, is not ownership. Under this Act the corridor has exactly one door that opens into title, and that door is the registered instrument. The great majority of Indian property litigation is generated by people who paid, occupied and waited, and then discovered that the law had never counted them as owners at all.

![The Transfer of Property Act 1882 and what a buyer owns at each stage between an agreement to sell and a registered conveyance - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-58.webp)

The Act is Act 4 of 1882. It received the assent that made it law on 17 February 1882 and came into force on 1 July 1882, and it carries one hundred and thirty-seven sections arranged in eight chapters. Its preamble states its business in one line: it is an Act to amend the law relating to the transfer of property by act of parties. Every word in that phrase does work. The statute governs transfers, which means deliberate dispositions of property from one living person to another. It governs transfers by act of parties, which means it has almost nothing to say about property that moves without any act of transfer at all, property passing on death, property seized by a court decree, or property taken by the state. And it is a statute about property in general, movable and immovable, although its most consequential chapters, on sale, mortgage and lease, are built around immovable property and it is for immovable property that it is read daily.

What a reader can take from the full treatment that follows is a working model of the whole machine: what the Act counts as a transfer, what may be transferred and what may not, who is competent to transfer, the conditions a transferor may and may not attach, the doctrines that protect a buyer against a seller whose title is defective, the precise legal content of sale, mortgage, lease, exchange, gift and actionable claim, and the sequence that separates a contract to convey from a conveyance. The article keeps returning to the distinction the Act itself treats as fundamental, because it is the distinction that decides real disputes: a contract creates rights against a person, while a transfer creates rights in the property, and Indian law refuses to let the first be mistaken for the second.

## What the Transfer of Property Act governs, and what it leaves outside

The operative definition sits in section 5. In the Act, the expression transfer of property means an act by which a living person conveys property, in present or in future, to one or more other living persons, or to himself and one or more other living persons, and to transfer property is to perform such act. The section then explains that a living person includes a company or association or body of individuals, whether incorporated or not. Three boundaries are drawn by that single provision. The transferor must be living, the transferee must be living, and the act must be a conveyance the parties perform themselves. A transfer to a person not yet in existence is handled, if at all, through the special machinery of section 13, which is examined below. A transfer that operates only on death is a will, and wills live in succession law, not here.

The savings in section 2 draw the outer fence. Nothing in the Act affects the provisions of any enactment not expressly repealed by it. Nothing in it disturbs terms or incidents of a contract or constitution of property that are consistent with the Act and allowed by the law in force. Nothing in Chapter II, the general chapter on transfers by act of parties, affects any rule of Muhammadan law, a saving that reappears in the gift chapter and matters in practice. And, save as provided by section 57 and Chapter IV, nothing in the Act reaches a transfer by operation of law or by or in execution of a decree or order of a court of competent jurisdiction. A sale held by a court in execution of a decree, a devolution of property on insolvency, a vesting that a statute itself performs: these move property without any act of parties, and the Act steps aside for them.

Compulsory acquisition is the largest modern example of property moving without a transfer in this sense. When the state takes land for a public purpose it does not buy the land under this Act at all; it exercises a sovereign power under its own legislation, and the framework reconstructed in the [land acquisition thematic guide](/2013/10/01/land-acquisition-law-india-guide/) governs that taking, its compensation and its consent rules. The contrast is instructive. A private buyer must find a willing seller and travel the corridor to a registered deed. The state, acting under acquisition law, needs neither, which is precisely why acquisition disputes are fought on the fairness of the taking rather than on the mechanics of conveyance, and why the doctrines in this Act, built for bargains between living persons, have no purchase there.

Succession sits on the same far side of the fence. Property that passes because its owner died, whether under a will or under the intestacy rules of a personal law, is not transferred by act of parties within the meaning of section 5. The Act therefore does not govern inheritance, and any account of it that drifts into who inherits a Hindu father's property has left the statute behind. Intestate and testamentary succession among Hindus runs through the Hindu Succession Act, whose most contested modern change, the conversion of daughters into coparceners by birth, is treated in the [account of the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005](/2016/10/15/hindu-succession-amendment-2005/). The boundary is not pedantry. A deed of gift executed by a living father is a transfer under this Act and must satisfy section 123. The same property reaching the same daughter on his death is succession, and this Act has nothing to say about it.

Two further limits shape the Act's real territory. The first is that the second chapter states general principles for all property, movable and immovable, while the chapters on sale, mortgage, lease, exchange and gift deal with immovable property, and the final chapter deals with a special class of movable property, actionable claims. Movable property generally passes by delivery and by the rules of the Sale of Goods Act and the Indian Contract Act; this Act touches it through the general chapter and through actionable claims. The second limit is territorial and federal, and it is substantial enough to deserve its own treatment later in this article: the Act did not extend of its own force to every part of India, states hold powers to exempt districts from its registration-linked provisions and to amend its application, and the result is that the Act is near-national without being perfectly uniform.

## Before the Act, and the amendments that rebuilt it

There was no general law of transfer before 1882. A conveyance in British India was judged by an unstable compound: the rules of English law and equity administered as justice, equity and good conscience in the mofussil courts, the personal law of the parties where the courts thought it applied, Regulation law on registration and stamps, and local custom that a party had to plead and prove. A Hindu's gift, a Muhammadan's mortgage, an English merchant's lease in a presidency town might each be governed by a different system, and the same transaction could be characterised differently in different courts. The codification programme of the nineteenth century, which had already produced the Indian Penal Code, the Codes of Civil and Criminal Procedure, the Indian Contract Act and the Indian Evidence Act, reached property last because property was where personal law, revenue law and English conveyancing practice overlapped most densely. The Act that emerged in 1882 was deliberately modest in method: it declared itself an Act to amend the law relating to transfer of property by act of parties, it defined and restated the rules it was confident of, it saved the rules of Muhammadan law in its general chapter, and it left the courts' equity jurisdiction alive beneath the text, which is why doctrines such as the clog on redemption are stated in the case law far more fully than in the sections that house them.

The amending history after 1882 falls into three movements. The first is the great restructuring of 1929. The Transfer of Property (Amendment) Act, 1929, Act 20 of 1929, carried into the statute the recommendations of the Special Committee that had reviewed the working of the Act, and its changes run through this article: it inserted section 53A and with it the statutory form of part performance, it added the Explanation to section 52 fixing when the pendency of a suit begins and ends, it added the proviso to section 58, clause (c), confining the mortgage by conditional sale to transactions whose condition is embodied in the sale document itself, and it recast large parts of the mortgage chapter, including the provisions on subrogation and on the rights of a mortgagee in possession. The second movement is the pair of fiscal and technical amendments at the turn of the present century: the Registration and Other Related Laws (Amendment) Act, 2001, which closed the unregistered route into part performance, and the Transfer of Property (Amendment) Act, 2002, which rewrote section 106 to end a generation of litigation over the form and period of notices to quit. The third movement is not an amendment of the Act at all but the growth of statutes around it that assume its concepts: the recovery legislation that lets secured creditors enforce mortgages without a suit, in the process cutting across section 69's careful allocation of sale powers; the benami legislation that struck at the split between ostensible and real ownership on which section 41 is built; and the state rent and tenancy codes that occupy the field the lease chapter leaves. A reader who knows only the 1882 text holds perhaps four fifths of the working law; the remaining fifth is this sediment of amendment and overlay, and the article flags each deposit where it lies across the path.

Section 4 connects the statute to its two neighbours. The chapters of the Act that relate to contracts are to be taken as part of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and the second and third paragraphs of section 54, together with sections 59, 107 and 123, are to be read as supplemental to the Indian Registration Act, 1908. That second limb is the statutory hinge on which this whole field turns. The Transfer of Property Act declares which transfers must be made by registered instrument; the Registration Act supplies the machinery, the office, the books and the consequences of default. Neither statute does the work alone, and most practical errors in Indian conveyancing are failures to read them together.

## The vocabulary the Act works with: section 3

A statute that decides disputes about old transactions needs its own dictionary, and section 3 supplies it. Immovable property is defined negatively: it does not include standing timber, growing crops or grass. The definition works with the wider law, under which land, benefits to arise out of land, and things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth are immovable. The exclusions are commercially serious. A sale of standing trees to be cut is a transaction in goods, not in land, and a buyer who treats it as a land purchase has chosen the wrong statute. An instrument is defined as a non-testamentary instrument, which is the Act's way of repeating that wills stand outside. Two definitions in section 3 carry a disproportionate share of the litigation: attested, and notice.

Attestation is defined with care because three chapters make validity depend on it. In relation to an instrument, attested means attested by two or more witnesses each of whom has seen the executant sign or affix his mark to the instrument, or has seen some other person sign the instrument in the presence and by the direction of the executant, or has received from the executant a personal acknowledgment of his signature or mark, and each of whom has signed the instrument in the presence of the executant. The definition adds that it is not necessary that more than one of the witnesses be present at the same time, and that no particular form of attestation is necessary. Every element has been litigated: whether the witness actually saw the signature or received an acknowledgment, whether the witness signed as a witness rather than as a scribe or an identifier, and whether signature in the executant's presence can be inferred. A mortgage deed or a gift deed that fails this definition fails altogether, which is why attestation is not a formality performed by bystanders after the real event but part of the real event.

### What counts as notice of a fact when buying property?

A person has notice of a fact when he actually knows it, or would have known it but for wilful abstention from an enquiry he ought to have made, or gross negligence. Registration is notice from the date of registration, possession of property is notice of the possessor's title, and notice to an agent is notice to the principal.

The first explanation makes the registration system do evidentiary work. Where a transaction relating to immovable property is required by law to be effected by a registered instrument and has been so effected, every person acquiring the property or any part of it, or any share or interest in it, is deemed to have notice of the instrument from the date of registration, provided the instrument has been registered in the manner prescribed by the Registration Act and the particulars have been correctly entered in the indexes the registering authority must keep. A buyer who omits the search of those indexes and then pleads ignorance of an earlier registered mortgage is not believed; the law treats the search he ought to have made as knowledge he already has. The second explanation performs the mirror operation for possession. A person acquiring immovable property is deemed to have notice of the title, if any, of any person who is for the time being in actual possession of it. This is why physical inspection of the property is a legal act and not a courtesy: the tenant in occupation, the relative living in the house, the cultivator on the field are each a walking notice that someone other than the seller claims an interest, and the buyer who does not ask them what they are doing there is fixed with what their answer would have been. The third explanation routes notice through agents, so that a fact communicated to a person's agent in the course of the business to which the fact is material counts as notice to the principal, subject to the exception for an agent who fraudulently conceals the fact from a party who would otherwise be charged. Together these rules convert the doctrine of notice from a question about what was in a buyer's head into a question about what a diligent buyer would have found, and they are the mechanism by which the Act rewards diligence and punishes its absence without requiring proof of anyone's inner state.

Attestation has a second life in the law of proof that conveyancers ignore at their clients' cost. A document that the law requires to be attested cannot be used in evidence until at least one attesting witness has been called to prove its execution, if such a witness is alive, capable of giving evidence and subject to the process of the court; the Evidence Act states this in its section on proof of documents required by law to be attested, and relaxes the requirement only where the document is registered and its execution by the person by whom it purports to have been executed is not specifically denied. The consequence is that a mortgage or gift deed proved in a suit decades later stands or falls on whether witnesses who signed as a courtesy at a family gathering can still be found and still remember the signature they attested, or whether the registration of the deed has carried the proof past that point. It is one more instance of the Act's recurring bargain: form is burdensome at the moment of the transaction and protective at every moment after it.

Section 3 also defines the actionable claim, the transfer of which closes the Act: a claim to any debt, other than a debt secured by mortgage of immovable property or by hypothecation or pledge of movable property, or to any beneficial interest in movable property not in the possession, either actual or constructive, of the claimant, which the civil courts recognise as affording grounds for relief, whether such debt or beneficial interest be existent, accruing, conditional or contingent. Arrears of rent, a book debt, the benefit of an executory contract for the supply of goods, a claim to money under a policy: these are property that cannot be touched or delivered, only assigned, and Chapter VIII gives them their own form of transfer.

## What may be transferred, and who may transfer it

Chapter II opens with a generous rule and then fences it. Section 6 declares that property of any kind may be transferred, except as otherwise provided by this Act or by any other law for the time being in force. The exceptions that follow in the same section are not drafting decoration; each one records a reason why a particular species of supposed property cannot be allowed to circulate. The chance of an heir apparent succeeding to an estate, the chance of a relation obtaining a legacy on the death of a kinsman, and any other mere possibility of a like nature cannot be transferred at all. This is the rule against trafficking in spes successionis, a mere hope of succession. An expectant heir has nothing, and the section prevents him from selling the nothing at a discount to a speculator who would then acquire an interest in harassing the family and contesting the succession. A mere right of re-entry for breach of a condition subsequent cannot be transferred to anyone except the owner of the property affected by the condition, which keeps forfeiture rights attached to the land they police. An easement cannot be transferred apart from the dominant heritage it serves. An interest in property restricted in its enjoyment to the owner personally cannot travel beyond him. A right to future maintenance, in whatsoever manner arising, secured or determined, cannot be transferred, because maintenance is protection for a person's subsistence and not an asset to be discounted. A mere right to sue cannot be transferred: the law will not let litigation itself become a commodity in which a stranger buys the quarrel, although a right to sue that is attached to property, or to a debt, travels with the property or the debt as an actionable claim. A public office cannot be transferred, nor can the salary of a public officer, whether before or after it has become payable. Stipends allowed to military, naval, air force and civil pensioners of the government, and political pensions, are equally locked. A transfer cannot be made if it is opposed to the nature of the interest affected, if it is for an unlawful object or consideration, or if the transferee is legally disqualified to be a transferee. And certain occupants whose right is by its nature untransferable, a tenant holding an untransferable right of occupancy, the farmer of an estate in respect of which default has been made in paying revenue, or the lessee of an estate under the management of a Court of Wards, cannot assign their interests at all.

The competence rule is section 7. Every person competent to contract and entitled to transferable property, or authorised to dispose of transferable property not his own, is competent to transfer such property either wholly or in part, and either in an unrestricted form or subject to conditions, to the extent and in the manner allowed and prescribed by any law in force. Competence to contract imports the Indian Contract Act tests of age, soundness of mind and absence of legal disqualification. A minor's transfer is not merely irregular; the Privy Council held in Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose that a minor's agreement is void, and a deed by a minor conveys nothing. A guardian may deal with a minor's property only within the powers a personal law or a court's permission allows, and a buyer from a guardian who skips those limits takes a voidable or void title depending on the regime that governs the family. Section 7 also contains the authorised-disposer limb: an executor, an administrator, an agent holding a power that authorises disposition, a liquidator, each transfers property that is not his own, and his competence is measured by the instrument or statute that authorised him. The buyer's question in every such case is the same: show me the authority, because section 7 makes authority, not possession of the deeds, the source of competence.

Section 8 states what a transfer carries with it when the deed is silent. Unless a different intention is expressed or necessarily implied, a transfer of property passes forthwith to the transferee all the interest which the transferor is then capable of passing in the property and in its legal incidents. For land, the legal incidents spelled out in the section are the easements annexed to it, the rents and profits accruing after the transfer, and all things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth. For a house, the incidents include the lock, keys, bars, doors, windows and other things provided for permanent use with it. For a debt or other actionable claim, the incidents are the securities for it, other than a security that is also a debt or actionable claim, and, where the debt has been sued upon, the decree and its fruits. The rule sounds obvious and decides real cases: a seller cannot convey the land and then claim the standing structure's fixtures as his own, a landlord selling a tenanted building conveys the right to the accruing rents, and a mortgagor's reversion carries what the Act calls incidents whether the deed lists them or not.

Section 9 closes the formalities gap from the other side: a transfer of property may be made without writing in every case in which a writing is not expressly required by law. The Act then proceeds to require writing, registered writing, and attestation for the transfers that matter most, in sections 54, 59, 107 and 123, so the practical field of section 9 is the smaller transactions and movable property. Its companion principle runs through the whole Act and is worth stating plainly: where the Act prescribes a form, the form is the transfer, and compliance is not a technicality about evidence but the event that moves the property.

## When the seller's title is defective: sections 41, 43 and their neighbours

The default rule of property law is unforgiving: no one gives what he does not have. A buyer from a person without title acquires no title, however honest the payment. The Act retains that default, because title has to come from somewhere, and then builds two carefully bounded exceptions for the buyer who dealt with the appearance of title rather than with title itself. The two exceptions protect different mistakes and must not be merged.

The first is section 41, the ostensible owner rule. Where, with the consent, express or implied, of the persons interested in immovable property, a person is the ostensible owner of such property and transfers the same for consideration, the transfer shall not be voidable on the ground that the transferor was not authorised to make it, provided that the transferee, after taking reasonable care to ascertain that the transferor had power to make the transfer, has acted in good faith. Every condition is a lock on the door. The real owner must, by consent express or implied, have clothed another with the appearance of ownership: placing a title deed in a son's hands and letting him deal with the world as owner, standing by while a manager sells and collects, allowing a benamidar to hold and transact. The transferee must have paid consideration. He must have taken reasonable care, which imports the section 3 standard: search of the registers, questions of the occupants, inspection of the deeds. And he must have acted in good faith, without notice of the real owner's interest. A real owner who never allowed the appearance gets his property back; the doctrine exists because, as between the owner whose conduct created the misleading appearance and the buyer who relied on it with diligence, the loss is placed on the person whose conduct made the loss possible.

The second exception is section 43, which the profession calls feeding the grant by estoppel. Where a person fraudulently or erroneously represents that he is authorised to transfer certain immovable property and professes to transfer such property for consideration, such transfer shall, at the option of the transferee, operate on any interest which the transferor may acquire in such property at any time during which the contract of transfer subsists. Nothing in the section impairs the right of a transferee in good faith for consideration who took without notice of the option. The mechanism is estoppel speeded up into a property rule: the transferor who sold what he did not have is not permitted, when he later acquires the very interest he pretended to sell, to tell his buyer that the sale was empty. The later-acquired interest feeds the earlier grant.

### What happens when a seller later acquires the property he sold without owning it?

If the transferor later acquires any interest in the property while the contract subsists, the transfer operates on that interest at the transferee's option. The transferee must have taken the transfer for consideration on the faith of the representation, and a later transferee in good faith, for consideration and without notice of the option, is not impaired.

The Supreme Court settled the relationship between section 43 and the spes successionis bar in Jumma Masjid, Mercara v Kodimaniandra Deviah, decided in 1962. Expectant heirs had transferred property representing that they held a present interest, when in truth they had only the hope of succeeding on a widow's death. The Court held that the transferee who took for consideration on the faith of the representation was entitled to the benefit of section 43 when the transferors acquired the interest, and that there is no conflict between the two provisions: section 6, clause (a), enacts a rule of substantive law about what interests can exist as transferable property, while section 43 enacts a rule of estoppel about what a transferor may be heard to say against his own representation, and the two operate in different fields. The Court added the limit that matters: a transferee who knew the transferor had no title cannot claim to have acted on the representation, and section 43 then has no application, so the transfer fails under section 6. The doctrine rewards reliance, not speculation with open eyes.

Sections 38 to 51 complete the general chapter with rules for the situations that recur whenever title is imperfect or divided. Section 38 protects a transferee where the transferor was authorised to transfer only under circumstances that were alleged to exist: a transfer for consideration stands, as against the persons interested, as if the circumstance had existed, provided the transferee acted in good faith after reasonable care and the circumstance was not one the transferee could have verified by enquiry without disproportionate difficulty. Section 39 addresses transfers by a person against whose property another holds a right to receive maintenance or a provision for advancement or marriage: the transferee takes subject to that right if he had notice of it or if the transfer was gratuitous, while a transferee for consideration without notice is protected. Section 44 gives a transferee from a co-owner the transferor's share and, subject to the conditions the section states, the right to joint possession and to enforce a partition. Sections 45, 46 and 47 work out what joint transferees and co-owners with distinct interests take when consideration comes from a common fund or from separate funds. Section 48 states the priority rule for successive transfers of the same property: where a person purports to create by transfer at different times rights in or over the same immovable property, and such rights cannot all exist or be exercised to their full extent together, each later-created right shall, in the absence of a special contract or reservation binding the earlier transferees, be subject to the rights previously created. First in time prevails, unless fraud, notice or a section 41 or 43 situation rearranges the order. Sections 49, 50 and 51 then deal with the proceeds of fire insurance on transferred property, rent bona fide paid to a holder under a defective title, and improvements made by a bona fide holder under a defective title, who on eviction by the true owner is entitled to the value of the improvements he made in the belief that he was the owner. The pattern across all three is the Act's instinct again: honest dealing founded on a defective title is protected to the extent of the honesty, and no further.

The priority rule of section 48 acquires its real-world texture from the registration system beneath it. Between two documents relating to the same property, the Registration Act gives a registered document effect against an unregistered one, and a registered deed, once registered, operates from the time it was executed rather than from the later date of its registration, so the race is to execution and registration both, in that order of inquiry. A first buyer who leaves his deed unregistered for months while a second buyer takes and registers a conveyance from the same seller will usually find that the second deed is the one the law can see, and his remedies collapse into a personal claim against a seller who has already spent the money twice. Section 48 is written as a rule about the order of creation; in practice it is a rule about the order of publicity, and the doctrines of notice are the bridge between the two, because the second buyer who knew of the first transaction, or who would have known of it from the possession of the first buyer or the state of the register, cannot convert his later registered deed into a priority the Act never gave him.

One modern statute has cut across section 41's territory and a buyer who relies on the doctrine must know of it. The benami legislation, the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988, as recast in 2016, prohibits transactions in which property is held by one person for the benefit of another who paid for it, subject to the exceptions it states for family arrangements and fiduciary holdings, and it bars the real owner from suing to recover benami property except within those exceptions. Section 41 presupposes that an owner may let another stand as ostensible owner and then protects the outsider who bought from the appearance; the benami statute attacks the arrangement itself. Where the holding is benami within the prohibition, the person who paid cannot set up his hidden ownership against anyone, and the transferee's protection comes to rest less on section 41 than on the fact that the hidden claim is itself legally disabled. The two statutes meet awkwardly, because the Act of 1882 was written for a world in which name-lending was a normal incident of family property, and the later law was written to end that world; in practice the buyer should treat any gap between the name on the deed and the person receiving the price as a question to be closed before payment, not as a doctrine to be invoked after it.

## Conditions, contingencies and the unborn: the rules for transfers that reach into the future

A transferor frequently wants to give property and to keep a hand on it after the gift: to direct how it shall be enjoyed, to forbid its sale, to make it depend on an event, to reserve it for persons not yet born. Sections 10 to 34 are the Act's answer, and the answer has a shape. The transferor may shape the gift, but he may not contradict it. A condition that negates the substance of what has been given is struck out, and the gift stands free of it.

Section 10 takes alienation first. Where property is transferred subject to a condition or limitation which restrains the transferee, or any person claiming under him, from parting with or disposing of his interest in the property, the condition or limitation is void, except in the case of a lease where the condition is for the benefit of the lessor or those claiming under him. The text has always been read as aimed at a restraint that in substance takes away the power of alienation altogether, because alienability is an incident of ownership and an owner who cannot sell is a contradiction. Restraints that are partial, confined to a class or a period or a manner, stand on different ground. The Supreme Court's treatment of a housing society's bye-laws in Zoroastrian Co-operative Housing Society Ltd v District Registrar, Co-operative Societies (Urban) illustrates the line: a member who had voluntarily taken a plot under bye-laws that confined transfers to persons qualified for membership, or back to the society, had accepted a qualified self-imposed restriction, and the Court held that such a restriction was not the kind of restraint section 10 voids. Alongside the lease exception, section 10 carries a second proviso of its period: where property is transferred to or for the benefit of a woman who is not a Hindu, Muhammadan or Buddhist, the transfer may impose a condition that she shall have no power during her marriage to transfer or charge the property or her beneficial interest in it. Courts construe restraints by asking what in substance they prevent. A condition that the buyer shall never sell to anyone is void. A condition that the buyer shall not sell outside the family at a dictated price can be equally void because it makes alienation practically worthless, while a genuine right of first refusal in favour of the transferor, operating for a limited period at a market price, is generally treated as a partial restraint and upheld. The doctrine is one of substance, and drafting around it by degrees fails at the point where the degrees add up to a prohibition.

Section 11 handles the twin error, the condition that dictates enjoyment. Where, on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created in favour of a person, and the transferor directs that such interest be applied or enjoyed by that person in a particular manner, the transferee is entitled to receive and dispose of the interest as if there were no such direction. A grantor who gives land outright and adds that the donee shall never build on it, or shall always use its income for a purpose the grantor prefers, has tried to give and to govern at once; the section takes the gift and discards the government. Two qualifications complete it. Where the direction is made for the beneficial enjoyment of a second piece of property belonging to the transferor himself, the transferor may enforce it against the transferred land; this is the root of the restrictive covenant discussed below. And in a lease, directions for the lessor's benefit survive, because a lease is a transfer of a limited right to enjoy, and its terms are its substance.

Section 12 voids a condition that makes an interest determinable on the transferee's insolvency or on his attempted transfer or disposition, saving again the case of a lease condition for the lessor's benefit. A grantor may give property until the donee goes bankrupt, meaning he may limit the interest by its own terms; what he may not do is give a full interest and annex a clause that snatches it back on the donee's financial failure or attempted sale.

Sections 13 and 14 govern gifts to the future, and they are read together in every examination hall and every succession dispute in the country. Section 13 provides that where, on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created for the benefit of a person not in existence at the date of the transfer, subject to a prior interest created by the same transfer, the interest created for the benefit of such person shall not take effect unless it extends to the whole of the remaining interest of the transferor in the property. The unborn person cannot be given a life interest, and no interest can be created directly in favour of someone who does not exist: the transfer must first create a life interest (or successive life interests) in persons living at the date of the transfer, and the unborn beneficiary, if he is to take at all, must take the entirety of what remains, subject only to those prior life interests. A transfer that gives a life interest to an unborn person fails in that part, because the Act requires the gate to the future to be narrow: living life tenants, then an outright vesting in a person who has come into existence by the time the last life interest ends.

Section 14 then stops the dead hand at a measured distance. No transfer of property can operate to create an interest which is to take effect after the lifetime of one or more persons living at the date of such transfer and the minority of some person who shall be in existence at the expiration of that period, and to whom, if he attains full age, the interest created is to belong. That is the rule against perpetuity. A transferor may postpone vesting for the whole of the lives of persons alive at the transfer, and then for the minority of a beneficiary who is alive when those lives end, minority ending at eighteen years. He may not postpone it a day longer. The reasons are policy, and they are stated openly in the case law: property must remain in commerce, and a settlement that ties land to conditions for a century withdraws it from the market, from creditors and from the living who might use it better. The Privy Council's examination of the machinery in Sopher v Administrator-General of Bengal shows the second condition doing its work: the period of grace is measured by the minority of a person in existence at the expiry of the lives in being, so a settlement that builds its timetable on the childhood of a person not yet conceived at that point fails. Three escapes surround the rule. Section 18 disapplies sections 14, 16 and 17 where the transfer is for the benefit of the public in the advancement of religion, knowledge, commerce, health, safety or any other object beneficial to mankind, so a perpetual charitable endowment is valid in a way a perpetual family settlement is not. Section 17 separately polices directions to accumulate income: a direction that income be accumulated for longer than the life of the transferor or a period of eighteen years from the date of the transfer, whichever is longer, is void as to the excess, with exceptions for directions serving the payment of the transferor's debts, the raising of portions for his children or remoter issue, and the preservation or maintenance of the transferred property. Section 16 provides that where an interest is created to take effect after or in default of another interest created by the same transfer, and the prior interest fails for a reason the Act names, the later interest fails with it.

The distinction between vested and contingent interests, sections 19 to 24, is the vocabulary in which these gifts are actually fought over. An interest is vested when it is created in favour of a person without specifying a time when it is to take effect, or in terms specifying a time that is bound to happen, or when the transfer declares it is to take effect forthwith or on the happening of an event which must happen. A vested interest is not defeated by the death of the transferee before he obtains possession; it is already his, and it passes to his heirs. A contingent interest, under section 21, takes effect only on the happening of a specified uncertain event, or on an uncertain event not happening, and becomes vested when the event happens or becomes impossible, as the case may be. Section 20 gives the unborn child who later takes under section 13 a vested interest on his birth, unless a contrary intention appears. Section 26 treats a condition precedent as substantially complied with where it has been fulfilled in substance, a rule that saves gifts from being lost to trivial mismatch, while section 29 insists that a condition subsequent, which divests an interest already vested, be strictly fulfilled, so that forfeiture provisions are read against the person who drafted them.

Sections 25 to 34 supply the grammar of conditional transfers, and their asymmetry is the point to carry away. A condition precedent, one on whose fulfilment the transfer depends, is treated indulgently: section 26 deems it substantially complied with when it has been fulfilled in substance, so that a gift to a nephew if he completes a degree is not lost because the university's name or the precise year differed from the deed's expectation. A condition subsequent, one that divests an interest already vested, is treated with hostility: section 29 requires strict fulfilment, because forfeiture is disfavoured and the person who drafted the divesting clause must bring the case precisely within it. Sections 31 and 33 to 34 then work the edges: a condition subsequent that is impossible, immoral or otherwise invalid cannot divest what has vested, a transfer conditional on the performance of an act within a stated time is defeated if the act is not done in time, and where no time is stated the transfer fails only if the transferee makes performance impossible or indefinitely postpones it by his own conduct. Section 15 performs the parallel work for transfers to a class: where an interest is created in favour of a class of persons and fails in regard to some of them for a reason the perpetuity sections name, it fails in regard to those persons only, a change made in 1929 from the harsher original rule under which the failure of one member's share destroyed the whole class gift.

Section 35, the doctrine of election, disciplines the transferor who tries to give away what is not his in the same document that benefits its owner. Where a person professes to transfer property which he has no right to transfer, and as part of the same transaction confers any benefit on the owner of the property, such owner must elect either to confirm such transfer or to dissent from it; and if he dissents, he must relinquish the benefit so conferred, and the benefit so relinquished reverts to the transferor or his representative, subject, in the cases the section states, to a charge for making good to the disappointed transferee the amount or value of the property attempted to be transferred to him. A testator of arrangements who leaves his own house to a stranger and leaves the stranger's house, in the same instrument, to its owner by way of consolation cannot have both generosity and possession: the owner must take under the instrument and let his house pass, or take against it and give the benefit back. The doctrine is equity's refusal to let a person approbate and reprobate the same transaction.

Two short sections tidy the consequences when an income-bearing property changes hands midstream. Section 36 provides that, in the absence of a contract or local usage to the contrary, all rents, annuities, pensions, dividends and other periodical payments in the nature of income are, for the purposes of transferor and transferee, deemed to accrue day by day and to be apportionable accordingly: the seller keeps the income of the days before the transfer and the buyer takes the income of the days after it, though the payment itself may be made whole to whoever is entitled to demand it on the day it falls due. Section 37 completes the adjustment where the property is severed and an obligation's benefit is divided among several owners, apportioning the benefit among them according to the value of their shares and protecting a debtor who pays the whole where he has had no notice of the severance. These are the Act's book-keeping rules, rarely read and constantly applied, because every tenanted building sold between rent days presents the question they answer.

Section 40 deserves separate statement because it is the working rule for the restrictive covenants printed in every modern development agreement. Its first limb provides that where a third person is entitled to the benefit of an obligation arising out of contract and annexed to the ownership of immovable property, but not amounting to an interest in the property or an easement, such obligation may be enforced against a transferee with notice of it or a gratuitous transferee of the property, but not against a transferee for consideration without notice and without the obligation being annexed in the manner the section contemplates. Its second limb protects the enforcement of a contract for the sale of immovable property against a subsequent transferee in corresponding terms. The English root of the first limb is Tulk v Moxhay, where a covenant that Leicester Square's garden be kept as a garden was enforced against a purchaser who had bought with notice of it. In the Act's hands the principle is that a negative promise about land, a promise not to build above a height, not to use the plot for a trade, not to block a neighbour's light, runs with the land against later hands that knew of it, while a purchaser for value without notice takes free. Positive obligations, promises to spend money or maintain a road, do not run in the same way, which is why developers' counsel spend their skill drafting covenants in negative form.

## What a buyer owns at each stage

Everything said so far converges on a sequence, and the sequence is the most practically useful thing in this field, because property disputes in India are almost never about the destination; they are about a journey abandoned at one of its earlier stations. The table below sets out the corridor stage by stage: what interest the buyer holds, what the seller remains free to do, what protection the buyer has, and what the buyer must do to move to the next stage. Its assumption throughout is a sale of tangible immovable property of a value of one hundred rupees and upwards, which is every real transaction, governed by section 54.

| Stage | What the buyer has | What the seller can still do | The buyer's protection | The buyer's next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oral understanding | Nothing enforceable about the property; at most a claim if a concluded oral contract and its breach can be proved | Sell to anyone, mortgage the property, walk away | None conferred by this Act; the general law of contract alone | Reduce the bargain to a written agreement |
| Written agreement to sell | A personal right against the seller to have the sale completed; no interest in or charge on the property (section 54) | Sell or encumber the property, subject to his contract liability and to the buyer's right to enforce the contract | A suit for specific performance under the Specific Relief Act; section 40 against a later transferee with notice; section 53A if its conditions are later met | Register the agreement where part performance protection is wanted; move to execution of the deed |
| Advance paid | The same personal right; the payment is evidence of the contract and of part performance of it | The same, though a court will treat a paid bargain with increased seriousness in specific performance | Earnest money recoverable on the seller's default; the payment strengthens the claim to a decree | Complete the balance and the deed rather than enlarge the advance |
| Possession delivered | Possession as a fact, held at the seller's sufferance under the contract; still no title | Retake the property by due process subject to the contract; sell the title to a third party, whose position then depends on notice | Section 53A, if the agreement is written, signed, and registered as the 2001 amendment requires, and the buyer has performed or is willing to perform; the possessor's presence is notice to later buyers under section 3 | Convert possession into title by a registered conveyance before disputes mature |
| Unregistered conveyance signed | A document that does not affect the property: a deed compulsorily registrable but unregistered cannot transfer the ownership it recites | Treat the property as still his, because in law it is; a second registered sale to another buyer defeats the first document | Only the contract rights the paper evidences, and specific performance while the seller still holds title | Register within the Registration Act's time limits or obtain a fresh registered deed |
| Registered conveyance | Ownership: the whole interest the seller was capable of passing, with its legal incidents under section 8 | Nothing in respect of the property, except what the deed reserves or a separate contract gives him | Title itself, ranked by section 48 against competing transfers, and enforceable against the world | Mutation in the revenue and municipal records, which records the title for fiscal purposes but does not create it |

Two stages repay a second look because they are the ones that generate litigation. The first is possession. An Indian buyer who has paid most of the price and moved his family in feels, with complete human justification, that the flat is his. The table shows what he holds in law: facts, not title, plus a statutory shield whose conditions are examined in the part performance discussion below. His danger is precise and statutory. The seller remains owner, and an owner can mortgage, gift, or resell; each later transferee's position then turns on notice, and the buyer's own possession is his best notice to the world, which is why the cases repeat that a buyer who skips verifying who is in occupation has disabled his own protection. The second stage is the unregistered conveyance, the saddest document in Indian law: a full deed, stamped or half-stamped, signed by everyone, often with a ceremony, which the Registration Act and section 54 combine to treat as legally inert for passing title. It is evidence of a contract. It is not a transfer. The distance between those two sentences is the distance across which fortunes are lost.

## Sale: the registered instrument, and the sentence that governs buyers

Chapter III, sections 54 to 57, is short and carries the Act's centre of gravity. Section 54 defines sale as a transfer of ownership in exchange for a price paid or promised or part-paid and part-promised. It then prescribes the mode. Such transfer, in the case of tangible immovable property of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards, or in the case of a reversion or other intangible thing, can be made only by a registered instrument. In the case of tangible immovable property of a value less than one hundred rupees, the transfer may be made either by a registered instrument or by delivery of the property, delivery taking place when the seller places the buyer, or such person as he directs, in possession of the property. The hundred-rupee threshold dates from 1882 and has never been revised, so it divides nothing in practice: every sale of land or a building that any reader will meet can be made only one way, by a registered instrument. The instrument itself, its presentation, its stamping and the register that receives it belong to the [companion account of the Registration Act and land titling](/2014/01/15/registration-act-land-titling-india/), which owns the machinery; this article owns what the machinery is for, namely the single moment at which ownership moves.

The section ends with the sentence on which this entire field rests. A contract for the sale of immovable property is a contract that a sale of such property shall take place on terms settled between the parties. It does not, of itself, create any interest in or charge on such property. Indian law thereby refuses the equitable conversion that English law developed, under which a specifically enforceable contract for the sale of land made the buyer owner in equity and the seller a trustee of the legal estate. The Indian buyer under an agreement to sell has rights against the seller: he may sue for specific performance and ask a court to compel the conveyance, he may claim damages, he may protect possession within the limits of section 53A. He has no rights in the land. If the land is destroyed before conveyance, if a third party acquires it and takes the statutory protections, if the seller's creditors descend, the agreement-holder stands in the queue of personal claimants rather than in the position of an owner. The drafting is deliberate, and its consequences were restated for the modern property market by the Supreme Court in Suraj Lamp and Industries Pvt Ltd v State of Haryana, decided on 11 October 2011 and reported at (2012) 1 SCC 656. The Court held that transactions of the nature of a sale agreement combined with a general power of attorney and a will, the SA/GPA/WILL pattern by which urban property changed hands for decades to avoid stamp duty, registration charges and transfer restrictions, do not convey title and do not amount to a transfer; immovable property can be legally and lawfully transferred only by a registered deed of conveyance; such arrangements can at best be treated as an existing agreement of sale, usable to seek specific performance or to defend possession under section 53A, and genuine transactions of agency, where a power of attorney is given to manage property or to complete a transaction on the owner's behalf, are a different thing and stand unaffected. The judgment is the modern authority for the oldest rule in the Act: the corridor ends at the registered deed, and every document before it is preparation.

The personal right the agreement gives was itself strengthened from an unexpected direction in 2018. Until then, specific performance under the Specific Relief Act, 1963 was a discretionary remedy, and a seller who preferred to pay damages could often keep the land. The Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 substituted section 10 of that Act so that specific performance of a contract shall be enforced by the court subject to the provisions the amended Act names, converting the buyer's claim from an equitable favour into the ordinary consequence of breach. Nothing in that change touches section 54 of this Act: the agreement still creates no interest in the property, and the buyer who wins his decree must still take a conveyance, in default of the seller's execution through the court, and have it registered before he owns anything. What changed is the reliability of the corridor's middle stages, not its end. An agreement that the seller cannot escape by tendering money is a stronger commercial instrument than one he can buy his way out of, and the amendment explains why registered agreements to sell have become the standard prelude to urban conveyancing rather than the informal token they were in smaller markets.

Section 55 supplies the default contract between buyer and seller where they have not made their own, and its allocations explain what the parties are really negotiating when they vary it. In the absence of a contract to the contrary, the seller is bound to disclose to the buyer any material defect in the property or in his title to it of which he is, and the buyer is not, aware, and which the buyer could not with ordinary care discover; to produce to the buyer on his request for examination all documents of title relating to the property which are in the seller's possession or power; to answer to the best of his information all relevant questions put to him by the buyer in respect to the property or its title; on payment of the amount due, to execute a proper conveyance at a proper time and place; between the date of the contract of sale and the delivery of the property, to take as much care of the property and all documents of title as an owner of ordinary prudence would take of his own; and on completion, to give the buyer such possession as the nature of the property admits, and to pay all public charges and rent accrued up to the date of sale and discharge encumbrances then subsisting, except where the property is sold subject to them. The buyer is bound to disclose to the seller any fact as to the nature or extent of the seller's interest of which the buyer is, and the seller is not, aware and which materially increases the value of that interest; to pay the purchase money at the time and place of completion; to bear losses from destruction or deterioration not caused by the seller after ownership has passed to him; and to pay public charges falling due after ownership passes. The seller is entitled to the rents and profits until ownership passes; the buyer is entitled, where ownership has passed before payment of the whole price, to a charge on the property for the price paid in advance, with interest, and to a like charge when he is improperly declined delivery. Section 56 gives a buyer of one of several properties subject to a common encumbrance the equity of marshalling: as against the seller, he may require the encumbrance to be satisfied out of the properties not sold to him so far as that can be done without prejudice to the encumbrancer or to later buyers. Section 57 empowers a court, on the sale of immovable property subject to encumbrances, to direct payment of incumbrance money into court and delivery of the property as if unencumbered, a machinery little used but conceptually important, because it shows the Act imagining the court, not the parties, sweeping the title clean.

One further limit on cancellation belongs in any practical account of sale. A registered deed, once executed and registered, cannot be cancelled by the seller acting alone. In Satya Pal Anand v State of Madhya Pradesh, reported at (2016) 10 SCC 767, the Supreme Court held that neither the seller nor the Sub-Registrar has authority to extinguish a registered sale deed unilaterally; the cancellation of a registered instrument is a matter for a civil court under section 31 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, and a registering authority that accepts a one-sided cancellation deed acts without jurisdiction. The symmetry with section 54 is exact. Only a registered instrument makes the transfer, and only a decree, or a fresh registered deed executed by both sides, can unmake it.

## Transfers under a cloud: lis pendens, fraud on creditors, and part performance

Three sections of Chapter II deal with transfers made while the property is already spoken for, by a court, by creditors, or by an earlier buyer in possession. Section 52 states the doctrine of lis pendens. During the pendency in any court having authority within India of any suit or proceeding which is not collusive and in which any right to immovable property is directly and specifically in question, the property cannot be transferred or otherwise dealt with by any party to the suit or proceeding so as to prejudice the rights of any other party thereto under any decree or order which may be made therein, except under the authority of the court and on such terms as it may impose. An Explanation added by the amending Act of 1929 fixes the period: pendency is deemed to begin from the date of presentation of the plaint or the institution of the proceeding, and to continue until the suit or proceeding has been disposed of by a final decree or order and that decree or order has been obtained, or has become unobtainable by reason of the expiration of the period of limitation prescribed for its execution. The doctrine is not that a pendente lite transfer is void; it is that the transferee steps into the transferor's shoes and takes the outcome of the litigation with the property, so that litigation cannot be defeated or endlessly multiplied by sales to strangers. The rationale is not notice, as the Privy Council explained in Faiyaz Husain Khan v Munshi Prag Narain, building on the English decision in Bellamy v Sabine; it is expediency and necessity, the impossibility of bringing suits to a conclusion if the subject matter could be alienated at every adjournment. A buyer of litigated property therefore buys the lawsuit as much as the land, and section 3's diligence rules, search of the court record as well as the register, are the only protection the system offers him.

Section 53 addresses the transfer designed to cheat creditors. Every transfer of immovable property made with intent to defeat or delay the creditors of the transferor shall be voidable at the option of any creditor so defeated or delayed, and nothing in the section impairs the rights of a transferee in good faith and for consideration. Where a transfer is made without consideration and with intent to defraud a subsequent transferee, it is voidable at that transferee's option, and the section preserves the operation of insolvency law. The intention is a question of fact read off the badges the common law has always collected: secrecy, haste, transfer to a close relation, retention of possession by the transferor, transfer of substantially everything the debtor owned on the eve of a claim. The Privy Council's decision in Musahar Sahu v Lala Hakim Lal fixes the other boundary: a debtor may lawfully prefer one genuine creditor over others, and a transfer in discharge of a real debt is not fraudulent merely because its timing disappoints the rest. Procedure disciplines the remedy; the Supreme Court held in Abdul Shukoor Saheb v Arji Papa Rao that a suit to avoid a transfer under section 53 must be brought in a representative capacity on behalf of all the creditors, not by one creditor for his private advantage, and that the charge of fraud must be pleaded and proved with the particularity fraud always requires.

Section 53A, part performance, is the Act's deliberate softening of its own strictness, and its history explains its limits. The section was inserted by the Transfer of Property (Amendment) Act, 1929, after the Privy Council, construing the law as it then stood, had declined to import the English equitable doctrine into India at large. As enacted it protected the transferee who had taken possession under a written contract and performed his part, even though the transfer had not been completed in the prescribed manner. In its present form it provides that where any person contracts to transfer for consideration any immovable property by writing signed by him or on his behalf, from which the terms necessary to constitute the transfer can be ascertained with reasonable certainty, and the transferee has, in part performance of the contract, taken possession of the property or any part of it, or, being already in possession, continues in possession in part performance and has done some act in furtherance of the contract, and the transferee has performed or is willing to perform his part of the contract, then, notwithstanding that the transfer has not been completed in the manner prescribed by law, the transferor or any person claiming under him is debarred from enforcing against the transferee any right in respect of the property other than a right expressly provided by the terms of the contract. The proviso preserves the rights of a transferee for consideration who had no notice of the contract or of the part performance.

The section's parentage matters because it explains why the Indian doctrine is narrower than the English equity it replaced. In English law the doctrine from Maddison v Alderson protected a party who had altered his position on the faith of an oral bargain about land, and it cut both ways, as sword and shield. When the Privy Council considered the matter in Ardeshir Mama v Flora Sassoon it declined to let the English equity override the Indian statutes of form at large, and the Special Committee whose recommendations produced the 1929 amendment chose to enact a confined version: written contract only, transferee only, defence only, and with possession as the visible fact that warns later purchasers. Section 53A is therefore not a general licence to enforce informal bargains about land. It is a statutory estoppel aimed at one sharp practice, the transferor who pockets the price, delivers the property, and then pleads the missing deed against the person he put in possession.

Every word of that has a case on it. The conditions, catalogued by the Supreme Court in decisions such as Shrimant Shamrao Suryavanshi v Pralhad Bhairoba Suryavanshi, require a contract for consideration, in writing and signed by the transferor, with terms ascertainable with reasonable certainty, possession taken or continued in part performance with an act in furtherance, and performance by the transferee or a subsisting willingness to perform. Willingness is not a formality: the Court held in Nathulal v Phoolchand that a transferee who has himself broken the bargain, who has not paid when payment was the substance of his promise, cannot shelter behind the section, because equity assists the party who stands ready, not the party who stands in default while pleading his possession. The section is a shield and not a sword: it debarres the transferor from displacing the transferee, but it confers no title and cannot be used by the transferee as plaintiff to eject a third party or to compel conveyance; his offensive remedy is a suit for specific performance, and the Supreme Court in Shrimant Shamrao confirmed that the shield survives even after the period for that suit has expired, since limitation bars remedies by action, not defences. The 2001 amendment then changed the ground on which the whole doctrine stands. The Registration and Other Related Laws (Amendment) Act, 2001, Act 48 of 2001, in force from 24 September 2001, inserted section 17(1A) into the Registration Act, providing that documents containing contracts to transfer immovable property for consideration, for the purpose of section 53A, shall be registered if executed on or after that commencement, and if not registered shall have no effect for the purposes of section 53A; the same amendment deleted from the proviso to section 49 of the Registration Act the words that had let an unregistered document be received as evidence of part performance, and amended the text of section 53A itself to remove the salvaging words about a contract that, though required to be registered, had not been registered. The result is a doctrine narrowed to its honest core: a buyer whose agreement is registered, who has taken possession under it and who stands ready to pay and perform, cannot be turned out by the seller for want of the final deed; a buyer on an unregistered agreement receives, from 2001 onwards, no protection from this section at all. The amendment was a fiscal measure as much as a doctrinal one, aimed at the practice of completing property deals on agreements precisely so that the deed, and with it stamp duty, would never have to happen.

## Mortgages: the transfer of an interest as security

Chapter IV, sections 58 to 104, is the longest chapter in the Act and the foundation on which institutional lending against land is built: the enforcement machinery that banks invoke against defaulting borrowers under later legislation, examined in the [account of the SARFAESI Act](/2013/02/15/sarfaesi-act-2002-explained/), presupposes at every step the mortgage, the charge and the priority rules that this chapter creates. Section 58, clause (a), defines a mortgage as the transfer of an interest in specific immovable property for the purpose of securing the payment of money advanced or to be advanced by way of loan, an existing or future debt, or the performance of an engagement which may give rise to a pecuniary liability. The transferor is the mortgagor, the transferee the mortgagee, the principal money and interest secured for the time being are the mortgage money, and the instrument, if any, by which the transfer is effected is the mortgage deed. Two features of the definition decide cases. The property must be specific: a floating allocation of whatever the borrower owns is not a mortgage under the Act. And what passes is an interest, not ownership: the mortgagor parts with a slice of his rights sufficient to secure the debt, and the residue of ownership, including the right to redeem the slice back, remains his.

The six kinds of mortgage are then set out in clauses (b) to (g) of section 58, and they are distinguished by three variables: whether possession passes, whether the mortgagor binds himself personally to pay, and what the mortgagee may do on default. In a simple mortgage, clause (b), the mortgagor does not deliver possession; he binds himself personally to pay the mortgage money and agrees, expressly or impliedly, that on his failure to pay the mortgagee may cause the property to be sold and the proceeds applied towards the debt. This is the standard security of Indian institutional and private lending: possession stays with the borrower, and the lender's remedy is a suit for sale through the court. In a mortgage by conditional sale, clause (c), the mortgagor ostensibly sells the property, on condition that on default of payment on a certain date the sale becomes complete, or that on payment the sale becomes void, or that on payment the buyer retransfers the property to the seller; a proviso added in 1929 confines the category, providing that no such transaction shall be deemed to be a mortgage unless the condition of retransfer is embodied in the very document which effects or purports to effect the sale. In a usufructuary mortgage, clause (d), the mortgagor delivers possession, or binds himself to deliver possession, and authorises the mortgagee to retain it until the mortgage money is paid and to receive the rents and profits of the property and appropriate them in lieu of interest, or in payment of the mortgage money, or partly in each; the mortgagee in possession is, in substance, repaid out of the land, and the mortgagor gives no personal covenant of the simple mortgage kind. In an English mortgage, clause (e), the mortgagor binds himself to repay the mortgage money on a certain date and transfers the property outright to the mortgagee, subject to a proviso that the mortgagee will retransfer it on repayment as agreed; it is the conveyancing form of England naturalised, absolute transfer and covenant to repay bound together with a right of retransfer. In a mortgage by deposit of title deeds, clause (f), a person in the towns of Calcutta, Madras and Bombay, and in any other town the state government notifies, delivers to a creditor or his agent documents of title to immovable property with intent to create a security on it; no writing is needed at all, and the deposit, made with the intention of creating security, is the whole transaction, which is why this form, the equitable mortgage of banking practice, dominates commercial lending in the notified cities. Any mortgage that is not one of these five is an anomalous mortgage under clause (g), a residual category of combinations and local forms whose rights and liabilities section 98 determines by the contract between the parties as proved, and, where the mortgage is in writing, by its terms, supplemented by local usage.

The boundary between the second kind and an outright sale with an agreement to repurchase has generated a jurisprudence of its own. In Pandit Chunchun Jha v Sheikh Ebadat Ali the Supreme Court laid down that where the sale and the agreement for repurchase are embodied in separate documents, the transaction cannot be a mortgage by conditional sale, because the proviso to clause (c) requires the condition to live in the same document that effects the sale; where both live in one document, the question is one of intention gathered from the document read with the surrounding circumstances, the relationship of the parties, the adequacy of the price, the presence of a debt. Vidhyadhar v Manikrao restated the point: the court asks whether the parties created the relationship of debtor and creditor, or of vendor and purchaser, and answers from the substance, because calling a transaction a mortgage when the parties made a sale would let a seller reclaim property he had genuinely parted with, and calling a mortgage a sale would let a lender keep land worth far more than the debt.

Section 59 prescribes the form. Where the principal money secured is one hundred rupees or more, a mortgage other than a mortgage by deposit of title deeds can be effected only by a registered instrument signed by the mortgagor and attested by at least two witnesses; where the principal is less than one hundred rupees, it may be effected either by a registered instrument so signed and attested or by delivery of the property. The deposit of title deeds stands outside the rule because it needs no instrument. Where parties to a deposit do reduce their bargain to writing, the writing changes everything if it is itself the contract of mortgage rather than a mere record of a deposit already made: the Supreme Court applied that distinction in State of Haryana v Navir Singh, as the Privy Council had before it in Rachpal Mahraj v Bhagwandas Daruka, holding that a memorandum recording a past deposit needs no registration, while a writing that embodies the terms on which the security is created must be registered to be effective.

The mortgagor's great right is section 60, the right of redemption. At any time after the principal money has become due, the mortgagor has a right, on payment or tender of the mortgage money at an appropriate time and place, to require the mortgagee to deliver up the mortgage deed and all documents in his possession or power relating to the mortgaged property, to deliver possession where the mortgagee is in possession, and to retransfer the property or execute and register an acknowledgment that the mortgagee's interest has been extinguished. The right continues until it is extinguished by act of the parties or by decree of a court. The courts police the right with the rule against clogs, expressed in the maxim that once a mortgage, always a mortgage. A term of the bargain, or of a contemporaneous arrangement, that in substance prevents the mortgagor from getting his property back on payment, or converts the mortgage into a sale on default, or reserves to the mortgagee an advantage that continues after redemption, is void as a clog on the equity of redemption.

### What is a clog on the equity of redemption?

A clog is a term of the mortgage bargain, or of an arrangement made with it, that fetters the mortgagor's right to recover the property on payment: a clause converting the mortgage into a sale on default, an oppressive postponement of redemption, or a collateral advantage surviving redemption. They are void: a mortgage is security, not a purchase in disguise.

The case law draws the line with care rather than by slogan. In Seth Ganga Dhar v Shankar Lal the Supreme Court upheld a term postponing redemption for eighty-five years, holding that a long term is not by itself a clog where the bargain was struck between parties dealing on equal terms and without oppression, while striking down the further clause that would have converted the mortgage into a sale if redemption was not completed within a short window after the term ended. In Pomal Kanji Govindji v Vrajlal Karsandas Purohit the Court refused to turn the inquiry into arithmetic: whether a term is a clog depends on the period fixed, the circumstances in which the mortgage was created, the economic and social conditions of the time, the financial position of the mortgagor and his relationship with the mortgagee, and the totality of the bargain. What the doctrine destroys is not hard bargaining but the use of the borrower's necessity to transform a loan into a land purchase at the loan's price.

The mortgagee's rights are the mirror of the security, and section 67 rations them by the kind of mortgage. The mortgagee has, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, at any time after the mortgage money has become due to him and before a decree for redemption has been made or the money has been paid or deposited, a right to obtain from the court a decree that the mortgagor shall be debarred of his right to redeem, which is foreclosure, or a decree that the property be sold. But the section withholds what the form does not carry: a usufructuary mortgagee as such can institute neither a suit for foreclosure nor a suit for sale, since his security is repayment from the rents he holds; a mortgagee by conditional sale can foreclose but cannot sue for sale, since his bargain was to become owner on default; a simple mortgagee can sue for sale but not foreclose, since his security never carried ownership. Section 68 gives the mortgagee a right to sue for the mortgage money itself in defined cases: where the mortgagor binds himself to repay it, where the property is wholly or partly destroyed or the security rendered insufficient by a wrong the mortgagee did not commit and the mortgagor fails to furnish further security, where the mortgagee is wrongfully deprived of the whole or part of his security, and where the mortgagor fails to deliver possession he bound himself to deliver. Sections 70 to 77 then work through accessions to the mortgaged property, renewal of leases comprised in it, the mortgagee's rights to spend on preservation and insurance, the proceeds of a revenue sale of the property or of its compulsory acquisition, and the accounting burdens of a mortgagee in possession, whose duty under section 76 is to manage as a person of ordinary prudence, to account for rents and profits, and to take the consequences of loss occasioned by his default.

The provisions between redemption and enforcement complete the mortgagor's side of the account. Section 60A lets a mortgagor who is entitled to redeem require the mortgagee, instead of retransferring to him, to assign the mortgage debt and transfer the property to a third person the mortgagor directs, the machinery by which a borrower refinances without a gap in the security. Section 61 preserves integrity: a mortgagor who has executed two or more mortgages in favour of the same mortgagee is not entitled, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, to redeem one without paying the others, while as between different mortgagees he may redeem each separately. Section 62 gives the usufructuary mortgagor his route back to possession when the mortgage money is paid or is satisfied out of the rents and profits in the mortgagee's hands. Sections 63 and 64 deal with accessions made to the mortgaged property while the mortgagee holds it and with renewals of leases the mortgagor had granted, treating both as subject to the security and available to the redeeming mortgagor. Section 65 implies on the mortgagor's side the covenants that his title is what he professes, that he will pay the public charges, that where the property is leasehold he will pay the rent and perform the lease conditions, and that he will indemnify the mortgagee against the consequences of his own defaults; section 65A, inserted in 1929, gives a mortgagor lawfully in possession a controlled power to lease the property in the ordinary course of management without destroying the security; and section 66 leaves a mortgagor in possession free of liability for deterioration, while making him answerable for acts that are destructive or permanently injurious to the property where the security is or would be rendered insufficient.

### When can a mortgagee sell the property without going to court?

Section 69 allows a sale without the court's intervention only in narrow cases: an English mortgage outside the statutory exclusions, an expressly conferred power where the property lay in Calcutta, Madras, Bombay or a notified town, and a mortgage in favour of the government. The selling mortgagee must give the notice the section prescribes and account for the proceeds.

The last group of provisions in the chapter governs the interaction of several securities over one property. Section 78 postpones a prior mortgagee whose fraud, misrepresentation or gross neglect induced a later lender to advance money on the property. Section 79, dealing with a mortgage expressed to secure a maximum amount for future advances, protects the prior mortgagee's advances made without notice of a later mortgage. Section 81 gives a subsequent mortgagee the equity of marshalling: if the owner of two or more properties mortgages them to one lender and then mortgages one or more of them to another, the subsequent mortgagee is entitled, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, to have the prior mortgage debt satisfied out of the properties not mortgaged to him, so far as the prior mortgagee's rights are not prejudiced. Section 82 states the converse discipline of contribution: where property subject to a mortgage belongs to two or more persons with distinct and separate rights of ownership, or one property is mortgaged first for one debt and again for another, the several properties are liable to contribute rateably to the debt secured, with a savings rule where a property that was mortgaged to secure one debt has, by a condition in a later transaction, been made security for that debt alone. Section 92 creates subrogation: a person who, being one of the persons entitled under section 91 to redeem, or a co-mortgagor, or a lender who advances money with an agreement that he shall be subrogated, redeems the property, steps into the shoes of the mortgagee he has paid off and acquires his rights against the mortgagor and the other security. Section 93 bars tacking, the practice by which a later mortgagee who later acquires the first mortgage could squeeze out the mesne mortgagee between them. Sections 83 and 84 let the mortgagor deposit the mortgage money in court and regulate when interest stops running against him.

Section 100 then distinguishes the charge, the security that is not a mortgage. Where immovable property of one person is by act of parties or operation of law made security for the payment of money to another, and the transaction does not amount to a mortgage, the latter person is said to have a charge on the property, and the provisions of the chapter that apply to a simple mortgage apply to the charge so far as may be. A charge is created by the decree of a court directing payment out of specified property, by a will charging legacies on land, by a maintenance arrangement made payable out of an estate. No interest in the property is transferred; the charge-holder has a right to be paid out of the property, enforceable like a simple mortgage, and the section protects a transferee for consideration without notice of the charge against its enforcement. The distinction from mortgage matters most at the moment of classification, since nearly every remedial rule in the chapter is borrowed wholesale for charges once the classification is made, and the difference reduces to the absence of a transferred interest and the consequences that absence carries for notice and for the rights of later transferees.

## Leases: a transfer of the right to enjoy

Chapter V, sections 105 to 117, governs the lease, a transaction every urban reader performs and few read. Section 105 defines a lease of immovable property as a transfer of a right to enjoy such property, made for a certain time, express or implied, or in perpetuity, in consideration of a price paid or promised, which is the premium, or of money, a share of crops, service or any other thing of value to be rendered periodically or on specified occasions to the transferor by the transferee, which is the rent. The transferor is the lessor, the transferee the lessee. The definition separates a lease from a licence, which confers no interest in the property and only a personal permission to use it; the Supreme Court's analysis in Associated Hotels of India Ltd v R N Kapoor directs attention to the substance of the arrangement and to exclusive possession: a person given exclusive possession of premises for a term, in circumstances showing an intention to create an interest in the property, is a lessee whatever the document calls him, while a permission to use premises that remain in the owner's legal possession creates only a licence.

Form follows the same pattern as sale. Section 107 provides that a lease of immovable property from year to year, or for any term exceeding one year, or reserving a yearly rent, can be made only by a registered instrument, and that all other leases may be made either by a registered instrument or by oral agreement accompanied by delivery of possession. Where a lease made by a registered instrument exists, the instrument must be executed by both lessor and lessee. The Supreme Court's decision in Anthony v K C Ittoop and Sons shows the mechanism when the form is not followed: an unregistered deed purporting to grant a five-year lease could not create the lease it recited, and the tenancy that in fact existed, by oral agreement and delivery of possession, fell into the second paragraph of section 107 and was treated as a monthly tenancy determinable under section 106. Section 106 supplies the duration where the parties have not: in the absence of a contract, local law or usage to the contrary, a lease for agricultural or manufacturing purposes is deemed to be from year to year, terminable by six months' notice, and a lease for any other purpose is deemed to be from month to month, terminable by fifteen days' notice. The section was substituted in its entirety by the Transfer of Property (Amendment) Act, 2002, Act 3 of 2003, with effect from 31 December 2002, to remove the crop of technical objections that had grown around notices: the period of a notice runs from the date of its receipt, a notice is not invalid merely because its period falls short of the statutory period if the suit or proceeding is filed after the full period has expired, and the section prescribes how notices must be given and served.

Section 108 sets out the rights and liabilities of lessor and lessee in the absence of a contract or local usage to the contrary, and its lessee's rights are the ones litigation uses. The lessor must disclose material defects in the property with reference to its intended use which the lessor is and the lessee is not aware of and which the lessee could not with ordinary care discover, and must put the lessee in possession on request. The lessee may, if the property is wholly destroyed or rendered substantially and permanently unfit for the purpose for which it was let by fire, tempest, flood, violence of an army or mob, or other irresistible force, treat the lease as void at his option. He may remove, even after the lease determines, all things he has attached to the earth while he is in possession, provided he leaves the property in the state in which he received it. He may transfer or sublet the whole or any part of his interest, unless a contract or local law forbids it, without ceasing to be liable on the lease unless the lessor accepts the assignee in his place. He must disclose facts materially increasing the value of the lessor's interest of which he is and the lessor is not aware, must pay the rent at the proper time and place, must keep and on determination restore the property in as good condition as it was when he was put in possession, ordinary wear and irresistible force excepted, must allow the lessor reasonable access for inspection and repair, and must not erect permanent structures or commit waste beyond what the section permits. The default code is a small fairness engine: most Indian tenancy disputes that are not captured by a rent statute are decided by reading section 108 against the documents the parties actually signed.

Duration itself is a term the parties command within limits the Act respects. A lease may be made in perpetuity: section 105 contemplates a transfer of the right to enjoy for a certain time, express or implied, or in perpetuity, and a permanent lease, once validly created by a registered instrument, binds successors on both sides and can be determined only as its own terms and the Act's determination rules allow. This is a marked contrast with the perpetuity rule of section 14, which does not strike at permanent leases, because the lessor's reversion remains a vested and transferable interest throughout: what the rule against perpetuity forbids is the postponement of vesting, not the length of an enjoyment that has already vested. The distinction explains the drafting habit of granting ninety-nine-year leases in development and ground-rent arrangements. A lease for a long fixed term vests at once, can be mortgaged and assigned as its terms permit, and avoids every difficulty that sections 13 and 14 place in the way of settlements on the unborn, at the price of an eventual hand-back the parties have priced from the start.

Section 109 governs the lessor's transfer of the property during the lease: the transferee acquires the lessor's rights, including the right to the rent falling due after the transfer, and the lessee cannot be worse off; if the lessee, having no notice of the transfer, pays rent to the old lessor, he is not liable again to the transferee. Section 110 excludes the day from which the term begins in computing it and handles a lease expressed to commence on a day on which its instrument is executed later. Section 111 collects the ways a lease determines: by efflux of the time limited by it; by the happening of an event on which it was made to determine; by the lessor's interest terminating or being disposed of; by merger, where the interests of lessee and lessor in the whole property become vested in one person in the same right; by express surrender, the lessee yielding his interest up to the lessor by mutual agreement; by implied surrender, as where the lessee accepts a new lease from the lessor to take effect during the continuance of the existing one; by forfeiture, where the lessee breaks an express condition that gives the lessor a right of re-entry, or renounces his character as lessee by setting up title in a third person or claiming title in himself, or is adjudicated insolvent where the lease gives the lessor a re-entry right on that event; and by the expiration of a notice to determine the lease or a notice to quit duly given by one party to the other. Sections 112 to 115 protect the edges: a forfeiture is waived by acceptance of rent that accrued after it, with knowledge of it; a notice to quit is waived by a later act showing an intention to treat the lease as subsisting; a court may relieve a lessee against forfeiture for non-payment of rent, and in some other forfeitures, on terms; and a surrender or forfeiture of the head lease does not, in itself, extinguish a sublease created in good faith, whose holder may hold under the lessor on the same terms instead.

The forfeiture provisions reward a closer look because they are where the chapter's equity shows. Where a lease is determined by forfeiture for non-payment of rent, section 114 lets the court relieve the lessee if, at the hearing of the landlord's suit for ejectment, the lessee pays or tenders the arrears together with interest and the landlord's full costs, or furnishes security for their payment within the period the court fixes; relief restores the lease as if the forfeiture had not occurred. Section 114A extends a narrower relief to forfeiture for breach of other express conditions that are capable of remedy: the landlord must first serve a notice specifying the breach and requiring the lessee to remedy it if it is capable of remedy, and only a failure to remedy within a reasonable time lets him sue. The exceptions matter as much as the rule: no relief is available for a breach of a condition against assigning, underletting, parting with possession or disposing of the property, nor where the lessee has renounced his character as tenant by setting up title in a third person or in himself. The scheme distinguishes the tenant who paid late from the tenant who denied the relationship, and it is the ancestor of the relief clauses written into every rent statute that followed.

### What happens if a tenant stays on after the lease has ended?

If the lessee remains in possession after the lease determines and the lessor accepts rent or otherwise assents to his continuing, the lease is renewed from year to year or from month to month according to its purpose, on the terms section 106 supplies. Without that assent the occupant is a tenant at sufferance, removable without the section's notice.

The chapter then makes its most consequential omission. Section 117 provides that none of the provisions of this chapter apply to leases for agricultural purposes, except in so far as the state government may by notification in the Official Gazette declare all or any of them to apply to such leases, together with the related provisions of the Act, a power states have used sparingly. Agricultural tenancy belongs to land revenue and tenancy codes, not to this Act. Within its field, too, the chapter operates as the general law underneath the rent control statutes that most cities have superimposed on it: those statutes protect sitting tenants, fix standard rents and restrict eviction grounds, and their history and the model tenancy framework that seeks to rebalance them are treated in the [account of rent control and the model tenancy legislation](/2013/12/15/rent-control-model-tenancy-act-india/). The practical reading is sequential: the lease chapter defines the tenancy, and the rent statute, where it applies, rewrites its termination.

## Exchange, gifts and actionable claims: the closing chapters

Chapter VI, sections 118 to 121, disposes of exchange in four sections by annexing the sale rules. When two persons mutually transfer the ownership of one thing for the ownership of another, neither thing, or both things, being money only, the transaction is called an exchange, and a transfer of property in completion of an exchange can be made only in the manner provided for the transfer of such property by sale. Section 119 protects the party who is deprived of the thing he received in exchange, in whole or in part, by reason of a defect in the other party's title: unless a contrary intention appears from the terms of the exchange, the party deprived may, at his option, require his counterparty either to return the thing originally transferred by himself, if it is still in that party's possession or power, or to pay compensation. Section 120 gives each party the rights and subjects him to the liabilities of a seller as to what he gives and of a buyer as to what he takes, and section 121 preserves the exchange of money, where the interest passing is simply the change of currency or coin.

Chapter VII, sections 122 to 129, governs gifts, the transfer without price, and disciplines it more strictly than any sale because nothing in the transaction tests the parties' seriousness except form. Section 122 defines a gift as the transfer of certain existing movable or immovable property made voluntarily and without consideration, by one person, called the donor, to another, called the donee, and accepted by or on behalf of the donee; such acceptance must be made during the lifetime of the donor and while he is still capable of giving, and if the donee dies before acceptance, the gift is void. Section 123 prescribes the mode: a gift of immovable property must be effected by a registered instrument signed by or on behalf of the donor and attested by at least two witnesses, while a gift of movable property may be effected either by a registered instrument signed as in the case of immovable property or by delivery of the property, in the manner in which goods sold may be delivered. Nothing in the section affects the validity of a gift of immovable property that consists of a share in undivided property made in the circumstances the section's savings recognise, and the concluding rule matters daily: section 129 provides that nothing in the chapter affects any rule of Muhammadan law, so that a hiba, a Muslim gift, remains valid on declaration, acceptance and delivery of possession without a registered instrument, a result that a century of decisions has preserved and that frequently surprises parties who treated the registration requirement as universal.

Three doctrines of the chapter repay separate statement. Section 124 splits a gift that mixes the present with the future: a gift comprising both existing and future property is valid as to the former and void as to the latter, because a man cannot give what does not yet exist to give. Section 126 governs revocation. The donor and donee may agree that on the happening of any specified event which does not depend on the will of the donor a gift shall be suspended or revoked; but a gift which the parties agree shall be revocable wholly or in part at the mere will of the donor is void wholly or in part, as the case may be. A gift may also be revoked in any of the cases, save want or failure of consideration, in which, if it were a contract, it might be rescinded: fraud, coercion and undue influence therefore undo gifts, while a donee's mere ingratitude does not, unless the deed itself made the gift conditional on an event that then occurred. Section 127 deals with onerous gifts, gifts of property burdened with obligations: a donee who accepts a single gift of several things, some of which carry burdens, takes them together or not at all where the transfer is one transaction, and a donee who is not competent to contract and accepts property burdened by an obligation becomes bound by the acceptance, and by his obligations in respect of it, when he becomes competent and, knowing of the obligation, retains the property. Section 128 makes the universal donee, the person to whom the donor gives his whole property, liable for all the debts due by and liabilities of the donor at the time of the gift, to the extent of the property comprised in it. The courts have added the practical glosses. Acceptance need not be theatrical: in Asokan v Lakshmikutty the Supreme Court held that acceptance may be inferred from the donee's conduct and the surrounding circumstances, and that the retention of the registered deed by the donor, without more, does not negate a gift the donee has accepted. In Renikuntla Rajamma v K Sarwanamma the Court upheld a registered gift under which the donor reserved possession and enjoyment for her own lifetime, holding that a donor may validly transfer the title while reserving a life interest, and that delivery of possession is not an element the section adds to a transfer completed by a registered and attested instrument.

The chapter's final saving is section 129 itself, which does two things at once: it preserves the rules of Muhammadan law from the chapter's formalities, and it saves donations mortis causa, gifts made in contemplation of death, from the chapter as well, leaving them to the law that governs gifts of that special kind, under which a gift over of property to take effect on the donor's deathbed is treated with the suspicion and the limits that succession law applies. Between them, the two limbs of section 129 show the draftsmen knowing exactly where their code should stop: at the boundary of a personal law they had undertaken in section 2 to respect, and at the boundary of death, where the Act's whole subject matter ends.

Chapter VIII, sections 130 to 137, returns to movable property of an incorporeal kind. Section 130 provides that the transfer of an actionable claim, whether with or without consideration, shall be effected only by the execution of an instrument in writing signed by the transferor or his duly authorised agent, that the transfer is complete and effectual on execution even before notice to the debtor, that the transferee may then sue or institute proceedings in his own name without the transferor's concurrence, and that the debtor who pays in good faith before receiving express notice of the transfer is discharged. The section's proviso protects a debtor who has notice through the instrument itself, and section 131 prescribes what the notice must contain. Section 132 fixes the transferee's exposure: he takes the claim subject to all the liabilities and equities to which the transferor was subject in respect of it at the date of the transfer, so an assignee of a book debt takes it with the set-offs the debtor held against the assignor. Section 133 makes the transferor's warranty of the debtor's solvency run only to the date of the transfer and only where consideration was given. Sections 134 and 135 route a mortgaged debt and the benefit of a fire insurance policy to the transferee. Section 136 protects the administration of justice by incapacity: no judge, legal practitioner or officer connected with any court of justice shall buy or traffic in, or stipulate for, or agree to receive any share of, or interest in, any actionable claim, and no court shall enforce such a bargain at his instance. Section 137 saves the negotiable instruments and mercantile documents of title that travel by their own law of endorsement and delivery.

## One Act, several versions: the state dimension

The Act's text pretends to a uniformity its first section disclaims. Section 1, as it stands after adaptation, extends the Act in the first instance to the whole of India except the territories which, immediately before 1 November 1956, were comprised in Part B States or in the States of Bombay, Punjab and Delhi, with power in a state government to extend it or any part of it by notification to the whole or any part of the excepted territories. The same section empowers a state government to exempt, retrospectively or prospectively, any part of its territories from the provisions of section 54, paragraphs 2 and 3, section 59, section 107 and section 123, the four provisions that make transfers depend on registered instruments. The exclusions were lived law rather than a footnote: in Punjab, where the Act was not extended wholesale for generations, its doctrines were applied by the courts as rules of justice, equity and good conscience, so that doctrines such as part performance and lis pendens operated there by judicial adoption rather than by statute, and local enactments supplied what the general Act would have given elsewhere. The Act reached the Union territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh with the reorganisation effected by Act 34 of 2019, in force from 31 October 2019, which extended it to those territories as part of aligning their private law with the rest of the Union.

State amendments then vary particular rules. Uttar Pradesh amended section 54 by its Act 57 of 1976, in force from 1 January 1977, deleting the hundred-rupee paragraphs and providing that a contract for the sale of immovable property in that state can be made only by a registered instrument, a reform that turns the agreement to sell itself into a registered document in the country's largest state and removes the ambiguity on which unregistered-deal litigation feeds. Maharashtra inserted section 1A by Bombay Act 57 of 1959 to settle the Act's application to transfers of agricultural land in the Saurashtra and Hyderabad areas of the then State of Bombay. Other states have amended thresholds, added local forms of mortgage, and supplemented the lease chapter with rent statutes of their own. The consequence for a practitioner is simple and frequently missed: this Act is read with its state application before it is read for its doctrine, and a deed perfectly regular in one state may be irregular across a border for reasons that have nothing to do with its fairness.

The machinery of commerce has nonetheless rebuilt itself around the final chapter. A modern lender does not merely take a mortgage of the borrower's land; it takes an assignment of the borrower's book debts, of the receivables under its supply contracts, of the sums payable under its insurance policies, and the legal engine for every one of those assignments is Chapter VIII read with the factoring and security-interest legislation that has grown beside it. The transferee of an actionable claim buys a lawsuit-shaped asset: he may have to sue for it in his own name, he takes it subject to the equities that bound his transferor, and a debtor who settles with the original creditor before receiving the notice the chapter prescribes is discharged as if the assignment had not happened. The chapter's rules about notice, equities and the incapacity of court-connected officers are therefore not antiquarian. They are the original Indian law of assignment, and the specialised statutes of secured transactions have borrowed their habits from it.

## The argument about strictness, and the verdict on the Act

The running criticism of this statute is that its formalism is a trap for the honest. A family that divides ancestral land by a deed everyone signed and nobody registered discovers generations later that the deed passed nothing. A buyer who pays an illiterate seller the full price and builds a house on the plot finds that the registered-deed rule ranks a later registered purchaser ahead of him. Part performance exists because Parliament itself recognised that an unqualified rule would reward sharp practice, and every softening provision in the Act, sections 41, 43, 50, 51 and 53A, is equity breaking through form where form would produce a result no court could defend. The critics of the strict rule speak from real cases, and their objection deserves its strongest form: a registration requirement taxes honesty with stamp duty and official delay, and in a country where title records are incomplete, insisting that one document alone moves ownership can freeze genuine arrangements while doing nothing to expose fraudulent ones.

The answer the record supports is the one the Supreme Court gave in Suraj Lamp. India has a system of presumptive title: registration records transactions, and no state authority certifies that the seller owned what he sold. In such a system the only defence a buyer has against competing claims is a bright public line marking the moment ownership moves, and a register that can be searched to find it. Every dilution of the rule multiplies the class of unrecorded, unsearchable claims that a later buyer cannot discover by any diligence, because an agreement or a power of attorney resting in a private drawer is invisible to the index search section 3 tells him to make. The Court in Suraj Lamp dissected what the GPA economy actually did: it avoided stamp duty and capital gains reporting, it defeated the transfer prohibitions imposed on allottees of public housing, it concealed the real parties from the record, and its beneficiaries were rarely the innocent purchasers of the rhetoric. The honest family division and the honest paid-up buyer are protected, but they are protected in the way the Act intends: by provisions that reward the visible, registered, diligent versions of their transactions, and the remedy for the family or buyer who did none of those things is regularisation by a registered deed, which Suraj Lamp expressly left open, and not the conversion of their papers into title by judicial sympathy.

The reform proposal that hovers over all of this is conclusive titling: a state register of owners, guaranteed by the state, from which title could be read off directly instead of reconstructed from a chain of deeds. Its advocates argue that the presumptive system this Act serves manufactures litigation, because every buyer must re-prove the seller's title through decades of paper, and every defect anywhere in the chain is a lawsuit in waiting. Its critics answer that a conclusive register is only as good as the adjudication that builds it, that converting a defective record into a guaranteed one by administrative fiat would extinguish genuine claims wholesale, disproportionately the claims of people whose holdings are customary, joint, or imperfectly documented, and that a state indemnity fund is a promise no budget has yet funded. The movement so far has been experimental and partial. Rajasthan enacted a certification of titles statute for urban land in 2016, the first conclusive titling legislation in the country, and the Union's policy institutions have circulated draft model legislation on conclusive land titling for states to adapt, while the national land records modernisation programme digitises the presumptive record rather than replacing it. The Transfer of Property Act is written for the presumptive world, and nothing in it would need to change for transactions to be recorded conclusively; what would change is the buyer's burden under section 3, since a guaranteed register would relieve him of the chain-of-title enquiry the doctrine of notice presupposes. Until a state actually builds such a register and funds its guarantee, the corridor this article has described remains the route ownership travels, and its tolls and traps remain the law of the transaction.

The deeper failure lies where the brief for this subject always locates it: not in the transfer law but in the fiscal law that prices people out of using it. Stamp duty on conveyances, levied by states as a percentage of a consideration the parties are tempted to understate, is the engine of the unregistered economy; registration charges and circle-rate valuations push the same direction. A state that wants registered titles behaves accordingly and lowers the duty on honest instruments, while a state that treats the deed as a revenue event manufactures the GPA market it then condemns. The Act of 1882 has survived because its drafts were built around a correct insight rather than around machinery that ages: separate the promise from the transfer, let the promise be enforced between the parties, let the transfer happen once, publicly, in a form the next stranger can find. The disputes do not arise because the rule is old. They arise because the transactions ignore it, and the Act's 137 sections, read with the diligence rules in section 3, amount to a single piece of advice given with statutory force: a reader holding deeds, drafts and stamped papers for a transaction in progress can check and organise them with care before signing, using the [office document viewer for opening deed copies, agreements and scanned records](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) where the files need to be read on a machine without office software. The general position set out in this article is legal information about a statute of general application; it is not advice on any individual transaction, for which the deed, the register and the limitation clock of the particular case have to be examined by a practitioner. Title is a sequence of acts done in the right order and in public. The statute that says so has outlasted every attempt to conduct the country's property business on trust instead, and on the evidence of its own litigation, it will go on being rediscovered, one disappointed corridor at a time, until the practice of conveyancing is finally conducted the way the Act has always prescribed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Transfer of Property Act govern?

The Act governs transfers of property by act of parties between living persons: sale, mortgage, lease, exchange, gift and the transfer of actionable claims, together with the general rules on what may be transferred, who may transfer, conditions, priorities and defective title. Its second chapter deals with property generally, while the sale, mortgage and lease chapters concern immovable property. It does not govern inheritance, wills, transfers by operation of law or by court decree, or compulsory acquisition by the state, which belong to succession law, execution law and acquisition law respectively. It is Act 4 of 1882, in force from 1 July 1882.

### Q: Who is competent to transfer property under the Transfer of Property Act?

Section 7 gives the rule. A person is competent to transfer property if he is competent to contract under the Indian Contract Act, meaning that he has attained majority, is of sound mind and is not disqualified by any law, and if he is either entitled to the transferable property or authorised to dispose of transferable property that is not his own. The second limb covers executors, administrators, guardians acting within their powers, agents holding an authority to dispose, and liquidators. A minor's transfer is void. A buyer from anyone in the second class should verify the instrument or order that confers the authority, because competence in such cases is exactly as wide as the authority, and no wider.

### Q: What property cannot be transferred under the Transfer of Property Act?

Section 6 lists the exceptions to the general rule that property of any kind may be transferred. The chance of an heir apparent succeeding to an estate, the chance of a relation obtaining a legacy, and any other mere possibility of a like nature cannot be transferred. A mere right of re-entry for breach of a condition subsequent can pass only to the owner of the property affected. An easement cannot be transferred apart from the dominant heritage. An interest restricted in enjoyment to the owner personally, a right to future maintenance, and a mere right to sue cannot be transferred. Public offices and the salaries of public officers cannot be transferred, nor can stipends and pensions of the kinds the section names, nor can any transfer be made that is opposed to the nature of the interest, for an unlawful object or consideration, or to a person legally disqualified to be a transferee.

### Q: Can property be transferred to a person who has not yet been born?

Yes, but only through the machinery of section 13, and never directly. The transfer must first create an interest for a person living at the date of the transfer, usually a life interest, and the interest created for the unborn person must extend to the whole of the remaining interest of the transferor in the property. An unborn beneficiary therefore cannot be given a life interest of his own, and cannot take at all unless he comes into existence before the prior interest ends; on his birth he acquires a vested interest under section 20 unless a contrary intention appears. The arrangement must also survive section 14, the rule against perpetuity, which confines the postponement of vesting to the permitted period.

### Q: What is the rule against perpetuity?

Section 14 states the rule. No transfer of property can operate to create an interest which is to take effect after the lifetime of one or more persons living at the date of the transfer and the minority of some person who shall be in existence at the expiration of that period, and to whom, if he attains full age, the interest created is to belong. In practice a transferor may tie property up for the whole lives of persons alive when he makes the transfer, and then until a beneficiary who is alive at the end of those lives turns eighteen. Vesting postponed beyond that point fails. Transfers for the benefit of the public in the advancement of religion, knowledge, commerce, health, safety or other objects beneficial to mankind are excepted by section 18, which is why a perpetual charitable endowment is valid where a perpetual family settlement is not.

### Q: What is the doctrine of election under the Transfer of Property Act?

Section 35 states the doctrine. Where a person professes to transfer property which he has no right to transfer, and as part of the same transaction confers a benefit on the owner of that property, the owner must elect either to confirm the transfer or to dissent from it. If he dissents, he must relinquish the benefit conferred, which reverts to the transferor or his representative, subject in the cases the section states to a charge for making good to the disappointed transferee the value of the property attempted to be transferred to him. The doctrine prevents a person from accepting the favourable part of one transaction while rejecting the part that burdens him: the instrument must be taken as a whole or not at all.

### Q: Who is an ostensible owner?

An ostensible owner is a person who, with the consent, express or implied, of the persons interested in immovable property, holds himself out and is held out as the owner of it, though the real ownership lies elsewhere. Section 41 gives the doctrine its force: a transfer for consideration by an ostensible owner is not voidable on the ground that the transferor was not authorised to make it, provided the transferee, after taking reasonable care to ascertain that the transferor had power to make the transfer, acted in good faith. The doctrine protects a diligent buyer against the real owner whose own conduct created the appearance of ownership, and it offers nothing to a buyer who skipped the search of the register or failed to question the persons in possession.

### Q: Does an agreement to sell create an interest in property?

No. Section 54 says so in terms. A contract for the sale of immovable property is a contract that a sale of such property shall take place on terms settled between the parties, and it does not, of itself, create any interest in or charge on such property. The buyer under an agreement to sell has personal rights against the seller: he may sue for specific performance, claim damages for breach, and, if he has taken possession under a registered agreement and performed or stands willing to perform his part, defend that possession under section 53A. He has no right in the land itself, and the Supreme Court confirmed in Suraj Lamp and Industries v State of Haryana that arrangements built on agreements to sell, powers of attorney and wills convey no title; ownership moves only on a registered deed of conveyance.

### Q: Is registration compulsory for the sale of immovable property in India?

Yes, for every transaction of practical significance. Section 54 provides that a sale of tangible immovable property of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards, and a sale of a reversion or other intangible thing of any value, can be made only by a registered instrument. For tangible immovable property worth less than one hundred rupees, the sale may be made either by a registered instrument or by delivery of the property. The threshold is a survival from 1882 and has never been revised, so in practice no sale of land or a building can be completed without a registered deed. Section 4 of the Act makes section 54 read as supplemental to the Registration Act 1908, which supplies the registration machinery. An unregistered deed for property above the threshold does not transfer ownership at all.

### Q: What happens when the same property is sold to two different buyers?

Section 48 states the governing principle: where a person purports to create by transfer at different times rights in or over the same immovable property, and those rights cannot all exist or be exercised to their full extent together, each later created right is, in the absence of a special contract or reservation binding the earlier transferees, subject to the rights previously created. Priority therefore generally follows the order of the deeds, and the second buyer who registers after an earlier registered sale takes nothing the first deed conveyed. The exceptions are themselves statutory: the first transferee may lose where his transferor was an ostensible owner within section 41, where section 43 feeds a later acquisition to an earlier transferee, where he is postponed for fraud, misrepresentation or gross neglect under section 78 in mortgage matters, or where the first buyer failed to take the precautions section 3 treats as notice and the second bought without notice of the first claim.

### Q: Can a seller cancel a registered sale deed unilaterally?

No. Once a sale deed has been executed and registered, the ownership it conveyed has passed to the buyer, and the seller acting alone cannot extinguish the deed or take the property back. The Supreme Court held in Satya Pal Anand v State of Madhya Pradesh that a Sub-Registrar has no authority to register a unilateral cancellation or extinguishment deed against a registered sale deed, and that cancellation of a registered instrument can be ordered only by a civil court in a suit under section 31 of the Specific Relief Act 1963, brought on grounds such as fraud or want of consideration within the limitation the Limitation Act prescribes. The parties may undo a sale jointly, by a registered deed of cancellation or reconveyance executed by both sides. A seller who believes his deed was procured improperly must sue; he cannot cancel.

### Q: What is the doctrine of lis pendens?

Lis pendens is the rule in section 52 that property which is the subject matter of pending litigation cannot be dealt with so as to defeat the outcome of that litigation. During the pendency of a suit or proceeding in a competent court which is not collusive and in which a right to immovable property is directly and specifically in question, the property cannot be transferred or otherwise dealt with by any party so as to prejudice the rights of any other party under the decree or order that may be made, except under the authority of the court and on terms it imposes. A transfer made during the pendency is not void, but the transferee takes the property subject to the result of the suit, stepping into the transferor's position whatever the decree turns out to be. Pendency runs from the presentation of the plaint until the decree is satisfied or becomes unenforceable.

### Q: How does part performance protect a buyer already in possession?

Section 53A debarres the transferor from enforcing, against a transferee who satisfies its conditions, any right in respect of the property other than a right expressly provided by the contract. The buyer must hold under a written contract signed by the transferor from which the terms of the transfer can be ascertained with reasonable certainty; he must have taken possession in part performance, or, being already in possession, continued in possession and done an act in furtherance of the contract; and he must have performed his part or be willing to perform it. The protection is defensive: the section gives the buyer a shield against eviction by the seller, not a title and not a sword against third parties. Since the 2001 amendment inserted section 17(1A) in the Registration Act, a contract executed on or after 24 September 2001 must be registered to earn this protection at all.

### Q: How many kinds of mortgage does Indian law recognise?

Six. Section 58 of the Transfer of Property Act sets them out in clauses (b) to (g). A simple mortgage leaves possession with the mortgagor, who binds himself personally to pay and agrees that the property may be sold on default. A mortgage by conditional sale is an ostensible sale that becomes complete on default or falls away on payment, with the condition embodied in the sale document itself. A usufructuary mortgage delivers possession to the mortgagee, who recoups interest or principal from the rents and profits. An English mortgage transfers the property outright to the mortgagee against his covenant to retransfer on repayment on a fixed date. A mortgage by deposit of title deeds is created by delivering title documents to the creditor in Calcutta, Madras, Bombay or a notified town with intent to create security. Every other form is an anomalous mortgage, governed by its own terms and local usage.

### Q: When can a mortgagor redeem a mortgage?

Under section 60, at any time after the principal money has become due. The mortgagor may then, on payment or tender of the mortgage money at an appropriate time and place, require the mortgagee to deliver up the mortgage deed and all documents relating to the property in his possession or power, to restore possession where the mortgagee holds it, and to retransfer the property or execute a registered acknowledgment that his interest is extinguished. He cannot redeem before the money is due if the mortgage fixes a term, because the right follows the bargain, but a term that converts the mortgage into a sale on default, or otherwise fetters redemption in substance, is void as a clog. The right endures until extinguished by act of the parties or by a decree of court, and a suit for redemption carries a limitation of thirty years under the Limitation Act.

### Q: What is the difference between a mortgage and a charge?

A mortgage is a transfer of an interest in specific immovable property to secure money advanced, a debt, or the performance of an engagement; the mortgagee receives a slice of the ownership itself. A charge, under section 100, arises where immovable property is by act of parties or operation of law made security for the payment of money to another and the transaction does not amount to a mortgage: no interest in the property is transferred, and the charge holder has only a right to have the money paid out of the property. Court decrees directing payment from specified property, legacies charged on land by a will, and maintenance obligations charged on an estate are typical charges. The practical distance is short, because section 100 applies the provisions governing a simple mortgage to a charge so far as may be, but the charge is weaker against later transferees: it cannot be enforced against a transferee for consideration who took without notice of it.

### Q: What is subrogation in a mortgage under the Transfer of Property Act?

Subrogation under section 92 is the substitution of one creditor for another. A person entitled to redeem, such as a subsequent mortgagee, a surety for the mortgage debt, a creditor who has obtained a decree for sale of the property, or a co-mortgagor, who redeems the mortgaged property, acquires on redemption the same rights as the mortgagee he redeemed had against the mortgagor and against any other mortgagee, so far as regards redemption, foreclosure or sale of the property. A stranger who advances money to the mortgagor to redeem a mortgage gets the same position only if the mortgagor has by a registered instrument agreed that he shall be subrogated. Subrogation keeps the redeemed security alive in the payer's hands instead of letting it collapse, which preserves the order of priorities among the remaining lenders. Section 93 accompanies it with a ban on tacking.

### Q: Does the Transfer of Property Act apply to agricultural land?

Only in part, and the boundary runs through the Act's own provisions and through state law. The general chapters apply to transfers of agricultural land as they apply to other property, and the Supreme Court reads the Act as a whole across land of every description. But section 117 withdraws leases for agricultural purposes from Chapter V altogether, unless the state government notifies that the chapter, or parts of it, shall apply, so agricultural tenancies are governed by state tenancy and land revenue codes. Section 1 also empowers state governments to exempt territories from the registration-linked provisions of sections 54, 59, 107 and 123, and states have amended the Act's application to agricultural land by local Acts, including Maharashtra's insertion of section 1A to settle its application in the Saurashtra and Hyderabad areas. The result is that no question about agricultural land should be answered from the central text alone.

### Q: What are the requirements for a valid gift of immovable property?

Three elements under sections 122 and 123. There must be a transfer of existing immovable property, made voluntarily and without consideration, by a donor to a donee. The transfer of immovable property must be effected by a registered instrument signed by or on behalf of the donor and attested by at least two witnesses, each of whom saw the donor sign or received his personal acknowledgment and signed in his presence. And the donee must accept the gift during the lifetime of the donor and while the donor is still capable of giving; if the donee dies before acceptance, the gift is void. Acceptance need not be express and may be inferred from conduct, as the Supreme Court recognised in Asokan v Lakshmikutty. A gift of future property is void as to the future part under section 124, and gifts under Muhammadan law stand outside the chapter by force of section 129.

### Q: What is an actionable claim under the Transfer of Property Act?

An actionable claim, as section 3 defines it, is a claim to any debt other than a debt secured by mortgage of immovable property or by hypothecation or pledge of movable property, or a claim to any beneficial interest in movable property not in the claimant's possession, actual or constructive, which the civil courts recognise as affording grounds for relief, whether the debt or interest is existent, accruing, conditional or contingent. Unsecured book debts, arrears of rent, the price due under a contract, and the money payable under an insurance policy are standard examples: property that cannot be delivered, only pursued. Chapter VIII governs its transfer. Under section 130 the transfer is effected only by an instrument in writing signed by the transferor, after which the transferee may sue in his own name; under section 132 he takes the claim subject to the liabilities and equities that bound the transferor at the date of transfer.
