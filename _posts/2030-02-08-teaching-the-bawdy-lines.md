Every classroom that takes up Romeo and Juliet meets the same moment, usually within the first week. The play opens not with the lovers but with two servants trading boasts, and the jokes they trade are bawdy jokes, built on double senses that a four-hundred-year-old shift in the language has half buried. Somewhere in the room, a student works out that a second sense exists, or half works it out, and looks up. What happens next is the subject of this article. The teacher can explain the joke, can skip it, can soften it into paraphrase, or can hope the moment passes unnoticed. Each of those choices teaches something beyond the line itself, because each tells the class what kind of book this is and what kind of reading the room practices. A skipped joke teaches that the edition cannot be trusted to say what the words mean. A leered-over joke teaches that the classroom is a place where embarrassment is entertainment. Between those failures stands the honest treatment, and the honest treatment has a precise shape.

![The servants' bawdy opening scene of Romeo and Juliet read aloud in a classroom - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-97.webp)

The verdict argued here is that the bawdy lines of Romeo and Juliet should be taught honestly, and that honesty has a level. A class should learn what kind of joke each joke is, who speaks it, where it stands in the play's architecture, and what work it performs in the hearing of the scenes around it. A class is not owed, and a teacher is not obliged to deliver, a full exposition of every second sense a pun can carry. That distinction, between mechanism and gloss, is the whole discipline of the subject. It satisfies the textual claim, because the bawdy is not decoration on the play but one of its load-bearing registers, the ground bass against which the lovers' lyricism is tuned and measured. It satisfies the cautionary claim, because a classroom is a compelled audience of minors, and compulsion changes what honesty requires: not less truth, but a governed dose, delivered at the level where the truth is about the play rather than about the students in the room. The history of the question supports the verdict. For two centuries the dominant technology was subtraction, the expurgated edition, and subtraction demonstrably falsified the text it claimed to protect. For the last half century the scholarly instruments, dictionaries, glossaries, and frank editions, have made the full record available to any teacher who wants it. The question is no longer whether the knowledge exists. The question is what a teacher does with it in a room of fourteen-year-olds, and that question deserves a reckoning rather than a shrug.

Romeo and Juliet is the most frequently assigned first Shakespeare in the schools of the United States and a standing set text in England, and its bawdy is therefore the most frequently negotiated bawdy in the canon. The negotiation happens along a fault line the play itself draws. The tragedy distributes its sexual language with great precision, giving the bawdy to servants, to an old nurse, and to a brilliant and doomed friend, while the lovers themselves speak a lyric idiom of light, pilgrimage, and worship. Any teaching that mutes the first register distorts the second, because the second was composed to be heard against the first. Any teaching that wallows in the first register betrays the room. The pages that follow map the bawdy's placement, trace the expurgation tradition that classrooms inherited, examine what the reference scholarship recovered, weigh the honest case against the cautious one, and set out the method the verdict requires, scene by scene, in the artifact named in the brief for this study, the InsightCrunch bawdy approach.

## Where Do the Bawdy Lines Sit in Romeo and Juliet? A Map by Speaker and Scene

The bawdy in Romeo and Juliet is not spread evenly through the text like seasoning. It is placed, and the placement is the first thing a class should learn, because the placement is the meaning. The play's opening words belong to Gregory: "Gregory, on my word, we'll not carry coals" (1.1.1). The exchange that follows between Gregory and Sampson, two Capulet servants killing time in a public place, builds from insult to boasting to the drawing of weapons, and the boasting runs on double senses from the start. Before any lover has been named, before the feud has been explained, the audience has heard the play's lowest register doing the play's foundational work: turning appetite into aggression. The servants' jokes and the servants' swords belong to the same gesture, and the scene ends in a street brawl that draws in both households, two old men in their gowns, and the Prince himself. A class that begins here, with the bawdy audible, begins with the tragedy's engine exposed. A class that begins with the scene trimmed begins with a brawl whose energy has no visible source.

### Which characters carry the play's bawdy, and which voices never touch it?

Three voices carry nearly all of it. Sampson and Gregory open the play in the register and vanish from it. The Nurse sustains it in a licensed, retrospective key, from the safety of age and service. Mercutio raises it to virtuosity, and his death in Act 3 ends the play's bawdy career.

The Nurse's bawdy occupies a different key from the servants', and the difference is a lesson in itself. In Act 1, Scene 3, in Lady Capulet's hearing, the Nurse tells and retells the story of Juliet's weaning and of a fall the toddler took, capping the memory with a jest, repeated with delight, that looks forward to the woman the child will become: the phrase that carries it is "fall backward" (1.3). The jest is about Juliet, spoken over Juliet, in the presence of Juliet's mother, and nobody in the room rebukes it, because the Nurse's age, her station, and her fourteen years of intimacy with the family license a freedom of speech that no one else in the Capulet household possesses. A class can be taught to see the license without being walked through the jest's content: what matters for reading the play is that the household's speech rules are visible, that an old servant may say in the great chamber what the daughter of the house may not think aloud, and that the play establishes this economy of permission in its third scene, long before the balcony. When the Nurse returns in Act 2, Scene 5, teasing Juliet with delay while the message from Romeo waits in her keeping, the same license powers the scene's comedy: the bawdy has become affection's vehicle, the household idiom in which love's news actually travels.

Mercutio is the register's virtuoso, and his bawdy needs separate mapping. In Act 1, Scene 4, on the way to the Capulet feast, he delivers the Queen Mab speech, beginning "O, then, I see Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4.53), a set piece of fantastical verse that begins in fairy miniature and darkens as it flies, desire shrinking to the dreams of courtiers, lawyers, soldiers, and maids. The speech is bawdy at its edges rather than in its grain, and classrooms have always found it teachable whole. His prose scenes are another matter. In Act 2, Scene 1, waiting under the orchard wall while Romeo hides, Mercutio calls to his friend in a conjuration whose terms parody the love poetry Romeo has been speaking, dragging the idiom of worship down into the idiom of appetite by main force. In Act 2, Scene 4 he greets the hour with the play's own name for the register: "the bawdy hand of the dial is now upon the prick of noon" (2.4). The Folger text, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, prints the line entire, and the line matters to this article for a reason beyond its wit. Shakespeare put the word "bawdy" in Mercutio's mouth as a term of description, not of shame. The play knows what register it is in, and says so. A fuller sorting of these passages, speaker by speaker, belongs to the companion study of [the play's bawdy language and its double meanings](/2025/01/24/bawdy-double-meanings-romeo-juliet/), and a concentrated account of Mercutio's share belongs to the study of [Mercutio's bawdy wit and wordplay](/2022/09/16/mercutio-bawdy-wit-wordplay/); the classroom question begins where those studies end, with the lines already in students' hands.

Two absences complete the map, and both are load-bearing. The first is Juliet. Juliet speaks about desire with a directness unmatched in the play, and she does it without a single bawdy joke. Her wedding-night speech opens "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1) and proceeds in the high lyric register, imperative, impatient, and entirely unembarrassed, yet its diction belongs to worship and to night, not to the street. The contrast is the play's central stylistic fact: the servants joke about the body, the Nurse remembers the body, Mercutio weaponizes the body, and Juliet simply wants, in language that treats wanting as a sacrament. The second absence is the Friar. Friar Laurence is the play's pharmacologist, the man whose long speech on herbs in Act 2, Scene 3 catalogues the properties of plants and men alike, and his verdict on Romeo's transferred affections is delivered in proverb, not innuendo: "Young men's love then lies / Not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3.67-68). A figure who deals in the body's chemistry all play long never touches the bawdy register at all. The map, completed, shows a play that has assigned its sexual language an address system: appetite below stairs and in the street, memory in the Nurse's chamber, virtuosity in the doomed friend, and silence, deliberate and meaningful silence, in the cell and at the altar.

Two refinements of the map matter for the classroom. The first is Romeo's brief apprenticeship in the register. In the first movement of Act 2, Scene 4, before the Nurse's entrance turns the scene, Romeo answers Mercutio in kind, trading jest for jest in the prose idiom of the street, and the exchange is the only sustained stretch of the play in which the lover speaks the bawdy's language as a native. The point for a class is the sequel: the apprenticeship ends the moment Romeo's marriage makes his desire a fact rather than a posture, and he never returns to the register. Read against Mercutio, who can speak nothing else, Romeo's trajectory through the play is a migration between idioms, from the Rosaline posturing Mercutio mocks, through the street's own tongue, into the lyric register of the orchard and the cell. A student who has only met a sanitized Romeo has met a static sighing figure; a student who has heard him hold his own in the prose duel has watched a boy change languages as he changes worlds, which is the play's actual subject. The second refinement is the parents' flash of the register. Lady Capulet's speech in Act 1, Scene 3, presenting Paris to Juliet through the extended conceit of the book that wants only a cover, carries a second sense the scholarship records, delivered by a mother to a daughter in a formal interview about marriage. The bawdy, that is, is not a lower-class contaminant in the play's world. It is the vernacular of the marriage market itself, spoken even by its managers when the goods are described plainly, and the scene teaches a class that the register's opposite is not respectability. The opposite of the bawdy in this play is not politeness. It is Juliet.

Even the play's frame belongs on the map. Before any servant speaks, the Chorus has delivered the Prologue in the most formal verse the play owns, a sonnet, and the sonnet's fifth line names the tragedy's origin in the body's plainest term: "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes" (Prologue.5). The ceremonial voice does not avert its eyes. The loins are in the sonnet, as the stars are, and the pairing is the play's whole method in miniature, fate and flesh in one line, each meaningless without the other. A teacher can make the point to a class in a sentence, and the sentence rearranges the room's assumptions: the bawdy is not a lapse in the play's dignity, tolerated in the low scenes and absent from the high ones. It is present at the summit of the form, in the mouth of the Chorus, in the fifth line of the work. The register system has no outside. Every voice in the play speaks from somewhere inside the economy the servants open, and the lessons that proceed as though the high scenes floated free of the low ones are teaching a play that does not exist.

## The Ground Bass: What the Bawdy Does Beneath the Lovers' Lyricism

Placement becomes argument when the map is read as a system. Romeo and Juliet is built on a register contrast as strict as its verse and prose contrast, and the bawdy is the lower term of that contrast. The lovers' lyricism does not float free. It is tuned, scene by scene, against speech that treats desire as appetite, transaction, and joke, and an audience that cannot hear the lower term cannot measure the upper one. When Romeo and Juliet meet at the feast and fall into a shared sonnet, two voices constructing a single perfect form out of the vocabulary of pilgrims and saints (1.5), the scene's miracle depends on the ear's memory of every coarser idiom the play has already used for the same subject. The sonnet is astonishing because the street has been heard. Remove the street, and the sonnet becomes merely pretty, a greetings-card sentiment that a class can admire without being changed by it. This is the sense in which the bawdy is a ground bass: a low, continuous line that most listeners do not attend to directly, whose removal collapses the harmony built above it.

### How does the opening servants' quarrel join sexual boasting to the feud's violence?

The join is mechanical, and it happens inside single speeches. The boasting measures manhood in two currencies at once, dominance over Montague's men and dominance over Montague's women, and treats the two as one scale, so the drawn sword reads as the boasting continued by other means, appetite become aggression without changing its language.

The feud point deserves its weight. Critics divide on whether the bawdy is the feud's decoration or its fuel, and the division is genuine. Coppélia Kahn, in her account of the play's masculinity, treats the bawdy as part of the feud's machinery rather than comic relief: the sexual boasting of the opening scene is the verbal form of the same competition that the swords settle, and the young men's idiom of conquest runs unbroken from the servants' talk to Tybalt's challenges to Romeo's killing frenzy after Mercutio falls. On the other side stands an older theatrical tradition, and the many editors and directors who have treated the opening scene's talk as dispensable colour, the groundlings' portion, cuttable in production and skippable in class without structural loss. The adjudication matters for teaching, because the two positions license opposite classroom decisions. Kahn's position is the stronger one, on the text's own evidence. The opening exchange is not detachable colour: it is the only scene in which the feud appears in its pure state, uncontaminated by love, inheritance, or grievance, two servants with no personal quarrel manufacturing a war out of idiom alone. The play needs its audience to have seen hatred produced by language before it shows them love produced by language, in the sonnet at the feast. A class that skips the first production will read the second as decoration. The bawdy, on this showing, is not the play's relief from seriousness. It is the play's first seriousness, wearing the mask its speakers can afford.

The register system also explains the play's famous turn. The bawdy does not fade from Romeo and Juliet; it is killed, at a specific moment, by a specific event. Mercutio is the register's virtuoso and its chief carrier, and when he dies in Act 3, Scene 1, cursing both houses in the idiom of a man still performing while the wound works, the play's sexual joking effectively ends with him. What follows is not a gradual sobering but a changed acoustic. The Nurse's later scenes are errands and griefs. The comic business of Act 4, Scene 5, Peter sparring with the musicians in a house of mourning, is comedy with the bawdy withdrawn, clowning about music and money in the presence of a supposedly dead girl. The tomb scenes are bare of the register entirely. Students who have been taught the bawdy's placement can be taught to hear this withdrawal as an event, the moment the tragedy's lower string is cut, and the hearing costs the teacher nothing but attention. Students who never heard the register cannot hear its absence, and the play's second half flattens for them into unrelieved gloom, the very quality that makes adolescents call the tragedy boring in the weeks after Mercutio's death. Every experienced teacher of the play knows the sag in the room's energy across Acts 4 and 5. The register argument gives that sag a diagnosis: the class has lost a voice it was never told it had.

There is a final structural service the bawdy performs, quieter than the others. It keeps the play's world populated. Verona, in the lovers' scenes alone, would be a city of six people and a friar, an airless chamber drama. The bawdy scenes are where Verona lives: servants killing time, a nurse gossiping in the street, musicians haggling at a funeral, the whole economy of a city in which bodies are born, fed, married, and buried by people who talk about bodies as their daily business. The lovers' tragedy lands as tragedy because it happens in that city, in earshot of that talk, and the talk is bawdy because the city's business is flesh. A sanitized text gives the class a Verona of principals only, a diagram rather than a place, and then wonders why the deaths diagrammed there move nobody. The ground bass is the city's noise. A teacher who mutes it has not protected the class from the play. The teacher has protected the class from the play's population, which is to say from the stakes.

Marriage sharpens the same point from the other side. The bawdy voices treat marriage, when they treat it at all, as appetite's settlement or the household's business: a bargain struck by parents, a burden cheerfully predicted by a nurse, a condition to be mocked by a bachelor wit. The play's actual marriage, by contrast, happens in a cell, in the clean diction of Friar Laurence, in a scene from which every trace of the bawdy has been excluded by the same playwright who filled the street with it. The exclusion is the argument. Shakespeare could have married his lovers in any idiom in his instrument, and he married them in the one register his bawdy speakers never touch, having first made certain that the audience had spent two acts hearing the alternative. A class taught the street hears the cell's quiet as a deliberate hush, the sound of the play changing its mind about what desire is for. A class protected from the street hears a merely administrative scene, a plot errand, and the tragedy's central claim, that this marriage is a different kind of event from everything Verona usually means by desire, goes unregistered. The ground bass is not an accompaniment to the play's meaning. At the points of maximum consequence, the wedding and the tomb, the ground bass and its withdrawal are the meaning, and both terms of the effect depend on the bawdy having been audible all along.

The register system has a physical signature that a class can be taught to hear, and it is the surest classroom evidence that the bawdy is architecture rather than decoration. The bawdy of Romeo and Juliet lives overwhelmingly in prose. The servants prose, the Nurse proses, Mercutio's great scenes are prose duels, and the play's verse, its sonnets, aubades, and epithalamia, is where the lovers live. Prose in this play is the medium of the body's world, of errands, appetites, money, and time; verse is the medium of the spirit's world, of worship, longing, and death. The two media interleave scene by scene like the play's two households, and a student trained to notice the switches has acquired an instrument that works on every scene without further instruction. The instrument catches what paraphrase destroys. When Romeo, fresh from the orchard, meets Mercutio in Act 2, Scene 4 and holds his own in prose, the class hears a young man still fluent in the street's world; when the same Romeo speaks verse to Juliet, the class hears the migration completed. Expurgated and softened texts flatten precisely this signature, since the prose scenes are where the cuts fall, and a text with its prose thinned teaches a play that seems to sigh in unbroken verse from first to last. The sighing play is the false one. The true play talks, in two tongues, and the bawdy is one of them.

The map is also an exit map, and following it gives a class the tragedy's shape in a single instrument. The lovers' story is a progressive departure from the bawdy's world. They meet inside a feast the street has prepared, marry in a cell the street cannot enter, and spend their one night in a chamber where the dawn is argued over in pure lyric: "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5.1). The aubade is the play's most intimate scene and its most formal, desire conducted as a debate about birdsong and light, with the register of the body nowhere in its diction. Juliet's potion speech in Act 4 is solitary lyric of the same purity, terror spoken in the high style. The pattern is exact: as the lovers move from the social world toward the tomb, the bawdy falls away behind them, station by station, until the silence of the vault is the last station of a journey the ear has been tracking all along. Students taught the register possess a thread through the whole tragedy, a way of hearing where they are in its world at any moment. Students denied it possess only the plot, and the plot, by itself, is the part of the play that a summary can carry, which is why summarized and softened schoolings so often end with classes who can recount the story and cannot say what the story felt like from inside.

## A Vocabulary That Died: Why Students Cannot Hear the Jokes Unaided

A difficulty stands in front of every pedagogical position on this subject, and it is linguistic before it is moral. The bawdy of Romeo and Juliet is largely inaudible to a modern student unaided, not because the student is innocent but because the language has moved. Double meanings depend on a word's second sense being alive in the hearer's ear, and the second senses of the 1590s died, in case after case, long before the plain senses did. Words narrowed, metaphors cooled, and whole families of innuendo that an Elizabethan apprentice caught in passing pass a modern reader in silence. The result is a curious classroom asymmetry. The jokes a teacher worries about are, for most students, jokes that will never be heard unless someone points at the place where a joke stands. The pointing is the decision. Silence does not preserve innocence in such a room; it preserves incomprehension, and it leaves the pointing to whoever else cares to do it, in whatever spirit, outside the room.

The recovery of the dead vocabulary was the work of lexicographers, and its history is itself an education in the subject's tensions. Eric Partridge, a lexicographer of slang, published Shakespeare's Bawdy in 1947, the first sustained attempt to catalogue the plays' sexual language in an essay and glossary addressed to serious readers. The book was a small and expensive production, a thousand copies in its first edition, and its appearance did not disturb the schoolroom: at the time of its publication, school editions of the plays were still bowdlerised, so the first full map of the bawdy circulated among scholars while the texts from which students actually read continued to omit the territory the map described. Partridge's own practice embodied the era's compromise. Stanley Wells, in Looking for Sex in Shakespeare (2004), notes with gentle malice that even Partridge, the great recoverer, resorted at points to sanitizing Latin in his explications, as though the glossary's compiler needed a fig leaf the playwright never wore. The criticism is just, and it is also a measure of the pressure the subject exerted: in 1947, an honest English account of the plays' sexual wordplay was still, in the strictest sense, unprintable in places, and Partridge printed most of it anyway.

The second great recovery came from inside the classroom, a fact too little celebrated. Frankie Rubinstein, whose A Dictionary of Shakespeare's Sexual Puns and Their Significance appeared in 1984, spent her working life as a schoolteacher, at Overbrook High School and the Philadelphia High School for Girls, and her dictionary, the fullest of its kind, was built by a woman who had faced the exact room this article discusses. Where Partridge had been tactful by reflex, Rubinstein was exhaustive by conviction, arguing that the second senses were part of the plays' significance and that to miss them was to misread. A. L. Rowse, reviewing the dictionary, endorsed the premise in terms a teacher can still use: not to appreciate the sexiness of Shakespeare's language, he wrote, impoverishes understanding of him. Gordon Williams then gave the subject its monumental form, A Dictionary of Sexual Language and Imagery in Shakespearean and Stuart Literature (1994), three volumes documenting the period's sexual vocabulary across its literature, later condensed into a one-volume glossary for Shakespeare alone. By the end of the twentieth century, no teacher could plead that the knowledge did not exist. The instruments were on the reference shelf of every serious library, compiled in part by a teacher, and the only remaining question was the use a school would permit itself to make of them.

Wells's 2004 audit introduces the necessary counterweight, and a serious article must adjudicate it rather than wave it past. Wells, a great editor and no prude, argued that the recovery had overshot in places: that some of the puns catalogued by the dictionaries exist only in the interpreter, and he posed the auditor's question of who is making the pun, the author through the character, or the interpreter through ingenuity. The question cuts, and every honest teacher should feel it cut, because a classroom that teaches an interpreter's invention as the playwright's fact commits the same offence as the expurgator, falsification, with the sign reversed. The adjudication is not difficult once the levels are separated. The core of the bawdy in Romeo and Juliet, the passages mapped in this article's second section, is not in dispute among Partridge, Rubinstein, Williams, and Wells: the speakers, the placement, and the register system stand on documentary ground, glossed in every modern scholarly edition. The disputed territory lies at the margins, in individual words where ingenuity outruns evidence. A teacher's obligation follows the same boundary. Teach the documented core with confidence, and where a sense is contested among the dictionaries, the contest itself is the teachable fact: students old enough for the play are old enough to learn that recovering a dead joke is detective work with rules of evidence, and that scholars argue about the evidence in print. That is not a diluted honesty. It is the only kind scholarship recognizes.

The segregation of the instruments deserves a paragraph of its own, because it governed classrooms for decades after 1947. The recovered vocabulary existed, in print, in English, compiled by scholars of standing, and yet the two halves of the settlement never met in the school: the dictionaries were priced and sized for the scholar's shelf, while the texts actually ordered for pupils continued to embody the older dispensation, their margins silent at the mapped passages. Even the Oxford English Dictionary, in its first edition across the turn of the century, had passed over many sexual words in silence, so the reference tradition's own flagship modelled the reticence the slang lexicographers were working against. The teacher of 1960 who wanted to know what a line meant could, in principle, consult Partridge. The teacher of 1960 who wanted an edition that admitted the question existed faced a school market still arranged around the protected reader. That lag, between knowledge available and knowledge admitted, is the precise space in which the classroom problem lived for most of the twentieth century, and it explains a fact about the profession's memory: generations of teachers were themselves taught from texts that hid the register, and then handed texts that hid it still, so that the silence reproduced itself as professional habit long after its scholarly justification had collapsed.

The recovery's descent into the classroom margin took a generation, and tracing it explains the modern edition's double nature. The scholarly editions of the later twentieth century, produced in the climate the dictionaries created, glossed the second senses as a matter of professional completeness: an edition that omitted them would have failed its reviewers, where an edition of 1900 that included them would have failed its market. The school editions followed at a distance, absorbing the frankness in diluted form, a gloss here and a silence there, so that the modern school margin is best read as a sediment: the expurgating century at the bottom, the recovery century settling over it, unevenly, edition by edition. E. A. Colman's work on the psychological dimension of the bawdy, in the decades after Partridge, marks the recovery's second phase, in which the question shifted from what the words mean to what the joking does to the jokers, the line of inquiry that leads directly to the classroom's own question about Mercutio's cruelty and the Nurse's license. The dictionaries answered the question of sense. The classroom inherits the question of function, and the inheritance is the recovery's unfinished business, which is why a pedagogy article, and not another glossary, is the recovery's proper next instrument.

## 1807 and the Invention of the Protected Reader: The Family Shakespeare and the Lambs' Tales

The expurgation tradition that classrooms inherited was not, at its origin, a school tradition at all. It was a family one, and its founding scene is a reading aloud. Thomas Bowdler remembered his father reading Shakespeare to the household and silently cutting as he read, passing over the difficult passages with such discretion that the family heard Lear, Hamlet, and Othello in delight without suspecting that anything had been omitted. The son's memory, recorded in the family's own account of itself, is the germ of the whole enterprise: the expurgator's first instrument was not the pen but the voice, skipping in real time, and the test applied was audibility in a family room. In 1807 the practice became a book. The Family Shakespeare appeared anonymously in Bath, in four small volumes covering twenty plays, edited, as its preface declared, to remove everything that could give just offence to the religious or virtuous mind. The editor was Henrietta Maria Bowdler, Thomas's sister, and the anonymity was itself an expurgation: a woman of reputation could not be known to have understood the passages she had removed, so her name was the first text the edition cut. Her preface stated the project's intended readers with a precision that should startle any teacher. The edition, she wrote, was one that "the parent, the guardian and the instructor of youth may place without fear in the hands of his pupils". The instructor of youth stands in the founding document of expurgated Shakespeare, named in 1807. The classroom did not adopt the Bowdlers' book as an afterthought. The book was addressed to the classroom from its first page.

### What exactly did the Bowdlers promise to remove, and what did they promise to leave untouched?

The promise had two clauses. Nothing was to be added to the original text, no substitute lines and no softened paraphrases of an editor's invention. Against that restraint, words and expressions were to be omitted wherever they could not with propriety be read aloud in a family. The method was subtraction only, under a single test.

The edition's honesty about its method is the one thing the tradition got right: the cuts were announced as a policy rather than disguised as the text. Thomas Bowdler took over the project and in 1818 published the enlarged edition under his own name, ten volumes covering the whole canon, and the second edition's career made expurgation a permanent feature of the Shakespeare landscape. He called the playwright, in his preface, an immoral author whose beauties were interwoven with defects, and he was honest about the limits of his method: for Othello, whose matter resisted the knife, he advised transferring the play from the parlour to the cabinet, from the family voice to the private eye. The first edition had removed roughly a tenth of the text it covered. The family's name, meanwhile, escaped the title page and entered the language: to bowdlerize, says the Oxford English Dictionary, is to expurgate a book by removing or altering material considered improper or offensive, and the verb arrived as an insult almost from birth, carrying the assumption that subtraction of this kind is a species of cowardice. Noel Perrin's Dr. Bowdler's Legacy (1969) remains the standard history of the expurgated book in England and America, and its evidence shows the practice ramifying for a century and a half through school texts, family editions, and children's versions, long after the Bowdlers' own volumes were superseded. The protected reader, invented at a Bath fireside, became the default reader of Shakespeare for generations of the young.

The same year, 1807, produced the gentler technology, and the comparison is instructive for teachers because the two methods still exhaust the softening options available to a classroom. Charles and Mary Lamb published Tales from Shakespeare, prose retellings "designed for the use of young persons", with a preface explaining that the tales were meant as an introduction to the study of Shakespeare and used his words wherever they could be brought in. Mary Lamb retold the comedies, fourteen of the twenty tales; Charles took the tragedies, among them this one. The retelling did by genre what the Bowdlers did by scissors. A prose tale of Romeo and Juliet keeps the feud, the meeting, the marriage, the deaths, and drops, without announcing any cut, the entire register system of the play: the servants cannot boast in narrative summary, Mercutio cannot duel in reported speech, and the Nurse's voice flattens into information. Sexual references and subplots were removed as a matter of the form's decorum, and the result was a beautiful, faithful story from which the play's ground bass had vanished more completely than any expurgator's knife could have managed, because there was nothing left to restore. Mary Lamb's name, like Henrietta Bowdler's, did not appear on the title page; it arrived only with the seventh edition, in 1838. The two women who did most to mediate Shakespeare to the young in 1807 were both, in their first editions, unnameable, and for the same reason: the culture that wanted the plays cleaned for daughters preferred not to know that daughters had done the cleaning. A teacher deciding how honestly to handle this material inherits both technologies and both silences. The scissors and the summary remain the live options wherever a department reaches for a school text, and the first step of honesty is recognizing them as choices with histories, not as the text itself.

The century between the Bowdlers and the modern scholarly edition filled the gap with an industry, and the industry's habits outlasted its founders. As the examination systems of the nineteenth century made set plays a fixture of schooling, a market in school Shakespeares grew to serve it, and the market's texts carried the expurgating assumption as a feature rather than a policy, the assumption that a student's copy is a different object from a reader's copy, filtered at the source. Perrin's history follows the expurgated book across the Atlantic and through this school trade, edition by edition, and the pattern his evidence establishes is the one the classroom inherited: each generation's school text was prepared by adapting the previous generation's, so the cuts of the age of delicacy persisted as compositorial habit into the age that no longer believed in them. The stage kept pace; the century's acting versions shortened and cleaned as a matter of course, and a student who saw the play in a theatre met roughly the text the school had issued. Expurgation, in short, was not a decision Victorian schools took about Shakespeare. It was the medium in which Shakespeare reached them, as water reaches a house, and the achievement of the twentieth century's scholarship was not to win an argument against the medium but to replace it, pipe by pipe, with editions built on an opposite assumption.

## The Editor Decides First: Glosses, School Editions, and the Margin

Long before a teacher speaks, an editor has already taken the classroom's first position on the bawdy, in the margin of the edition the department ordered. The modern scholarly edition is frank. René Weis's edition of the play for the Arden Shakespeare third series (2012), succeeding Brian Gibbons's Arden second series edition of 1980, supplies what its reviewers call expansive editorial glosses, on-page commentary that discusses the language's full range of sense as a matter of scholarly duty. The Folger text used throughout this article prints every line of the play and glosses for a general reader without flinching, and the Folger makes that text freely available, so that the complete play, apparatus and all, stands within reach of any student with a connection. Against this frankness, the school edition occupies a designed middle position. The great school series, Cambridge School Shakespeare, founded and edited by Rex Gibson, and Oxford School Shakespeare among them, print the full text of the plays and surround it with activities, staging questions, and selective glossing pitched to the classroom: the words are all present, but the margin explains some second senses and passes others in silence, and the pattern of explanation and silence is an editorial decision about what a class needs, taken in an office the class will never see.

### What does a school edition's silence at a bawdy line teach before the teacher says a word?

It teaches a ranking. A glossed difficulty announces itself as knowledge the institution expects a student to have; an unglossed one announces itself as a difficulty the institution expects a student not to notice. Students read the margin as attentively as the text, and they draw the obvious conclusion when the notes fall silent where the room's energy stirs.

The practical consequence is that a teacher does not choose whether the bawdy question will be negotiated, only where. If the edition glosses frankly, the negotiation happens in the margin, and the teacher's work is to govern the discussion the note has already licensed. If the edition is silent, the negotiation happens anyway, in the corridor and on the screen, because the text's presence guarantees discovery: a class reading a complete text in the age of the complete text will find its jokes, and the only question is whether the finding happens inside a governed discussion or outside any discussion at all. Departments that imagine a silent edition has settled the matter have confused their own silence with their students'. The scholarly tradition's frankness, Partridge to Williams to Weis, has settled the record. The school's margin is a throttle, not a wall, and throttles are for governing a flow that exists.

The gloss is not the margin's only instrument, and a department auditing its edition should read the whole apparatus with the same suspicion. The scene summary that precedes each act in a school edition performs the Lambs' manoeuvre inside the modern book: a summary cannot carry a register, so a summarized scene arrives pre-softened, its bawdy dissolved into plot before the student reaches the lines, and a class that reads the summary first meets the passage itself as an anomaly the book has already apologized for. The activity apparatus cuts both ways. An activity that sets students to perform a scene restores the register to the voice, where it belongs; an activity that sets them to rewrite a scene in modern dress quietly performs the soften, training the class to treat paraphrase as comprehension. None of this makes the school edition an enemy. It makes it a text with a politics, like every text in the room, and the professional act is to read the politics before issuing the book. A teacher who has audited the margin can counterweight it: the summary skipped, the silent line glossed aloud at mechanism level, the activity chosen for the voice rather than the worksheet. The edition decides first, but it does not decide last, unless the teacher lets the first decision stand unexamined.

## What Teachers Do with the Lines: Skip, Soften, Perform, Teach

Documented practice, across the education literature of the last half century, sorts into four strategies, and naming them plainly is the precondition of judging them. The first is the skip: the passage is passed over in reading, assigned for homework without comment, or quietly dropped from the scenes the class performs, a Bowdlerism of the timetable rather than the text. The second is the soften: the line is read, and its sense paraphrased into harmlessness, the joke converted to a vague remark about teasing or rudeness, so that the words survive and the meaning is falsified. The third is performance: the passage is put on its feet, spoken in role, and allowed to land as theatre, trusting the dramatic context to carry what exposition would embarrass. The fourth is the teach: the line is stopped at, its kind named, its placement shown, its function in the scene and the play's register system made the explicit object of study. The first two strategies are the expurgation tradition continuing by other means. The last two are the recovery tradition entering the classroom, and each has its documented champions.

### Which of the teacher's four options costs the play the most?

The soften costs the most, worse than the skip it pretends to improve on. A skipped line leaves a gap a student can later discover and fill; a softened line fills the gap in advance with a falsehood, teaching that the text means less than it says and that paraphrase is a respectable name for evasion.

Rex Gibson's Teaching Shakespeare (1998), the handbook of the Cambridge School Shakespeare series he founded, made the systematic case for the third and fourth strategies fused: the play as a script "to be played with, explored, actively and imaginatively brought to life by acting out", in his own formulation, with the classroom reconceived as a rehearsal room in which students speak the whole text and discover its senses in the speaking. Gibson's Shakespeare and Schools project at the Cambridge Institute of Education, and the active methods that descended from it into the Royal Shakespeare Company's teaching practice, rest on a claim directly relevant to the bawdy: a line's meaning is an event between speakers, and a class that performs Mercutio's scenes learns his register in the body, as timing, target, and energy, at a level where embarrassment is displaced by craft. Peggy O'Brien built the American counterpart at the Folger Shakespeare Library, founding its education division, establishing the Teaching Shakespeare Institute in 1984, and editing the Shakespeare Set Free series (1993 to 1995), whose five-week units for individual plays were tested in actual classrooms and published for teachers everywhere. The Folger method's tenets are performance-based to the core: that the way to learn Shakespeare is by performing Shakespeare, and that students of every level can do it. In a Folger classroom the bawdy lines are not a special problem requiring a policy. They are lines, to be spoken by students in role, inside a method that has already normalized the speaking of everything.

Ayanna Thompson and Laura Turchi, in Teaching Shakespeare with Purpose (2016), supply the discipline that keeps performance honest, and their contribution matters to this subject precisely because it is not permissive. Their argument frees teachers from the notion that teaching Shakespeare means teaching everything: a unit should have an articulated purpose, an intentional frame, and entry points chosen for the specific students in the room, rather than a march through the text in which every line gets the same unexamined thirty seconds. Applied to the bawdy, their framework forbids both reflexes. The teacher who skips by default has no purpose, only an avoidance. The teacher who lingers by default has no purpose either, only a taste for the stir. The purposeful teacher decides, in advance and in writing if the department is well run, what the class is to learn from the play's register system, and lets that purpose set the dose. The framework is the strongest modern support for the verdict this article defends, because it relocates the question from the teacher's comfort to the unit's design.

The performance settlement has since been tested as well as preached, and the testing matters to a department choosing its method on evidence rather than on taste. The Royal Shakespeare Company's education work built its teaching on rehearsal-room practice, the room run as actors run it, and evaluations of rehearsal-based approaches, among them the studies associated with Joe Winston and Steve Strand, recorded gains in students' attitudes toward the plays and toward school itself that exceeded what chance would explain. The finding should not be oversold: attitude is not attainment, and no evaluation can certify that performance teaching settles the bawdy question by itself. What the record supports is narrower and sufficient. Students who speak the text whole, in role, inside a disciplined room, do not experience the difficult lines as a breakdown of the lesson; the lines arrive as work, with a craft attached. The embarrassment that the skip exists to prevent is largely an artefact of the silent classroom, where a joke has nothing to do but hang in the air. On its feet, the same joke has a speaker, a target, and a cue, and a class busy with speakers, targets, and cues is a class doing literary study in its oldest form.

A word belongs here to the teacher's own preparation, the least discussed variable in the whole question. The profession's memory runs through editions: a teacher's feel for which lines are difficult was formed, in most cases, by the text that teacher was taught from and the text that teacher trained on, and where those texts were silent, the silence is inherited as instinct. Departments therefore misdiagnose the problem when they treat it as one of individual nerve. The settlement proposed here is institutional before it is personal: an edition audited in common, a purpose agreed in common, a method rehearsed in common, so that the teacher facing the room's question is executing a department's design rather than improvising a private courage. Gibson's project, O'Brien's institute, and the Folger's teacher corps all worked at this institutional level, building the settlement into training rather than leaving it to temperament, and their longevity is the evidence that the level is the right one.

One frontier of the question sits outside the classroom's walls, and honest planning has to include it: homework. The bawdy scenes are read at kitchen tables and on buses, in houses whose standards the school does not set, and the first audience for a student's discovery is often a parent reading over a shoulder. Departments sometimes imagine that a careful lesson plan governs the encounter; the encounter, in fact, is distributed across every room where the book is opened. That distribution is an argument for the governed classroom, not against it. A class whose teacher has named the register, its speakers, and its function sends its students home with a frame already installed, so that the kitchen-table discovery arrives pre-interpreted, as a feature of a mapped system rather than as contraband. A class whose teacher has skipped sends its students home defenceless in both directions: unarmed against the joke, and armed with the suspicion that the school was hiding it. The parent's letter, when it comes, is answered far better by the department whose framing the student can repeat at home than by the department whose only answer is that the passage was never discussed. Homework is where the expurgated timetable does its worst damage, because the omission is discoverable there nightly, in the student's own copy, at the student's own pace.

## The Case for Honesty: Texture, Trust, and the Joke a Student Finds Alone

The affirmative case rests on three grounds, of which the first is textual and has been made already: the bawdy is structural. A treatment that mutes the register teaches a different and lesser play, one in which the lovers' lyricism has no contrast, the feud no verbal engine, and the second half no lost voice to mourn. Departments would not accept an edition that silently removed the play's imagery of light, on the ground that students might find it intense; the register of the body is entitled to the same presumption, because the play's meanings run through it with the same density. The second ground is trust. Adolescents are precise instruments for detecting institutional evasion, and a class that watches its teacher flinch, skip, or paraphrase at a joke the class has half understood learns a syllabus beyond the play: that adults' texts cannot be trusted to mean what they say, that difficulty is managed by pretence, that the approved response to an awkward truth is a change of subject. That syllabus, once learned, applies to every other text and every other subject the school offers. The teacher who names the joke's kind, calmly and briefly, teaches the opposite syllabus at the moment of maximum attention: that the book means what it says, that the room can be told, that awkward material is handled by accuracy rather than by theatre.

The third ground is the simplest and the hardest to answer. The joke will be found. The complete text is in the students' hands in any reputable edition, in the library without restriction, and free on the Folger's site; the dictionaries that recover the second senses stand in the reference collection; the students' own devices hold the sum of the commentary tradition in a search box. A policy of classroom silence does not create a room in which the bawdy is unknown. It creates a room in which the bawdy is known from ungoverned sources, framed by whoever ranks highest in a search result, discussed in the register of the corridor, and associated in the students' minds with the school's pretence that it is not there. Teachers who have written about the play's classroom life return to this discovery with the regularity of a law: the passages a lesson refuses are the passages the class studies hardest, alone. Honesty, on this ground, is not a liberal indulgence but a seizure of jurisdiction. The discussion will happen. The only live question is whether a teacher is in it.

Laughter belongs in this accounting, because the fear underneath many a skip is the fear of the room's laughter, and the fear misreads the evidence of the text. The play wants laughter at its bawdy; the laughter is the sound of the register working, of an audience placed by the dramatist inside the street's economy of appetite and insult. A class laughing at Mercutio's timing is a class hearing the play. The pedagogical task is not to prevent the laughter but to educate it, and the education has a clear direction: from laughter at a word, which is the corridor's level, to laughter at a mechanism, which is the theatre's. When a teacher can bring a room to laugh at how a joke is built, at the exact point where appetite puts on the costume of love poetry in Mercutio's conjuration, the laughter has become analysis without ceasing to be laughter, and the line has been taught more thoroughly than any gloss could teach it. The distinction between laughter that studies and laughter that merely stirs is the whole art of the subject, and it is learnable, teachable, and observable in any room where it is practiced.

The Folger's own practice gives the discovery argument its sharpest form, because the institution holds both ends of it. The library whose teaching method insists that students learn the play by performing it also makes the play's complete text freely available to anyone, the same text, entire, that its editions print for schools. A student who checks a half-heard line in the evening therefore meets it intact, glossed by scholars, in the keeping of the most respectable Shakespearean institution in the English-speaking world. Against that availability, a single classroom's silence is not a protection but a discrepancy, and adolescents are connoisseurs of discrepancy between what institutions say and what institutions do. Every teacher who has been caught by a class in a softened paraphrase knows the temperature of the room in the moment after: the lesson's subject changes, permanently, from the play to the teacher's credit. The honesty case is sometimes presented as a matter of principle, and it is; but it is also a matter of tactics in a world where the text cannot be contained. The discussion will be sourced from somewhere. The only choice a school retains is whether its own voice is among the sources, early, calm, and in command of the evidence, or absent, and quoted against itself.

## The Case for Caution: A Compelled Audience of Minors

The cautionary case, stated at full strength, is not prudery and should not be answered as though it were. A classroom is not a seminar, a theatre, or a book club. Its members are compelled to attend, they are minors, they are known to one another and must face one another the next morning, and their differences of maturity, experience, faith, and home teaching are not the teacher's to override. A university student who finds a lecture's treatment of sexual material crude can withdraw, complain through adult channels, or argue back as an equal. A fourteen-year-old whose class is laughing at a joke whose target she half suspects is her own situation has none of those defences, and the teacher who created the moment cannot uncreate it. The asymmetry is the argument: compelled audiences are owed a higher standard of care than voluntary ones, and the standard governs the delivery even where it does not touch the content. Henrietta Bowdler's instinct, that the young reader's situation differs in kind from the adult's, was not an error. Her remedy was the error. The difference in the audience is real; the conclusion that the text must therefore be falsified does not follow, and the whole modern settlement, frank editions, purpose-driven units, governed discussion, is an attempt to honour the instinct without repeating the remedy.

A second strand of the caution is epistemic, and Wells's audit gives it scholarly form. The teacher's authority in the room is such that a gloss delivered from the front will be received as fact, including the glosses that the scholarly tradition itself disputes. Where Partridge is cautious and Rubinstein adventurous and Wells sceptical, a teacher who retails the most adventurous available sense as the line's meaning has miseducated more efficiently than the teacher who skipped the line, because the skip at least left the question open. Caution, on this strand, is not about protecting students from the text. It is about protecting them from the teacher's overconfidence, and it prescribes the habits the verdict requires anyway: teach the documented core, name the disputes at the margins, and model the difference between a sense the editions agree on and a sense one dictionary proposes.

The civic dimension of caution can be treated briefly here, because it belongs by assignment to another study: the history of formal challenges to the play in schools, the complaints, hearings, and rulings through which communities have contested the text's presence, is recorded in [the companion account of the play banned and challenged in schools](/2029/09/28/banned-challenged-in-schools/), and a department preparing its rationale should know that record cold. What belongs to this article is the narrower observation that most classroom trouble over the bawdy has never arrived as a formal challenge at all. It arrives as the parental letter about a specific lesson, the governor's question about a specific page, the student's specific discomfort reported at home. Those arrivals are governed, in practice, by whether the department can show a purpose, a method, and an edition chosen with open eyes. The cautionary case, fully honoured, thus ends by prescribing the same instruments as the honesty case: a declared purpose for the unit, a method pitched at mechanism, an edition whose margin the teacher has read. The two cases converge on the discipline and differ only on the dose, which is why the dose, and not the principle, is where professional judgment lives.

The caution has a distributional edge that general statements about the room conceal, and it should be faced in its concrete form. A single class of fourteen-year-olds contains a spread of maturities wider than the spread between year groups, students raised in households of every degree of frankness and reserve, students for whom the play's material touches nothing personal, and students for whom a scene's subject is not literature but biography. A treatment of the bawdy is therefore not to be judged by its effect on the room's average member, who will survive any method, but by its effect on the most exposed member, who did not choose the text, the class, or the moment. Judged by that standard, the methods rank themselves with unusual clarity. The leer fails outright, since its entertainment is manufactured from somebody's discomfort. The lingering gloss fails nearly as badly, holding the room's attention on the territory where the exposed student least wants it held. The skip and the soften fail more subtly, marking the material as shameful in the hearing of the very students who can least afford the marking. The mechanism method passes the distributional test as it passes the textual one, because its attention is fixed on the play's construction, a subject in which every student in the room stands in exactly the same relation to the material: as a reader of a four-hundred-year-old text, at the same distance, with the same work to do. Equality of distance is the method's kindness, and it is not a small one.

Community difference belongs to the same accounting. The households a school serves hold every position on frankness that the surrounding culture holds, and a teacher cannot survey the room's consciences before the lesson begins. Thompson and Turchi's insistence on the intentional frame is the professional answer available: a unit whose purpose has been articulated in advance, in terms of the play's language and structure, gives every family the same account of what is being taught and why, and gives the school a position it can state without apology or improvisation when the account is requested. The frame does not ask any family to change its standards. It declares the school's: that the text under study is whole, that the study is of the text's construction, and that the depth of treatment is set by the students' years and the unit's declared aims. Families have lived with that declaration from schools for as long as schools have taught difficult books, and what provokes conflict is rarely the declaration itself. It is the discovery, usually by accident, that no declaration existed, that a classroom drifted into or away from the material on one teacher's unexamined instinct. Purpose is the caution's fulfilment, not its enemy.

## Mechanism, Not Gloss: The Decorous Method That Refuses Both Failures

The method that satisfies both cases can be stated as a small set of questions, applied to any bawdy passage in the play, in a fixed order, until the routine is the class's own. Who is speaking? The register belongs to servants, to the Nurse, to Mercutio, and its distribution is a fact of the text that a class can map without a single explicit gloss. To whom is the joke addressed, and who else is meant to hear it? The servants perform for each other and for the street; the Nurse performs for Lady Capulet and for the audience; Mercutio performs for Romeo, and the joke's target is usually Romeo's posture rather than any person present. Where does the passage stand in the play's arc? A joke in Act 1 belongs to a comedy still in possession of its world; the same energy in Act 3 is a man performing while his world ends. What kind of joke is it? The taxonomy is short and teachable: the boast, the taunt, the fond memory, the duel of wits, the teasing delay, and each kind does different dramatic work. What would the scene lose if the passage vanished? That final question is the honesty engine, because it converts every bawdy line from an embarrassment to be managed into a beam whose removal must be justified, and classes that have run the five questions on the opening scene can run them unaided by the time Mercutio greets the hour in Act 2.

### At what point does honest explanation tip into prurience?

The line falls where the explanation's object changes. Teaching stays honest while its object is the play, the speaker, the kind, the placement, the effect on the scene. It tips into prurience when its object becomes the students, their experience, their reactions, their embarrassment, held up as the room's entertainment or its test.

Worked examples fix the method better than precepts. Take the opening scene. A mechanism lesson spends no time on any individual word's second sense. It asks why a tragedy about noble households opens in the street, why the feud's first soldiers are servants with no stake in the quarrel, and why their talk runs on boasting in two currencies at once, so that the brawl, when it comes, reads as the talk continued by other means. The class has learned the feud's psychology, the play's register system, and the scene's function, and no one has been asked to contemplate anything but the play's construction. Take Mercutio's conjuration in Act 2, Scene 1. The mechanism is parody: a lover's idiom dragged downward by a friend who believes the idiom is a sickness and the dragging a cure. The lesson asks what Romeo's love language sounds like after Mercutio has finished with it, and why the play needs that deflation administered before the balcony scene can risk its sincerity an hour later. Take the Nurse in Act 2, Scene 5. The mechanism is delay: a message the audience and Juliet burn to hear, withheld inside reminiscence and body talk by the one character whose license permits the withholding. The lesson asks what the delay does to the scene's temperature and to Juliet's, and the bawdy has been taught, in full, as dramaturgy. In each case the teacher has been entirely honest about the presence, the kind, and the work of the sexual joking, and entirely decorous about its content, because content was never the point on offer. The point was the play.

The method also scales downward in age without breaking, which the next section takes up, and it has a property the expurgators never achieved: it is self-limiting. A mechanism question exhausts itself. Once the class has established who speaks, what kind of joke it is, and what it does, there is nothing left to ask, and the lesson moves on at the speed of the play rather than the speed of the room's giggling. Prurience, by contrast, is inexhaustible, because its object is not in the text. Departments that fear the bawdy lesson will expand to fill the week are imagining gloss lessons, in which each word's sense must be unpacked and the unpacking has no natural floor. The mechanism method has a floor built in. It also answers Wells's audit from the classroom side: a teacher teaching kinds, speakers, and placements is teaching facts no dictionary disputes, and the contested single senses, the territory where the interpreter may be inventing the pun, simply never become the lesson's load-bearing claims. The decoration of the method is austerity, and austerity, in this subject, is what honesty looks like when it is also kind.

A fourth worked example shows the method handling the play's most concentrated bawdy scene without strain. Act 2, Scene 4 sets the two bawdy voices against each other: Mercutio, who greets the hour in the register's own vocabulary, and the Nurse, arriving on love's errand with Peter in tow, and the scene's sport is a duel for Romeo's allegiance fought between the street's apprentice-master and the household's licensed voice. Run the five questions. The speakers are the register's two poles, virtuosity and license. The addressees are each other, with Romeo as the prize both are really playing to. The placement is the hinge of the morning after the balcony, the last hour in which the comedy's world still holds. The kinds are duel and errand, wit against gossip, and the duel's outcome is the scene's meaning: the Nurse, dismissed and mocked, nonetheless carries the future in her message, while the victor of the exchange is carrying his death in his character. Remove the scene, as shortened texts have always been tempted to do, and the play loses the only meeting of its two bawdy systems, and with it the measure of what Romeo is choosing between. A class that has run those questions has studied the scene's bawdy more rigorously than any paraphrase could manage, and the scene's content has never once been the lesson's object. The example generalizes: the harder the passage, the more the method earns, because hardness in this play is a function of concentration, and concentration is exactly what the five questions are built to anatomize.

## The Age Gradient: One Play at Thirteen, Another at Seventeen

The play is met at different ages in different systems, and the gradient of honest treatment follows the reader, not the text. In England the tragedy stands on the Shakespeare lists of the GCSE examinations, taken at sixteen after two years of study that begin at fourteen; in much of the United States it is a ninth-grade fixture, met at fourteen or fifteen; younger pupils meet it in adapted and retold forms, and older students meet it again at university with the full scholarly apparatus. The broader craft of pitching the tragedy to adolescents, its hooks, its activities, and its hazards, is treated in the companion study of [teaching Romeo and Juliet to teenagers](/2030/01/25/teaching-romeo-and-juliet-to-teens/); the bawdy question inside that craft has its own gradient, and it is a gradient of analysis rather than of access. The text does not change between the thirteen-year-old's classroom and the seventeen-year-old's. What changes is the depth of the questions the room is equipped to ask, and honesty consists in matching the depth to the room while never falsifying at any depth.

At the younger end, the mechanism method operates at its most architectural. A class of thirteen-year-olds can map the speakers, name the kinds, and track the register's death at Mercutio's fall with complete intellectual honesty and complete decorum, because every one of those operations is about the play's construction. What the younger room is not asked to do is adjudicate the dictionaries. At the older end, the apparatus itself becomes the lesson, and a sixth-form or eleventh-grade class can be set the genuine scholarly problem: here is a passage, here is Partridge's treatment of its kind, here is Rubinstein's fuller recovery, here is Wells asking whether the interpreter has invented the pun, and the class must rule. That lesson teaches the bawdy, the reference tradition, and the ethics of evidence in a single movement, and it is available only because the younger lesson was honest about placement: students cannot adjudicate a recovery they were never told had been suppressed. The gradient thus runs from architecture to evidence, and at no point on it does the honest teacher face a choice between the truth and the room. The choices are all of depth, and depth is the ordinary currency of teaching every other difficult thing.

One boundary deserves plain statement. The gradient governs the lesson, not the book. A school that responds to the age question by giving younger pupils an expurgated text and older pupils the whole one has repeated the Bowdler error at the level of the timetable, and has added a cruelty the Bowdlers spared their readers: the younger pupil will meet the complete play, in a library copy or on a screen, and will learn from the difference exactly what the school thought of her capacity and exactly what the school was hiding. The Lambs understood the alternative form of the same error: the retelling that gives the young a story first and the play later works only if the later arrival is real, in a curriculum that actually returns to the text entire. Where the return is guaranteed, sequence is pedagogy. Where it is not, sequence is suppression with a postponement attached. The age gradient, honestly drawn, varies the questions and keeps the text whole at every age at which the text is set at all.

The gradient has a tail that departments rarely plan for: the second meeting. Students who met the play at fourteen meet it again at university, in the theatre, in the culture's endless recycling of its scenes, and the second meeting is an audit of the first. The adult who discovers, in a frank edition or a full production, the register that school withheld does not merely learn new lines; the discovery regrades the schooling, and the regrade is rarely generous, since the withheld material turns out to have been structural rather than scandalous. The Lambs' design is the honourable version of postponement, a foretaste honestly labelled as a foretaste, with the whole play promised and delivered in older years. A modern curriculum can rarely make that promise good, since the play is set once, at one age, and the return is left to chance. That curricular fact is the final argument for honesty at the single meeting: for most students the schoolroom encounter is the only governed encounter there will be, and a governed encounter that conceals the play's register system sends its students into every later meeting undefended against the discovery that they were managed. The gradient's rule can thus be stated whole: vary the questions with the years, keep the text entire at every year, and treat the first meeting as the only one the school is promised.

Below the age at which the play itself is set, the young meet its story in retellings, and the retelling shelf is where the Lambs' technology survives in modern dress. The story-book Shakespeare industry, picture books, prose versions, and simplified editions for primary readers, transmits plot without register as a condition of its form, and for young children the loss is no loss: a seven-year-old is owed the story, not the system. The gradient question is one of succession. A retelling in the primary years is a promise that the play itself waits further up the school, and the promise is kept or broken by the secondary curriculum, not by the story book. Where the succession is real, where the whole text is actually set and actually reached, the retelling stands in the honourable line of the Lambs, a foretaste labelled as a foretaste. Where the succession fails, where the simplified version is the last Shakespeare a student is issued, the school has reproduced the expurgation outcome by the retelling route, and the fact that no scissors were involved will not distinguish the result for the student who never meets the play. Departments auditing their provision should therefore trace the whole ladder their students climb, from the first story book to the set text, and ask at which rung the register arrives. If the answer is no rung, the ladder is the problem, and it will not be fixed at any single rung.

## The Verdict: Honesty at the Level of the Joke's Working

The reckoning can be closed. The bawdy of Romeo and Juliet should be taught, taught whole, and taught at the level of the joke's working: its speaker, its kind, its placement, its function in the tragedy's register system. The text should never be expurgated for the classroom, by scissors, by summary, or by the timetable, because expurgation falsifies a structure that the play's meaning depends on, and because the falsification is always discovered, to the cost of the institution's credit with its students. The treatment should never descend to the retailing of senses for their own sake, because the audience is compelled and young, because the dictionaries themselves dispute the margins of the record, and because the play's own artistry runs on placement rather than on inventory. Between the Bowdlers' subtraction and the corridor's leer stands the professional settlement the last half century has built: frank editions in scholars' hands, recovered vocabularies in the dictionaries, purpose-driven units in the departments, and teachers who answer the room's real question, which is never what a word means in the dark. The room's real question is what kind of book this is, and whether the adults in charge of it can be trusted with it. A class that watches its teacher handle the play's earthiest passages with accuracy, calm, and evident respect for both the text and the students has learned the tragedy's deepest lesson about speech: that what is said plainly, in the right register, at the right moment, does not corrupt a room. It consecrates the room's attention, and attention is the whole of teaching.

Three objections survive the argument and deserve their answers in the open. The first holds that some material is simply unsuitable for minors whatever its structural value. The answer is the gradient: suitability governs the depth of analysis, never the integrity of the text, and a school that judges the whole play unsuitable at a given age should not set the play at that age, rather than set a falsified version of it, since the falsified version teaches the falsification along with the play. The second objection holds that mechanism teaching is a euphemism, a respectable way of not teaching the lines at all. The answer is empirical and textual: the mechanism lesson covers more of the bawdy than any skipping or softening regime ever taught, names every passage the expurgators cut, and explains the cuts' cost, which is more honesty about the bawdy than the bawdy's self-appointed champions usually deliver. The third objection assigns the decision to parents rather than to the profession. The profession's answer is the rationale, prepared in advance, grounded in the scholarship, and open to inspection: a department that can show its purpose, its edition, and its method has met the parental claim in the only currency that settles such claims, which is demonstrated care. The civic contests over the text belong to another study in this series; the classroom settlement belongs to this one, and it stands.

Romeo and Juliet begins with two servants joking in a street, and the joke is the tragedy's first truth: that in Verona the body's business and the feud's business are one business, conducted in one language, by people who will be dead of it before the week is out. A classroom honest enough to hear that joke properly, in its kind and its placement, has heard the play. A classroom protected from it has been protected from the play, and protection of that kind, however kind its motives, is the one lesson the play itself refuses to endorse.

Honesty, finally, has a price, and the verdict should carry its own cost accounting rather than pretend the settlement is free. The method asks more of a department than silence does: an edition chosen by audit rather than by habit, a unit purpose written down and defended, preparation in the reference literature that many teachers were never given in their own schooling, and the steady nerve to run a governed discussion in a room whose laughter is real. Those costs are the ordinary costs of teaching anything difficult well, and they fall where such costs belong, on the institution's preparation rather than on the students' text. Silence is cheaper in every currency except the one that matters, which is the credit of the school's word with the young people required to trust it. The Bowdlers paid the opposite price and thought it small: a tenth of a text here, a name off a title page there, against the safety of the young. The centuries since have rendered the account. What was saved was embarrassment, briefly, for adults. What was spent was the play, for generations, and the trust of every reader who eventually found the uncut text and did the subtraction in reverse.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What changes in a class's hearing of the balcony scene when the bawdy scenes have been taught honestly first?

The balcony scene becomes audible as the elevation it was composed to be. A class that has mapped the street register, the servants' boasting, the Nurse's licensed memory, and Mercutio's duelling wit hears the orchard scene against that ground bass, and the lyricism registers as an achievement rather than a default. Juliet's plainness reads as courage, her directness as a new kind of speech in a play whose other idiom for desire is the joke. Without the groundwork, the scene reaches students as pretty sentiment in an old vocabulary, admired at a distance and forgotten at the same distance. The bawdy lessons are what make the love scenes legible as the play's miracle rather than its decoration.

### Q: What is the difference between explaining how a bawdy joke works and spelling out what it means?

The first teaches the play; the second teaches a dictionary entry, often the most disputed one available. Explaining how a joke works means naming its speaker, its kind, its target, its placement in the arc, and its effect on the scene, all of which are facts of the text that no scholar contests. Spelling out what it means means inventorying a second sense, work that belongs to the reference shelf, that the dictionaries themselves argue about at the margins, and that has no natural stopping point in a classroom. The mechanism lesson exhausts itself and moves on. The gloss lesson lingers where the play does not, and makes the students, rather than the scene, the object of attention.

### Q: Why do students meet the play's bawdy lines whether or not the lesson plans for them?

Because the complete text is everywhere the lesson is not. Any reputable edition prints the whole play, the Folger text stands free online, the dictionaries that recover the second senses wait in the reference collection, and a search box holds the commentary tradition entire. A policy of silence does not produce a room in which the bawdy is unknown; it produces a room in which the bawdy is learned from ungoverned sources, in the corridor's framing, with the school's authority already spent on a pretence the class has seen through. The meeting will happen. Planning for it is the only version of the meeting a teacher can shape, time, and bring to a point.

### Q: When a student asks in front of the whole class what a joke means, what honest options does a teacher have?

Three honest options exist, and all three treat the question as legitimate. The teacher can answer at the level of mechanism on the spot, naming the joke's kind, speaker, and business in the scene, which satisfies the curiosity that is really about the play. The teacher can place the question visibly in the lesson's sequence, promising its treatment when the class reaches the passage's place in the register map, and keep the promise. Or the teacher can hand the question to the edition, directing the class to the margin's note and letting the room rule on the note's adequacy. The dishonest options are the flinch, the invented harmless sense, and the laugh that makes the asker the joke.

### Q: Why is the Queen Mab speech so often taught in full while Mercutio's prose jokes get paraphrased?

Because verse confers a protection that prose does not. The Queen Mab speech is a canonical set piece, anthologized, praised, and examined for generations, and its bawdy elements sit at the edges of a fantastical catalogue, cushioned by imagery. Mercutio's prose jokes arrive in the students' own medium, the idiom of actual speech, and so feel nearer, rawer, and harder to hold at a scholarly distance. Teachers follow the canon inside the play: what the anthologies have blessed feels teachable, and what speaks in prose feels exposed. The distinction is one of costume, not of content, and a department aware of it can correct for it, since the prose scenes carry the register's structural weight.

### Q: How does a class's age change what honest teaching of the bawdy requires?

It changes the depth of the questions, never the integrity of the text. A younger class works at the level of architecture, mapping who speaks the bawdy, what kinds of jokes they are, and where the register dies, all operations of complete honesty conducted entirely on the play's construction. An older class can take up the evidence itself, ruling between Partridge, Rubinstein, and Wells on contested senses and examining what editions choose to gloss. At no age does honesty require lingering past the play's needs, and at no age does caution license falsifying the words on the page. The variable is analysis. The constant is the whole text.

### Q: Why is the Nurse's bawdy easier for a class to take than Mercutio's?

Because the play frames the Nurse's bawdy as licensed, retrospective, and affectionate, and frames Mercutio's as combat. The Nurse jokes from age and long service, about her own past and in the middle of love's errands; nobody in her scenes is wounded by the joking, and the household that hears her treats the freedom as her office. Mercutio's wit has targets, Romeo's postures, the Nurse herself, every piety in reach, and its energy is the duel's energy, brilliant and wounding in the same breath. A class feels the difference without instruction: laughing with the Nurse is joining a household, while laughing with Mercutio is joining a fight, and the second laughter asks more of a room.

### Q: Why does Mercutio's wit turn cruel, and why is the cruelty part of the honesty question?

Because his register is competitive to its root: wit as duelling, in which every exchange must be won and every tenderness is a posture offered for puncturing. The cruelty is not an accident of temperament pasted onto the joking; it is the joking's logic, the feud's idiom of dominance speaking through the play's most charming mouth, and it completes itself when the dying Mercutio turns the performance into a curse on both houses. A lesson that presents his bawdy as harmless fun sanitizes in the opposite direction from the Bowdlers, and just as falsely. Honesty runs both ways: the class should learn that the register delights, and that the delight has blood in it, because that doubleness is the tragedy's point about Verona's whole way of talking.

### Q: What role should laughter play when a class meets the play's sexual wordplay?

Laughter is evidence, not disruption: it is the sound of the register working, of an audience placed where the dramatist intended, inside the street's economy of appetite and insult. The teacher's task is to educate the laughter rather than to suppress it, moving the room from laughing at a word, which is the corridor's level, to laughing at a mechanism, at timing, target, and construction, which is the theatre's. Suppressed laughter does not vanish; it goes underground and attaches itself to the teacher's discomfort. Welcomed and then examined, it becomes the most precise instrument the class owns for hearing how the play is built, because a room laughs exactly where the writing lands.

### Q: What separates laughing with the play's bawdy from laughing at Juliet?

The object of the laughter. The play's bawdy aims at postures: boasting servants performing a manhood neither possesses, a lovesick friend deflated by a wittier one, a nurse delaying for the pleasure of delay. Juliet is never the butt of the register she is excluded from; she is its standard, the speaker whose candour exposes the joking as the household's lesser idiom. Laughter that lands on Juliet herself, on her age, her trust, or her desire, has left the text and attached to a figure uncomfortably shaped like a student, and every room holds students for whom the scene is not abstract. That laughter a teacher must refuse to host. The line is bright: the play's clowns are fair game, its lovers are not, and classmates never are.

### Q: Why does the bawdy thin out after Mercutio's death, and how can a teacher use that quiet?

Because Mercutio was the register's virtuoso and its licence, and the voices that remain, the Nurse on her errands of grief, Peter haggling with the musicians, work in affection and routine without his edge. The quiet is therefore an event a class can be taught to hear. Set the musicians' scene of Act 4 beside the street scenes of Act 2 and ask what instrument is missing from the scoring: students discover the absence themselves, and the discovery teaches the register's value more sharply than its presence did. The tragedy's second half is played on a reduced orchestra, and a class that can name the missing voice has understood the play's architecture in its bones.

### Q: Why did nineteenth-century expurgation begin with family reading rather than with schools?

Because the parlour, not the classroom, was where the age consumed its plays, and reading aloud was the parlour's method. A line read silently can be skipped by an eye; a line read aloud becomes an event in company, with daughters and servants present, and the founding scene of expurgation is a father performing the cuts in real time as he read to his family. The Family Shakespeare translated that voice into print, naming the instructor of youth among its intended buyers but applying the household's test, propriety in family reading, as its rule. Schools inherited a technology built for the fireside and kept its assumptions long after the fireside itself had changed.

### Q: How did the word bowdlerize enter English, and why did it become an insult?

The verb comes from the Bowdler family's name on the Family Shakespeare, and dictionaries had recorded it within decades of the 1818 edition. It became an insult because the culture settled its judgment on the method: subtraction dressed as stewardship, protection achieved by falsifying the protected thing. The editors' own honesty made the charge stick. Since nothing was to be added and every cut followed an announced rule, the violence done to the text could be measured exactly, and measured violence to a beloved author reads as cowardice rather than care. Two conscientious editors thus gave their name to the language's standard word for a failure of nerve, a warning every later expurgator inherits with the practice.

### Q: Why is reading aloud the test that expurgators and classrooms both apply to the play?

Because a play is a score for voices, and a line's true difficulty declares itself only when it must be pronounced in company. The Bowdlers' father cut what could not be said in the family circle; the Folger method puts the whole text into students' mouths for the same structural reason, that speaking exposes sense, timing, and register as silent reading cannot. Eye and ear are different censors, and both traditions know the ear is the stricter and the truer. The tests agree on the medium and divide on the remedy. The expurgator silenced the line that failed the ear. The classroom teaches it, at the level of its working, until it can be spoken without flinching by anyone in the room.

### Q: Why do examinations seldom test the bawdy passages, and what does that omission do to lessons?

Because examinations need extracts with stable, markable vocabularies of response, and the bawdy passages sit outside the canonical scene list around which marking schemes, revision guides, and departmental drills are built. The omission exerts a quiet washback on teaching. Under time pressure, what the examination ignores drifts toward what the lesson skips, and the drift selects, by structural accident, exactly the passages on which the play's register system rests. A department can thus teach from a complete edition and still deliver a bowdlerized curriculum, the scissors replaced by the timetable. Naming the washback is the first defence against it: the passages the exam does not reward are the passages the department must deliberately protect.

### Q: What do students mishear in the lovers' lyric speeches when the bawdy register has been muted?

They mishear elevation as the play's default temperature. With the street's idiom absent from the ear, the shared sonnet at the feast sounds like ordinary love talk in old words rather than a formal miracle performed under pressure. Romeo's early Petrarchan posturing sounds sincere, because Mercutio's deflation, the play's own marker of the posture as posture, was never heard doing its work. Juliet's directness sounds naive rather than brave, since the household idiom it rises above has been removed from the evidence. The lyric register means by distance: its every effect is measured against the bawdy beneath it, and a muted ground bass leaves the melody playing in a room with no acoustics.

### Q: How does the play's own comic-then-tragic shape give a teacher a sequence for the hardest passages?

Because the play sequences itself, and the teacher can borrow the dramatist's order instead of inventing one. The bawdy belongs overwhelmingly to the comic first movement, where a class's laughter is the play's own weather and the jokes arrive inside scenes built to hold them. As the tragedy closes, the register withdraws, and the withdrawal itself becomes the final lesson in the sequence, an absence students can be asked to locate and explain. No passage must be met out of its weather. Met in the text's order, each bawdy scene is framed by the emotional climate Shakespeare constructed for it, and the gradient of the unit is the gradient of the play, from street noise to the silence of the tomb.

### Q: Why did expurgated texts persist in classrooms long after scholarly editions had restored the full play?

Because institutions buy for the protected reader by default, and a school text is an institution's purchase, not a scholar's. The scholarly restoration of the twentieth century served the universities that produced it, while school purchasing continued to serve caution, price, series loyalty, and the schemes of work already written around the copies on the shelf. A text embedded in a department's habits outlives the scholarship that superseded it, as Perrin's history of the expurgated book shows across a century and a half of school and family editions. The persistence was bureaucratic, not critical: nobody kept the expurgated text because it had won an argument. It stayed because replacing it required a decision, and decisions about this material are the ones institutions are built to postpone.
