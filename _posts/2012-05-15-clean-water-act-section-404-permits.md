Few federal programs touch more ordinary land transactions than the Section 404 wetlands permit program, and few are as widely misunderstood. The program governs the discharge of dredged or fill material into covered waters of the United States, which means that a farmer reshaping a low corner of a field, a developer grading a subdivision pad, a pipeline company boring beneath a stream, and a county widening a rural road all pass through the same regulatory doorway. The doorway is Section 404 of the Clean Water Act, codified at 33 U.S.C. 1344, and it is unusual in almost every respect: the permit is issued by a military engineering agency, the substantive standards are written by an environmental agency, that environmental agency can veto the permit after it is issued, and the question of whether the program reaches a given parcel at all is frequently the entire dispute.

![Wetlands and the Section 404 permit program under the Clean Water Act - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-23.webp)

The first misunderstanding to clear away is that this is a permit to pollute. The Clean Water Act's discharge permits under its better known section 402 program authorize the release of pollutants from pipes into rivers under technology based limits, which is the system the [complete guide to the Clean Water Act](/2012/02/15/clean-water-act-1972-complete-guide/) explains as that statute's definitional foundation. Section 404 does something different. It authorizes the placement of material itself: rock, sand, soil, and the spoil from dredging, placed into wetlands, streams, and other covered waters. The distinction matters because the regulatory question is not how clean the discharge is but whether the fill should go there at all, and if so, under what conditions and with what compensation for the aquatic resources destroyed in the process. That difference in the question produces a difference in the entire machinery.

The second misunderstanding is that the environmental agency issues these permits. It does not. The United States Army Corps of Engineers, the same agency that has managed federal waterway permits since the nineteenth century, issues every Section 404 permit in the country except in the handful of states that have assumed the program. The Environmental Protection Agency, in conjunction with the Secretary of the Army, develops the guidelines the Corps must apply, and it holds a veto over individual disposal sites. This split is the program's defining structural feature, and everything else about the program, the permit types, the sequencing of review, the mitigation market, the state certification lever, and the enforcement posture, is built around it.

The third misunderstanding is that the program stops development. It conditions development, prices it, and occasionally reroutes it, but the numbers on authorizations show a program that says yes far more often than it says no. The Corps' nationwide permits alone authorize on the order of twenty five thousand reported activities every year plus roughly thirty thousand more activities that proceed without any reporting to the Corps at all, according to the Corps' own fact sheet for the 2026 permit reissuance. Individual permits, the heavyweight review most people picture when they hear the word permit, are a small fraction of the total. Denials are rarer still. The accurate description of the program is not a wall but a toll road with several lanes, and this article maps every lane. Here is the claim to carry out of this article: the federal government does not need to own the wetlands to regulate the filling of them.

## The Split Authority: One Statute, Two Agencies

Section 404 sits in the Clean Water Act as an anomaly that only makes sense as a historical inheritance. When Congress wrote the 1972 amendments that became the modern Clean Water Act, it faced a practical question: who would administer permits for dredging and filling? The Corps of Engineers had been issuing permits for work in navigable waters under section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act of 1899 for more than seventy years, and it had the district offices, the engineers, and the dredging expertise. Congress therefore gave the Corps the new permit authority in section 404(a), which authorizes the Secretary of the Army, acting through the Chief of Engineers, to issue permits for the discharge of dredged or fill material at specified disposal sites. The environmental standards those permits must meet, however, were assigned to the new Environmental Protection Agency in section 404(b)(1), which directs EPA, in conjunction with the Secretary of the Army, to develop guidelines for the specification of disposal sites. The result was a deliberate division of labor: the builder's agency holds the pen, and the environmental agency writes the rules the pen must follow.

### Why does the Army Corps of Engineers issue these permits instead of the environmental agency?

The Corps administered waterway permits under the Rivers and Harbors Act since 1899, so Congress assigned the dredge and fill permits to the agency with the district offices and engineering capacity. EPA received the guideline development role, exercised in conjunction with the Secretary of the Army, plus the veto function, giving environmental protection a role without dismantling a permit bureaucracy.

The guideline writing power in section 404(b)(1) is not advisory. EPA's guidelines, published at 40 CFR Part 230 and first issued in 1975 with a major revision in 1980, bind the Corps as a matter of law. A Corps permit that fails to comply with the guidelines is unlawful, and a court can set it aside. The guidelines contain the two most consequential commands in the entire program. The first is the practicable alternatives test of 40 CFR 230.10(a): no discharge of dredged or fill material may be permitted if there is a practicable alternative to the proposed discharge that would have a less adverse impact on the aquatic ecosystem, so long as that alternative does not present other significant adverse environmental consequences. The second is the sequencing requirement that flows from it: the applicant must first avoid impacts, then minimize impacts that cannot be avoided, and then compensate for what remains. The Corps runs the proceeding, but EPA's text decides what counts as a lawful outcome.

The veto in section 404(c), codified at 33 U.S.C. 1344(c), is the other half of EPA's structural role, and it is the strangest power in American environmental law: one federal agency may prohibit or restrict the use of a disposal site even after another federal agency has issued a permit for it. The statutory trigger is a finding that the discharge will have an unacceptable adverse effect on municipal water supplies, shellfish beds and fishery areas including spawning and breeding areas, wildlife, or recreational areas. The procedures sit at 40 CFR Part 231, and they run through a proposed determination, public comment and hearings, and a final determination signed by the EPA Administrator. The veto is used rarely. The January 30, 2023 final determination restricting disposal sites in the Bristol Bay watershed associated with the proposed Pebble mine in Alaska was described by EPA as only the third use of the authority in thirty years and the fourteenth in the history of the Clean Water Act. The rarity is the point of the design: the veto's existence shapes Corps behavior in thousands of ordinary permits, because no district engineer wants to author the next one.

Between issuance and veto sits a third instrument, the section 404(q) coordination process, which prevents the two agencies from blindsiding each other. Section 404(q) required the Secretary of the Army and the Administrator to enter into agreements to minimize duplication and delay, and the resulting 1992 memorandum of agreement created an elevation procedure: when EPA's regional office believes a pending individual permit application raises policy concerns or would violate the guidelines, it can elevate the matter through the agencies' headquarters before the Corps decides. The 404(q) process is the reason the program can run for long stretches without a veto, since disputes that might otherwise become vetoes are negotiated into permit conditions, project redesigns, or withdrawals long before the final determination stage. The decades long gaps between final 404(c) actions are widely attributed to this elevation machinery, which resolves disputes before they ripen into vetoes.

The Corps' own decision standard adds a further layer that has no counterpart in EPA's guidelines. Under 33 CFR 320.4(a), every Corps permit decision, whether for an individual or a general permit, is subject to a public interest review: the decision must reflect the national concern for both protection and utilization of important resources, and the Corps weighs the reasonably foreseeable benefits of the proposal against its reasonably foreseeable detriments across a long list of factors that includes conservation, economics, aesthetics, wetlands values, fish and wildlife, flood hazards, navigation, recreation, water supply, and the needs and welfare of the people. This balancing is the Corps' own, not EPA's, and it explains why two permits for similar fills can come out differently: the guidelines set the environmental floor, and the public interest review decides whether the project clears the broader bar.

The field structure behind these decisions shapes outcomes as much as the legal standards. The Corps administers the regulatory program through thirty eight districts organized into eight divisions, and the district engineer is the decision maker for individual permits, jurisdictional determinations, and enforcement actions within the district's territory. Division commanders oversee the districts, approve regional conditions for nationwide permits, and hear administrative appeals of approved jurisdictional determinations and individual permit denials. Headquarters, under the Chief of Engineers, issues the regulatory guidance letters that standardize practice across the country, such as the guidance establishing the five year validity period for approved jurisdictional determinations. This decentralization means that the program's texture varies by district: a district dominated by coastal wetlands develops deep expertise in tidal permitting, while a district in the arid West focuses on ephemeral streams and playa lakes. Applicants who learn the district's standard practices, its typical regional conditions, its mitigation preferences, and its pre application meeting customs, move faster than applicants who treat every district as interchangeable. The statute is national; its administration is local.

## The Two Doors: Individual Permits and General Permits

The single most useful thing a reader can learn about the Section 404 program is that it has two doors, and almost everyone walks through the smaller one. The individual permit, called the standard permit in Corps regulations, is the full proceeding: a detailed application, a public notice with a comment period, coordination with federal and state resource agencies, an alternatives analysis under the 404(b)(1) guidelines, a public interest review, and often an environmental assessment or impact statement under the National Environmental Policy Act. The general permit is the streamlined path: the Corps has already done the analysis for a category of activities, published the permit with its conditions, and the applicant's task is to show that the project fits inside the category and meets the conditions. The overwhelming majority of authorized activity proceeds through general permits, which is why the recurring belief that every project needs an individual permit is one of the errors the program's own data most clearly refutes.

The individual permit process begins when an applicant files a detailed application with the Corps district, including drawings, a project purpose statement, and a description of the waters affected. The district issues a public notice under 33 CFR 325.3, typically with a fifteen to thirty day comment period, and circulates the proposal to federal and state fish and wildlife agencies, the state historic preservation office, EPA, and any tribe with interests in the area. The Corps evaluates the proposal against the 404(b)(1) guidelines, which requires the practicable alternatives analysis: for a non water dependent activity, the guidelines presume that practicable alternatives that do not involve special aquatic sites are available, and the applicant must rebut that presumption with evidence. The Corps also conducts the public interest review, considers cumulative effects, and decides whether the activity complies with water quality standards, the Endangered Species Act, and historic preservation law. If the decision is a denial, the applicant can appeal through the Corps' administrative appeal process at 33 CFR Part 331 or seek judicial review. If the decision is an approval, it comes with conditions, and often with a mitigation requirement that can exceed the cost of the fill itself.

The nationwide permits are the workhorse of the general permit system. Authorized by section 404(e), which permits the Corps to issue general permits for categories of activities that will cause only minimal adverse environmental effects, both individually and cumulatively, the nationwide permits are a published set of authorizations that apply across the country unless a division commander or district engineer revokes or adds conditions for a region. The Corps reissues them on a five year cycle, as section 404(e) limits any general permit to a period of not more than five years. The 2021 reissuance covered fifty seven nationwide permits; the reissuance published in the Federal Register on January 8, 2026 carried forward fifty six of them and added one new permit for fish passage improvements, for a total of fifty seven permits effective March 15, 2026 through March 15, 2031, with one 2021 permit for finfish mariculture not reissued. Each nationwide permit carries acreage or linear foot limits, general conditions that apply to every permit in the set, and in many cases a pre construction notification requirement: the applicant notifies the district, and the district verifies in writing that the activity fits the permit, sometimes within weeks. The Corps' fact sheet for the 2026 reissuance reports that the nationwide permits authorize approximately twenty five thousand reported activities per year plus approximately thirty thousand activities that do not require reporting, which is the statistical backbone of the claim that the program conditions and prices development rather than blocking it.

### What keeps a nationwide permit from becoming a blank check for developers?

Three constraints prevent it. The statute limits general permits to activities with minimal individual and cumulative effects. The Corps attaches general and regional conditions to every permit. Many permits require pre construction notification, so the district verifies the fit before work begins, and projects exceeding the caps must seek individual permits.

Regional general permits are the middle lane: issued by a Corps division or district rather than at headquarters, they authorize categories of activity within a defined geography, often for recurring public works such as county road maintenance, utility corridor work, or beach nourishment in a coastal district. Like nationwide permits they rest on the minimal effects finding of section 404(e) and the five year maximum term, and they frequently reflect close coordination with the state resource agencies in the region. For applicants they function much like a nationwide permit, with the difference that the conditions were written with a particular watershed or coastline in mind.

Letters of permission close out the permit taxonomy, and they are the least understood of the four. A letter of permission is a form of individual permit issued through an abbreviated procedure: under 33 CFR 325.2(e)(1)(ii), a district engineer may use it for work that in the district engineer's judgment would be minor, would not have significant individual or cumulative impacts on environmental values, and should encounter no appreciable opposition. The letter of permission requires coordination with federal and state fish and wildlife agencies but no public notice, and the district resolves it through correspondence rather than a full proceeding. It is the program's acknowledgment that not every fill deserves a public hearing, and it handles the genuinely small matters, a boat ramp repair, a minor bank stabilization, a small dock, that would be crushed by the weight of the individual permit process.

A Corps permit is also a federal action for purposes of the National Environmental Policy Act, which is why the individual permit track so often produces an environmental assessment and sometimes a full impact statement. The [guide to the National Environmental Policy Act](/2012/02/01/national-environmental-policy-act-guide/) explains the statute whose review obligation attaches the moment a federal agency proposes to act, and the [NEPA statement guide](/2012/04/01/nepa-environmental-impact-statement/) walks through the document sequence that a large individual permit generates, from scoping through the draft and final statement to the record of decision. Applicants sometimes experience this as a second permit program stacked on top of the first, and in practical terms it is: the 404 decision and the NEPA document move together, and delay in one is delay in both.

## The Nationwide Permit Machine in Detail

The nationwide permit system deserves a closer look than the overview above, because it is where the statute's abstract promise of minimal adverse effects becomes a working bureaucracy. Section 404(e) authorizes the Corps to issue general permits for categories of activities that are similar in nature, will cause only minimal adverse environmental effects when performed separately, and will have only minimal cumulative adverse effect on the environment. The provision caps every general permit at five years, which forces the reissuance rulemaking on a fixed cycle and gives each cycle's critics a scheduled opportunity to challenge the minimal effects finding. The reissuance is a rulemaking in the full administrative sense: the Corps publishes a proposal in the Federal Register, takes public comment, prepares decision documents analyzing the environmental effects of each permit, and issues the final permits with general conditions, definitions, and in many cases new or revised terms. Division engineers then supplement the national documents with regional conditions tailored to the waters in their territories, and districts determine which state water quality certification conditions and coastal zone management concurrences attach to each permit locally.

The pre construction notification, usually abbreviated PCN, is the hinge between the streamlined promise and the Corps' need to verify. Many nationwide permits require the applicant to notify the district before beginning work, submitting project drawings, a delineation of affected waters, and a description of proposed mitigation. The district engineer reviews the PCN and responds in writing, either verifying that the activity is authorized, adding project specific conditions, or telling the applicant that an individual permit is required. For a large group of the permits, the terms provide that if the district does not respond within forty five days of receiving a complete PCN, the applicant may proceed, which creates a genuine incentive for districts to process verifications promptly. Where the permit requires no PCN, the authorization is self executing: the applicant confirms the project fits the permit's terms and conditions and proceeds, bearing the risk that an enforcement review later disagrees with the self assessment.

The general conditions are the fine print that keeps the system honest. The 2021 set carried more than thirty of them, and they read like a condensed environmental law course: navigation must not be impaired, aquatic life movements must not be substantially disrupted, spawning areas must be avoided during spawning seasons, migratory bird breeding areas must be avoided to the maximum extent practicable, activities cannot jeopardize threatened or endangered species or destroy critical habitat, historic properties must be considered, water quality standards must be met, and the activity must comply with coastal zone management programs. Several general conditions incorporate other statutes by reference, which means a nationwide permit verification can trigger an Endangered Species Act consultation or a historic preservation review even though no individual 404 proceeding exists. Applicants who treat the nationwide permit as a rubber stamp tend to discover the general conditions at the worst possible moment, after equipment is mobilized.

The reissuance history shows the system adapting to politics and litigation. The 2012 reissuance, published February 21, 2012 at 77 FR 10184, reissued forty eight of the forty nine existing permits and added two new ones, and those permits expired March 18, 2017. The Corps split the 2021 reissuance into two Federal Register actions, one published January 13, 2021 at 86 FR 2744 and the remainder published December 27, 2021 at 86 FR 73522, producing a set of fifty seven permits that expired March 14, 2026. The January 8, 2026 final action at 91 FR 768 reissued fifty six of those permits and added one new nationwide permit, NWP 60, for activities that improve passage for fish and other aquatic organisms, for a total of fifty seven permits effective March 15, 2026 through March 15, 2031. NWP 56, the permit for finfish mariculture, was not reissued. The 2026 action also widened eligibility in ways that reflected the economy's direction: NWP 39 for commercial and institutional developments now explicitly lists data centers, including artificial intelligence and machine learning facilities, as an example of covered development. Each reissuance also reopens the state certification question, because states certify the permits as categories at reissuance, and a state's denial or conditioning of a particular nationwide permit reshapes what applicants in that state can use for the next five years.

The most litigated nationwide permit has been number twelve, the utility line permit, which authorizes discharges associated with the construction, maintenance, and repair of utility lines including pipelines. Environmental organizations challenged the 2017 reissuance of that permit over its Endangered Species Act compliance, and a federal district court in Montana vacated it as applied to new oil and gas pipeline construction, a ruling that threatened to force individual permits for pipeline stream crossings nationwide. The Supreme Court stayed the vacatur in 2020 in U.S. Army Corps of Engineers v. Northern Plains Resource Council, except as it applied to the Keystone XL pipeline, preserving the permit's availability while the litigation continued. The episode illustrates the structural vulnerability of the general permit system: a single successful challenge to the programmatic analysis can strand thousands of projects that were counting on the streamlined lane, which is why the Corps invests heavily in the decision documents that defend each reissuance.

Districts retain one more power that applicants sometimes overlook: a division commander or district engineer may revoke a nationwide permit within a state or region, or add regional conditions that effectively narrow it, without waiting for the national reissuance cycle. Revocation is rare, but regional conditions are routine, and they are where local knowledge enters the system. A district with exceptional trout streams adds timing restrictions. A coastal district adds conditions for seagrass. The national permit sets the ceiling; the regional conditions set the local floor. The sophisticated applicant reads both, because the verification letter will enforce both.

## The Avoid-Minimize-Compensate Ladder

The avoid-minimize-compensate ladder: the entire wetlands permitting system is one sequence applied in a fixed order, and a reader who knows that order can predict what a regulator will ask for at every stage of a project.

The sentence above is the namable claim of this article, and it is also the most practical knowledge the program offers. The sequence is not a slogan. It is the operative structure of the 404(b)(1) guidelines, elaborated in a 1990 memorandum of agreement between EPA and the Army on mitigation, and it dictates the order in which the Corps evaluates every permit application. Avoidance comes first: the applicant must demonstrate that there is no practicable alternative to the proposed discharge that would have a less adverse impact on the aquatic ecosystem. Minimization comes second: for the impacts that cannot be avoided, the applicant must show that the project has been designed to reduce them as far as practicable, through measures such as narrowing a road footprint, relocating a building pad, using retaining walls instead of graded slopes, or scheduling work outside sensitive seasons. Compensation comes third: for the residual unavoidable impacts, the applicant must provide compensatory mitigation, which means restoring, establishing, enhancing, or preserving aquatic resources to offset the loss.

The fixed order matters because it forecloses the most natural instinct of a permit applicant, which is to offer money first. A developer who arrives at the district office proposing to buy mitigation credits before demonstrating avoidance has skipped to step three, and the Corps will send the applicant back to step one. The practicable alternatives analysis is the evidentiary heart of avoidance: the applicant must examine alternative sites, alternative configurations, and the alternative of not building the project at all, the no action alternative, and show why each is either impracticable or more damaging. For non water dependent activities, meaning activities that do not require access to water to fulfill their basic purpose, such as housing subdivisions, shopping centers, or most commercial development, the guidelines impose a rebuttable presumption that practicable alternatives avoiding special aquatic sites, including wetlands, are available. The applicant rebuts that presumption with evidence about cost, logistics, and technology, and the Corps judges whether the evidence is clear. This presumption is the single most powerful sentence in the guidelines for ordinary development, because it places the burden of proof on the party that wants to fill the wetland.

Minimization is where engineering meets ecology, and it rewards applicants who bring the Corps a project that already reflects it. The Corps looks for design choices that shrink the footprint in waters: steeper side slopes on a road embankment, a bridge instead of a culverted crossing, directional drilling beneath a stream instead of open trenching, staging areas kept out of wetlands, and construction sequencing that limits the duration of disturbance. None of this is glamorous, and that is the point. Minimization is the unglamorous middle of the ladder, and applicants who treat it as a box to check rather than a design discipline tend to discover that the Corps has its own ideas about what is practicable, informed by what other applicants have achieved on comparable sites.

Compensation is the bottom rung, and it is the only rung that involves paying for something rather than not doing something. The guidelines permit compensation only for unavoidable impacts, and the 2008 mitigation rule requires that it be sufficient to replace the lost functions and values of the aquatic resource. The rule expresses a preference for in kind mitigation, meaning the same type of resource, over out of kind, and for restoration over creation, because decades of experience showed that created wetlands frequently failed to replicate the functions of the wetlands they replaced. The amount of compensation is measured in credits, debits, ratios, and functional assessments, and the district engineer sets the required amount based on the resource type, the quality of the impacted site, and the likelihood that the mitigation will succeed. Ratios greater than one to one are common: filling one acre of high quality forested wetland might require purchasing three or more acres of credits, because the replacement is uncertain and the temporal loss, the years between the fill and the maturation of the mitigation site, is real.

### Where in the permit file does the alternatives analysis actually decide the outcome?

It decides the outcome at the avoidance step, before mitigation enters the discussion. The Corps asks whether a practicable alternative with less aquatic impact exists, and applicants for non water dependent projects carry a rebuttable presumption that one does. An unexamined practicable alternative kills the permit regardless of the compensation offered.

The ladder also explains the program's enforcement posture. An applicant who fills first and seeks permission later has inverted the sequence, and the Corps treats the inversion as the violation it is: there is no way to demonstrate avoidance after the wetland is gone. This is why unauthorized fills are so difficult to cure with money alone. The regulator cannot evaluate alternatives to a discharge that has already occurred, so the remedies skew toward restoration, removing the fill and reestablishing the wetland, rather than toward after the fact compensation. The sequence is a one way ratchet, and the enforcement section of this article returns to that point.

The ladder has one more consequence worth stating plainly, because it answers the question every applicant asks first: how much will this cost. The honest answer is that the cost is front loaded in avoidance and design, not in the mitigation credits at the end. Redesigning a subdivision to avoid a wetland costs engineering fees and sometimes lost lots. Minimization costs construction complexity. Credits are priced in a market and are knowable in advance. Applicants who budget only for the credits and not for the redesign are budgeting for the last step of a three step process, and the surprise arrives at step one.

## The 404(b)(1) Guidelines Up Close

The guidelines at 40 CFR Part 230 are the substantive law of the Section 404 program in a way the statute's spare text is not, and they repay close reading. EPA first issued them in 1975 and revised them comprehensively in 1980, and they have not been fundamentally rewritten since, which means four decades of permit decisions, administrative appeals, and judicial review have accreted around stable regulatory text. The guidelines bind the Corps: a permit that does not comply with them cannot lawfully issue, and the Corps documents its compliance in a written statement of findings that becomes part of the administrative record a court reviews. Understanding the guidelines' four tests is understanding what the Corps must find before it may say yes.

The first and most important test is the practicable alternatives requirement of 40 CFR 230.10(a): no discharge of dredged or fill material shall be permitted if there is a practicable alternative to the proposed discharge which would have less adverse impact on the aquatic ecosystem, so long as the alternative does not have other significant adverse environmental consequences. The regulation defines practicable with care: an alternative is practicable if it is capable of being done after taking into consideration cost, existing technology, and logistics in light of the overall project purposes. That final phrase, in light of the overall project purposes, is where most alternatives disputes are fought. The Corps does not ask whether the applicant could build something else entirely; it asks whether the applicant's stated purpose, defined at a reasonable level of generality, could be achieved with less aquatic impact. An applicant who defines the project purpose as building a shopping center on this particular parcel will face a different analysis than one who defines it as providing retail space in the county, and the Corps scrutinizes purpose statements that appear drawn to eliminate alternatives by definition.

The water dependency presumptions sharpen the test. For non water dependent activities, the guidelines establish a rebuttable presumption that practicable alternatives that do not involve special aquatic sites are available, and a second presumption that all practicable alternatives to a proposed discharge into a special aquatic site that do not involve such a site are presumed to have less adverse impact on the aquatic ecosystem. Special aquatic sites are the habitats the guidelines single out for extra protection: sanctuaries and refuges, wetlands, mud flats, vegetated shallows, coral reefs, and riffle and pool complexes. The double presumption means that a housing developer proposing to fill a wetland begins the proceeding losing on avoidance and must produce evidence, on cost, technology, and logistics, sufficient to rebut both presumptions. The Corps evaluates rebuttal evidence against the record of what comparable projects have achieved, which is why consultants maintain files of alternative site analyses from prior permits: the practicability of avoidance is judged comparatively, not in the abstract.

The second test, at 40 CFR 230.10(b), bars any discharge that would violate applicable water quality or toxic effluent standards, and the third, at 40 CFR 230.10(c), bars any discharge that will cause or contribute to significant degradation of the waters of the United States. The significant degradation test looks to four factors: effects on human health and welfare including municipal water supplies and fish and shellfish for human consumption; effects on the life stages of aquatic life and other wildlife dependent on aquatic ecosystems, including transfer and concentration of contaminants through biological processes; effects on aquatic ecosystem diversity, productivity, and stability, including loss of fish and wildlife habitat; and effects on recreational, aesthetic, and economic values. This is the test EPA's 404(c) veto standard echoes, and it is the guidelines' substantive backstop: even a project with no practicable alternative and a generous mitigation package cannot be permitted if it would significantly degrade the waters.

The fourth test, at 40 CFR 230.10(d), requires appropriate and practicable steps to minimize potential adverse impacts of the discharge on the aquatic ecosystem, which is the regulatory home of the minimization rung. Section 230.11 then requires the Corps to make factual determinations on the record about the physical substrate, suspended particulates and turbidity, water quality, current patterns and water circulation, normal water level fluctuations, and salinity gradients, among others. These factual determinations are the unglamorous foundation of the decision: a permit file without them is a file a reviewing court can vacate for failing to build the record the guidelines demand.

The sequencing itself, the avoid minimize compensate order, was elaborated in a February 1990 memorandum of agreement between EPA and the Army on the determination of mitigation under the guidelines. The MOA gave regulatory flesh to the sequence: first avoid, then minimize, then compensate for unavoidable impacts, with the agencies endorsing the national goal of no overall net loss of wetlands functions and values, a goal first articulated by the National Wetlands Policy Forum in 1988 and adopted as federal policy the following year. The MOA also clarified that the sequence applies to all discharges, that compensation cannot substitute for avoidance, and that the agencies would evaluate mitigation proposals for their likelihood of success. The 1990 agreement is the document practitioners cite when a district engineer is pressed to accept a lavish compensation offer in lieu of a serious alternatives analysis: the agencies agreed, in writing, that the order is not negotiable.

The guidelines must be distinguished from the Corps' public interest review, because the two standards do different work and applicants confuse them constantly. The guidelines set the environmental floor: if the discharge fails the practicable alternatives test, violates water quality standards, causes significant degradation, or lacks adequate minimization, the permit cannot issue no matter how beneficial the project. The public interest review at 33 CFR 320.4(a) is the balancing that happens above the floor: weighing the project's benefits against its detriments across the full list of public interest factors. A project can clear the guidelines and still be denied on public interest grounds, and the Corps' statement of findings must address both. The distinction matters on judicial review as well, because courts examine guidelines compliance as a question of whether the Corps followed its binding substantive standards, while public interest balancing receives the deference courts give to an agency's predictive and policy judgments.

## The Fill Material Rule and the Hard Cases

The entire Section 404 program turns on the meaning of two words, fill material, and the agencies spent decades fighting over their definition before settling it in a 2002 rulemaking that remains one of the program's most consequential legal events. The statute itself does not define fill material. The Corps' regulatory definition at 33 CFR 323.2(e) describes material placed in waters where the material has the effect of replacing any portion of a water with dry land or changing the bottom elevation of any portion of a water, and lists examples including rock, sand, soil, clay, and construction debris. The definition's breadth is the program's jurisdictional engine: anything that raises the bottom or creates dry land counts, regardless of the material's composition or the placer's motive. But the boundary cases, the materials and activities at the definition's edge, generated three decades of litigation that shaped the program as much as any permit decision.

The first boundary fight concerned mechanized land clearing in wetlands. In 1993, the Corps and EPA issued the Tulloch rule, which provided that the redeposit of dredged material incidental to mechanized land clearing, ditching, and channelization constituted a regulable discharge. Wetlands developers and the forestry industry challenged the rule, and in 1997 a federal district court invalidated it in American Mining Congress v. United States Army Corps of Engineers, holding that Congress had not authorized the agencies to regulate incidental fallback, the small volume of material that falls back into the water in the course of digging it out. The D.C. Circuit affirmed in 1998. The Tulloch episode established a durable limit: the agencies can regulate the placement of material, but they cannot stretch the discharge concept to cover the unavoidable residue of excavation itself. The line between regulable redeposit and incidental fallback remains one of the most fact intensive inquiries in the program, turning on the volume of material moved and the method of excavation.

The second boundary fight concerned mining waste, and it ended with the 2002 fill material rule. Before 2002, the Corps and EPA maintained different regulatory definitions of fill material, and the Corps had taken the position that waste from surface coal mining, including the overburden placed in valleys adjacent to mountaintop removal mines, could be authorized as fill. Environmental organizations argued that mining waste was pollution subject to section 402, not fill subject to section 404, and that the Corps was using the 404 program to authorize valley fills that destroyed hundreds of miles of headwater streams. The agencies responded with a joint rulemaking published May 9, 2002 at 67 FR 31129 that conformed the two definitions and expressly provided that fill material includes material with the effect of replacing waters with dry land or changing bottom elevation regardless of purpose, a definition broad enough to encompass mining overburden. The rule was challenged immediately and upheld by the courts, and it stands today as the legal foundation for the valley fill authorizations that remain among the most controversial individual permits in the program. The controversy illustrates the program's structural reality: once material qualifies as fill, the 404 machinery, alternatives analysis, sequencing, mitigation, can authorize its placement, and opponents of a project must fight within that machinery rather than outside it.

The third boundary is statutory rather than regulatory: the exemptions of section 404(f). Congress provided at 33 U.S.C. 1344(f)(1) that the discharge of dredged or fill material from normal farming, silviculture, and ranching activities, from maintenance of existing structures, from construction of farm and stock ponds and irrigation ditches, and from several other specified activities, is not prohibited by the permit requirement, provided the activity is part of an established, ongoing operation. The exemption was the price of the statute's passage through an agricultural Congress, and it removes a vast category of rural earthmoving from the program entirely. But Congress added a recapture clause at 404(f)(2): the exemption does not apply where the activity's purpose is to bring a water into a use to which it was not previously subject and where the flow or circulation of the waters may be impaired or the reach of the waters reduced. Draining a wetland to convert it to cropland, the classic recapture scenario, requires a permit despite the farming exemption. The recapture clause is the provision that keeps 404(f) from swallowing the program in agricultural regions, and its application turns on the fact specific question of whether the operation is genuinely established and ongoing or a conversion disguised as maintenance.

Together the three boundary episodes teach the same lesson from different directions. The program's reach is defined less by the statute's broad commands than by the accumulated settlements of its definitional wars: what counts as a discharge, what counts as fill, and what Congress exempted. The applicant's first question is always whether the activity needs a permit at all, and the answer lives in these boundaries.

## Compensatory Mitigation and the Credit Market

Compensatory mitigation is where the Section 404 program most visibly became a market. The concept is simple: when a permit authorizes unavoidable impacts to wetlands or streams, the permittee must offset those impacts by restoring, establishing, enhancing, or in limited cases preserving other aquatic resources. The execution, however, spent two decades failing before the agencies fixed it, and the fix, the 2008 compensatory mitigation rule, is the most important rulemaking in the program's history after the guidelines themselves.

The failure that prompted the rule is documented in a National Research Council report published in 2001, Compensating for Wetland Losses Under the Clean Water Act, which examined permittee responsible mitigation, the traditional practice in which the developer itself built the replacement wetland, often on a leftover corner of the project site. The Council found that many of these projects did not meet their permit conditions, that monitoring was thin, that long term stewardship was frequently nobody's job, and that the program could not demonstrate that it was achieving no net loss of wetlands functions. The finding was not that mitigation was a bad idea but that the institutional design was: the party with the least ecological expertise and the strongest incentive to minimize cost was responsible for the ecological outcome, and the regulator lacked the resources to babysit thousands of small projects to maturity.

The 2008 rule, published on April 10, 2008 and codified at 33 CFR Parts 325 and 332 with a parallel EPA regulation at 40 CFR Part 230 Subpart J, rebuilt the system around three mechanisms and a preference order. The mechanisms are mitigation banks, in lieu fee programs, and permittee responsible mitigation. A mitigation bank is a site where wetlands, streams, or other aquatic resources have been restored, established, enhanced, or preserved in advance, and the ecological lift is quantified as credits that the bank sponsor may sell to permittees. An in lieu fee program is run by a government agency or nonprofit sponsor that collects fees from permittees and pools them to fund larger mitigation projects, selling advance credits before the projects are complete under an approved compensation planning framework. Permittee responsible mitigation is the old model: the permittee does the work itself. The rule's preference order, at 33 CFR 332.3(b), directs the district engineer to prefer mitigation bank credits first, in lieu fee credits second, and permittee responsible mitigation last, with discretion to override the order where the considerations behind it point the other way, for example where an in lieu fee program has released credits from an approved project or a permittee responsible project would restore an outstanding resource on rigorous scientific footing.

The preference order reflects a judgment about scale and accountability. Mitigation banks consolidate compensation into larger sites managed by specialists whose business depends on ecological success, because credits cannot be sold until performance standards are met and the bank instrument, the legal document that establishes the bank, requires financial assurances and long term management funding. In lieu fee programs operate at a watershed scale under a compensation planning framework that identifies high priority resource needs, which addresses the postage stamp problem of tiny scattered mitigation sites. Permittee responsible mitigation remains available where banks and in lieu fee programs lack the right credits in the service area, and the rule requires it to follow a watershed approach where practicable. The district engineer may depart from the preference where the facts warrant, but the default reshaped the market: bank credits became the standard currency of 404 compliance, and a nationwide industry of mitigation bankers, credit brokers, and ecological consultants grew up to supply them.

The rule also imposed discipline that applies to all three mechanisms. Mitigation plans must include measurable performance standards, monitoring periods, typically a minimum of five years, financial assurances such as bonds or escrow accounts, and long term management plans with dedicated funding, often through conservation easements held by land trusts and endowments for stewardship. The Corps tracks debits and credits through its Regulatory In lieu fee and Bank Information Tracking System, and the public can see which banks hold credits in a given service area. None of this guarantees ecological success, and the literature continues to debate whether the program achieves functional replacement, but the 2008 rule moved the program from a regime of promises to a regime of instruments, assurances, and ledgers.

### Who holds the legal responsibility for a mitigation bank credit after it is sold?

The bank sponsor holds it. Once a permittee buys credits from an approved bank, the permittee's compensation obligation is satisfied and long term responsibility, meeting performance standards and managing the site in perpetuity, transfers to the sponsor under the bank instrument. This liability transfer is a central reason the 2008 rule prefers bank credits.

The credit market has its own economics that applicants must understand. Credits are denominated by resource type and service area: a forested wetland credit in one watershed cannot offset an emergent wetland impact in another, and prices vary enormously with scarcity, from a few thousand dollars per credit in credit rich regions to well into six figures where banks are few and impacts are many. The service area is typically defined by watershed boundaries, often the eight digit hydrologic unit, and the district engineer can approve out of service area use only with justification. Applicants sometimes discover that no bank serves their project area, in which case the choice narrows to in lieu fee or permittee responsible mitigation, and the timeline lengthens accordingly. The market prices the scarcity of ecological function, which is precisely what the program intends, but it means that mitigation cost is a site selection variable as much as a compliance cost, and sophisticated developers evaluate credit availability before they buy land.

The contrast with single agency rulemaking is instructive here. The [EPA Clean Air Act rulemaking guide](/2011/12/01/epa-clean-air-act-rulemaking/) describes a model in which one agency writes the rule, implements it, and enforces it, with states implementing under delegated programs. The mitigation rule is the opposite: a joint Corps and EPA rulemaking that creates a market administered through individual permit decisions by district engineers, with EPA retaining guideline authority and oversight. The joint authorship is why the rule took the form it did, detailed enough to constrain district discretion but flexible enough to let districts adapt to regional ecology, and it is a case study in how the split authority of section 404 propagates into every corner of implementation.

The preference hierarchy is only one of the 2008 rule's structural innovations. The rule also imposed the watershed approach, requiring that compensation be sited and designed to address the watershed's needs rather than merely replacing acreage near the impact site. A district engineer evaluating a mitigation proposal asks where in the watershed aquatic functions are most degraded and where restoration would produce the greatest functional lift, and a proposal that restores a degraded floodplain upstream can outrank a proposal that creates a small wetland adjacent to the project. The rule operationalizes this through the service area, the geographic area within which a bank or in lieu fee program may provide compensation for authorized impacts. Credits generated outside the service area generally cannot compensate for impacts inside it, which means the applicant's choice of compensation is constrained by geography as well as by the preference hierarchy.

The rule also disciplined the timing of compensation through the credit release schedule. A mitigation bank does not receive all of its credits on the day the banking instrument is approved. Credits are released in phases tied to performance milestones: the bank's establishment, the completion of earthwork and planting, the achievement of hydrologic performance standards, and the demonstration of ecological success over successive monitoring years. The district engineer, advised by the Interagency Review Team of federal and state resource agencies, approves each release after reviewing monitoring reports. This phased release is the mechanism that prevents the old failure mode in which compensation was promised, credits were sold, and the wetland never materialized. A bank sponsor finances the restoration upfront and recovers the investment as credits release, which aligns the sponsor's economic incentives with ecological performance in a way the old permittee responsible system never did.

The rule took a skeptical view of preservation as compensation. Protecting an existing wetland from future destruction, the rule provides, may count as compensation only in exceptional circumstances, and only where the preservation is combined with restoration, establishment, or enhancement of other aquatic resources. The reasoning is arithmetic: preservation alone produces no net gain in aquatic functions, so crediting it at face value would allow the program to authorize net losses while claiming compensation. Districts apply this restriction strictly, and applicants who propose to satisfy their obligations by buying and protecting an existing wetland are routinely told to add a restoration component or choose a different mechanism.

For permittee responsible mitigation, the rule's mitigation plan requirements remain the most demanding in the program. The plan must address site selection, baseline conditions, the determination of credits, the work plan, performance standards, monitoring, long term management, adaptive management, and financial assurances, among other elements, and the district engineer must approve the plan before issuing the permit. The financial assurances requirement, bonds, letters of credit, or other instruments sufficient to fund the mitigation if the permittee defaults, was a direct response to the history of abandoned mitigation sites. The plan's long term management provisions must identify the party responsible for the site in perpetuity and the funding mechanism for that stewardship, typically an endowment or a transfer to a conservation organization. The days when a permittee could satisfy the compensation rung with a sketch and a promise ended with this rule.

## Section 401: The State Certification Lever

Section 401 of the Clean Water Act, codified at 33 U.S.C. 1341, gives states and authorized tribes a power that is easy to underestimate until it is exercised: no federal license or permit for an activity that may result in a discharge into covered waters may issue unless the state or tribe certifies that the discharge will comply with applicable water quality requirements, or waives certification. For Section 404 purposes, this means the Corps cannot issue an individual permit, and in many cases cannot verify a nationwide permit, without the state's certification decision. The state may grant certification outright, grant it with conditions that become conditions of the federal permit, deny it, or waive it by inaction within the statutory reasonable period, which may not exceed one year. A denial is functionally a veto of the federal permit, exercised not by EPA but by the state.

The scope of what a state may consider in certification has been the most fought over question in the provision's history, and the answer has swung with the rulemakings. The 2020 certification rule, published July 13, 2020, narrowed the scope to the discharge itself and imposed procedural constraints that states viewed as stripping their authority, including a narrow definition of water quality requirements and limits on the conditions states could impose. States and tribes challenged the rule, a federal district court vacated it in October 2021, the Ninth Circuit stayed the vacatur while the appeal proceeded, and the regulatory landscape seesawed until EPA finalized a new rule on September 27, 2023, effective November 27, 2023. The 2023 rule restored the broader scope: the certifying authority evaluates whether the activity as a whole will comply with applicable water quality requirements, not merely the point source discharge. It set a default reasonable period of six months for the state to act, which the federal agency and the certifying authority may extend by agreement up to the one year statutory maximum. It required early engagement through a pre filing meeting request and defined the contents of a certification request, and it limited the federal agency's review of the certification decision to whether the proper authority acted within its procedures and within the reasonable period.

The practical consequence is that section 401 is the provision through which states exercise the most direct control over federally permitted projects, and it has become a major lever in energy infrastructure disputes. A pipeline, a hydroelectric dam relicensing, or a liquefied natural gas terminal needs federal permits, and the state certification decision can condition the project with requirements that reshape its economics or deny it outright. The provision's defenders describe this as cooperative federalism working as designed: the state that lives with the water quality consequences gets a say. Its critics describe it as a second veto layered on top of the federal review, wielded for purposes beyond water quality. The 2023 rule's activity as a whole scope sits squarely in the middle of this fight, and the litigation over it continues, which is why applicants track the certification docket as closely as the permit docket.

### When does a section 401 certification become the decisive veto in a federal permit?

It becomes decisive when the state denies certification or attaches conditions the project cannot absorb economically. Because the Corps cannot issue the federal permit without the certification, a denial stops the project as surely as a permit denial. Applicants therefore run the state process as a parallel track with its own timeline, record, and litigation risk.

For nationwide permits, section 401 operates at two levels, and the distinction confuses many applicants. When the Corps reissues the nationwide permits, states certify the reissuance itself: a state may grant, condition, deny, or waive certification for each nationwide permit as a category. If a state denies certification for a particular nationwide permit, applicants in that state cannot use that permit and must seek individual permits or alternative authorizations. Then, for individual projects, some states require project specific certification even where the nationwide permit was certified at reissuance, particularly where the permit requires pre construction notification. The result is a matrix: the nationwide permit, the state certification of the permit, and the project level verification, each with its own conditions. Applicants who assume that a nationwide permit is self executing in all fifty states discover the matrix the hard way.

Tribal authority under section 401 deserves separate mention. Tribes with treatment as a state status may certify federal permits affecting their waters, and the 2023 rule's framework applies to tribal certifying authorities on the same terms. The tribal role has grown as more tribes have obtained the status, and it adds a sovereign whose water quality standards and certification procedures the applicant must learn alongside the state's.

## Section 401: The Rule Swings

The certification requirement has one of the most volatile regulatory histories in the Clean Water Act, because its scope determines how much power states hold over federal permits, and the energy industry and the states have fought over that question for thirty years. The provision began as section 21(b) of the 1970 Water Quality Improvement Act and was carried into the 1972 statute as section 401 with language requiring state certification that any federally licensed activity that may result in a discharge into navigable waters will comply with water quality requirements. For two decades the provision operated quietly, its scope unexamined. Then the Supreme Court gave it two landmark readings that expanded state power, and two administrations wrote two incompatible rules that shrank and restored it.

The first landmark was PUD No. 1 of Jefferson County v. Washington Department of Ecology, decided in 1994 at 511 U.S. 700. A public utility district sought a federal hydropower license for a dam on the Dosewallips River, and Washington conditioned its certification on minimum stream flows to protect salmon. The utility argued that the state could only regulate the discharge itself, not the broader activity. The Court rejected that reading, holding that section 401(d) authorizes conditions on the activity as a whole, not merely the discharge, and that the state's minimum flow requirement was a legitimate water quality condition. Twelve years later, S.D. Warren Co. v. Maine Board of Environmental Protection, 547 U.S. 370 (2006), held that a dam's release of water downstream constitutes a discharge triggering the certification requirement, closing the industry's argument that dams merely move water without discharging it. Together the two decisions made section 401 the potent state lever described above: states could condition federally licensed projects on the full range of water quality protections, and dam operators could not escape certification by definitional maneuver.

The 2020 rule reversed that understanding. Issued by EPA on July 13, 2020 at 85 FR 42210 and effective September 11, 2020, the rule confined certification to the discharge rather than the activity, limited the conditions states could impose to water quality requirements narrowly defined, and gave federal licensing agencies the power to determine the reasonable period of time for state action within the one year statutory maximum, with waiver as the consequence of state delay. Industry groups and several states welcomed the rule as a cure for what they described as state misuse of certification to block pipelines and other infrastructure; environmental groups and many states denounced it as an unlawful surrender of the Clean Water Act's cooperative federalism.

The rule's legal life was chaotic. A federal district court in California vacated the rule in October 2021. In April 2022 the Supreme Court stayed that vacatur, restoring the 2020 rule while the appeal proceeded. The Ninth Circuit reversed the vacatur order in February 2023, and the case was dismissed as moot in January 2024 after the 2023 rulemaking superseded the 2020 framework. The seesaw ended when EPA issued a new rule on September 27, 2023 at 88 FR 66558, effective November 27, 2023, that repealed the 2020 framework and restored the activity as a whole scope that PUD No. 1 had recognized. The 2023 rule required a pre filing meeting with the certifying authority, specified seven categories of information a certification request must contain, set a default six month reasonable period that the federal agency and the certifying authority could extend by agreement up to the one year statutory maximum, and confined federal agency review of state decisions to verifying that the certification was issued by the appropriate authority, that public notice procedures were followed, and that the certification was acted on within the reasonable period. A preliminary injunction challenge brought by eleven states and industry groups was denied, leaving the rule in place as the operative framework.

The 2023 rule also preserved the neighboring jurisdiction procedure of section 401(a)(2), the provision that gives downstream states a voice in upstream federal permits. When a federal agency receives a certification and a license application for a discharge that may affect the water quality of another state, the agency must notify EPA, which in turn notifies the potentially affected state. That state then has thirty days to object on the ground that the discharge will violate its water quality requirements, and if it objects, the federal agency must hold a public hearing on the objection. The licensing agency must condition the license as necessary to ensure compliance with the objecting state's requirements. The procedure is rarely invoked, but it is the Clean Water Act's answer to the interstate externality problem: pollution does not respect state lines, and the downstream state's water quality standards deserve protection even when the federal permit is issued upstream. For interstate pipelines and transmission projects that cross multiple states, the neighboring jurisdiction process is the sleeper provision that can generate a second front of state level review after the certifying state's process has concluded.

The most famous certification fight remains New York's 2016 denial of section 401 certification for the Constitution Pipeline, a natural gas pipeline proposed to cross New York waterways. The state's environmental agency denied certification on the ground that the application failed to demonstrate compliance with water quality standards, and the denial survived federal court review, effectively killing the project after years of investment. The episode became the industry's exhibit for certification overreach and the states' exhibit for the provision working as Congress intended, and it was cited repeatedly in the rulemaking debates of both 2020 and 2023. For the Section 404 applicant, the lesson is practical rather than ideological: the state certification process runs on its own timeline, builds its own administrative record, and can end the project regardless of how well the Corps file is managed. Applicants who sequence the state and federal processes as a single coordinated campaign fare better than those who treat certification as an afterthought.

## The Species Consultation Overlay

No account of Section 404 permitting is complete without the Endangered Species Act, because the section 7 consultation requirement attaches to nearly every federal permit decision and has killed or reshaped more 404 permits than any other overlapping statute. Section 7(a)(2) of the Endangered Species Act requires every federal agency, in consultation with the Fish and Wildlife Service or the National Marine Fisheries Service, to insure that any action authorized, funded, or carried out by the agency is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of any listed species or result in the destruction or adverse modification of designated critical habitat. A Corps permit decision is an agency action within that mandate. The Corps, as the action agency, must consult whenever its permit may affect a listed species or critical habitat, and the consultation must conclude before the permit issues.

The mechanics run through 50 CFR Part 402. The Corps first determines whether listed species or critical habitat may be present in the action area, using the Services' information, planning, and consultation systems and often a biological survey of the project site. If the Corps finds no effect, the inquiry ends with documentation. If the Corps finds the action may affect but is not likely to adversely affect listed species, it seeks the Service's written concurrence through informal consultation. If adverse effects are likely, the Corps enters formal consultation, preparing a biological assessment of the action's effects and submitting it to the Service, which then issues a biological opinion determining whether the action would cause jeopardy or adverse modification. A jeopardy opinion stops the permit unless the Corps adopts the opinion's reasonable and prudent alternatives, project modifications that would avoid jeopardy. A no jeopardy opinion typically includes an incidental take statement authorizing a specified level of take of listed species incidental to the project, with reasonable and prudent measures to minimize that take. The applicant implements those measures as permit conditions.

The consultation overlay changes the economics and timeline of 404 permitting in ways applicants underestimate. Formal consultation has a statutory timetable, ninety days for consultation and forty five days for the biological opinion, with extensions by mutual agreement, but the biological assessment that precedes it can take months to prepare, and the Services' workload means the timetable is often aspirational. More importantly, the jeopardy standard is substantive: a project that satisfies the 404(b)(1) guidelines can still fail section 7 if it would push a listed species toward extinction. The Corps cannot lawfully issue the permit until consultation concludes, which gives the Services an effective veto over 404 permits affecting listed species, a veto exercised through the biological opinion rather than through the Clean Water Act's own machinery.

The most famous intersection of the two statutes is TVA v. Hill, decided by the Supreme Court in 1978 at 437 U.S. 153. The Tennessee Valley Authority had nearly completed the Tellico Dam when the snail darter, a small fish listed as endangered, was discovered in the Little Tennessee River. The Court held that the Endangered Species Act required the dam's impoundment to stop, famously declaring that Congress had intended endangered species to be afforded the highest of priorities. Congress later exempted the Tellico project through a rider, and the dam was completed, but the case established the principle that the species consultation requirement is not a procedural nicety: it is a substantive constraint that can halt a nearly finished federal project. Every Corps district engineer who has sat through a difficult formal consultation knows the case, because it is the precedent that gives the consultation its gravity.

Programmatic consultations cover the nationwide permits, which is one reason the general permit system can function at the Corps' volume. The Corps consults with the Services on the reissuance of the nationwide permits as a program, and the general conditions include the endangered species condition requiring applicants to notify the district when listed species may be affected. But programmatic coverage does not eliminate project level consultation: where a specific project under a nationwide permit may adversely affect a listed species, individual consultation follows, and the verification waits for it. The applicant who assumes that the nationwide permit lane bypasses the Endangered Species Act is the applicant whose project stops at the biological opinion stage.

## Jurisdiction: The Determination That Decides Everything

Every Section 404 analysis begins with a question that precedes the permit: is the water a covered water at all? The statute prohibits discharges into navigable waters, defined as waters of the United States, and the scope of that phrase has been the most litigated boundary in American environmental law. The Supreme Court has addressed it repeatedly, most consequentially in Sackett v. EPA in 2023, which narrowed the test for adjacent wetlands to those with a continuous surface connection to covered waters such that the wetland is indistinguishable from the water. The details of that decision belong to the statute's jurisdictional history, which the complete guide carries. For the permit practitioner, the consequence is procedural: before designing a permit strategy, the applicant needs the Corps' answer on jurisdiction, and the instrument for obtaining it is the jurisdictional determination.

The Corps issues two kinds of jurisdictional determinations, and confusing them is expensive. An approved jurisdictional determination is the Corps' official, written, appealable finding that jurisdictional waters are present or absent on a site, documented on the approved JD form with supporting data. It is valid for five years from the date of the transmittal letter, unless new information warrants revision or the district engineer has identified rapidly changing conditions in the area, and it binds the federal government: a negative approved JD, finding no covered waters, protects the landowner from federal enforcement for its five year duration. A preliminary jurisdictional determination is advisory only: the Corps indicates that there may be covered waters on the site without making a definitive finding, which allows the applicant to proceed to permitting without conceding or contesting jurisdiction. The preliminary JD cannot be appealed, carries no expiration date, and does not bind the government in a later enforcement action. Applicants choose the preliminary route to move faster, and the approved route to obtain certainty, and the choice is one of the earliest strategic decisions in any project touching possible wetlands.

The five year validity of the approved JD reflects a judgment that landscapes and law both change. Wetlands migrate, drainage patterns shift, and the legal definition of covered waters has been rewritten by rulemaking and litigation on a cycle that has rarely rested for long. An approved JD issued under one jurisdictional rule may describe a different set of waters than the same site would yield under its successor, which is why the Corps reserves the right to revise on new information and why sophisticated applicants calendar the expiration date. The preliminary JD avoids the expiration problem by making no finding at all, at the cost of leaving the jurisdictional question open for the life of the project.

### What is the practical difference between an approved and a preliminary jurisdictional determination?

An approved determination is binding, appealable, and valid for five years, and a negative finding shields the landowner from federal enforcement while it lasts. A preliminary determination is advisory only: it lets the applicant advance a permit without litigating jurisdiction, but it creates no shield, carries no expiration, and cannot be appealed.

Approved JDs are appealable through the Corps' administrative appeal process at 33 CFR Part 331, which covers permit denials, declined proffered permits, and approved JDs. The appeal goes to the division engineer, must be filed within sixty days, and is decided on the administrative record. Beyond the administrative appeal, the Supreme Court held unanimously in U.S. Army Corps of Engineers v. Hawkes Co. in 2016 that an approved JD is final agency action subject to judicial review under the Administrative Procedure Act, because it marks the consummation of the agency's decisionmaking process and creates legal consequences by binding the government. The Hawkes decision matters because it gave landowners a courthouse door at the jurisdictional stage: rather than filling the wetland and awaiting enforcement, or enduring the full permit process to challenge jurisdiction at the end, a landowner with an adverse approved JD can seek judicial review of the jurisdictional call itself.

Wetland identification on the ground follows the Corps' 1987 Wetlands Delineation Manual and its regional supplements, which require three parameters for a positive wetland finding: hydrophytic vegetation, hydric soils, and wetland hydrology. The delineation is a technical exercise performed by trained consultants, reviewed by the Corps, and frequently contested at the margins, where the difference between a hydric and a non hydric soil, or between seasonal ponding and wetland hydrology, decides whether a multi acre parcel needs a permit. The delineation manual is not the jurisdictional rule, a distinction worth preserving: the manual identifies wetlands as a scientific matter, and the jurisdictional rule decides which of those wetlands the Clean Water Act covers as a legal matter. Sackett narrowed the legal category without changing the science, which is why delineations continue to find wetlands that the current jurisdictional test does not cover.

## Jurisdictional Determinations: Mechanics and Strategy

The determination process is the Section 404 program's front door, and its mechanics reward applicants who understand that the Corps' jurisdiction finding is a technical product built from field data, not a legal opinion handed down from Washington. A jurisdictional determination begins with a request from an affected party, typically the landowner or a prospective purchaser, submitted to the district where the property sits. The Corps expects the requester to supply a delineation of the aquatic resources on the site prepared in accordance with the 1987 wetlands delineation manual and the applicable regional supplement, with data sheets documenting vegetation, soils, and hydrology at representative sample points. The district reviews the submission, frequently conducts its own site visit, and issues either an approved or a preliminary determination. The quality of the requester's delineation matters enormously: a sloppy data set invites the district to do the delineation itself, which takes longer and may find more water than the applicant hoped.

The 1987 manual remains the methodological backbone. It requires positive indicators of all three parameters, hydrophytic vegetation, hydric soils, and wetland hydrology, under normal circumstances, with atypical situation procedures for sites that have been disturbed. The ten regional supplements refine the indicators for the country's major ecological regions, from the arid West to the Atlantic and Gulf coastal plain, and the supplement for the site's region controls which field indicators the district accepts. Practitioners learn the supplements the way litigators learn local rules: the outcome of a close call on hydric soil indicators or hydrology observations often turns on which supplement applies and how the district has applied it in prior determinations nearby.

The two determination types create a genuine strategic choice. An approved determination, governed by Corps guidance that treats it as valid for five years, is the formal, appealable finding that waters of the United States are present or absent. A negative approved determination is the most valuable piece of paper in the program: it is the Corps' binding word that no federal permit is required, and enforcement cannot reach the landowner while it stands. A preliminary determination, by contrast, is an advisory indication that waters may be present, issued so the applicant can move a permit application forward without spending months or years litigating the jurisdiction question first. The preliminary determination creates no shield, cannot be appealed, and carries no expiration date, but it lets the applicant get to the permit decision faster. The choice depends on the landowner's risk tolerance: a developer confident that the site is dry may seek the negative approved determination and its five year protection, while a developer who needs to start construction may accept the preliminary determination and fight the jurisdiction question inside the permit proceeding if it becomes necessary.

Disputes over jurisdiction between the Corps and EPA are resolved by a separate institutional mechanism that applicants rarely see but should know exists. A 1979 opinion of the Attorney General assigned EPA the final administrative authority to determine the reach of the term navigable waters under the Clean Water Act, and a 1989 memorandum of agreement between the two agencies established the procedure for jurisdictional determinations on which they disagree, including special case determinations in which EPA resolves the dispute. Corps training materials describe the arrangement as giving EPA the last word on Clean Water Act jurisdiction. In practice, district determinations are rarely elevated to that level, because the Corps applies the regulatory definition in effect at the time and EPA intervenes only in cases with significant policy implications. But the mechanism explains why the definition of waters of the United States matters so intensely: every approved determination is issued under the rule then in force, and a change in the rule changes the jurisdictional answer for the next determination even when the landscape has not changed.

The five year validity period creates its own strategy. A negative determination obtained early in a project's planning can expire before construction begins, forcing a new request under whatever definition then applies. Phased developments face the recurring question of whether to seek one determination for the whole project area or staged determinations as phases advance, trading the certainty of a single finding against the risk that a later determination under a broader rule finds more jurisdiction. After Hawkes, the appealability of approved determinations gives the landowner a procedural check on an adverse finding, but the appeal is decided on the administrative record under the definition then in effect, so the timing of the request relative to rulemaking cycles remains the variable the landowner can least afford to ignore.

## Enforcement: What Unauthorized Filling Exposes a Landowner To

The enforcement side of Section 404 is where the sequence the guidelines require becomes a one way ratchet. The statute's prohibition is simple: section 301(a) of the Clean Water Act, 33 U.S.C. 1311(a), makes the discharge of any pollutant, including dredged or fill material, unlawful except in compliance with a permit, and section 404 supplies the permit. A landowner who places fill in covered waters without a 404 authorization has violated section 301, and the remedies available to the government are severe by design, because the resource destroyed by filling cannot be unfilled by paying a fine.

The Corps' enforcement process runs under 33 CFR Part 326 and typically begins with discovery: a neighbor's complaint, a referral from a state agency, satellite imagery review, or a Corps inspector's site visit. The district investigates, documents the violation, and issues a notice of violation or a cease and desist order directing that work stop. The district then seeks resolution, and the options range across voluntary restoration, where the violator removes the fill and reestablish the wetland under Corps supervision, to an after the fact permit application, where the violator applies for the permit that should have been sought first. The after the fact permit is not a forgiveness mechanism: the Corps evaluates it under the same guidelines, including the practicable alternatives test, and the fact that the fill is already in place does not make it the permitted alternative. Where the violation cannot be resolved administratively, the Corps refers the matter to the Environmental Protection Agency or the Department of Justice for civil or criminal enforcement.

The civil penalty structure is the part of the program that most concentrates the mind. Section 309(d) of the Act, 33 U.S.C. 1319(d), authorizes civil penalties of up to twenty five thousand dollars per day for each violation in a judicial enforcement action, and EPA's inflation adjustment rules raised the adjusted figure substantially over the following decades: the January 2025 inflation rule set the adjusted maximum at sixty eight thousand four hundred forty five dollars per day per violation, and that figure remained operative after the 2026 inflation increase was cancelled. Section 309(g), 33 U.S.C. 1319(g), authorizes administrative penalties in two classes: Class I penalties up to ten thousand dollars per violation with a twenty five thousand dollar aggregate cap, and Class II penalties up to ten thousand dollars per day with a one hundred twenty five thousand dollar aggregate cap, each likewise adjusted upward for inflation. Because the penalty runs per day per violation, a fill that sits unremediated for months accrues exposure arithmetically, and the government's settlement leverage reflects that arithmetic. Criminal liability under section 309(c) attaches to knowing violations, with heightened penalties for knowing endangerment.

### What happens first when the Corps discovers fill placed without a permit?

The district investigates and documents the site, then typically issues a notice of violation or cease and desist order stopping further work. Resolution follows through voluntary restoration under Corps supervision, an after the fact permit judged under the full guidelines, or referral to EPA or the Justice Department where the violation cannot be resolved administratively.

Citizen suits add a private enforcement dimension. Section 505 of the Act, 33 U.S.C. 1365, authorizes any citizen to sue any person alleged to be in violation of an effluent standard or limitation, which includes the section 301 prohibition on unpermitted discharges, subject to notice requirements and to bars where the government is diligently prosecuting. The citizen suit is the reason an approved JD's five year shield matters so much: a landowner holding a negative approved JD has the government's own determination to answer a citizen plaintiff, while a landowner proceeding on a consultant's delineation alone does not. Environmental organizations have used section 505 to police fills that the agencies lacked the resources to pursue, and developers treat the citizen suit exposure as a reason to obtain the approved JD rather than relying on the preliminary route.

Restoration, not money, is the remedy that defines 404 enforcement. Courts routinely order violators to remove unauthorized fill and restore the site's prior hydrology and vegetation, under plans approved by the Corps and monitored for years. The restoration order reflects the ladder's logic: because the violator skipped avoidance and minimization, the only way to reconstruct the sequence is to undo the discharge and start over. After the fact permits are available in theory, and the Corps does issue them where the fill turns out to satisfy the guidelines, but the violator pays a premium in delay, penalty exposure, and the near impossibility of demonstrating that no practicable alternative existed for a project already built. The rational strategy is the one the program's structure advertises: determine jurisdiction first, walk the ladder in order, and never let the fill precede the permit.

## The Enforcement File: Procedure Step by Step

Corps enforcement under 33 CFR Part 326 follows a defined administrative choreography that landowners should understand before they need it, because each step narrows the options that remain. The process typically begins when a district learns of a possible violation, through a public complaint, a referral from another agency, an aerial photograph, or a permit compliance inspection that finds work outside the authorized footprint. Under 33 CFR 326.3, the district may order initial corrective measures, including requiring the alleged violator to stop work and to take steps to prevent further harm while the investigation proceeds. The district then documents the site: photographs, surveys, and a delineation of the filled waters establish the baseline against which restoration or penalties will be measured. The quality of this documentation matters, because the entire enforcement file will be judged against it if the matter goes to court.

Section 326.4 gives the district three paths to resolve an unauthorized activity. The first is voluntary restoration: the violator agrees to restore the site to its pre violation condition under a restoration plan the Corps approves and supervises, with performance standards and monitoring to confirm the work. The second is the after the fact permit: the violator applies for a permit for the work already done, and the Corps evaluates the application under the full 404(b)(1) guidelines, including the practicable alternatives test. This is the path applicants misunderstand most. The after the fact permit is not a rubber stamp for completed work; the guidelines apply with full force, and the alternatives analysis can be harder to satisfy after the fact because the applicant has already demonstrated a preference for the chosen site by building on it. If the guidelines cannot be met, the permit is denied and restoration follows. The third path is referral: where the violation cannot be resolved administratively, the district refers the matter to EPA or to the Justice Department for civil or criminal enforcement under section 309.

The division of labor between the Corps and EPA reflects their different tools. The Corps resolves most violations administratively through restoration or after the fact permitting. EPA takes the cases that need the Clean Water Act's heavier machinery: repeat violators, violations causing significant environmental harm, cases where the violator refuses to cooperate, and cases where penalties beyond the Corps' administrative capacity are warranted. The Justice Department litigates the civil judicial cases and prosecutes the criminal ones. Section 309(c) provides criminal liability for negligent violations, for knowing violations, and for knowing endangerment, where a knowing violation places another person in imminent danger of death or serious bodily injury, with the latter carrying the statute's most severe penalties. The responsible corporate officer doctrine, under which senior managers can face liability for violations they had the authority to prevent, has been applied in Clean Water Act prosecutions and gives corporate defendants a reason to ensure that field supervisors understand the permit boundaries.

Citizen suits add a third enforcement front that operates independently of the agencies. Section 505 authorizes any citizen to sue any person alleged to be in violation of an effluent standard or limitation, which includes the requirement to hold a Section 404 permit for a discharge of dredged or fill material, after giving sixty days notice to the alleged violator, the state, and EPA. The diligent prosecution bar prevents the citizen suit from proceeding if the government is already diligently prosecuting a civil or criminal action for the same violation, but an administrative compliance order does not always trigger the bar, which means a landowner who settles with the Corps may still face a citizen suit. Citizen plaintiffs can obtain injunctions and civil penalties, with penalties payable to the United States Treasury, and prevailing plaintiffs can recover attorney's fees. The practical effect is that the enforcement risk does not end when the agencies close their file: any person with standing and patience can reopen it.

Restoration plans, where the violation is resolved through restoration, are technical documents that deserve respect. A typical plan specifies the grading needed to restore pre violation elevations and hydrology, the removal of unauthorized fill, the planting of native vegetation appropriate to the wetland type, controls for invasive species, and a monitoring period of several years with performance standards for vegetation cover, hydrology, and species composition. The Corps approves the plan and supervises its implementation, and failure to meet performance standards extends the obligation. For the landowner, the cost of a supervised multi year restoration routinely exceeds the cost of having sought the permit in the first place, which is the economic logic that makes the enforcement system work: the expected price of violation is designed to exceed the price of compliance.

One final procedural note concerns time. Civil penalties under the Clean Water Act are subject to the five year limitations period of 28 U.S.C. 2462, which runs from when the claim first accrues. For a continuing violation, such as fill that remains in the waters, courts have treated each day the fill remains as a new violation for limitations purposes, which means the clock does not necessarily save the landowner who filled years ago and has heard nothing since. The prudent assumption is that unauthorized fill is a liability that does not age out, and that the cheapest time to address it was before the first truckload was dumped.

Districts do not pursue every violation with equal intensity, and understanding the enforcement priorities helps landowners assess risk realistically. The Corps focuses its limited enforcement resources on violations involving high value aquatic resources, such as impacts to rare wetland types or habitat for listed species, on repeat violators who treat penalties as a cost of doing business, on violations discovered through the permit compliance program where the permittee exceeded the authorized footprint, and on cases referred by other agencies or brought to the district's attention by public complaints with documentation. Each district designates enforcement coordinators who manage the caseload, and the division reviews significant enforcement actions for consistency. The practical consequence is that a small, inadvertent fill in a low value ditch is likely to be resolved through voluntary restoration, while a deliberate large scale fill in a sensitive resource draws the full weight of the administrative and judicial machinery. The enforcement program is designed to be risk based, and the risk calculus is visible to anyone who reads the districts' enforcement reports.

## State Assumption and the Retained Waters Line

Section 404(g) offers states the most complete form of program authority the Clean Water Act provides anywhere: a state may assume administration of the dredge and fill permit program for waters within its borders, issuing permits in place of the Corps. The application is demanding. The state must submit a full program description, a statement from its attorney general that state law provides adequate authority, and a memorandum of agreement with the Corps and EPA allocating responsibilities, after which EPA approves the program following public notice and a hearing. The state program must have authority to issue permits that comply with the 404(b)(1) guidelines, to inspect and monitor, and to enforce against violations, and EPA retains oversight including the authority to object to individual state permits and to withdraw program approval under section 404(i) if the state fails to administer the program consistent with the Act.

The central technical question in every assumption is which waters the state takes and which the Corps retains. The statute provides that the Corps retains authority over waters traditionally used for navigation and their adjacent wetlands, but the precise line between assumed and retained waters has been contested for decades. A federal advisory subcommittee on assumable waters reported in 2015 with recommendations for clarifying the line, and the Corps issued a memorandum in August 2018 that limited retained waters to waters subject to the ebb and flow of the tide and waters presently used or susceptible to use for interstate or foreign commerce, the traditional section 10 waters, plus their adjacent wetlands. EPA finalized a rule in December 2024, effective January 17, 2025, that codified procedures for assumption and clarified the retained waters description, the Tribal and state coordination requirements, and the scope of EPA oversight. The retained waters line matters because it determines whether a given project needs a state permit, a Corps permit, or both, and because the line's complexity is one reason so few states have assumed the program.

Two states have operated assumed programs for decades. Michigan assumed the program in 1984 under Part 303 of its Natural Resources and Environmental Protection Act, and New Jersey assumed it in 1994 under its Freshwater Wetlands Protection Act. Both programs issue the permits the Corps would otherwise issue for non retained waters, apply the federal guidelines, and submit annual reports to EPA. Their longevity demonstrates that assumption can work, but their rarity demonstrates the barriers: the state must fund a permitting bureaucracy equivalent to the Corps districts it replaces, must defend the retained waters line in every permit, and must accept EPA oversight that includes the power to veto individual permits. For most states, the cost benefit analysis has favored leaving the program with the Corps.

Florida's brief assumption is the cautionary tale. EPA approved Florida's program in December 2020, and the state began issuing permits for non retained waters. Environmental organizations sued in January 2021, arguing that EPA's approval had failed to satisfy the Endangered Species Act's consultation requirements and other legal obligations. In February 2024, a federal district court vacated the approval, returning the program to the Corps overnight and stranding permit applications in midstream. The litigation continued through rehearing petitions in 2026, with the state seeking to revive the program and the challengers defending the vacatur. Whatever the final outcome, the episode taught every state considering assumption that the legal risk is not theoretical: an approved program can be unwound by a court, and the transition back to federal administration is disruptive for applicants caught in the switch.

The policy question underlying assumption is whether decentralization improves the program. Proponents argue that state agencies are closer to the waters they regulate, can integrate wetlands permitting with state land use and water programs, and can move faster than federal districts. Skeptics argue that states face stronger development pressure, that the retained waters line creates a bifurcated system more confusing than the federal program alone, and that EPA's oversight tools are too blunt to correct a state program that drifts toward permissiveness. The statute's answer is to permit assumption but not to require it, and the record, two long standing programs and one vacated program, with most other states never having applied, suggests that the offer is less attractive than its drafters hoped.

## The Applicant's Playbook: Sequencing a 404 Strategy

The program's complexity rewards applicants who treat permitting as a campaign with a defined sequence rather than a form to file. The practitioners who move projects through the Corps districts efficiently follow a playbook whose steps reflect the structure this article has mapped: jurisdiction first, then the permit lane, then the alternatives record, then the state and federal overlays, then mitigation, with the enforcement risk managed at every stage.

The campaign begins with the delineation, before any application is filed and ideally before the property is purchased. A qualified wetlands scientist delineates the aquatic resources on the site under the 1987 manual and the regional supplement, producing the data sheets and mapping the district will need. The sophisticated buyer makes the purchase contingent on the delineation results, because the acreage and configuration of jurisdictional waters determine the project's economics more reliably than any other single variable. The delineation then supports the jurisdictional determination request, and the applicant chooses between the approved and preliminary tracks based on the strategy discussed above: the negative approved determination for the buyer who needs certainty, the preliminary determination for the applicant who needs speed.

The second step is the pre application meeting with the district. The Corps encourages these meetings, and experienced applicants treat them as the most valuable hour in the process. The district staff will indicate which permit lane the project likely fits, identify the sensitive resources on the site, flag the likely need for species consultation or historic property review, and describe the alternatives analysis the district will expect. The applicant's goal is to leave the meeting with a record of what the Corps asked for, because a permit file that delivers what the district requested is a file that moves. Applicants who skip the meeting to save time typically spend more time later answering the questions the meeting would have surfaced.

The third step is building the alternatives analysis before the application is filed, not after the Corps asks for it. The applicant's consultants identify the candidate alternatives, evaluate them against cost, technology, and logistics in light of the project purpose, and document the analysis in a report that tracks the language of 40 CFR 230.10(a). For non water dependent projects, the report must rebut the presumption that less damaging practicable alternatives exist, which means the analysis must be comparative and evidence based rather than conclusory. The strongest reports include engineering cost estimates, logistics assessments, and documentation of comparable projects, because the district evaluates practicability against what the industry has actually achieved, not what the applicant asserts is impossible.

The fourth step is coordinating the state certification and the federal environmental reviews as parallel tracks. The section 401 certification request goes to the state early, because the one year clock runs from a complete request and the state process has its own comment periods and hearing requirements. The NEPA scoping for an individual permit begins alongside, and the species consultation is initiated as soon as the biological information is available, because the consultation timetable is the least controllable element in the schedule. The applicant who sequences these reviews in series, finishing the Corps application before starting the state process, adds a year to the project for no reason.

The fifth step is securing the mitigation before the permit decision. For bank credits, the applicant negotiates a reservation or purchase agreement with the bank sponsor for the service area covering the project, confirming credit availability and price. For in lieu fee programs, the applicant confirms that the program's compensation planning framework covers the project area and that credits or advance credits are available. For permittee responsible mitigation, the applicant prepares the full mitigation plan with site control, performance standards, monitoring, and financial assurances, because the district will not issue the permit until the plan is approved. Mitigation arranged after the permit issues is mitigation that delays construction.

The final step is the application package itself: the ENG Form 4345 or the district's electronic equivalent, the project drawings, the delineation, the alternatives analysis, the mitigation plan, the 401 certification or evidence of the pending request, and the NEPA and consultation documentation. The district's completeness review is the gate: an incomplete application does not start the substantive review clock. The common failure modes are all avoidable: a delineation that does not follow the regional supplement, an alternatives analysis written to justify a predetermined site rather than to evaluate alternatives, a 401 request filed months late, a mitigation plan without site control, and the perennial favorite, construction begun before the permit issues, which converts the applicant into a violator and the application into an after the fact proceeding judged under the full guidelines.

## Closing Assessment: Conditions and Prices, Not Blocks

The Section 404 program, viewed whole, is an implementation story about the distance between a statutory sentence and an operating reality. The sentence is spare: no discharge of dredged or fill material into covered waters without a permit from the Corps meeting EPA's guidelines. The reality is two agencies, four permit types, a three rung sequence, a credit market, a state certification lever, a jurisdictional threshold that decides everything, and an enforcement regime built around restoration. The series thesis for this article is the gap between text and operation, and the gap is filled by exactly the institutions the statute created: the Corps' districts, EPA's guidelines and veto, the mitigation banks, and the jurisdictional question.

The complication the brief requires this article to address is the belief that the program blocks development, and the evidence points the other way. The nationwide permits authorize tens of thousands of activities every year with light review. Individual permits, the heavy proceeding, are a minority of decisions, and denials within that minority are uncommon. The program's real effect is to condition and price: avoidance redesigns projects, minimization complicates construction, mitigation credits add a line item that varies with ecological scarcity, and the timeline adds carrying costs. Developers experience this as friction, and friction is real money, but friction is not prohibition. The landowner burden is genuine, particularly for small holders who cannot spread compliance costs across a large project, and the wetland loss concerns are genuine too, since a program that authorizes most of what it reviews will preside over continuing incremental loss even when every permit is conditioned. Both descriptions can be true at once: the program rarely says no, and the resource continues to shrink at the margins.

The no net loss goal deserves a final word, because it illustrates how a policy slogan becomes an administrative standard and then a measurement controversy. When the National Wetlands Policy Forum articulated the goal in 1988 and the federal government adopted it the following year, the ambition was that wetlands losses authorized by programs like Section 404 would be fully offset by gains from restoration and creation. The 1990 mitigation memorandum of agreement translated the slogan into the sequencing the Corps applies. But measuring compliance with the goal turned out to be harder than announcing it. The program counts acres readily and functions poorly: an acre of restored emergent marsh is not the functional equivalent of an acre of filled forested wetland, and the time lag between the impact and the maturation of the compensation means that even successful mitigation produces a temporal loss of function. The 2008 mitigation rule's functional assessment requirements, performance standards, and credit release schedules were designed to close this gap, and they improved the accounting substantially. Yet the fundamental difficulty remains: the program authorizes the destruction of mature, functioning aquatic systems and accepts in return the promise of immature systems that may or may not achieve equivalence. Whether that exchange satisfies the no net loss ambition depends on judgments about functional assessment methods, mitigation success rates, and enforcement rigor that reasonable experts dispute. The program's defenders point to the avoidance rung, which prevents the losses that never enter the ledger, and to the mitigation standards that have raised the quality of compensation. Its critics point to the authorized losses that accumulate across thousands of individually minimal permits and to the mitigation failures that monitoring continues to reveal. The honest assessment is that the program transformed wetlands destruction from an unpriced externality into a regulated, conditioned, and compensated activity, and that the distance between that achievement and the no net loss goal is the measure of the work remaining.

Whether the conditions imposed translated into retained wetlands and functioning streams is a question this article does not decide, because it belongs to the outcomes inquiry. The wetlands record, the measured gains and the remaining losses, is carried by the [Clean Water Act outcomes article](/2012/05/01/clean-water-act-outcomes/), which evaluates the statute against its own aims with the numbers and the caveats the evidence requires. The permit program is the mechanism; the outcomes article is the verdict. Readers who want to know whether the machine worked should go there next.

The neutrality this subject demands is structural, not decorative. Wetlands permitting sits on the fault line between property rights and infrastructure politics on one side and aquatic resource protection on the other, and every procedural choice in the program advantages one side's theory of the case. The practicable alternatives presumption advantages the resource. The nationwide permit system advantages the applicant. The state certification lever advantages the statehouse over the federal district office. EPA's veto advantages the fishery over the mine. Describing these advantages factually, with the mechanisms and the numbers attached, is the article's obligation, and taking a position on the jurisdictional scope, the proper stringency of mitigation, or the wisdom of any particular veto would trade that obligation for advocacy. The program's design is the story; the reader's judgment is the verdict.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a section 404 permit under the Clean Water Act?

A section 404 permit is the federal authorization required before anyone may discharge dredged or fill material into covered waters of the United States, issued under section 404 of the Clean Water Act at 33 U.S.C. 1344. Dredged material is the spoil excavated from waters; fill material is rock, sand, soil, or similar material placed into waters to create dry land or to reshape the bottom. The permit does not regulate the cleanliness of a discharge the way the act's industrial discharge permits do; it regulates whether the material should be placed in the water at all, and under what conditions. The Army Corps of Engineers issues the permit, applying environmental guidelines written by the Environmental Protection Agency, and the state must certify the activity under section 401. Most authorized activity proceeds under streamlined general permits rather than full individual review.

### Q: Who issues Clean Water Act wetlands permits?

The United States Army Corps of Engineers issues Clean Water Act wetlands permits, acting through its district offices under authority Congress assigned to the Secretary of the Army in section 404(a). The common assumption that the Environmental Protection Agency issues them is wrong, though understandable: EPA writes the binding section 404(b)(1) guidelines the Corps must apply, develops the environmental criteria, and holds a veto over disposal sites under section 404(c). States may also issue the permits where they have assumed the program under section 404(g); Michigan and New Jersey have done so, while Florida's 2020 assumption was vacated by a federal court in 2024 and returned to the Corps. Everywhere else, the Corps district engineer signs the permit.

### Q: What is a nationwide permit under the Clean Water Act?

A nationwide permit is a general permit issued by the Army Corps of Engineers under section 404(e) that authorizes a category of activities across the country without an individual permit proceeding for each project. The statute allows general permits only for categories of activities that will cause minimal adverse environmental effects, individually and cumulatively, and limits each permit to a five year term. The Corps reissues the set on a five year cycle; the January 2026 reissuance carried fifty seven permits effective March 2026 through March 2031. Each permit sets acreage or linear foot caps, general conditions, and often a pre construction notification requirement under which the district verifies the project fits. The Corps reports roughly twenty five thousand reported activities per year under these permits, plus about thirty thousand more that need no reporting.

### Q: Can EPA veto an Army Corps Clean Water Act permit?

Yes. Section 404(c) of the Clean Water Act, at 33 U.S.C. 1344(c), authorizes the Environmental Protection Agency to prohibit or restrict the use of an area as a disposal site for dredged or fill material whenever it finds the discharge would have an unacceptable adverse effect on municipal water supplies, shellfish beds and fishery areas, wildlife, or recreational areas. The veto can apply even after the Corps has issued a permit, which makes it one of the most unusual powers in environmental law: one federal agency overruling another's licensing decision. EPA exercises it through procedures at 40 CFR Part 231, ending in a final determination signed by the Administrator. It has been used fourteen times in the statute's history, including in January 2023 for the Bristol Bay watershed and the proposed Pebble mine, which EPA called the third use in thirty years.

### Q: How long does a Clean Water Act 404 permit take?

The honest answer is that it depends entirely on which door the project walks through. Activities that qualify for a nationwide permit and require no pre construction notification can proceed immediately once the applicant confirms the fit. Where the permit requires pre construction notification, district verification commonly takes weeks to a few months. Individual permits, the full proceeding with public notice, alternatives analysis, interagency coordination, and often environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act, commonly take many months to more than a year from the submission of a complete application. Projects with complex mitigation, endangered species consultation, or controversial settings take longer. Applicants shorten the timeline by determining jurisdiction early, designing avoidance into the project before applying, and confirming credit availability in advance.

### Q: What is a section 401 certification under the Clean Water Act?

Section 401 certification, at 33 U.S.C. 1341, is the state or tribal approval required before any federal license or permit for an activity that may result in a discharge into covered waters can issue. The certifying authority, usually the state environmental agency, must certify that the activity will comply with applicable water quality requirements, or the federal permit cannot issue. The state may grant certification, grant it with conditions that become conditions of the federal permit, deny it, or waive it by failing to act within the statutory reasonable period, which cannot exceed one year. A 2023 EPA rule set a default six month review period and restored the scope to the activity as a whole rather than the discharge alone. Because a denial stops the federal permit, certification functions as a state level veto.

### Q: What is mitigation banking under the Clean Water Act?

Mitigation banking is a system in which wetlands, streams, or other aquatic resources are restored, established, enhanced, or preserved in advance at a dedicated site, and the resulting ecological lift is quantified as credits that the bank sponsor sells to permittees who need compensatory mitigation for their projects. The bank operates under a legal instrument approved by the Corps and an interagency review team, with performance standards, monitoring, financial assurances, and long term management funding. When a permittee buys credits, its mitigation obligation is satisfied and responsibility for the site transfers to the sponsor. The 2008 compensatory mitigation rule made bank credits the preferred form of compensation, ahead of in lieu fee programs and permittee responsible mitigation. Credits are denominated by resource type and watershed service area, and prices vary widely with scarcity.

### Q: What are the penalties for filling wetlands without a Clean Water Act permit?

Placing dredged or fill material into covered waters without a section 404 permit violates section 301 of the Clean Water Act, and the exposure is severe. In a judicial enforcement action under 33 U.S.C. 1319(d), civil penalties run up to twenty five thousand dollars per day for each violation as enacted, with inflation adjustments raising the figure over time; EPA's January 2025 inflation rule set the adjusted maximum at sixty eight thousand four hundred forty five dollars per day per violation, and the 2026 inflation increase was later cancelled so the 2025 figure remained operative. Administrative penalties under section 309(g) reach ten thousand dollars per violation in Class I proceedings and ten thousand dollars per day in Class II proceedings, each with aggregate caps, also inflation adjusted. Knowing violations can draw criminal penalties. Courts also order restoration: removal of the fill and reestablishment of the wetland. Because penalties accrue daily, unremediated fills compound quickly.

### Q: How does the avoid-minimize-compensate sequence work in a Clean Water Act 404 permit application?

The sequence is the fixed order in which the Corps evaluates every application under EPA's section 404(b)(1) guidelines. Avoidance comes first: the applicant must show there is no practicable alternative to the proposed discharge with less aquatic impact, and for non water dependent projects the guidelines presume such alternatives exist until the applicant proves otherwise. Minimization comes second: the project must be designed to shrink unavoidable impacts through measures like narrower footprints, bridges instead of culverts, or directional drilling beneath streams. Compensation comes third: residual impacts must be offset through mitigation banks, in lieu fee programs, or permittee responsible work, under the preference order of the 2008 rule. The order is mandatory, so an applicant cannot offer to buy credits as a substitute for demonstrating avoidance.

### Q: What is the difference between a nationwide permit and a regional general permit under the Clean Water Act?

Both are general permits under section 404(e), limited to activity categories with minimal individual and cumulative effects and to five year terms, but they differ in authorship and geography. Nationwide permits are issued by Corps headquarters and apply across the country unless a division or district adds regional conditions or revokes one locally; the 2026 reissuance carried fifty seven of them. Regional general permits are issued by a Corps division or district for a defined area, often covering recurring local work like county road maintenance or coastal nourishment, with conditions written for that region's waters. For the applicant the experience is similar: confirm the project fits the category, meet the conditions, and provide pre construction notification where required.

### Q: What is a letter of permission under the Clean Water Act?

A letter of permission is the most streamlined form of Corps authorization under section 404, available under 33 CFR 325.2(e)(1)(ii) for work the district engineer judges to be minor, with no significant individual or cumulative environmental impact and no appreciable opposition expected. It is processed through an abbreviated procedure: the district coordinates with federal and state fish and wildlife agencies but issues no public notice, and the authorization arrives as correspondence rather than a formal permit document. Typical candidates include small dock repairs, minor bank stabilization, and modest boat ramp work. The letter carries conditions, and mitigation is uncommon at this scale. It exists because the full individual permit proceeding would crush genuinely small projects under its own weight.

### Q: How do you challenge a Clean Water Act 404 permit denial in court?

A denied applicant first uses the Corps' administrative appeal process at 33 CFR Part 331, filing with the division engineer within sixty days of the denial and arguing on the administrative record that the district misapplied the 404(b)(1) guidelines or the public interest review. If the administrative appeal fails, the applicant may seek judicial review in federal district court under the Administrative Procedure Act, which asks whether the Corps' decision was arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law. Courts give the Corps deference on technical judgments but will set aside permits or denials that ignore the guidelines' requirements, such as a missing practicable alternatives analysis. Third parties, including environmental organizations and neighboring landowners, can also challenge issued permits they believe violate the guidelines.

### Q: Can a state assume the Clean Water Act 404 permit program?

Yes, under section 404(g), which allows a state or tribe to assume administration of the dredge and fill permit program for certain waters after EPA approves its program as meeting federal requirements. The Corps retains permitting authority over retained waters, generally those subject to the Rivers and Harbors Act section 10, even in assuming states. Michigan assumed the program in 1984 and New Jersey in 1994, and both continue to run their programs under EPA oversight with annual reporting. Florida became the third assuming state in December 2020, but a federal district court vacated EPA's approval in February 2024, returning permitting to the Corps while litigation over the program's future continues. EPA published a final rule in December 2024 streamlining the assumption process for other interested states and tribes.

### Q: What is the 2008 compensatory mitigation rule under the Clean Water Act?

The 2008 compensatory mitigation rule is the joint Corps and EPA regulation that rebuilt how unavoidable wetland and stream losses are offset, published April 10, 2008 at 33 CFR Parts 325 and 332 with a parallel EPA rule at 40 CFR Part 230 Subpart J. It responded to a 2001 National Research Council finding that permittee responsible mitigation, where the developer builds its own replacement wetland, frequently failed. The rule established three mechanisms, mitigation banks, in lieu fee programs, and permittee responsible mitigation, with a preference order directing district engineers to favor bank credits first and permittee responsible work last. It required watershed based site selection, measurable performance standards, monitoring, financial assurances, and funded long term management for all three mechanisms, moving the program from promises to enforceable instruments.

### Q: What is an in-lieu fee program under the Clean Water Act?

An in lieu fee program is a compensatory mitigation mechanism in which a government agency or nonprofit sponsor collects fees from permittees and pools the money to build larger, strategically sited mitigation projects, rather than each permittee building its own small replacement wetland. The sponsor operates under an instrument approved by the Corps, with a compensation planning framework that identifies watershed scale conservation priorities, and may sell advance credits before the projects are complete. The 2008 mitigation rule ranks in lieu fee credits second in its preference order, behind mitigation bank credits and ahead of permittee responsible mitigation, because pooled projects tend to be larger, better planned, and more ecologically valuable than scattered individual efforts. The district engineer may override the preference where the facts support it.

### Q: What triggers a section 401 water quality certification under the Clean Water Act?

Certification is triggered whenever a federal license or permit may result in a discharge into covered waters of the United States. For section 404, that means the Corps cannot issue an individual permit, and in many states cannot verify a nationwide permit authorization, until the state or authorized tribe grants, conditions, or waives certification. The applicant requests certification from the certifying authority, usually the state water quality agency, supplying the federal application and water quality materials; a 2023 EPA rule added a pre filing meeting step and a default six month decision period, extendable to the one year statutory maximum. The trigger is the potential discharge, not the permit type, so pipelines, dams, dredging projects, and fill permits all pass through it. Denial stops the federal authorization.

### Q: What is a jurisdictional determination under the Clean Water Act?

A jurisdictional determination is the Army Corps of Engineers' formal answer to the threshold question of whether a site contains waters of the United States covered by the Clean Water Act. The Corps issues two kinds. An approved jurisdictional determination is the official written finding, documented on the Corps' JD form, that covered waters are present or absent; it is valid for five years, can be relied on by the recipient, is appealable under 33 CFR Part 331, and, as the Supreme Court confirmed in U.S. Army Corps of Engineers v. Hawkes Co. in 2016, is subject to judicial review as final agency action. A preliminary jurisdictional determination is an advisory, nonbinding indication that waters may be present, useful for advancing a permit application without litigating jurisdiction, but it creates no legal shield and cannot be appealed.

### Q: Can you appeal a Clean Water Act jurisdictional determination?

An approved jurisdictional determination can be appealed; a preliminary one cannot. The Corps' administrative appeal regulation at 33 CFR Part 331 allows an affected party, meaning a permit applicant, landowner, or holder of a legal interest in the property, to appeal an approved JD to the division engineer within sixty days, with the appeal decided on the administrative record. Beyond that, the Supreme Court's unanimous 2016 decision in U.S. Army Corps of Engineers v. Hawkes Co. held that an approved JD is final agency action reviewable in federal court under the Administrative Procedure Act, because it concludes the agency's decisionmaking and binds the government. A landowner who receives an adverse approved JD therefore has two doors: the Corps' internal appeal and the federal courthouse. A preliminary JD, being advisory, opens neither.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in U.S. Army Corps of Engineers v. Hawkes Co. about Clean Water Act jurisdiction?

In a unanimous 2016 decision, the Supreme Court held that an approved jurisdictional determination issued by the Army Corps of Engineers is final agency action subject to judicial review under the Administrative Procedure Act. Chief Justice Roberts' opinion reasoned that the JD satisfies both requirements for finality: it marks the consummation of the Corps' decisionmaking process, since approved JDs are treated as valid for five years and typically not revisited, and it creates legal consequences, because the Corps and EPA treat JDs as binding on the federal government. The practical effect was to give landowners a courthouse door at the jurisdictional stage. Before Hawkes, a landowner disputing the Corps' claim of jurisdiction generally had to endure the full permit process or risk enforcement; after Hawkes, the jurisdictional call itself can be tested in court.

### Q: How are Clean Water Act wetlands violations enforced by the Army Corps?

Corps enforcement runs under 33 CFR Part 326 and usually begins with discovery through complaints, referrals, or inspections, followed by a site investigation and a notice of violation or cease and desist order halting further work. The district then pursues resolution: voluntary restoration under Corps supervision, an after the fact permit application judged under the full 404(b)(1) guidelines, or referral to the Environmental Protection Agency or the Department of Justice where the violation cannot be resolved administratively. Civil penalties reach sixty six thousand seven hundred twelve dollars per day per violation as inflation adjusted under 33 U.S.C. 1319(d), and knowing violations can draw criminal penalties under section 309(c). Citizens may also sue under section 505. Restoration, removing the fill and reestablishing the wetland, is the characteristic remedy.
