A homebuyer reaches the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 at a particular moment, and it is rarely a calm one. The possession date written into the agreement for sale has passed. The tower stands half finished, or finished but unoffered, or offered without the occupation certificate that would make taking possession lawful. The instalments have been paid, the home loan is running, rent is being paid on the house the family actually lives in, and the promoter's office answers letters with revised dates that carry no more conviction than the dates before them. RERA homebuyer remedies exist for exactly this situation, and they are the subject of this article: not the architecture of the statute, which the pillar article on the Act itself sets out, but the route a buyer actually travels, from the missed possession date to money recovered, and the points along that route where the journey most often breaks down.

The route has more design intelligence in it than buyers usually expect. The Act gives the allottee a right to a refund with interest that the Supreme Court has called indefeasible. It splits the money claims between two forums so that a straightforward refund does not wait on a contested assessment of loss. It makes a promoter fund the amount awarded before an appeal against that award will even be heard. And it converts an unpaid order into a claim on the district revenue machinery, the oldest collection apparatus the Indian state possesses. A buyer who understands the whole route before filing will frame the complaint correctly, ask each forum only for what that forum can give, and treat the order as the midpoint of the matter rather than its end.

![RERA homebuyer remedies in practice, tracing the route from a missed possession date through complaint, order, appeal and recovery - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-18.webp)

That last discipline matters because of the finding that organises everything that follows. The order is easier to get than the money. The statute gives an allottee an unqualified right to a refund with interest and a forum that will order it within months, and it entrusts execution to the district revenue machinery, so the binding constraint on this remedy is not adjudication but recovery. The evidence for that finding is set out in its own section below, in figures published by the Authorities themselves, and it changes how a sensible buyer behaves from the first day: the complaint is drafted with execution in mind, the promoter's assets and the project's escrow position are part of the case from the outset, and every document that a Collector will eventually need is preserved before it is needed rather than assembled in panic afterwards.

This article follows the route in the order a buyer travels it. It begins with what the statute has already promised before any complaint is filed, because the content of the promise decides what the complaint should ask for. It then takes the filing itself, the sorting of prayers between the Authority and the adjudicating officer, the interest computation and its deliberate symmetry, the hearing and its statutory clock, the appeal ladder and the pre-deposit that guards it, the recovery certificate and the Collector, the recovery gap in the published figures, the insolvency contingency that stops the whole process, and the parallel routes that run beside the Act. It closes with the record a remedy runs on, because in this field the difference between a buyer who recovers and a buyer who merely wins is very often a filing cabinet.

## What the statute has already promised before a complaint is filed

A RERA complaint is not a request for discretion. It is the enforcement of promises the Act made in advance, and the buyer who knows the content of those promises frames a shorter, sharper complaint than the buyer who narrates grievance at large. The central promise is in Section 18. Where a promoter fails to complete a project or is unable to give possession of an apartment, plot or building by the date specified in the agreement for sale, or where possession cannot be given because the promoter's business has been discontinued or its registration revoked or suspended, the section gives the allottee two doors and lets the allottee choose between them.

The first door is withdrawal. An allottee who wishes to withdraw from the project is entitled, on demand, to the return of the amount received by the promoter in respect of that apartment, with interest at the rate prescribed, and with compensation in the manner provided by the Act. The entitlement is expressed to be without prejudice to any other remedy available to the allottee, which means the refund claim does not exhaust or extinguish the wider claims the buyer may hold. The second door is to stay. An allottee who does not intend to withdraw is entitled to be paid interest for every month of delay until possession is handed over, again at the prescribed rate. The choice between the doors belongs to the buyer, not to the promoter, and it is made against the facts of the project: a tower that is structurally complete and awaiting a certificate is a different proposition from a project whose promoter has diverted the collections and stopped work, and the statute leaves the election where it belongs, with the person whose money is locked inside the concrete.

Two features of Section 18 explain much of the litigation that followed the Act. The first is that the right carries no fault inquiry of the kind contract law would ordinarily conduct. The section does not ask why possession was not given. It asks whether possession was given by the agreed date, and if it was not, the consequences follow. The second is that the interest obligation is computed on the amounts the promoter actually received, from the dates of receipt, which makes the payment record, rather than any estimate of loss, the foundation of the money claim. A refund claim under the Act is therefore an arithmetical claim before it is an argumentative one, and it can be proved from bank records alone in the ordinary case.

### Can a promoter defeat a refund claim by pointing to force majeure or pending approvals?

No. In Newtech Promoters and Developers Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided on 11 November 2021, the Supreme Court held that the right under Section 18(1) is indefeasible, that the promoter's justification for the delay is insignificant against it, and that the Authority may determine the refund on a summary scrutiny of undisputed facts.

The reasoning repays attention because it governs thousands of files. Promoters resisting refund claims habitually plead the familiar liturgy of the industry: approvals that arrived late, a labour shortage, a funding crunch, a pandemic, a dispute with a contractor. In contract, some of those pleas might found an argument about frustration or about time not being of the essence. Under Section 18 they miss the point, because the provision is not a damages clause for breach of contract. It is a statutory option given to the allottee when a dated event, delivery by the agreed date, has not occurred. The Supreme Court in Newtech treated the allottee's election as the operative fact and the promoter's excuses as beside it, and it confirmed the same reading the Court had given the section a year earlier in Imperia Structures Ltd. v. Anil Patni, where the right to a refund with interest at the prescribed rate was described as unqualified. Between the two decisions, a promoter who has missed the date has no defence to the refund claim itself. The argument that remains open to him concerns computation, the dates and amounts of receipt, and the compensation claim, and it is there that his evidence belongs.

Section 18 is the centre of the promise, but it is not the whole of it. Section 12 addresses the buyer who was induced by a false statement or misleading advertisement, in a prospectus or in the material on the basis of which the booking was made, and gives that buyer a route to the return of the investment with interest and compensation where the falsehood caused loss or damage. Section 14(3) addresses the defect that appears after possession: where a structural defect, or a defect in workmanship, quality or the provision of services, is brought to the promoter's notice within five years of possession being handed over, the promoter must rectify it within thirty days without charge, and where the promoter fails, the allottee is entitled to compensation in the manner the Act provides. Section 19 collects the allottee's own entitlements and duties, including the right to obtain information about the project's sanctioned plans and stage-wise progress, the right to the documents on the execution of the registered agreement for sale, and the duty to make payments in accordance with that agreement. A complaint drafted well pleads the specific promise that was broken, section by section, because the Authority's jurisdiction and the adjudicating officer's jurisdiction between them cover distinct promises, and a complaint that mixes them costs its author months.

Behind the promises stands the design feature that was meant to make them less necessary. Section 4(2)(l)(D) requires the promoter, at registration, to deposit seventy per cent of the amounts realised from allottees into a separate account in a scheduled bank, to be withdrawn only for the cost of construction and the cost of the land, in proportion to the stage of completion and against certificates from the project's engineer, architect and chartered accountant. The escrow discipline was the statute's answer to the diversion that had defined the pre-RERA market, in which collections from one project routinely financed the land purchase for the next. Its relevance to remedies is direct. A promoter who has observed the discipline has a project whose completion is funded and whose refund liability, if it arises, stands against an account the Authority can see. A promoter who has not observed it has usually converted a delay problem into a solvency problem, and it is at that point that the buyer's route bends toward the insolvency contingency examined later in this article.

The promises also carry a jurisdictional fact the buyer should register at the outset. The Act's complaint machinery is operated by the Authority of the State where the project is situated, under rules made by that State, and although the central text is uniform, the forms, the fees and the prescribed interest rate differ from State to State. The statute the buyer invokes is national. The file the buyer opens is provincial. Everything in the sections that follow is written against that duality.

## Where to file: the complaint under Section 31

The complaint route opens with Section 31. Any aggrieved person may file a complaint with the Authority, or with the adjudicating officer, for any violation or contravention of the provisions of the Act or of the rules and regulations made under it, in the form and manner and accompanied by the fee prescribed by the State rules. Every element of that sentence carries practical weight. The complainant is any aggrieved person, a wider door than the allottee alone: promoters complain under the Act, agents complain, and an association of allottees may carry a complaint for its members, a standing the Act confirms expressly at the appellate stage as well. The subject matter is any violation of the Act, which is why the complaint is the vehicle for everything from a missed possession date to a failure to execute the registered agreement for sale, a refusal to share the project's approvals, or a charge levied on the buyer that the Act does not permit. And the form, manner and fee are prescribed by State rules, which is why the filing experience in Lucknow is not the filing experience in Mumbai even though the right being enforced is identical.

The statute to which all of this belongs, its registration scheme, its disclosure duties and its penalty architecture, is treated as a whole in [the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016 explained](/2018/12/01/rera-act-2016-explained/), and this article does not retrace that ground. What matters here is the working shape of the filing. Most Authorities operate the complaint through an online portal. The complainant registers on the portal of the Authority for the State where the project stands, selects the project by its registration number, states the facts and the relief sought, uploads the documents, and pays the prescribed fee electronically. In Uttar Pradesh the fee for a complaint is one thousand rupees; in Maharashtra it is five thousand rupees; other States prescribe fees within that general order. Several States prescribe distinct forms for the two forums the Act creates, so that in Uttar Pradesh and in Kerala the complaint to the Authority travels in one form and the application to the adjudicating officer in another, and the portal's first real question to the buyer is not about the facts at all. It is about which of the Act's two doors the buyer is knocking on.

That question deserves a section of its own, and it gets one below, because the division between the Authority and the adjudicating officer is the single most consequential sorting decision in the entire route. What belongs in this section is the rest of the filing discipline. The complaint must identify the project and its registration particulars, the complainant's interest in it, the agreement for sale and its possession date, the amounts paid and their dates, the specific provisions said to be violated, and the relief sought, stated as a prayer the forum can actually grant. Authorities conduct the proceeding on the complaint as filed; a prayer that asks the Authority for compensation invites an objection and a reference, and a prayer that asks the adjudicating officer for a refund invites the mirror image of the same delay. The portals also ask the complainant to declare whether the same dispute is pending before another forum, because the Act's remedies, although cumulative in design, do not permit the same claim to be recovered twice, and a concealment at this stage surfaces later at the worst possible moment.

A threshold anxiety deters some buyers from filing at all: the fear that the project is outside the Act. Two versions of the fear recur. The first is the project that was never registered although the Act required registration, where the promoter markets and sells in defiance of Section 3. That defiance is itself the violation, and the complaint route is open against it; Authorities entertain complaints and reports concerning unregistered projects, several maintain a distinct facility for reporting them, and the promoter who sells without the registration the Act mandates exposes himself to a penalty that may extend to ten per cent of the estimated cost of the project, with imprisonment of up to three years, or a further fine, or both, where the default continues in disobedience of the Authority's directions. The second fear is the project that began before the Act and was ongoing when the Act commenced. The registration provisions were written to capture ongoing projects that had not received a completion certificate, and the complaint machinery attaches to a registered ongoing project in the ordinary way; the genuinely difficult questions about the Act's reach into pre-Act agreements belong to the pillar article and to the courts, and they do not justify a buyer with a registered project and a missed date staying home.

### Does an arbitration clause in the agreement for sale shut the RERA door?

No. The complaint under Section 31 is a statutory remedy before a statutory forum, and neither an arbitration clause in the agreement for sale nor a clause naming the civil courts as the exclusive forum takes the dispute out of the Authority's hands.

The Act bars the civil courts from entertaining matters the Authority is empowered to determine, and its remedies operate alongside the general law rather than beneath private dispute clauses. Authorities and appellate tribunals have consistently entertained complaints notwithstanding arbitration clauses in the underlying agreement, on the reasoning that an allottee's statutory election cannot be contracted away by a standard-form document the allottee had no hand in drafting.

The promoter who wishes to resist a complaint therefore cannot ordinarily resist the forum. His resistance goes to the facts: that the amounts claimed were not received, that the possession date was extended by a registered supplementary agreement, that the delay falls within a grace period the agreement itself contains, that the buyer is in default of instalments and the promoter's obligation stood suspended in consequence. Each of those is a real argument, and the buyer filing should anticipate them in the complaint itself, annexing the payment record complete rather than selective, and dealing with the buyer's own payment history before the promoter raises it. A complaint that concedes and answers the obvious defences in its own text is heard differently from a complaint that hopes the defences will not be noticed.

One further filing decision has consequences out of proportion to its size: the decision to file promptly. The Act prescribes no limitation period for a complaint under Section 31, and the point is examined in the questions at the end of this article, but the absence of a limitation clock is not an invitation to wait. Interest runs in the buyer's favour, which tempts some buyers to treat delay as an investment strategy. It is a poor one. The promoter's solvency is the real security behind every RERA order, and solvency is a wasting asset in a stalled project; the buyer who files early stands earlier in every queue that matters, including the queue of creditors if the insolvency contingency arrives. The complaint is cheap, the forum is designed for the unrepresented, and the only party who profits from the buyer's patience is the promoter holding the buyer's money.

## Sorting the prayers: refund and interest before the Authority, compensation before the adjudicating officer

The Act builds two money forums and gives each a distinct subject. The Authority, the regulator itself, hears complaints and may issue the directions the Act authorises, including a direction to refund the amount received with interest. The adjudicating officer, a judicial officer who is or has been a district judge, appointed by the Authority in consultation with the State Government, exists for one purpose stated in Section 71: to adjudicate compensation under Sections 12, 14, 18 and 19. Compensation, in the Act's vocabulary, is the quantified money equivalent of a loss that must be assessed: the loss flowing from a misleading advertisement, from a defect the promoter failed to rectify, from the delay and disruption a failed project inflicts beyond the bare return of money with interest. Refund and interest are not compensation in this sense. They are the restoration of what the buyer paid, plus the price of the time for which the promoter held it, computed arithmetically at the prescribed rate. The distinction is the filing decision of the whole case, because a prayer placed before the wrong forum is not decided by that forum; it is objected to, referred, refiled and reheard, and the months spent in the corridor between the two doors are months in which the promoter continues to hold the money.

For years after the Act commenced, the boundary between the two forums was litigated with an energy that cost buyers heavily. Some Authorities routed refund claims to adjudicating officers; some adjudicating officers decided them; promoters appealed on jurisdiction whichever way the routing went, because a jurisdictional objection, win or lose, consumed time. The Supreme Court settled the boundary in Newtech Promoters and Developers Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Uttar Pradesh on 11 November 2021, and the settlement is the operating rule of the remedy. Reading Sections 18 and 19 together, the Court held that when the claim is for refund of the amount and interest on it, or for interest for delayed delivery of possession, or for penalty and interest on penalty, it is the Authority that has the power to examine and determine the outcome of the complaint. When the claim is for compensation, it goes to the adjudicating officer under the procedure Section 71 prescribes, assessed against the factors Section 72 enumerates. The Court added the practical corollary that where a complaint carries both kinds of prayer, the Authority first determines the refund and interest entitlement, and the compensation claim is then taken up by the adjudicating officer, so the buyer is not required to choose between the two halves of his loss at the door.

The procedural consequence of the division deserves emphasis, because it explains why the refund half of a case usually finishes first. The Authority's determination of refund proceeds, in the Supreme Court's words, on a summary scrutiny of undisputed facts. The agreement, the possession date, the receipts: if those are established, the refund follows, and the promoter's narrative about why the project failed does not convert the proceeding into a trial. The compensation claim is different in kind. Section 72 directs the adjudicating officer to have due regard to the amount of disproportionate gain or unfair advantage the default produced, wherever quantifiable; the amount of loss caused to any person as a result of the default; the repetitive nature of the default; and any other factor the officer considers necessary. That is an inquiry into consequences, and it takes evidence: the rent the family paid while waiting, the appreciation lost on a market that moved, the cost of the loan carried through the delay, the mental agony that consumer jurisprudence has long priced into awards of this character. A buyer who understands the division files the refund claim at once, in the forum that can decide it on documents, and builds the compensation claim as the separate evidentiary exercise it is.

### Does withdrawing from the project end the buyer's claim for compensation?

No. The right to withdraw and take a refund under Section 18 is expressed to be without prejudice to any other remedy available to the allottee, and the compensation claim survives the refund to be decided by the adjudicating officer on its own evidence.

A buyer does not have to choose between getting the money back and being compensated for what the failed purchase cost. The Act treats those as distinct claims travelling by distinct routes, under Sections 12, 14, 18 and 19, and the refund order neither decides nor diminishes the compensation claim that follows it.

The division also disciplines the promoter's side of the file, which a fair account should register. A promoter facing a compensation claim is entitled to have the loss actually assessed rather than assumed, and the factors in Section 72 give his counsel real material: whether the gain from the default was disproportionate, whether the loss claimed was in fact caused by the default rather than by the market, whether the default was a single failure or a pattern. The Authority has no mandate to conduct that assessment on the refund claim, and a promoter who is made to fund a refund quickly retains a genuine hearing on the compensation that follows. That is the scheme's balance, and it is more considered than either side's rhetoric usually allows.

## What compensation actually pays for

Because compensation is assessed rather than computed, a buyer preparing a claim before the adjudicating officer needs to know the heads under which officers actually award it, and the evidence each head demands. The most concrete head is the cost of substitute accommodation: the rent the family in fact paid for housing during the period of delay, proved by the rent agreement and the payment trail, and running for the period the default caused rather than the period the buyer finds convenient. The second is the cost of money: the interest the buyer paid his own lender on the home loan disbursed into the stalled project, a head that overlaps in subject matter with the statutory interest on the refund without duplicating it, since the statutory interest prices the promoter's use of the money and this head prices the buyer's cost of borrowing it, and officers examine the claim to keep the two apart. The third is loss of appreciation or of bargain, the difference between the price contracted and the price of an equivalent apartment when the buyer was finally able to re-enter the market, a head that requires market evidence and is awarded with caution. The fourth is the head consumer jurisprudence made familiar: compensation for the mental agony, harassment and disruption of a family's settled plans, awarded in sums that are modest beside the money heads but real, and sensitive to the length of the default and the promoter's conduct within it. The fifth is the cost of the litigation itself. An adjudicating officer in Gurugram, in a reported order, awarded a buyer couple Rs 11.24 lakh across exactly these heads, for loss of property appreciation, mental agony and litigation expenses, in a case where possession due in 2016 had still not been delivered; the figure is one officer's assessment on one file, and it is quoted here for the structure it displays rather than the sum. The discipline Section 72 imposes runs through all of it: the gain the promoter made from the default, the loss the buyer can prove, and the repetitiveness of the default are the scales, and a claim built head by head on documents will always outrun a claim stated as a round number with grievance attached.

## Withdrawal, cancellation and the forfeiture question

Every refund discussion eventually meets the promoter's ledger entry marked forfeiture, and the buyer should understand when money may lawfully be kept and when it may not. The governing distinction is whose default produced the exit. Where the allottee withdraws because the promoter has failed to give possession by the agreed date, the withdrawal is the exercise of the Section 18 right, and the entitlement is to the return of the amount received with interest; a clause in the agreement for sale purporting to forfeit a percentage of the price on such a withdrawal does not survive the statutory entitlement, because the Act's remedy is not a creature of the agreement and cannot be signed away in the promoter's standard form. Authorities have accordingly directed refunds of the full amount received in default cases without the deductions promoters habitually claim under their cancellation clauses, and the promoter's remedy for his own expenditure lies nowhere in the allottee's deposit. Where, by contrast, the allottee withdraws without promoter default, because his circumstances or his mind have changed, the exit is governed by the agreement for sale and the rules of the State, and the promoter may deduct what the agreement lawfully permits on cancellation, subject to the discipline the Authorities have developed against unconscionable forfeiture and to the terms of the model agreement where the State has prescribed one. The two situations are opposite in law although they look identical in a sales office, and the complaint should establish, as its first factual task, which of them the case is: the missed date and the demand made under it are what convert a cancellation into a statutory withdrawal, which is one more reason the written demand electing to withdraw, sent and preserved, is worth more than any conversation.

A connected trap catches buyers who accept a promoter's cancellation letter at face value. A promoter facing a refund claim sometimes responds by cancelling the allotment himself, alleging the buyer's default in payment, and tendering a cheque for the balance after forfeiture. The cancellation's validity then becomes the case: whether the instalments were in truth unpaid, whether the agreement's termination procedure was followed, whether the demand notices the agreement requires were sent, and whether the alleged default is a pretext assembled after the possession date had already passed. Authorities examine the sequence with care, and a promoter whose own default predates the buyer's is poorly placed to found a termination on it. The buyer served with such a letter should not treat the enclosed cheque as the settlement of anything; encashing a tendered amount in such circumstances should be done, if at all, under protest recorded in writing, and the complaint should attack the cancellation directly rather than argue only about the arithmetic of the deduction.

## The buyer's first moves after the missed date

Sequence is a remedy in itself, and the order in which a buyer does the obvious things decides how much of the statute's strength he actually receives. The first move is the demand, made in writing, delivered in a manner that can be proved, electing between the two doors of Section 18 and calling on the promoter to refund with interest or to pay delay interest as the case may be. The demand does three kinds of work: it fixes the election before the promoter can characterise the buyer's silence; it starts the documentary record of default in the buyer's own hand rather than the promoter's; and it gives the promoter the short opportunity to comply that makes the subsequent complaint read as enforcement rather than ambush. The second move is the assembly of the file, the agreement, the receipts, the registration particulars and the correspondence, made while the documents are in the buyer's own custody and before any of them must be requested from the other side. The third move is the collective one: finding the association of allottees, joining it if it exists, and learning the state of the project's other complaints, because the Authority's handling of the project, and every later contingency from conciliation to insolvency, will be shaped by the organised buyers and not by the scattered ones. The fourth move is the filing itself, in the correct forum for each prayer, with the interest computation annexed and the buyer's own payment history dealt with squarely. And the fifth move, which buyers omit most often, is calendaring the other side's obligations from the day the complaint is filed: the reply date, the hearing dates, and later the order's compliance window and the appeal window, because the route rewards the party who treats every period in it as his own deadline rather than the system's. None of these moves requires counsel, money beyond the filing fee, or any quality rarer than promptness. Together they are the difference between the buyer the statute was designed for and the buyer who arrives at each stage a season late.

## The directions a complaint can actually extract

The refund is the headline direction, but a complaint before the Authority can move the Authority's wider powers, and a buyer who knows their range frames a complaint that does more than ask for money. Section 38 arms the Authority, where a contravention is established, to impose penalty or interest and to issue the directions the case requires. In practice the direction set in complaint orders includes the refund and delay interest already examined; directions to execute the registered agreement for sale where the promoter has collected money without executing one; directions to hand over possession with the occupation certificate and to execute the conveyance under Section 17; directions to charge only what the Act and the agreement permit, cancelling demands for amounts the statute does not authorise, such as charges for increases in carpet area beyond the tolerance the Act allows or for amenities the agreement never promised at a price; directions to rectify defects within the Section 14(3) discipline; and, in the Authority's regulatory capacity, directions touching the project itself, including restraint on sales in defined circumstances and, at the limit, the revocation machinery of Section 7. The penalty jurisdiction runs alongside: a promoter who contravenes the Act's provisions in the course of the conduct complained of exposes himself to the penalties of the relevant charging sections, and although the penalty is the State's money rather than the buyer's, its presence in the case changes the promoter's arithmetic about the cost of resistance. A complaint should still remain a buyer's instrument. Prayers exist to be granted, and a prayer list that reads as a general indictment of the promoter invites the bench to treat the file as rhetoric. The discipline is to pray for the directions that answer the buyer's defaults one by one, each tied to the provision that authorises it, and to let the penalty consequences follow from the findings rather than headline the claim.

## Interest: the prescribed rate and the symmetry the Act insists on

Every money direction under the Act runs through interest, and the interest regime has three features a buyer must grasp, because errors about each of them circulate freely. The first feature is that the Act does not contain a number. It defines the interest payable by reference to the rate prescribed, and the prescribing is done by the rules of each State. The second feature is the structure of what the States prescribed. The standard structure links the rate to the highest marginal cost of lending rate of the State Bank of India, the benchmark by which banks price their own funds, and adds a stated margin above it. Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Assam are among the States that prescribe the State Bank of India's highest marginal cost of lending rate plus two per cent. Uttar Pradesh prescribed the marginal cost of lending rate plus one per cent, a fact the Supreme Court recorded in the Newtech judgment itself when it observed that the prescribed rate left the Authority no discretion in that State. The third feature is the one buyers most often get wrong: there is no single national RERA interest rate, and any page that quotes one without naming a State and its rules is quoting fiction. The rate that governs a complaint is the rate prescribed by the rules of the State where the project stands, applied to the amounts actually paid, from the dates on which they were paid, until refund or possession as the case may be.

The computation itself is simple interest on a running account of receipts. Each instalment earns interest from its own date of payment, which is why the payment record, instalment by instalment, is the spine of the claim and why a buyer who paid partly in cash without receipts has damaged his own case in a way no advocacy repairs. Where the buyer withdraws, interest runs on the amounts received until the refund is made. Where the buyer stays, the proviso to Section 18(1) speaks of interest for every month of delay, and Authorities compute it on the same receipts basis up to the offer of possession. The model agreement for sale appended to the central rules adds a discipline on the promoter's side of the refund: the refund, when it becomes due, is to be made within forty five days, a period that orders of the Authorities commonly track when they set the compliance window.

The symmetry is the feature the brief for this article insists be addressed honestly, and rightly, because buyers routinely discover it late and resent it. The same prescribed rate runs against the allottee. Section 19(6) obliges the allottee to make payments in accordance with the agreement for sale, and Section 19(7) makes an allottee who fails to pay liable to pay interest at the same prescribed rate on the amount in default. Maharashtra's model agreement states the identity expressly, applying the State Bank of India highest marginal cost of lending rate plus two per cent to amounts due from the allottee, and MahaRERA has applied exactly that rate against a defaulting buyer while observing that in the event of default on either side, each party is entitled to interest for the default as prescribed. The design is deliberate. Interest under the Act is not a punishment calibrated to blame; it is the price of money over time, charged to whichever party is holding money that belongs, in the transaction's economy, to the other. A buyer whose own instalments are in arrears should expect the promoter's counterclaim for interest to be computed at the identical rate, set off in argument if not in the order itself, and should file with that arithmetic done rather than leave it to be done by the other side.

### How is the interest on a refund different from compensation for the same delay?

Interest is arithmetic and compensation is assessment. Interest at the prescribed rate follows the money automatically once the default is established, requires no proof of loss, and is decided by the Authority. Compensation requires the adjudicating officer to be persuaded that a loss was suffered, that the default caused it, and what money sum answers it.

The two run in parallel over the same delay without double counting, because they price different things: interest prices the use of the buyer's money, and compensation prices the harm the failed transaction did around that use, assessed against the factors Section 72 lists.

The interest regime has one more consequence that shapes settlement behaviour, and promoters understand it better than buyers usually do. Interest accrues for as long as the money is held, at a rate tied to a bank lending benchmark and therefore comfortably above what the promoter's stalled project earns by holding the funds. Every month of procedural delay is priced, and priced against the party in default. That accrual is the statute's quiet answer to the oldest tactic in Indian property litigation, which is to make the process itself the remedy for the defendant. Under this Act the process runs a meter, and the meter runs against the promoter.

## The computation on an ordinary file

Because interest is arithmetic, a worked illustration fixes the method better than any description, and the figures that follow are an illustration of method at an assumed prescribed rate, not a statement of the rate in any State. Suppose a buyer booked an apartment for a total consideration of Rs 60 lakh and paid Rs 42 lakh in instalments across the construction period: Rs 6 lakh on booking, and four construction-linked instalments of Rs 9 lakh each at yearly intervals. Suppose the agreement promised possession at the end of the fourth year, that possession was not given, that the buyer withdrew, and that the prescribed rate in the State stood at eleven per cent for the whole period. The refund claim is then the return of Rs 42 lakh, and the interest claim is the sum of six separate computations, one for each payment, each running from the date that payment was made until the refund: the booking amount earning for the longest period, the final instalment for the shortest. On those assumed figures the interest comes to a sum in the region of Rs 11 lakh, a figure that surprises buyers who expected interest to be a small fraction of the principal and promoters who had not priced their own delay. Two features of the illustration generalise. The interest base is the money actually received by the promoter, not the price of the apartment, so a buyer who has paid ninety per cent of the price claims on ninety per cent. And the interest is simple, not compound, in the ordinary application of the prescribed rate, which is why the instalment dates matter as much as the instalment amounts and why a computation annexed to the complaint, payment by payment, is worth more than any submission about hardship.

## When the buyer wants the flat, not the money: remedies aimed at completion

The refund route dominates discussion of the Act because it is the route in distress, but a large class of buyers does not want out. The project is months rather than years late, the location cannot be replaced at the price paid, and the buyer's object is the apartment with interest for the wait, not the money with interest for the exit. The Act serves that buyer too, and the complaint should be framed for completion where completion is the real objective. The staying buyer's core entitlement is the proviso interest under Section 18(1), interest for every month of delay until possession is handed over, and the Authority's direction in such a case couples that monetary entitlement with directions addressed to the project itself: to complete the remaining work, to apply for and obtain the occupation certificate, to offer possession in accordance with the agreement, and to execute the conveyance the Act requires the promoter to execute in the allottee's favour. Section 17 obliges the promoter, on completion, to execute a registered conveyance deed of the apartment together with the undivided proportionate title in the common areas in favour of the allottee or the association of allottees, and to hand over the relevant documents; a buyer who has been given a flat without the conveyance has received only part of what the Act promises, and the complaint route remains open for the balance.

At the far end of incompletion stands the remedy that treats the project rather than the claim. Where a promoter's defaults are grave enough, Section 7 empowers the Authority to revoke the project's registration, on grounds that include breach of the Act, of its conditions, or of the approvals on the faith of which registration was granted. Revocation is not the end of the project but a change of its management in the public interest: the Authority may debar the promoter from the project's accounts and listings, and Section 8 provides that on revocation the Authority shall consult the State Government and may facilitate the remaining development work to be carried out by the competent authority or by the association of allottees. The association route returns in the insolvency discussion below, because the organised body of buyers is the recurring instrument by which failed projects are finished: it is the association that can take over a project under Section 8, hold the class vote in an insolvency, and negotiate a completion settlement no individual buyer could extract. A buyer in a failing project who has not joined or formed the association of allottees has left the strongest collective instrument the Act creates unused, and the complaint history of the project will usually show that the buyers who organised earliest shaped the outcome the rest received.

The completion frame also governs the defect complaint, which is the remedy of the buyer already in possession. The five-year window in Section 14(3) runs from the handing over of possession, and it covers structural defects and defects in workmanship, quality and the provision of services agreed to be provided. The promoter must rectify a notified defect within thirty days without additional charge, and the buyer whose notice is ignored is entitled to compensation in the manner the Act provides, which routes the claim, on the Newtech division, to the adjudicating officer. The discipline here is notice: the defect must be brought to the promoter's notice within the window, in writing that can be proved, and a buyer who spends the window in telephone calls has spent the substance of the right.

## Delay interest in the staying buyer's hands

The staying buyer's interest raises two practical questions the complaint should anticipate. The first is when the interest is to be paid. The proviso to Section 18(1) speaks of interest for every month of delay, and Authorities give that language effect in two broad ways: some direct the promoter to pay the accrued delay interest periodically, or in a lump within a stated period, while the project moves to completion; others compute the entitlement up to the offer of possession and direct its payment or adjustment at handover, against the final statement of account between the parties. The difference matters to a buyer carrying a home loan, for whom a periodic payment services the loan the delay is extending, and the complaint that has a preference should pray for it expressly and explain why, rather than leave the mode of satisfaction to be settled by default at the end. The second question is what happens to the staying position if the project deteriorates after the complaint: the buyer who elected to stay is not locked into the election by having made it. The election answers the facts as they stand when it is made, and a project that passes from delay into abandonment presents new facts on which the buyer may withdraw and claim the refund with interest, with the delay interest for the period of staying absorbed into the larger computation rather than lost. What the buyer cannot do is hold both elections open against the same period in the hope of choosing later with hindsight; the Authority will require the position to be stated, and the complaint, the demand letter and the conduct of the parties will be read together to find it. That requirement of a stated position is not a trap. It is the mechanism by which the promoter, the Authority and the buyer's fellow allottees can all tell which project, the completed building or the distributed fund, the buyer is committed to, and in a distressed project that information is itself a resource the other buyers need.

## How a complaint is heard, and the clock the statute sets for it

The proceeding that follows a filing is deliberately unlike a civil suit. The Authority is vested, for the purposes of its inquiries, with specified powers of a civil court: summoning and enforcing the attendance of persons, requiring the discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavit, and issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses or documents. Within that frame the procedure is summary in character. The complaint is registered and scrutinised, notice goes to the promoter, the promoter files a reply, the parties are heard, and the Authority passes an order. Strict trial procedure, with its pleadings rounds and its evidence stages, is not imported; what is imported is the discipline of natural justice, that each side knows the case against it and is heard before an order is made. Cross-examination is the exception rather than the rule, because the ordinary RERA file is decided on documents whose authenticity is rarely in genuine dispute: the agreement, the receipts, the registration record, the correspondence.

The statute's own expectation of speed is stated as an endeavour, and the buyer should understand both the expectation and its legal texture. The adjudicating officer, under Section 71, must dispose of a compensation application as expeditiously as possible, must endeavour to dispose of it within sixty days of receiving it, and must record reasons in writing where disposal within that period proves impossible. The Authorities' complaint procedure works to the same sixty-day measure: dispose of the complaint within sixty days as far as possible, with the reasons for any longer course recorded rather than the delay left unexplained. Neither clock is a jurisdictional guillotine. An order passed on the sixty fifth day is not void, and no buyer should be advised that it is. What the clocks do is set the norm against which the system's actual performance, examined below, is to be judged, and they arm the complainant with a legitimate grievance when a file sleeps without reasons.

Actual disposal times vary by Authority, by bench strength and by the character of the case. A refund complaint on clean documents, against a promoter who does not appear or whose defence is the standard liturgy, can conclude within a small number of hearings spread over a few months. A complaint that becomes the vehicle for a project's entire distress, with hundreds of allottees, rival associations, lender intervention and a promoter in visible decline, will not, because the Authority is then managing a failure rather than deciding a claim. Buyers in the second situation are not wrong to file; they are wrong only if they mistake the filing for the whole strategy, a distinction the insolvency section of this article takes up.

Two procedural instruments deserve notice because buyers underuse both. The first is the interim order. Section 36 empowers the Authority, during an inquiry, to pass an interim order restraining a party where it is satisfied that an act in contravention of the Act is being or is about to be committed, after recording reasons and, ordinarily, after hearing the party affected, with provision for the exceptional case in which an order must precede the hearing. In the life of a complaint this is the instrument that stops a promoter from selling the very apartment in dispute to a third party, or from creating charges over project assets while the refund claim is pending. The second instrument sits before the hearing altogether. Several Authorities, Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh among them, maintain conciliation forums in which a complaint, or a dispute on its way to becoming one, is placed before a panel that attempts a negotiated settlement between promoter and allottees. Uttar Pradesh's Authority has reported its conciliation forum resolving disputes involving over sixteen hundred allottees in a single reporting period. Conciliation produces outcomes a contested order cannot: revised possession schedules with the promoter's money committed, settlements of interest claims at agreed figures, allotments swapped between towers. It also produces delay when a promoter uses it as a waiting room, and the buyer's discipline is to enter it with a deadline of his own and a filed complaint behind it, so that the failure of conciliation returns the matter to a bench rather than to a beginning.

The evidence in these proceedings deserves a word of its own, because buyers habitually over-prepare the wrong half of it. The Authority decides on the documents, and the decisive documents are few: the agreement for sale with its possession date and any supplementary agreement varying it; the receipts and bank proof for every payment claimed; the project's registration record, which the Authority holds itself; and the correspondence that establishes the demand and the default. Against that core, the promoter's evidence goes to payment disputes, extension events and the buyer's defaults, and the Authority tests both against the registry file, which has the quiet advantage of being the promoter's own sworn disclosure made at registration and updated quarterly. A buyer does not need witnesses to prove that a tower is unfinished when the promoter's own quarterly certificates record the stage of construction, and does not need an economist to prove the loss of his money's use when the Act prices that loss at the prescribed rate. Where evidence genuinely matters is the compensation claim: there, the rent actually paid, the loan actually carried and the market movement actually suffered must be documented like any other money claim, and the adjudicating officer's inquiry under Section 72 is the forum in which thin documentation is found out. Buyers who file the refund claim on the documents and then treat the compensation claim as an afterthought to be argued from sentiment have inverted the evidential demands of the two proceedings.

Real RERA litigation is also, very often, mass litigation, and the individual buyer should understand the collective mechanics into which his complaint will be absorbed. A stalled tower produces complaints by the hundred, in materially identical terms, and Authorities manage them by clubbing the complaints of a project together, hearing common questions once, and passing orders that dispose of batches while preserving each complainant's individual computation. The association of allottees sits at the centre of this practice as complainant, as the repository of the project's shared documents, and as the negotiating counterparty in conciliation and settlement. For the individual buyer the consequences are practical. His complaint will be heard in the project's caravan rather than alone, which slows the clean-document case and disciplines the weak one. His interest computation remains his own, and errors in it are not cured by his neighbours' accuracy. And the strategic decisions of the mass, whether to press for refund or for completion, whether to settle in conciliation at a discount or litigate to the order, will be made by whoever organises, which returns to the recurring instruction of this article: the organised buyer is the remedy's intended beneficiary, and joining the organisation is a filing step as real as the portal form.

### Can the Authority restrain a promoter while a complaint is still pending?

Yes. Section 36 empowers the Authority to pass an interim order during an inquiry where a contravention of the Act is being committed or is threatened, on recorded reasons. A buyer may ask for restraint as soon as the threat appears rather than waiting for the final order.

The application is at its strongest where the promoter is selling the disputed unit, diverting collections, or alienating project assets while the complaint is pending, and it may be heard urgently where the act threatened would defeat the complaint altogether.

Representation in these proceedings is the buyer's choice. Section 56 permits a person to appear before the Authority, the adjudicating officer and the Appellate Tribunal in person, or through an authorised representative, a chartered accountant, a company secretary, a cost accountant or a legal practitioner. The design assumption is that a buyer with documents in order can carry a complaint without professional help, and many do. Professional help earns its cost at the pressure points: the drafting of a compensation claim that must survive Section 72 scrutiny, the computation of interest across years of instalments, the appeal where a promoter with counsel tests the order, and the execution stage where the file leaves the Authority's building and enters the district administration. The choice is the buyer's, and it can be revisited at each stage rather than made once for the whole route.

## The promoter's defences, taken seriously

A complaint is only as strong as its answers to the defences, and the defences under this Act follow a stable repertoire that a well-advised buyer will meet in the text of the complaint itself. The first is the grace period: many agreements for sale promise possession by a date and then allow the promoter a further period of grace, commonly six months, before the allottees' remedies are enlivened. Where the agreement contains such a clause, the Authority reads the possession obligation through it, and the default date is the date the grace expires. The buyer's complaint should plead the clause and its expiry rather than be ambushed by it. The second is the extension clause for approvals and events beyond the promoter's control, which agreements commonly carry and which the promoter deploys as a contractual force majeure. Its reach is the reach of its words: an extension for the period of an identified impediment is a different thing from a general absolution, and after Newtech the clause cannot be inflated into a defence to the refund election itself, although it may move the date from which interest runs where the agreement genuinely so provides and the impediment is proved. The third defence is the buyer's own default. A promoter whose allottee is in arrears of instalments may plead that his obligation to deliver stood suspended, and Section 11(5) arms him with the sharper weapon of termination of the agreement in accordance with its terms where the allottee defaults persistently, subject to the Act's discipline on what may then be forfeited and what must be refunded. The buyer in arrears is not outside the Act, but he files from a weaker square, and the interest symmetry examined earlier means his arrears are accruing a liability at the prescribed rate while he complains of the promoter's.

The fourth defence is documentary: that what was offered was possession in law. A promoter may contend that possession was offered on a date evidenced by a letter, that the buyer failed to take it, and that interest stopped at the offer. The contention succeeds only where the offer was a lawful one, of an apartment complete in accordance with the agreement and supported by the occupation certificate where the law requires it; an offer of possession of an incomplete unit, or one unaccompanied by the certificate the buyer needs to occupy lawfully, is not an offer that stops the meter, and tribunals have treated the buyer's refusal of such an offer as justified rather than as a default. The fifth defence is jurisdictional in a narrower sense than the forum objections already discussed: that the complaint belongs, in whole or part, to the period before the Act, or to a project phase outside the registered project, or that the buyer's grievance is in substance a title dispute the Authority cannot try. Each of these can be real in the right case, and each is fact-specific, which is why the complaint that survives appeal is the complaint whose facts were pleaded tightly from the start. What the repertoire does not contain, after the Supreme Court's settlement of the Act, is a defence that the project failed for reasons the market will sympathise with. Sympathy is not the currency of Section 18.

## Appeals: the sixty-day ladder and the deposit that guards it

An order under the Act is not the end of the contest, and the Act builds a two-rung ladder above it. The first rung is the Real Estate Appellate Tribunal. Section 43(5) gives any person aggrieved by a direction, decision or order of the Authority or of an adjudicating officer an appeal to the Appellate Tribunal having jurisdiction, to be filed within sixty days of the order's communication, with a power in the Tribunal to entertain a later appeal where sufficient cause for the delay is shown. The Explanation to the sub-section widens the door in terms: person includes an association of allottees and any registered voluntary consumer association, so the collective appellant is contemplated by the text. The Tribunal works to the same discipline of speed as the forums below it, endeavouring to dispose of appeals expeditiously and within sixty days of receipt, and its orders are executable as decrees of a civil court under Section 57. The second rung is the High Court. Section 58 permits a person aggrieved by a decision or order of the Appellate Tribunal to appeal to the High Court within sixty days of its communication, but only on one or more of the grounds specified in Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which confines the appeal to cases involving a substantial question of law. The High Court may condone delay for sufficient cause, and no appeal lies at all against a decision the Tribunal made with the consent of the parties. The ladder is therefore short and steep: two appeals, each with a sixty-day fuse, the second confined to law rather than fact, which is why the evidentiary battle must be won at the Authority and the Tribunal, where the record is still open.

The gate on the first rung is the provision buyers find most reassuring in the Act and promoters find most onerous, and both reactions are intelligible. The proviso to Section 43(5) provides that where a promoter files an appeal, it shall not be entertained without the promoter first having deposited with the Appellate Tribunal at least thirty per cent of the penalty, or such higher percentage as the Tribunal may determine, or the total amount to be paid to the allottee including interest and compensation imposed on him, if any, or with both, as the case may be, before the appeal is heard. Read in sequence, the alternatives track the character of the order under challenge. Where the order imposes a penalty, the deposit is measured against the penalty. Where the order directs payment to the allottee, the deposit is the total amount payable under it, principal, interest and compensation together. The deposit is not a fee and not a fine; it is the appellant's own money lodged with the Tribunal as the price of suspending the order's operation while he argues against it, and it follows the result of the appeal.

The Supreme Court treated the condition as mandatory in the Newtech judgment and stripped it of the softening readings that had gathered around it. The pre-deposit is a condition of the appeal being entertained at all, the Court held; it is not a discretionary requirement the Tribunal may waive or reduce in a sympathetic case, and the challenger who has not deposited has no appeal to be heard. The Court has since refused, in terms, the argument that a tribunal can exempt a promoter from the requirement where the order challenged directs payment of compensation: the promoter must deposit the entire amount payable, interest and compensation included, before the challenge proceeds. Promoters' appeals in practice therefore begin with a treasury problem, and a significant number of them end at the defect stage, dismissed for non-compliance after opportunities to comply have been given and exhausted. For the buyer, the practical lesson is to watch the appeal's defect list as attentively as its merits, and to press for dismissal when the deposit is not made, because an undeposited appeal that is allowed to drift confers on the promoter the very pause the proviso was designed to deny him.

The promoter's case against the proviso is not frivolous, and this article's even-handedness obligation requires it to be stated at strength. An appeal, the promoter says, is the only corrective against an Authority that has erred, and Authorities composed for speed do err: on jurisdiction, on the interest computation, on findings made against a promoter who was not properly heard. A condition that requires a promoter already carrying a stalled project, unpaid lenders and a frozen escrow to find the entire awarded sum in cash before any appellate judge will look at the error converts the right of appeal, for the promoter nearest the edge, into a formality that concludes his business. The constitutional challenge built on this argument was pressed and rejected: the Punjab and Haryana High Court repelled the attack on the proviso, holding that the right of appeal is a creature of statute which the legislature may condition, and that promoters are a class the legislature could reasonably treat distinctly for this purpose. The buyer's case is the mirror image and equally concrete. Without the deposit, an appeal costs a promoter a filing fee and buys him a year, during which the buyer's money remains in the project and the buyer's own obligations run on. The buyer has already funded the project once; an unconditioned appeal makes him fund the promoter's litigation time as well. The legislature chose between these two accounts of fairness, the courts have upheld the choice, and the honest summary is that both sides are right about different risks: the proviso does make some meritorious appeals impossible, and its absence would make many unmeritorious ones irresistible.

Two operational points complete the appellate picture. The first is asymmetry: the pre-deposit condition is addressed to promoters, and an allottee who appeals, against a dismissal or against an inadequate award, files without any equivalent deposit, a deliberate legislative judgment about which party's delay the system should price. The second is vigilance. An appeal does not automatically stay an order in the buyer's hands; the buyer should ascertain whether a stay operates, should appear and defend the order, and should remember that the deposit, once made, is security in the truest practical sense, money already inside the system against the day the appeal fails. Many refund matters settle at this rung, and they settle because the promoter has had to convert his position from paper to cash in order to argue at all.

The Tribunal's own powers on appeal complete the picture, because a buyer defending an order should know what the bench above can do with it. The Appellate Tribunal may confirm, modify or set aside the order appealed against, and may remand a matter where the record does not permit a just conclusion; it is not confined to the Authority's reasoning and may reach its own conclusion on the record, which is why the appeal is a rehearing in substance for the party who lost below on fact or computation. Its orders, when they become final, are executable as decrees of a civil court under Section 57, which gives the successful appellant or respondent a decree-holder's machinery in addition to the Act's recovery route. Settlement at this rung wears a particular legal dress: a decision of the Tribunal recorded with the consent of the parties is, by Section 58(2), not appealable to the High Court at all, so a consent order at the Tribunal is the most final instrument in the system short of a Supreme Court appeal, and a buyer offered terms there should understand that accepting them closes the file in law as well as in fact, just as the promoter should understand that his deposit, once the appeal is dismissed or withdrawn, stands available against the amount the order awards. The deposit's journey, lodged at the threshold, held through the hearing, and applied or released at the end, is the proviso working exactly as designed: the appellant's money waits inside the system while his arguments are tested, and it is there, rather than in a stalled project's accounts, when the arguments fail.

## Settlements: how these cases actually end

For all the architecture of forums and ladders, the largest single category of concluded RERA matters concludes by agreement, and the buyer should understand the settlement economy he is inside. The Uttar Pradesh figures in this article show settlements on certificates amounting to about Rs 300 crore in that Authority's first four years, against about Rs 150 crore recovered coercively: agreement outproduced execution two to one. The reason is structural. A promoter confronted with a final order, an appeal he must fund in cash to pursue, an accruing interest meter and a recovery certificate in prospect will very often prefer a negotiated payment schedule to the sequence the statute has in store, and a buyer confronted with the Collector's timetable will very often prefer a discounted certainty to a full entitlement collected over years. Settlements in this field take recognisable shapes: a refund in instalments over an agreed period, secured where the buyer is well advised by post-dated cheques, by an undertaking recorded before the Authority, or by a charge over identified unsold inventory; a swap of the booked unit into a ready or readier project of the same promoter, with the price and interest adjusted; a completion settlement in which staying buyers accept a revised possession date in exchange for delay interest paid or credited on a schedule; and the haircut settlement, in which the buyer surrenders a portion of interest for immediate payment of the whole principal and the balance. Each shape has its own failure mode, and the discipline that protects the buyer is the same in all of them: the settlement must be recorded, before the Authority or the Tribunal where proceedings are pending, so that it hardens into a consent order enforceable like any other order, rather than resting as a private letter whose breach would require the whole route to be walked again. A settlement recorded as a consent order at the Tribunal carries the further finality that Section 58(2) denies any appeal against it. The buyer who settles privately and withdraws his complaint, by contrast, has traded the statute's machinery for the promoter's promise, and if the promise fails he returns to the beginning with the interest meter as his only consolation. The art of the RERA settlement is to take the promoter's money seriously and his paper lightly: agree the figures generously if the figures are real, and record them relentlessly.

## Enforcing the order: the recovery certificate and the Collector

When the compliance window in an order expires without payment, the buyer's file changes character. Inside the Authority, the matter was adjudication; outside it, the matter becomes execution, and the Act's execution design is a deliberate borrowing from the revenue state. Section 40(1) provides that if a promoter, an allottee or a real estate agent fails to pay any interest, penalty or compensation imposed by the adjudicating officer or the Authority, the amount shall be recoverable from that person as an arrear of land revenue, in the manner prescribed. The manner prescribed runs through a recovery certificate. The buyer applies to the Authority for enforcement, the Authority quantifies the amount due under its order, and a certificate issues to the District Collector of the district where the promoter or his property is situated, directing recovery of the certified sum as if it were land revenue in arrear.

The machinery the certificate sets in motion is the machinery the State uses to collect its own dues, and it is not decorative. Under the land revenue codes and recovery statutes of the States, the Collector and the officers beneath him may proceed by demand notice, by attachment and sale of movable property, by attachment and sale of the defaulter's immovable property, and by the other coercive processes the revenue law allows, including in some States the arrest and detention of a wilful defaulter. MahaRERA's certificates are executed under the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code, 1966 by the district collectorates of that State; Uttar Pradesh executes through its district magistrates under its own revenue law. The buyer who reaches this stage has, on paper, exchanged a regulator's order for a claim enforced by the district administration itself, with the powers the administration reserves for the recovery of public money.

Two statutory pressures stand behind the certificate, and the buyer should know both, because an application that invokes them is drafted differently from one that merely complains of non-payment. The first is the penalty for disobedience of the Authority itself. Section 63 provides that a promoter who fails to comply with the orders, decisions or directions of the Authority is liable to a penalty for every day during which the default continues, which may cumulatively extend to five per cent of the estimated cost of the real estate project: a running meter on disobedience, measured against the project rather than the individual claim, which gives even a modest buyer's order an institutional weight the promoter cannot shrug off. The second stands behind orders of the Appellate Tribunal. Section 64 provides that a promoter who fails to comply with the Tribunal's orders is liable to a penalty for every day of default cumulatively extending to ten per cent of the estimated cost of the project, or to imprisonment for a term extending to three years, or to both. The Act's enforcement pyramid is therefore coherent: the order, the certificate, the daily penalty for the Authority's orders disobeyed, and imprisonment in the frame for the Tribunal's orders disobeyed. That the pyramid is climbed slowly and unevenly in practice is the subject of the next section; that it exists is a reason for the buyer to climb it deliberately rather than drift into private negotiation from a position the statute has already strengthened.

The buyer's own conduct at this stage matters more than at any other. The enforcement application should be filed as soon as the compliance window closes, with the order, the computation of the amount due updated to the date of application, and proof that the amount was not paid. It should state what the buyer knows of the promoter's assets: the project land and unsold inventory, other projects of the same promoter, bank accounts identified in the project's registration filings. The Authority's registry file on the project, which the registration scheme makes substantially public, contains disclosures the buyer can use. And the buyer should track the certificate after it issues, obtaining its number and the office to which it was sent, because a certificate that has left the Authority's building is a file in another administration's queue, and files in queues move measurably faster for the claimant who appears, inquires and is seen to be waiting.

Inside the Collector's office, the certificate becomes a revenue file and travels through the stages the State's revenue law prescribes for any arrear. A demand notice issues to the defaulter calling for payment within the period it states. On continued default, the process moves to attachment: the defaulter's movable property and bank accounts may be attached and sold, and his immovable property, including land and unsold units standing in his name, may be attached and brought to sale by public auction, with the sale proceeds applied to the certified amount. Where the defaulter's property lies in another district, the certificate is transmitted for execution to the Collector of that district, a routine feature of revenue recovery that still adds its own transit time to the file. Maharashtra's published procedure illustrates the administrative shape of the process in a State that has systematised it: certificates received by a collectorate are scrutinised and forwarded to the tehsildar within days, a demand notice follows with a short period for payment, and the defaulter may be called upon to file an affidavit of his movable and immovable assets, on which the attachment programme is then built. The affidavit stage is where the buyer's own knowledge earns its keep a second time, because a defaulter's disclosure is tested against what the claimants know, and the buyer who has tracked the promoter's projects, accounts and inventory through the registration file can point the revenue officer at assets the disclosure omits. The stages are unglamorous, sequential and slow, and they are also the only stages in the entire route at which the State's coercive apparatus works directly for the buyer; a buyer who treats the certificate's issue as the conclusion of his effort misunderstands what the certificate is, which is an admission ticket to a process that rewards attendance.

Execution begins, in practice, inside the Authority rather than at the Collector's door, and the buyer should understand the internal stage because it decides the quality of the certificate that eventually issues. On the buyer's application, the Authority takes up the enforcement of its own order: the promoter is called upon to show compliance or explain default, the amount due is computed to date, including interest that has continued to accrue on the unpaid sum, and the Authority may deploy the instruments the Act gives it before certifying the debt to the district, including the Section 63 penalty proceedings for disobedience of its orders and directions restraining further sales or withdrawals in the project. Uttar Pradesh's Authority, in the four-year review drawn on below, recorded that benches had decided thousands of enforcement proceedings of exactly this character, and that about half of the orders due for compliance in the period had been complied with, a figure that reflects this internal stage working before any certificate moved. The lesson for the buyer is that the enforcement application is itself a proceeding to be worked, with updated computations filed and hearings attended, and not a formality to be lodged and forgotten. A promoter who intends to pay usually pays here, under the shadow of the certificate and the penalty, and the settlement figures in the next section are substantially the product of this stage.

## Why the money lags the order: the recovery gap in the published figures

The design described above is formidable. The record is the corrective, and the record, on the Authorities' own published figures, is one of partial conversion: orders in quantity, certificates in quantity, recovery in a fraction. This is the article's central practical finding, and it is presented here with the attribution the subject requires, State by State and period by period, because aggregate national claims about RERA recovery are precisely the kind of figure this series does not print.

Take Uttar Pradesh, the State whose Authority has carried the heaviest complaint load in the country. Reviewing its first four years, the Uttar Pradesh Authority reported that it had issued 4,724 recovery certificates to the district magistrates for the recovery of money due to homebuyers. Against about 1,000 of those certificates, about Rs 150 crore had been recovered and transferred to the accounts of homebuyers. Settlements between promoters and buyers accounted for certificates amounting to about Rs 300 crore more, and the Authority put the position as almost a third of the demand contained in the certificates having been settled by recovery and settlement together. The same review recorded the scale of the enforcement queue behind those certificates: of some 16,400 orders whose compliance dates had expired, about 11,200 complainants had approached the Authority for enforcement. Read together, the figures describe the system's shape faithfully. The adjudication machine worked at volume. The execution machine, fed with nearly five thousand certificates in four years, converted a minority of them into money, with negotiated settlement after the certificate issued doing a substantial part of the converting.

Take Maharashtra, whose Authority is among the most institutionally developed in the country. Figures published by MahaRERA put the recovery orders it had issued since its establishment in May 2017 at Rs 1,037.86 crore, in favour of 1,531 complainants. Of that total, Rs 369.45 crore had been recovered through the district collectorates: just over a third of the amount ordered, recovered over the whole life of the Authority. The same published figures record a further Rs 209.16 crore on which recovery proceedings stood restricted, and the composition of that restricted block is itself instructive: Rs 104.70 crore entangled in proceedings before the National Company Law Tribunal, and Rs 104.46 crore caught by proceedings under the Maharashtra Protection of Interest of Depositors Act, 1999. A fifth of everything MahaRERA had ever ordered recovered was, at the time of publication, legally frozen inside other processes. Maharashtra's administrative response to the gap is also part of the record: the State moved to designate resident deputy collectors and additional district collectors as dedicated revenue recovery officers for these cases, and collections in the year that followed that designation rose noticeably against the earlier run rate. The gap, in other words, is not a mystery of defiance alone. It is substantially a problem of administrative capacity and priority, and it responds, where it responds, to officers being assigned to the work.

The structural reasons for the gap are consistent across States, and a buyer who understands them will neither be surprised by the wait nor passive inside it. The Collector's office recovers the State's own land revenue, agricultural dues, and a lengthening list of statutory certificates from many departments, of which RERA certificates are one queue among several, and no period is fixed by the Act within which the certificate must be executed. The promoter against whom the certificate issues frequently holds assets that are formally impressive and practically encumbered: project land already mortgaged to the project's lenders, unsold inventory whose sale the Authority itself may have restrained, receivables assigned to financial creditors. Attachment reaches what is attachable, and the charge of a secured lender stands in the way of much of it. Promoters in the deepest default, finally, tend to be companies whose only asset was the project and whose only fund was the buyers' money, spent; a certificate against such a company is a claim on an empty vessel, and no revenue machinery yet devised recovers money that no longer exists. None of this makes the certificate pointless. The Uttar Pradesh figures show certificates producing settlements at scale, because a promoter who can pay discovers, when the certificate issues, that the negotiation has acquired a deadline. It makes the certificate a beginning rather than an end, which is the operational meaning of this article's organising claim: the buyer who plans for execution from the day of filing, pressing for interim restraint, identifying assets, and pursuing the certificate actively, recovers in the cases where recovery is possible at all, and recovers sooner.

## The event that stops the whole process: the promoter enters insolvency

Every stage described so far assumes the promoter remains a legal person capable of being proceeded against. One event removes that assumption, and the brief for this article identifies it as the single most consequential practical point in the field: the admission of the promoter into a corporate insolvency resolution process under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. On admission, Section 14 of the Code imposes a moratorium. The institution of suits or the continuation of pending suits or proceedings against the corporate debtor is prohibited, and so is the execution of any judgment, decree or order in any court of law, tribunal or arbitration panel, along with the transfer or alienation of the debtor's assets and recovery action by secured creditors against its property. A pending RERA complaint against such a promoter stops where it stands. A RERA order already obtained cannot be executed: the recovery certificate cannot be pressed, the Collector's machinery is stayed, and the Section 63 meter loses its object, because the debtor's affairs have passed out of the bilateral frame altogether and into a collective process administered by a resolution professional under the supervision of the National Company Law Tribunal. The Code's scheme, its moratorium and its creditor machinery are treated in [the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 guide](/2012/04/01/insolvency-bankruptcy-code-2016-guide/), and its working record in [the IBC implementation record](/2012/04/15/ibc-implementation-record/); what the buyer needs here is the translation of his own position.

The translation begins with status. By the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Second Amendment) Act, 2018, an Explanation was inserted into Section 5(8)(f) of the Code deeming amounts raised from allottees under a real estate project to have the commercial effect of a borrowing, and therefore to be financial debt. The allottee is accordingly a financial creditor of the promoter. The Supreme Court upheld the amendment in Pioneer Urban Land and Infrastructure Ltd. v. Union of India in 2019, holding that allottees were within the mischief the definition addressed and that the remedies under the two statutes are concurrent remedies between which the allottee elects. Financial creditor status carries a seat, through the committee of creditors, in the process that decides the promoter's fate: allottees vote as a class, through an authorised representative appointed under Section 25A, who casts the class's vote in accordance with the decision of the majority of the class present and voting. The buyer who held an individual statutory remedy under RERA wakes, on admission, as one member of a creditor class inside a negotiation about whether the company is resolved, sold, or liquidated.

What the buyer must then do is different in kind from everything before it, and it is time-bound in a way the RERA route never was. The claim must be filed with the resolution professional, in the form and within the period the public announcement of the process prescribes, supported by the same documents that founded the RERA complaint: the allotment and agreement, the payment record, and any RERA order in the buyer's favour, which enters the process as evidence of the debt. A buyer who continues to pursue the frozen RERA execution instead, out of attachment to a remedy that felt more personal, risks missing the insolvency claim window altogether, and with it the vote and the distribution. Where a resolution plan is approved, its treatment of allottees, typically an offer of completed possession on a revised schedule, sometimes a refund in tranches, binds every member of the class including those who voted against it. Where no plan is approved, liquidation follows, and the allottee's claim ranks in the waterfall as an unsecured financial debt, behind insolvency costs, workmen's dues and secured creditors who relinquish their security, which is the legal form of the proposition that in a failed project the buyers' money is recovered, if at all, in annas.

The threshold for starting this process deserves a buyer's attention even where he did not start it, because it shapes the politics of every stalled project. Allottees may themselves apply as financial creditors to initiate the process, but the 2020 amendment to Section 7 requires the application to be filed jointly by not less than one hundred allottees of the same project, or by allottees constituting not less than ten per cent of the total number of allottees under that project, whichever is less; the Supreme Court upheld the threshold in Manish Kumar v. Union of India in 2021, accepting the legislative purpose of preventing a single allottee from using insolvency, with its company-wide consequences, as a pressure instrument in an individual dispute. The consequence for the individual buyer is that insolvency is never his private remedy. It arrives, when it arrives, at the instance of lenders whose loans dwarf every buyer's claim, or of an organised body of his fellow allottees, or of the promoter's other creditors, and his task is not to trigger it but to be correctly positioned when it comes: claim filed, class vote informed, documents in order.

The deeper discomfort of the insolvency contingency should be stated plainly, because the series thesis of this cluster runs through it. The escrow discipline of Section 4(2)(l)(D) was designed to make promoter insolvency survivable for buyers, by keeping seventy per cent of collections inside the project that generated them. Where that discipline was observed, insolvency is an administrative event: the project has funds, a resolution plan can complete it, and buyers emerge with apartments rather than claims. Where it was not observed, the insolvency reveals that the remedy system has been pursuing, through the Authority, the Tribunal and the Collector, a defendant whose assets were consumed before the first complaint was filed. The moratorium is the moment the buyer learns which of those two projects he bought into. No drafting of a complaint can change that; only the prevention discipline, verifying the project's registration and escrow compliance before and during payment, addresses it, and it is to the record and to prevention that this article turns after a necessary word on the routes that run beside the Act.

Two features of the insolvency landscape complete the buyer's picture. The first is the home loan that runs through it. Most buyers in a stalled project are paying a lender for an asset that does not exist, under a tripartite arrangement in which the lender disbursed against the promoter's undertaking and the buyer's liability to the lender is independent of the promoter's default. Insolvency of the promoter does not suspend the buyer's equated monthly instalments, and the buyer's claim in the process, for the amounts paid to the promoter, sits alongside rather than inside his continuing obligation to his bank. The practical consequence is brutal and should be faced in the complaint strategy itself: a buyer deep in a failing project is financing both sides of the failure, which is a further reason the refund route, pursued early while the promoter is still a going concern, is worth more than the same route pursued after admission. The second feature is the direction of travel within insolvency practice, which favours completion over distribution. Resolution plans for real estate debtors are typically structured around finishing the project, by the existing promoter under supervision, by a replacement developer, or by the allottees' association itself with funding arranged against unsold inventory, because a completed project is worth more to every class of creditor than a liquidated shell. The Supreme Court's own intervention in the Amrapali group litigation, Bikram Chatterji v. Union of India, though delivered in writ jurisdiction rather than inside a resolution process, reflects the same instinct at the largest scale: the Court cancelled the RERA registrations of the group's projects, ordered a forensic audit of the diversion of buyers' funds, attached properties, and directed completion of the stalled projects through a public sector construction company, treating the buyers' flats rather than the buyers' refunds as the outcome the law should strain to deliver. A buyer class that organises, files its claims completely and votes coherently is positioned to claim that outcome; a scattered class is positioned to be planned around.

## The complaint as a regulatory event: what one filing sets in motion

A buyer files for his own relief, but the filing is also an input into the Authority's supervision of the project, and understanding that second life of the complaint explains experiences that puzzle individual complainants, including benches that seem more interested in the project's survival than in any single file. The Authority wears both hats by design: it is the adjudicator of the complaint and the regulator of the project, armed with the power to inquire into contraventions of its own motion, to call for information, and to act on what the complaint record reveals about the escrow account, the quarterly certificates and the promoter's other obligations. A pattern of complaints about one project is therefore data the regulator uses: it triggers scrutiny of the separate account, directions about further sales and withdrawals, and in the gravest cases the revocation proceedings under Section 7 that reposition the project under new management. The individual buyer benefits from this machinery without directing it, and is sometimes frustrated by it, where the Authority's project-level judgment, that the buyers as a body are best served by a supervised completion over eighteen months, sits uneasily with his own judgment that he wants his money in eighteen weeks. Both judgments can be honestly held. The statutory scheme prefers the survival of the project where survival is possible, because completed apartments satisfy more buyers than any distribution of a failed promoter's residue, and the complaint that acknowledges the tension, pleading the individual entitlement firmly while addressing the project's actual state candidly, is heard by a bench that manages both hats as the more credible document. The buyer's entitlement under Section 18 does not depend on the project's prospects, and Newtech forecloses any inquiry that would make it do so; but the sequencing of relief in a project under regulatory management is a practical matter on which the informed buyer keeps his eyes open, particularly where an Authority-supervised completion is genuinely under way and the choice between the refund door and the staying door must be re-examined against new facts.

## The parallel routes, and the mistakes that cost buyers their case

RERA was not enacted into an empty field, and it does not occupy the field alone. Section 88 provides that the Act's provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, the provisions of any other law in force, a saving the Supreme Court gave operative effect in Pioneer Urban when it held the remedies under the RERA, the consumer protection law and the insolvency Code to be concurrent. In Imperia Structures the Court proceeded on the same architecture: the allottee's statutory rights under Section 18 coexist with the complaint the same facts support before a consumer commission, and the existence of the RERA route does not extinguish the consumer route. The choice between the forums, what each gives, what each costs in time, and how a buyer should elect between them where both are open, is a comparison with an owner of its own, and it is made in [RERA vs consumer protection for homebuyers](/2019/02/15/rera-vs-consumer-protection-homebuyers/). What belongs here is the boundary discipline. Concurrency is a choice of forum, not a duplication of recovery: the same refund and interest cannot be recovered twice, parallel proceedings on the same claim invite objection and embarrassment in both forums, and the declaration the RERA portal requires about pending proceedings should be answered with the same accuracy as every other statement in the complaint. A buyer with connected but distinct claims, a refund claim under the Act and a grievance that sounds in a different wrong, should take advice on the sequencing; a buyer with one claim should put it in one forum and pursue it to the end.

The mistakes that lose or devalue RERA cases are, in the main, neither exotic nor legal. They are filing mistakes and record mistakes, and they recur with a regularity that justifies listing them in prose. The first is the prayer mistake: compensation asked of the Authority, refund asked of the adjudicating officer, and months spent in reference that a correct reading of Newtech would have avoided. The second is the computation mistake: interest claimed as a lump percentage of the price rather than instalment by instalment from dates of payment, which invites recalculation on the promoter's terms. The third is the record mistake: receipts missing, cash payments unprovable, the agreement for sale unregistered or superseded by side letters the buyer cannot produce. The fourth is the sleep mistake: an order obtained and left unenforced while the compliance window and then the promoter's remaining assets drain away, or an appeal by the promoter left undefended because the buyer treated the order as the victory. The fifth is the parallel-proceeding mistake: the same claim pressed in two forums until each forum learns of the other. And the sixth, the gravest, is the insolvency mistake: pursuing a frozen execution while the claim window in the corporate insolvency process closes. Every one of these is a buyer's error, not a promoter's trick, and every one is avoidable on the day the complaint is filed by a buyer who has read the route whole.

## The record a remedy runs on, and the asset queue behind it

Every stage of the route described in this article is decided on documents, and the documents are the buyer's to keep or to lose. The allotment letter and the registered agreement for sale fix the possession date from which everything runs. The payment receipts, bank statements and the promoter's own acknowledgements fix the interest base, instalment by instalment. The project's RERA registration number unlocks the Authority's file, with its approvals, its quarterly progress certificates and its escrow disclosures. The correspondence, the promoter's letters promising revised dates, the notices and the replies, prove the default and answer the defences. A buyer pursuing a complaint and then a recovery is, in substance, presenting this file four times to four audiences: to the Authority, to the adjudicating officer, to the Tribunal if the case travels, and to the insolvency professional if the worst arrives. Keeping it as one ordered, private and encrypted file from the first payment onwards, rather than as a shoebox assembled under pressure, is the cheapest advantage available in the entire process; [keeping the allotment letter, the registered agreement, every receipt and the full correspondence together in an encrypted workspace](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) is written for the journalist's sources in that account, and it describes, document for document, the discipline a homebuyer's remedy depends on. The general position set out in this article is legal information about how the remedy works; it is not advice on any individual matter, and limitation periods and forum rules are unforgiving enough that a buyer whose facts are unusual should take advice on them before the windows described here begin to close.

The record matters at the recovery stage for a reason that goes beyond proof, and it brings into view the queue of claimants standing around the promoter's assets. The project land against which a recovery certificate is executed is very commonly mortgaged to the promoter's lenders, and a lender enforcing its security under [the SARFAESI Act 2002 explained](/2013/02/15/sarfaesi-act-2002-explained/) moves against that land through a machinery of its own, faster and older in practice than the Collector's, which is why the attachment a certificate produces so often fastens on unsold units and receivables rather than on the land itself. A buyer who knows what the promoter owns, what it is charged with, and what remains unencumbered, knowledge the registration file substantially supplies, aims the certificate at the assets that can actually answer it. Execution, in this field, is a branch of information before it is a branch of force.

Prevention is the same discipline practised earlier. Before paying, the buyer verifies the project on the Authority's portal: that it is registered, that the registration is valid for the period in which possession is promised, that the approvals the promoter claims are the approvals uploaded, that the promoter's other projects and their complaint history are visible and read. During payment, the buyer pays against the registered agreement, by traceable instruments, and keeps the receipts. During construction, the buyer reads the quarterly certificates the registration scheme requires the promoter to file, because the divergence between certified progress and collected instalments is the earliest legible symptom of the diversion that ends in insolvency. None of this converts a bad project into a good one. It converts the buyer from the last to know into the first to act, and in a system where the remedy's constraint is recovery rather than adjudication, acting first is the whole of the advantage there is.

The verdict on the remedy can be stated in the terms the evidence supports. As an adjudication system, RERA homebuyer remedies are the strongest instrument Indian law has given the retail property buyer: an indefeasible refund right, a forum sorted by the character of the claim, interest that prices delay against the defaulter, an appeal gated by the appellant's own money, and a tribunal ladder that ends quickly in law. As a recovery system, the same remedy is only as strong as the district machinery and the promoter's remaining assets, and the Authorities' own figures, a third or so of ordered money recovered in the two largest systems examined here, mark the distance between the right and the money. A buyer cannot close that distance by wishing, and cannot be asked to. What a buyer can do is refuse to add to it: file in the right forum, on a complete record, at the first default rather than the fifth, press the appeal deposit, drive the certificate, and be correctly positioned on the day the insolvency notice, if it comes, is published. The statute rewards exactly that buyer. It was, on the evidence, designed for him.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Where and how does a homebuyer register a RERA complaint?

A complaint is filed under Section 31 of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 with the Real Estate Regulatory Authority of the State where the project is situated, or with that Authority's adjudicating officer where the claim is for compensation. Any aggrieved person may file, in the form and with the fee the State rules prescribe. Most Authorities run the process through an online portal: the buyer registers, selects the project by its RERA registration number, states the facts and the relief sought, uploads the agreement for sale and payment receipts, pays the prescribed fee, and receives a complaint number. Hearings follow on notice to the promoter, and the proceeding is summary in character, decided largely on documents.

### Q: What relief can a RERA authority order?

The Authority can direct the promoter to refund the amounts received from the allottee with interest at the prescribed rate where the allottee withdraws on the promoter's failure to give possession by the agreed date. It can direct payment of interest for every month of delay to an allottee who stays in the project. It can direct the promoter to complete the project and hand over possession, to execute the registered agreement for sale and the conveyance deed, to rectify defects, and to stop charging amounts the Act does not authorise. It can also impose penalties for contraventions of the Act. What the Authority does not itself assess is compensation for loss, which belongs to the adjudicating officer.

### Q: How is RERA interest on a delayed project calculated?

Interest is charged at the rate prescribed by the rules of the State where the project stands, because the Act itself fixes no number. The standard prescription links the rate to the highest marginal cost of lending rate of the State Bank of India plus a stated margin: Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Assam prescribe that benchmark plus two per cent, while Uttar Pradesh prescribed it plus one per cent. The rate is applied as simple interest to each amount the promoter actually received, from the date of its receipt, until refund is made or possession is handed over. The computation therefore runs payment by payment on the buyer's receipts, which is why the payment record is the foundation of the claim.

### Q: How is a RERA order actually enforced?

An order of the Authority is first an instruction with a compliance window, and a large share of orders are complied with or settled at that stage, under pressure of the daily penalty Section 63 imposes for disobedience. Where the promoter does not pay, Section 40(1) makes the interest, penalty or compensation recoverable as an arrear of land revenue. The buyer applies to the Authority for enforcement, the Authority issues a recovery certificate for the amount due, and the certificate goes to the District Collector, whose revenue machinery executes it by demand notice, attachment and sale of the promoter's movable and immovable property. Orders of the Appellate Tribunal are additionally executable as decrees of a civil court under Section 57.

### Q: What pre-deposit must a promoter make to appeal?

Before a promoter's appeal to the Real Estate Appellate Tribunal is entertained, the proviso to Section 43(5) requires him to deposit with the Tribunal at least thirty per cent of any penalty imposed, or such higher percentage as the Tribunal determines, or the total amount to be paid to the allottee including interest and compensation imposed on him, or both, as the case may be. Where the order directs payment to the allottee, the deposit is therefore of the whole amount payable under it. The Supreme Court in the Newtech judgment of 2021 held the requirement mandatory: it is a condition of the appeal being heard at all, and the Tribunal has no discretion to waive or reduce it.

### Q: What happens to a RERA case if the builder enters insolvency?

On the admission of a corporate insolvency resolution process against the promoter, the moratorium under Section 14 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 prohibits the continuation of proceedings against the company and the execution of any order against it. A pending RERA complaint stops, and a RERA order already obtained cannot be enforced through the recovery certificate route. The allottee's remedy becomes a claim in the insolvency, where allottees rank as financial creditors under Section 5(8)(f) of the Code, file claims with the resolution professional, and vote as a class through an authorised representative. The RERA documents and any RERA order become the evidence supporting the insolvency claim.

### Q: How long does a RERA complaint take?

The statute works to a sixty-day measure. The adjudicating officer must endeavour to dispose of a compensation application within sixty days of receiving it and record reasons where that proves impossible, and the Authorities' complaint procedure adopts the same discipline of disposal within sixty days as far as possible. Practice varies widely by State, by bench strength and by the character of the case. A refund complaint on undisputed documents can conclude within a few hearings spread over some months. A complaint absorbed into the distress of an entire project, with many allottees and intervening lenders, takes considerably longer, and enforcement after the order adds its own period, which no provision of the Act fixes.

### Q: What should a homebuyer check before paying a builder?

The buyer should verify the project on the State Authority's portal before paying anything substantial: that the project is registered under the Act, that the registration covers the phase and the period in which possession is promised, and that the approvals, sanctioned plans and title documents the promoter claims are the documents actually uploaded. The buyer should read the promoter's other projects and their complaint history on the same portal, insist on the registered agreement for sale before paying beyond the booking amount the law permits, pay only by traceable instruments against receipts, and confirm that instalments are demanded in step with construction as the agreement and the registration disclosures provide. Prevention under this Act is a documentary habit, not a legal proceeding.

### Q: Who decides a claim for compensation under RERA?

Compensation is decided by the adjudicating officer, a judicial officer who is or has been a district judge, appointed by the Authority in consultation with the State Government under Section 71. The officer adjudicates compensation arising under Sections 12, 14, 18 and 19: loss from a misleading advertisement, from an unrectified defect, from a failed or delayed project, and from breach of the allottee's rights. In deciding, Section 72 requires the officer to weigh the disproportionate gain or unfair advantage the default produced, the loss caused, the repetitive nature of the default, and any other relevant factor. The Supreme Court confirmed this division in the Newtech judgment: refund and interest go to the Authority, and compensation goes to the adjudicating officer.

### Q: Where can a homebuyer appeal against a RERA authority order?

A homebuyer aggrieved by an order of the Authority or the adjudicating officer may appeal to the Real Estate Appellate Tribunal of the State within sixty days of the order's communication, under Section 43(5); the Tribunal may entertain a later appeal where sufficient cause for the delay is shown. No deposit is required of an allottee appellant, the pre-deposit condition being addressed to promoters alone. From the Tribunal, a further appeal lies to the High Court within sixty days under Section 58, but only on a ground specified in Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which confines the appeal to a substantial question of law. No appeal lies against a Tribunal decision recorded with the parties' consent.

### Q: How much does it cost to file a RERA complaint?

The fee is prescribed by the rules of each State and is modest by design, since the forum was built for unrepresented buyers. In Uttar Pradesh the fee for a complaint is one thousand rupees, paid online through the Authority's portal. In Maharashtra the fee is five thousand rupees. Other States prescribe fees within that general order, and separate, usually similar, fees may apply to an application before the adjudicating officer and to an appeal before the Appellate Tribunal. Beyond the fee, the buyer's costs are the costs of assembling documents and attending hearings; there is no court fee calculated on the amount claimed, which distinguishes the route sharply from a civil suit for the same money.

### Q: What documents should a homebuyer produce with a RERA complaint?

The core file is small and decisive. It comprises the allotment letter and the registered agreement for sale, which fix the unit, the price and the possession date; proof of every payment claimed, meaning receipts and bank statements showing each instalment and its date, because interest is computed payment by payment; the project's RERA registration particulars, taken from the Authority's portal; and the correspondence with the promoter, including the buyer's written demand and the promoter's replies promising revised dates. Where compensation is claimed, the buyer adds proof of the loss: rent receipts for the waiting period, the home loan statement, and documents showing expenses the default caused. A complete file at filing is the strongest single predictor of a quick order.

### Q: Can a buyer who stays in the project claim interest for delayed possession?

Yes. The proviso to Section 18(1) gives an allottee who does not intend to withdraw from the project the right to be paid interest for every month of delay until possession is handed over, at the prescribed rate, on the amounts paid. The claim is made before the Authority, and it does not require the buyer to prove any separate loss, because the interest is the statute's price for the period during which the promoter held the buyer's money without delivering the apartment. The election is the buyer's: withdraw and take refund with interest, or stay and take delay interest with the apartment when it comes. The promoter cannot force the buyer from one door to the other.

### Q: What interest does a homebuyer pay for delayed instalments?

The same prescribed rate that runs against the promoter. Section 19(6) obliges the allottee to make payments in accordance with the agreement for sale, and Section 19(7) makes an allottee who defaults liable to pay interest at the rate prescribed by the State rules, the rate built on the State Bank of India's highest marginal cost of lending rate plus the State's margin. Maharashtra's model agreement states the identity expressly, applying that rate to amounts due from the allottee. The symmetry is deliberate legislative design: interest under the Act prices the use of money over time, and it is charged to whichever party is holding money that belongs to the other, promoter or allottee alike.

### Q: Can an association of allottees file a complaint for its members?

Yes. Section 31 opens the complaint to any aggrieved person, and associations of allottees routinely file and pursue complaints on behalf of their members, particularly where the grievance is common to a tower or a project. The Act recognises the association expressly at the appellate stage as well: the Explanation to Section 43(5) provides that person, for the purpose of an appeal, includes the association of allottees and any voluntary consumer association registered under a law in force. In practice the association is also the vehicle for conciliation settlements, for the takeover of an incomplete project under Section 8, and for organising the allottees' class vote where the promoter enters insolvency. Individual members' interest computations remain their own and must be filed accurately for each member.

### Q: Is there a time limit for filing a RERA complaint?

The Act prescribes no limitation period for a complaint under Section 31. Courts and tribunals have declined to import one: the Chhattisgarh High Court has held that the three-year period in Article 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963 does not apply to complaints before the Authority or the adjudicating officer, and the Haryana Appellate Tribunal has held that the Limitation Act is not incorporated into the Act's scheme. That is not a licence to wait indefinitely. Some Authorities test stale compensation claims against a standard of reasonable time, evidence decays, and the promoter's solvency, which is the real security behind any order, deteriorates in a stalled project. The absence of a clock is a reason not to panic, not a reason to delay.

### Q: Can a RERA complaint be filed for a project that is not registered?

Yes. Selling a project that the Act requires to be registered, without registering it, is itself a violation of Section 3, and the complaint route is open against it; several Authorities maintain a distinct facility for reporting unregistered projects. The promoter who markets and sells without the mandated registration exposes himself under Section 59 to a penalty that may extend to ten per cent of the estimated cost of the project, and, where he continues in default or disobeys the Authority's directions on registration, to imprisonment extending to three years, or a further fine extending to ten per cent of the estimated cost, or both. The buyer's own claims in respect of the money paid are pursued before the Authority in the ordinary way, and the absence of registration is the promoter's offence, not the buyer's obstacle.

### Q: Does a homebuyer need a lawyer to pursue a RERA complaint?

No. Section 56 permits a person to appear before the Authority, the adjudicating officer and the Appellate Tribunal in person, or through an authorised representative, who may be a chartered accountant, a company secretary, a cost accountant or a legal practitioner. The portals, forms and summary procedure are designed for a buyer carrying his own documents, and many complaints are filed and argued by buyers themselves. Professional assistance earns its place at identifiable pressure points: drafting a compensation claim that must satisfy Section 72, computing interest across years of instalments, meeting a promoter's jurisdictional and contractual defences, conducting an appeal, and driving execution after the order. The choice belongs to the buyer and can be made stage by stage.

### Q: Can a RERA dispute be settled through conciliation before a final order?

Yes. Several Authorities, Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh among them, maintain conciliation forums in which a dispute is placed before a panel that attempts a negotiated settlement between the promoter and the allottees, and many Authorities route suitable complaints to conciliation before or alongside the hearing. Uttar Pradesh's Authority has reported its forum resolving disputes involving over sixteen hundred allottees in a single reporting period. Conciliation can produce revised possession schedules, agreed interest figures and payment plans that a contested order cannot. Its risk is delay where a promoter treats it as a waiting room. A settlement reached should be recorded before the Authority, so that it becomes enforceable as an order rather than surviving only as the promoter's private promise.

### Q: Why do RERA recovery certificates often remain unexecuted?

The certificate is executed not by the Authority that issued it but by the District Collector's revenue machinery, which carries the State's own dues and certificates from many departments in the same queue, and the Act fixes no period within which execution must complete. The promoter's assets are the second constraint: the project land is commonly mortgaged to lenders whose charges stand ahead of the certificate, unsold inventory may be restrained or illiquid, and the promoter in deepest default is often a company whose only fund, the buyers' collections, has already been spent. The published figures show the result. Uttar Pradesh's Authority issued 4,724 certificates in its first four years and reported about Rs 150 crore recovered against about 1,000 of them, with settlements doing much of the remaining work.
