The 1971 election and the Bangladesh war are usually shelved in separate histories, one under electoral politics and the other under foreign policy, as if the year had produced two unrelated triumphs for Indira Gandhi by coincidence. The Election Commission of India counted the first triumph in its Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Fifth Lok Sabha, published in 1972: 352 seats out of 518 for the Congress (R), a majority no Indian leader had assembled since independence. The second triumph was recorded in the instrument of surrender signed at Dhaka on 16 December 1971, when Lieutenant General A. A. K. Niazi laid down the Pakistani Eastern Command's arms before Lieutenant General Jagjit Singh Aurora. This article defends a single claim about the space between those two documents: 1971 was one political event sequence, in which the mandate enabled the war, the war legitimised a centralisation of constitutional authority, and the Allahabad High Court judgment of 12 June 1975 tested whether the sequence's product could survive judicial scrutiny of the mandate itself.

The separated reading has distinguished defenders. Sumit Ganguly's Conflict Unending (2001) treats the 1971 war inside a structural account of India and Pakistan rivalry, where elections are background. Srinath Raghavan's 1971: A Global History of the Creation of Bangladesh (2013), the most complete modern treatment, reconstructs the war through the diplomacy of Washington, Moscow, Beijing, Islamabad and New Delhi, and the Indian electorate appears in that account as context rather than as cause. Against both, the unified-sequence reading draws on the domestic record that Inder Malhotra assembled in Indira Gandhi (1989) and on the constitutional history Granville Austin wrote in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999). Read through those sources, the sequence has four links, each documented by a dated primary record: an election deliberately advanced to manufacture political room, a refugee emergency that consumed that room and demanded more, a war that the room made decidable, and an amendment cycle that converted victory into durable constitutional change. The links are causal, not merely chronological, and each link can be checked against a named document.

A note on the evidence governs everything that follows. The article privileges records whose dates cannot be rearranged: the Election Commission's Statistical Report of 1972 for the verdict, the treaty text of 9 August 1971 for the diplomacy, the Lok Sabha debates of 1971 for the government's stated reasoning at the moment of decision, and the cabinet papers of November and December 1971, partially declassified in 2008, for the war decision's internal calendar. The secondary authorities are used for what each does best. Raghavan supplies the international system and the Pakistani failure in unmatched archival depth, and the article's complication section adopts his apportionment where the evidence points that way. Malhotra supplies the political calculation inside the Congress and the cabinet. Austin supplies the constitutional mechanism, the route by which refreshed authority became amended text. Where these authorities conflict, the conflict is the named disagreement the article adjudicates: whether the election and the war were two events that happened to share a year, or one sequence whose links caused each other. The division lists, the amendment dates and the judgment of 12 June 1975 are the exhibits on which the adjudication rests, and each exhibit is a public record any reader can open.

## The 1971 Election Began in December 1970

The sequence opened with an act of timing. On 27 December 1970, President V. V. Giri dissolved the Fourth Lok Sabha on the advice of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, more than a year before its term expired, and India went to a midterm poll for the first time in its history. The decision is sometimes described as a gamble forced on a minority government, and Indira Gandhi did govern after the 1969 split with outside support from the Communist Party of India, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and others. Malhotra's biography records the calculation differently: the dissolution was an offensive move by a leader who had spent 1970 accumulating plebiscitary assets, among them bank nationalisation and the attempt on the privy purses, and who intended to convert personal popularity into a parliamentary majority before the opposition could organise against her and before events in East Pakistan, already deteriorating after the Awami League's December 1970 victory in the Pakistani elections, could narrow her options.

The timing carried a second, quieter design. The 1971 poll was delinked from state assembly elections, so voters chose a national government without simultaneously choosing state governments. Earlier Indian elections had fused the two contests, which meant that state level party machines, the district bosses and provincial committees that the Congress organisation controlled, could drag national candidates across the line on local organisation alone. After the split, those machines belonged largely to the Congress (O). Delinking cut the national contest loose from machinery Indira Gandhi did not own and made the election a referendum on the Prime Minister herself, fought constituency by constituency on a national slogan. The Election Commission's Statistical Report preserves the mechanical consequence: the Congress (R) contested the poll as a party with thin organisation and a single recognisable asset, its leader, and the asset proved sufficient in 352 constituencies.

### What did delinking the 1971 poll from state elections change?

Delinking separated the national verdict from state verdicts, so a voter could punish a state ministry without touching the central government and reward the Prime Minister without endorsing her party's provincial bosses. Because the Congress (O) held the state machines, delinking neutralised its chief asset and turned the election into a plebiscite on Indira Gandhi personally.

The plebiscitary design also explains the campaign's shape. Indira Gandhi addressed hundreds of public meetings across the country in roughly six weeks, travelling by air and road at a pace the Election Commission's report and the press record of February 1971 both document, and the campaign spoke over the heads of every intermediary between the Prime Minister and the voter. Party manifestos mattered less than the exchange of slogans. The opposition coalition, styling itself the Grand Alliance, campaigned on the cry of Indira Hatao, remove Indira. The Congress (R) answered with Garibi Hatao, remove poverty, and Indira Gandhi sharpened the exchange into the election's defining contrast at rally after rally: the opposition's programme was a person, hers was a condition. The contrast worked because it was structurally accurate about the delinked contest. A plebiscite asks who should rule. Garibi Hatao answered with a claim about what rule was for, and it attached that claim to the only figure the delinked ballot actually presented to most voters.

The opposition's weakness was organisational as much as rhetorical. The Grand Alliance joined parties that agreed on little beyond removing the Prime Minister: the Congress (O) of K. Kamaraj and Morarji Desai, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the Swatantra Party and the Samyukta Socialist Party divided constituencies among themselves in many states but carried no common programme and no common leader. The alliance's arithmetic assumed that anti-Congress (R) votes would pool. The delinked plebiscite defeated that assumption, because voters choosing a Prime Minister were not summing grievances against a party; they were selecting between Indira Gandhi and a committee. When the results came, the committee's components were reduced to fragments, and the fragments could not even combine into a plausible alternative government.

## Garibi Hatao: A Slogan Built to Replace a Machine

Garibi Hatao deserves reading as constitutional rhetoric and not only as campaign decoration. The slogan promised the removal of poverty by a state that the preceding eighteen months had taught voters to see as an instrument the Prime Minister was prepared to use against entrenched property. [The 1969 bank nationalisation](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/), executed by ordinance in July 1969 and defended through the Supreme Court's R. C. Cooper judgment of February 1970, had established the pattern: a single executive decision, a legislative consolidation, and a public argument that institutional vetoes were shelters for privilege. The privy purse abolition attempt of September 1970 extended the pattern to the former princes and failed, first in the Rajya Sabha and then in the Supreme Court's Madhavrao Scindia judgment of 15 December 1970. The failure was folded into the campaign as evidence. A leader blocked twice by institutional vetoes asked the electorate for the numbers to override them, and Garibi Hatao named the purpose for which the numbers would be used.

The slogan's vagueness was functional. Garibi Hatao specified no programme, no timetable and no fiscal arithmetic, and critics inside and outside the Congress noted the absence at the time. What the slogan specified was an alignment: the poor, the landless labourer, the small tenant, the unemployed graduate and the minority voter were invited to identify the same obstacle, entrenched privilege guarded by courts, princes, bank owners and party bosses, and the same instrument, a Prime Minister with an unencumbered majority. Malhotra records that the slogan was chosen at the top of the Congress (R) and tested on the campaign trail until it displaced every other message the party carried. Its economy was the point. A delinked plebiscite needs a sentence a first time voter in a Bihar village and a mill worker in Bombay can repeat identically, and Garibi Hatao was that sentence.

The election's opponents understood the danger and misread its source. Congress (O) leaders such as Morarji Desai argued that the slogan was a promise no government could redeem and that the electorate would punish its inevitable failure. That argument treated Garibi Hatao as a policy pledge to be audited later. The slogan's real function was present tense and constitutive: it defined the majority being assembled as a majority for a purpose, so that later uses of that majority, including constitutional amendments against property rights and princely privilege, could claim a specific electoral warrant. When the Twenty-Fourth, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Sixth Amendments moved through Parliament between November 1971 and April 1972, the government defended each one in the vocabulary of the mandate. The slogan had been written, in effect, as the amendments' preamble, a year before the amendments were drafted.

There is a harder edge to the same point, and the article does not soften it. A mandate defined by a slogan rather than a programme is a mandate whose content the leader alone interprets. The 352 members elected in March 1971 owed their seats, in the main, to Indira Gandhi's campaign rather than to local standing, and a parliamentary party assembled that way does not bargain with its leader. The Congress Parliamentary Party of the Fifth Lok Sabha was, by the testimony of participants recorded in Malhotra's biography and in Austin's interviews, the most leader dependent majority since independence. That dependence is the transmission belt of the whole sequence: it is what converted an electoral verdict into freedom of manoeuvre in a war cabinet in December 1971 and into two thirds majorities for constitutional amendment in the same season. The mandate did not merely precede the war and the amendments. Its internal structure, plebiscitary and personal, determined what both could be.

The campaign's organisation matched its rhetoric in one decisive respect: it was run as a national headquarters operation rather than as a federation of state campaigns. Candidate selection in the Congress (R) was centralised to a degree the undivided Congress had never attempted, with the Prime Minister's secretariat and a small circle of loyalists vetting lists that state units submitted, and with sitting members denied renomination where their loyalty to the organisation had outweighed their loyalty to the leader. The party fought the election with a fraction of the funds and functionaries the Congress (O) disposed of, and compensated with the leader's tour, the state broadcasting system that carried her speeches, and electoral understandings with the Communist Party of India and regional formations such as the DMK, which aligned the anti Grand Alliance vote in state after state. The Election Commission's report records the understandings' yield in the results tables: in constituency after constituency the alliance arithmetic that the syndicate had relied on since 1967, the pooling of anti Congress votes behind a single challenger, failed because the challenger faced a governing alliance broader than his own.

The slogan's translation across India's languages carried the same centralisation in miniature. Garibi Hatao was coined in Hindi, the language of the heartland the Congress (R) most needed to recover, and it travelled without alteration into the party's campaigning in the south and east, transliterated on posters from Madras to Calcutta. A slogan that does not translate its promise into regional idioms is making a claim about the nation it addresses: that the poor of Tamil Nadu and the poor of Bihar face one obstacle and require one instrument, and that the instrument speaks from New Delhi. Critics in the regional parties named the implication at the time and voted against it where they could; the DMK, governing Tamil Nadu and allied to the Congress (R) for the poll, accepted the alliance while guarding its own state idiom, a reservation that foreshadowed the federal frictions of the following years. The slogan's career after the election, painted on state government hoardings and invoked in every budget speech of the Parliament it elected, completed its transformation from campaign cry into the mandate's standing title deed.

## The March Verdict: 352 Seats and a Rewritten Arithmetic

Polling for the Fifth Lok Sabha ran from 1 to 10 March 1971 across 518 constituencies, before an electorate of roughly 274 million names, and the Election Commission's Statistical Report of 1972 records a turnout of 55.27 percent. The Congress (R) won 352 seats on 43.68 percent of the vote. The rest of the house scattered: the Communist Party of India (Marxist) took 25 seats, the Communist Party of India 23, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam 23, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh 22, and the Congress (O), the organisation that had expelled the Prime Minister sixteen months earlier, collapsed to 16 seats on 10.43 percent of the vote. The Swatantra Party, second largest in the Fourth Lok Sabha, fell to 8. The report's tables settle the comparison that frames this article: 352 seats exceeded the tally of the undivided Congress in 1967 and rivalled the majorities of the Nehru era, and it was won by a party that had existed, as a separate formation, for barely a year.

The verdict closed the question that [the 1969 split's structural pivot](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/) had opened. Between November 1969 and March 1971, Indian politics had contained two plausible claimants to the Congress inheritance: the organisation, which held the party's name in the Election Commission's initial reckoning, its state units and its bosses, and the Prime Minister, who held office, the government's legislative initiative and the public stage. The election adjudicated the inheritance. The Congress (O) retained the machine and lost the country; the Congress (R) lost the machine and won the country so completely that the qualifying letter after its name became a courtesy rather than a necessity. K. Kamaraj, the former Congress president and the syndicate's most respected figure, was defeated in his own contest in Tamil Nadu, where the DMK alliance with the Congress (R) swept the state. An organisational leadership that had selected two Prime Ministers in 1964 and 1966 could not deliver its own president's seat.

The regional pattern inside the verdict mattered as much as the total. In West Bengal, where the refugee emergency would shortly land, the Congress (R) and its allies displaced the United Front order that had governed the state through its years of instability, and the Communist Party of India (Marxist) held its strongholds without converting them into a governing claim. In Tamil Nadu the alliance with the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam delivered the state and destroyed Kamaraj personally. In Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Madhya Pradesh, the heartland states whose seats decide Indian majorities, the Congress (R) swept constituencies it had lost in 1967 to the Jana Sangh and the socialists, on the strength of a campaign that had bypassed the district organisations in favour of the leader's tour. The Election Commission's constituency tables show the recovery's breadth: the party's vote share rose in nearly every state, including states where its organisation had been seized entire by the Congress (O). A majority assembled against the party machine, in the machine's own territories, was a new kind of fact in Indian politics, and state level leaders of every party read it as such.

The verdict also redrew the federal map in ways the war would shortly exploit. Because the poll was delinked, state governments of several complexions survived the national landslide, including ministries the Congress (R) did not control. Yet the size of the Lok Sabha majority changed the terms on which every state government dealt with New Delhi. A Prime Minister with 352 seats, a two thirds capacity for constitutional amendment when her allies were counted, and a demonstrated ability to win a midterm poll at will held instruments over state politics that no predecessor since Nehru's first years had possessed. The dismissals, defections and chief ministerial changes of the following years all ran downhill from March 1971. The majority was not only a number in the Lok Sabha. It was a standing threat and a standing promise in every state capital, and it was assembled before the first refugee crossed the border, which is why the sequence's second link met a state already concentrated enough to absorb it.

The majority's first parliamentary season showed the uses to which it would be put. Indira Gandhi was sworn in for her second term on 18 March 1971, and the new Lok Sabha's opening sessions carried the government's programme in the mandate's own vocabulary: the President's address promised the removal of poverty as the state's organising task, and the budget of 1971 raised taxes on wealth and expenditure in the programme's name. The season's hardest measure passed with little of the resistance the old Parliament would have mounted. The Maintenance of Internal Security Act, enacted in the summer of 1971, restored and widened the preventive detention powers that had lapsed with the old Preventive Detention Act in 1969, authorising detention without trial on grounds that included the security of India and the maintenance of public order. The Act was defended as a wartime necessity in waiting, and the war came within six months to validate the description; it remained on the statute book to become, four years later, the Emergency's principal instrument of arrest. The sequence's links are sometimes legal as well as political: the detention law of the mandate's first season and the detention regime of the Emergency are one statute, carried across the decade by the majority of March 1971.

## Ten Million Refugees: The Crisis That Entered Indian Politics

On the night of 25 March 1971, a fortnight after the Indian count concluded, the Pakistan Army launched Operation Searchlight in East Pakistan, striking Dhaka University, the Rajarbagh police lines and Hindu neighbourhoods of the old city, arresting Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and driving the elected majority of Pakistan into resistance and flight. The Awami League had won 160 of East Pakistan's 162 National Assembly seats in December 1970, an absolute majority for all Pakistan, and the crackdown annulled that verdict by force. What reached India was people: through the spring and summer of 1971 the refugee flow across the West Bengal, Tripura, Assam and Meghalaya borders grew until Indian government statements, and the relief administration's own registers, placed the total near ten million by November 1971. The Lok Sabha debates of 1971 return to the figure repeatedly, because the figure was the argument: a burden of that size, carried by some of India's poorest states, converted a Pakistani internal repression into an Indian fiscal, epidemiological and political emergency.

The emergency had a constitutional geography that Indian politics had carried since 1947. The refugees crossed borders drawn by Partition, into states whose own politics had been formed by the earlier migration, and [the post-Partition citizenship and security architecture](/2019/01/08/partition-constitutional-consequences-india/) supplied both the administrative categories through which the newcomers were processed and the communal anxieties their arrival stirred. West Bengal's government under President's rule, Tripura's transformation into a territory where refugees approached the local population in number, and the cholera outbreaks in the camps of May and June 1971 all appear in the parliamentary record as matters of state capacity rather than charity. Indira Gandhi toured the camps in the border states in the spring of 1971 and stated the government's position in terms that hardened across the year: the refugees were Pakistan's responsibility, they would return to a political settlement that guaranteed their safety, and India would not absorb them permanently. The insistence that return required political change inside East Pakistan was, in diplomatic language, a statement that the status quo in Dhaka could not survive.

The fiscal pressure was quantified in public. Indian estimates presented to Parliament and to foreign governments during 1971 placed the cost of relief in the hundreds of crores of rupees, against international contributions that covered only a fraction of the outlay, and the Finance Ministry's spokesmen treated the gap as a standing charge on a budget already committed to the Garibi Hatao programme's promises. The pressure cut both ways politically. War would be expensive beyond any relief bill, and the government's own economists said so. Drift was also expensive, and it compounded monthly, in cholera, in border district resentments and in the militarisation of the camps, where the Mukti Bahini's guerrilla formations trained on Indian soil with Indian knowledge. By the autumn of 1971 the choice before the cabinet was not between war and peace but between a war at a chosen moment and an emergency without a terminus.

The refugees were also becoming soldiers, and that transformation tightened the timetable. The Mukti Bahini, built from Bengali regulars of the East Bengal Regiment and the East Pakistan Rifles who had revolted in March, from Awami League volunteers and from student cadres, trained through 1971 in camps on Indian soil under Indian instructors, and its guerrilla campaign inside East Pakistan escalated from ambushes on communications in the monsoon to coordinated operations against river craft, bridges and cantonments by the autumn. On 21 November 1971 Indian and Mukti Bahini forces were placed under a joint command for the eastern theatre, a formal acknowledgement of the partnership the training camps had embodied for months. Pakistani armour clashed with Indian units at Garibpur on 21 November and at Boyra in the air the following day, engagements the Indian government described as defensive and the Pakistani government described as invasion. The distinction between a supported insurgency and an undeclared war had narrowed, by late November, to the width of a diplomatic formula, and both governments knew it. The emergency's logic had carried India to the edge of the war the cabinet had scheduled for December, with the refugees still in their camps and the relief bill still compounding.

The relief operation that carried the refugees through 1971 was itself an administrative achievement with political consequences. The Government of India ran the camps through the state governments and a dedicated rehabilitation machinery, registering refugees, issuing ration entitlements and organising the cholera and smallpox inoculation drives that held camp mortality far below the levels the first panic had predicted. The United Nations designated Prince Sadruddin Aga Khan, the High Commissioner for Refugees, as the focal point for international assistance, and the appeals that followed raised contributions that Indian statements to Parliament contrasted, pointedly, with the bills the Indian taxpayer was meeting. The contrast was a diplomatic instrument: every parliamentary answer on relief costs restated the government's central claim, that India was paying in cash and in stability for a crisis made by Pakistan's generals and tolerated by Pakistan's patrons. By the autumn the camps had become a standing exhibit in the government's case for war, visited by foreign correspondents, parliamentarians and the Secretary General's representatives, and their existence disciplined Indian rhetoric as much as it armed it: a government that had fed ten million people for eight months could not be accused of manufacturing the emergency it proposed to end.

Indira Gandhi spent October and November 1971 testing whether diplomacy could produce the terminus. Her tour of Western capitals, Brussels, Vienna, London, Washington, Paris and Bonn between 24 October and mid November, sought support for a political settlement in East Pakistan and met courtesy without commitment. The Washington meeting with President Richard Nixon on 4 and 5 November 1971 was the coldest of the tour: Nixon and his National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger regarded Pakistan as the indispensable channel to China and the refugee crisis as an Indian lever, and the conversation produced no movement toward pressuring President Yahya Khan. Raghavan's reconstruction of the tour, drawn from the American, British and Indian records, shows a Prime Minister confirming a conclusion she had carried since the summer: no external power would return the refugees, and the political room her March mandate had created would have to be spent at home. She returned to New Delhi on 13 November 1971 with the diplomatic option documented as exhausted, a sequence of meetings that later served, in the December debates, as the government's evidence that war had been the residual choice rather than the preferred one.

## The Indo-Soviet Treaty and the Foreign Policy Indira Inherited

The diplomatic preparation for December was signed on 9 August 1971 in New Delhi, when External Affairs Minister Swaran Singh and Soviet Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko concluded the Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation between India and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. The treaty's operative provision, its ninth article, committed each party, in the event of an attack on the other or a threat of one, to enter immediately into mutual consultations to remove the threat and take appropriate effective measures for peace and security. The language stopped short of automatic military assistance and was drafted to stop there. What the treaty actually transferred was risk: after August 1971, any Pakistani or American calculation about a war in Bengal had to price in Soviet behaviour, and any Chinese calculation had to price in the Soviet Union's treaty interest on China's southern flank. The treaty ran for twenty years and was registered and published, a public instrument whose publicity was part of its function.

The instrument marked a departure from the framework India's foreign policy had inherited. [The Nehruvian-era foreign-policy framework](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) had treated nonalignment as a discipline of refusal, keeping both blocs at equal distance so that India's voice carried weight in both. Indira Gandhi's August 1971 signature exchanged a measure of that equidistance for insurance, and the exchange shows the logic that [the foreign-as-domestic-politics framework](/2019/03/04/nehru-foreign-policy-domestic-politics/) identifies in the earlier era working in reverse: a foreign commitment whose primary audience and primary function were calculations about a domestic emergency. The treaty was not sought to change India's world role. It was sought to make a domestic decision, intervention in East Pakistan, survivable against the three external reactions the cabinet most feared: American pressure, Chinese intervention and Pakistani escalation in the west.

The American reaction was the one the treaty most plainly answered. The Nixon administration's opening to China, dramatised by Kissinger's secret journey to Beijing through Pakistan on 9 to 11 July 1971, had made Pakistan a protected intermediary in Washington's diplomacy. American policy during 1971, as the record assembled by Raghavan and by the Blood Telegram dissent of the American consulate in Dhaka attests, subordinated the East Pakistan repression to that protection. The treaty told Washington that pressure on India would operate against a Soviet counterweight rather than against an isolated government. When war came in December, the Soviet Union vetoed American sponsored ceasefire resolutions in the Security Council on 4 and 5 December 1971 that would have frozen Pakistani authority in the east, and Soviet naval units moved toward the Indian Ocean as the American carrier Enterprise entered the Bay of Bengal. The insurance had been bought in August and was collected in December, on the schedule the cabinet papers of November and December 1971, partially declassified in 2008 and preserved at the National Archives of India, show the government working to protect.

China was the treaty's second addressee. India had fought China in the Himalayan autumn of 1962, and every Indian war plan for Pakistan carried the standing question of Chinese movement on the northern border, whether as diversion, as pressure on the passes or as occupation of disputed ground in support of an ally. Beijing's conduct through 1971 was rhetorically violent on Pakistan's behalf and operationally inert: Zhou Enlai's April 1971 message to Yahya Khan promised support against Indian aggression, Chinese statements through the year condemned Indian interference, and yet no Chinese formation moved when the war came, and the passes were closing with snow in any case. Raghavan's account, working the Chinese evidence available, treats Beijing's restraint as overdetermined: the Lin Biao affair of September 1971 had convulsed the Chinese leadership, the Soviet divisions on China's northern frontier priced any southern adventure, and the treaty had added a consultation commitment that a Chinese move might trigger. The Indian cabinet's autumn decision to wait for winter converted the Himalayan calendar into a Chinese deterrent of its own. The treaty and the snow arrived together in December, and the northern border stayed silent through the fourteen days, a silence the war's planners had purchased with both instruments and which the domestic audience, remembering 1962, registered as part of the victory's completeness.

### Why did the United States send the Enterprise into the Bay of Bengal?

President Nixon ordered Task Force 74, around the carrier Enterprise, into the Bay of Bengal on 10 December 1971 to signal resolve in New Delhi, deter an Indian move against West Pakistan and reassure Islamabad and Beijing. It arrived too late to affect the eastern campaign and was shadowed by Soviet units, so its effect was demonstrative rather than military.

The treaty also disciplined India's own timetable. General S. H. F. J. Manekshaw, the Chief of Army Staff, had told the cabinet in April 1971, in the account Malhotra and the memoir literature preserve, that an immediate offensive would fail in the monsoon, against unready logistics and with the Himalayan passes open to Chinese movement. The government's acceptance of that military calendar, intervention deferred to winter when the passes snowed shut and the eastern terrain dried, is sometimes read as caution imposed on political impatience. The unified sequence reads it the other way. A government holding a fresh two thirds mandate could afford to wait seven months for its army's season; a government living on borrowed parliamentary support could not have sustained the refugee emergency, the treaty negotiation and the military preparation across that interval without its majority eroding. The mandate's durability is what made patience possible, and patience is what made the December campaign a fourteen day operation instead of a monsoon entanglement.

## The War Decision: Cabinets, Calendars and the Uses of a Majority

The decision for war was taken in instalments, and the instalments are documented. The cabinet papers of November and December 1971, declassified in part in 2008 and preserved at the National Archives of India, record a government managing three calendars at once: the military calendar, which required dry ground in Bengal and snow in the Himalayan passes; the diplomatic calendar, which required the treaty signed, the Western capitals visited and the Security Council's likely arithmetic counted; and the refugee calendar, which measured the emergency in cholera returns, camp expenditure and border district tension. The under-cited character of these papers matters for the historiography. Raghavan's global history works the diplomatic archives of five countries; the Indian cabinet record, partial as it is, shows the decision from the chair where the three calendars were reconciled, and at that chair the March mandate was the enabling fact. Each instalment of the decision, the April acceptance of Manekshaw's timetable, the August treaty, the autumn build up, the November joint command, was politically expensive, and a government without a secure majority would have paid for any one of them with its survival.

The April instalment deserves the closest reading, because it fixed the year's shape. When the refugee flow began, voices in the cabinet and the press demanded immediate intervention, and Manekshaw's refusal, delivered to the cabinet in April 1971 in the presence of the Prime Minister, was absolute: the army was unprepared, the monsoon was coming, the harvest and the railways were committed, and a war begun in April would be fought on Pakistan's terms. The general later recalled offering his resignation if the government insisted. The government did not insist. Indira Gandhi absorbed the political cost of seven months of apparent passivity, against opposition charges that she had missed the moment, because the majority of March made the absorption affordable and because the interval could be used: for the treaty, for the stockpiling of the eastern theatre, for the Mukti Bahini's training and for the diplomatic tour that documented the exhaustion of alternatives. The sequence's logic is visible nowhere more plainly than here. The mandate did not push the government toward war. It gave the government the time in which a winnable war could be prepared, and time, in 1971, was the scarcest strategic resource India possessed.

The December instalment closed the account. When Pakistani aircraft struck Indian airfields on 3 December, the government's war aims, as stated to Parliament in the debates of December 1971, were limited and eastern: the liberation of Bangladesh, the return of the refugees and the defence of the western frontier without annexationist ambition. Limited aims were a political choice as much as a military one. They kept the Soviet insurance valid, denied Washington the spectacle of Indian expansion that might have triggered intervention, and matched the war to the mandate's own rhetoric, which had promised the removal of a burden rather than the acquisition of territory. The cabinet papers and the debate record together show a government that understood its war as the middle link of a domestic sequence, fought to restore the conditions in which the March promises could be pursued, and concluded, with the unilateral ceasefire of 17 December, the moment its political object was achieved. Wars entered for limited aims and ended on schedule are rare in the twentieth century's record. This one was ended on schedule because its aims had been defined by a political sequence whose next link, the amendment cycle, was already waiting in the parliamentary timetable.

## The Instrument: A Professional Army Under a Political Mandate

The war's executability rested on an institution the sequence did not create but decisively used: a professional military leadership whose standing had been rebuilt from the wreckage of 1962. The service chiefs of 1971, General S. H. F. J. Manekshaw at the Army, Admiral S. M. Nanda at the Navy and Air Chief Marshal P. C. Lal at the Air Force, were officers of a generation that had absorbed the China war's lesson, that political direction without military candour produces disaster, and Manekshaw's April confrontation with the cabinet was the lesson applied: a chief who would tell the Prime Minister that her preferred timetable meant defeat, and offer his resignation on the point, was exercising the professional autonomy the post 1962 reforms had promised. The government's acceptance of his terms, and its protection of his planning interval against press and parliamentary impatience, closed the compact. In return the military delivered a campaign designed to political specification: limited aims, a short duration, an eastern decision and a western holding action, concluded before the international system could impose a halt.

The compact had a domestic-political dimension that the separated histories understate. Civil military trust of the 1971 kind is not produced by constitutions; it is produced by governments strong enough to wait and generals confident enough to speak. The mandate supplied the government's side of the bargain. Aurora's eastern headquarters planned through the monsoon on a political guarantee that the offensive would not be ordered prematurely for presentational reasons and would not be halted prematurely for diplomatic ones, and the guarantee held on both points until the surrender made halting moot. The Indian state's performance in December 1971, the synchronisation of diplomacy, refugee administration, military preparation and parliamentary management across nine months, is the strongest single exhibit for the unified sequence's premise: such synchronisation is what a concentrated mandate is for, and it is precisely the capacity that the fragmented parliament of 1969 to 1970 could not have sustained and that the plebiscitary verdict of March 1971 was, in effect, designed to restore.

The compact's terms were visible in the campaign's restraint as much as in its speed. The eastern offensive was ordered to accept the surrender of a capital rather than the occupation of a country, to protect the Bengali population whose state was being created, and to leave the western frontier a theatre of defence and limited riposte rather than conquest, and each restraint was a political instruction transmitted through a military chain that trusted its source. Armies that doubt their governments fight for ground. This army fought for a timetable and a ceremony, because the government it served had defined the war's object as a political condition, the existence of Bangladesh and the return of its refugees, and had held its own nerve through nine months of preparation. The surrender at Dhaka, when it came on the campaign's thirteenth day, therefore arrived as the fulfilment of a stated design rather than as a fortune of arms, and the domestic reception read it exactly so.

## Fourteen Days in December: The War in the East

Pakistan opened the formal war. On the evening of 3 December 1971 the Pakistan Air Force struck Indian airfields across the western frontier in Operation Chengiz Khan, and Indira Gandhi broadcast to the nation near midnight that Pakistan had declared war on India, announcing a state of emergency and the government's resolve to repel aggression. In the east, the Indian Eastern Command under Lieutenant General Jagjit Singh Aurora, operating alongside the Mukti Bahini and the joint command arrangements concluded in the autumn, advanced on Dhaka in converging columns that bypassed fortified towns and raced for the river crossings and the capital. The campaign's design, set by Aurora's headquarters and supplied through the autumn build up, traded territorial completeness for speed, and speed for the diplomatic clock: every day of war increased the chance of an imposed ceasefire that would strand the campaign short of Dhaka. The eastern garrison of Pakistan, cut off by air and sea, its reinforcement routes closed and its position among a hostile population untenable, collapsed toward the capital in twelve days.

India recognised Bangladesh on 6 December 1971, three days into the war, with Indira Gandhi informing the Lok Sabha that the Government of India had decided to grant recognition to the Government of Bangladesh, an announcement the December debates record as the war's political premise stated aloud: the objective in the east was not territory but the conversion of the refugee producing regime into a sovereign state that could receive its people back. The recognition also fixed the war's legal and moral architecture for the domestic audience. India fought, in the government's account to Parliament, beside a recognised government against an occupying army, and the account held because the March mandate's holder spoke for a Parliament that endorsed the recognition without division.

### When did India recognise Bangladesh?

India recognised Bangladesh on 6 December 1971, three days after Pakistan's air strikes opened the war, when Indira Gandhi announced the decision in the Lok Sabha. Recognition converted the eastern campaign's objective into a political fact: the refugees' return had a sovereign destination, and India's war aim was declared as liberation rather than territorial gain.

On 16 December 1971, at the Ramna Race Course in Dhaka, Lieutenant General A. A. K. Niazi signed the instrument of surrender before Lieutenant General Aurora, and the Eastern Command of Pakistan laid down its arms. Some 93,000 Pakistani personnel passed into Indian custody as prisoners of war, the largest military surrender since the Second World War, a figure the subsequent diplomatic record and the Simla negotiations treated as India's principal bargaining asset. The western front, where the war's territorial fighting had been heavier and less decisive, stopped with the ceasefire India declared unilaterally on 17 December, effective that evening, after the eastern objective was complete. Fourteen days separated Chengiz Khan from the ceasefire, and the war's asymmetry was the point the domestic audience absorbed: total victory in the east, restraint in the west, and a Prime Minister who had chosen both.

### Who signed the instrument of surrender at Dhaka?

Lieutenant General Amir Abdullah Khan Niazi, commanding Pakistan's Eastern Command, signed the instrument of surrender at Dhaka's Ramna Race Course on 16 December 1971, surrendering to Lieutenant General Jagjit Singh Aurora of India's Eastern Command. The ceremony ended organised Pakistani resistance in the east and placed some 93,000 Pakistani personnel in Indian custody as prisoners of war.

The war's other theatres supplied the completeness the reception required. In the west, Indian forces fought the war's heaviest armour battles in the Shakargarh salient and held the line in Rajasthan and Kashmir against a Pakistani offensive that gained little ground at high cost. At sea, the Indian Navy's missile boats struck Karachi harbour in Operation Trident on the night of 4 December 1971 and again in Operation Python on 8 December, the carrier Vikrant's aircraft worked over East Pakistan's ports from the Bay of Bengal, and the Pakistani submarine Ghazi sank off Visakhapatnam on the night of 3 to 4 December on its mission against the Vikrant. The naval war mattered politically out of proportion to its tonnage: it demonstrated that the victory was not a local accident of the eastern terrain but a general superiority, achieved by a state that had planned the campaign across all three services through the autumn. When the ceasefire took effect on 17 December, India held roughly 15,000 square kilometres of Pakistani territory in the west against a few hundred square kilometres of its own soil in Pakistani hands, an exchange balance the Simla negotiations would later liquidate, and the asymmetry entered the domestic memory alongside the Dhaka ceremony.

The political reception of the victory in India exceeded anything the March mandate had conferred. Indira Gandhi was felicitated in Parliament by opposition leaders who had spent the year opposing her; Atal Bihari Vajpayee of the Jana Sangh praised her leadership in terms his party's campaign had not anticipated, and the press across the political spectrum treated the Dhaka surrender as a national restoration after the humiliation of 1962. The comparison with 1962 was made everywhere and was available to everyone, because the same political generation that had watched the Himalayan defeat had watched a campaign planned, supplied and concluded on its own government's timetable. The reception matters more to this article's argument than the campaign itself, for reasons the complication section states plainly: legitimation is produced by reception, not by operations. What December 1971 deposited in Indira Gandhi's account was not merely popularity. It was a presumption, shared well beyond her party, that her judgment in a crisis had been vindicated against every institution that had doubted it, the syndicate in 1969, the Supreme Court in 1970 and the American presidency in 1971. The amendment cycle that bracketed the war drew on exactly that presumption.

## Victory as Legitimation: The Amendment Cycle of 1971 and 1972

The amendment cycle had a legal problem at its centre, and the problem had a name: Golaknath. In I. C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967, a divided Supreme Court had held that Parliament could not amend the fundamental rights, freezing the constitutional text against the redistributive legislation the Congress left regarded as its programme. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment, passed by both houses in the autumn of 1971 and assented to on 5 November 1971, answered Golaknath directly: it amended the amending article to declare that Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution including Part III, it excluded constitutional amendments from the definition of law that fundamental rights could void, and it obliged the President to assent to amendment bills. Austin's account in Working a Democratic Constitution treats the Twenty-Fourth as the hinge of the era, the moment the post 1967 Parliament reclaimed constituent authority from the Court. The hinge was installed on the mandate's arithmetic: the amendment required a majority of total membership and two thirds of those present and voting, numbers the March verdict had placed at the government's disposal.

The Twenty-Sixth Amendment closed the account the campaign had opened with the princes. [The post-1971 amendment cycle](/2019/04/06/privy-purse-abolition-1971-india/) reached its symbolic peak on 28 December 1971, twelve days after the Dhaka surrender, when the Constitution (Twenty-Sixth Amendment) Act received presidential assent: the articles guaranteeing privy purses and princely privileges were omitted from the Constitution, a new provision barred recognition of rulers, and the payments, roughly four crore rupees a year charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, ended. The parliamentary arithmetic measures the war's contribution. The abolition bill of September 1970 had failed in the Rajya Sabha by a fraction of a vote and its executive substitute had been voided by the Supreme Court in Madhavrao Scindia on 15 December 1970. The December 1971 bill passed the Lok Sabha by 381 votes to 6 and the Rajya Sabha by 167 votes to 7. The sums involved were fiscally trivial, as the amendment's critics and defenders both conceded; the four crore rupees were never the substance. The substance was that an obligation written into the integration settlements of 1947 to 1949, defended by the Court as a constitutional guarantee, was extinguished by the amendment power within a year of the Court's defence, and the extinguishing majority was the mandate's, refreshed by the victory's reception.

The Twenty-Fifth Amendment, assented to on 20 April 1972, completed the cycle's architecture. It replaced the word compensation in the property article with the word amount, weakening judicial review of acquisition terms, and it inserted Article 31C, which provided that laws giving effect to the directive principles on equitable distribution of material resources could not be voided for inconsistency with the rights to equality, freedom and property. Read together, the three amendments form a single instrument: the Twenty-Fourth restored the amending power, the Twenty-Sixth demonstrated it against the oldest property guarantee in the Constitution, and the Twenty-Fifth aimed it at the property regime itself. None of the three was a war measure. All three were defended in debate, and received in the press, inside the political atmosphere the war had created, in which the government's democratic warrant had been certified by arms and the institutional objections of 1967 to 1970 had been marked as the scruples of a defeated order. Austin's constitutional history is careful on the mechanism: the amendments were legally the work of the majority, and politically the work of the majority's refreshed authority. The sequence's claim is precisely that the refreshment, not the majority alone, is what the war supplied.

A caution belongs inside the argument rather than against it. The amendment cycle would likely have been attempted on the mandate alone; the Twenty-Fourth Amendment's November date proves the intention predated the victory. What the victory changed was the cycle's cost. Contesting the amendments judicially and politically became, in the months after Dhaka, an act against the government that had won the war, and the opposition parties, several of whose leaders had felicitated the Prime Minister in December, could not rebuild a veto coalition in that atmosphere. The Kesavananda Bharati challenge to the Twenty-Fourth and Twenty-Fifth Amendments was argued through 1972 and decided on 24 April 1973, and even its famous compromise, the basic structure doctrine, was delivered by a court working in the long shadow of the surrender ceremony. Legitimation, in the sequence's sense, is measurable in that shadow: in the interval between what institutions were willing to attempt in 1970 and what they were willing to attempt in 1972.

## The Habit of Command: States and Courts After the Victory

The centralisation had a federal register as well as a constitutional one. In March 1972, a year after the Lok Sabha landslide, elections were held to state assemblies across the country, and the Congress won the overwhelming majority of them, carrying fourteen of the sixteen states that went to the polls in the standard count and seating ministries that owed their existence to the Prime Minister's campaign rather than to provincial organisation. The pattern of the campaigns repeated the delinked plebiscite in reverse: state contests were fought on the national leader's authority, and the chief ministers who emerged were installed, and thereafter removable, by New Delhi's preference rather than by legislature party election in the older Congress manner. The practice converted state governments into administrative extensions of the mandate, and it is the institutional habit that [Indira's four-phase office-rebuilding trajectory](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/) places at the centre of the era: the Prime Minister's office rebuilt as the single source of political authority in the Union and, through the chief ministerial appointment power, in the states as well.

### What happened in the 1972 state assembly elections?

The March 1972 assembly elections extended the verdict into the states: the Congress won fourteen of the sixteen assemblies polled, and the new chief ministers owed their offices to Indira Gandhi's campaign and to New Delhi's selection. The results converted the 1971 mandate into federal control and normalised the practice of chief ministers holding office at the Prime Minister's pleasure.

The judiciary met the same habit in April 1973. The Kesavananda Bharati judgment of 24 April 1973 upheld the Twenty-Fourth Amendment and much of the Twenty-Fifth while holding, by the narrowest majority of a thirteen judge bench, that Parliament's amending power did not extend to the Constitution's basic structure. On the following day, 25 April 1973, the government appointed Justice A. N. Ray as Chief Justice of India, superseding three senior judges, Justices J. M. Shelat, K. S. Hegde and A. N. Grover, who had decided against the government's position in Kesavananda and who resigned in protest. Austin treats the supersession as the executive's answer to the basic structure doctrine: unable to overturn the doctrine, the government signalled that judicial advancement ran through acceptability to the executive. The episode belongs to the sequence because its enabling condition was the post victory atmosphere of 1972 and 1973, in which the costs of confronting the mandate's holder had been repriced for every institution, and because it normalised a doctrine of the committed judiciary that the Emergency would later invoke by name.

The states and the Court were the two institutional fields in which the Congress System had historically dispersed power away from the Prime Minister. By the spring of 1973, both fields had been entered by the centre's authority in forms the pre 1971 order would not have sustained: chief ministers as nominees, and a Chief Justice appointed over the heads of judges who had ruled against the government. Neither development required the Emergency's machinery. Both were accomplished under the ordinary Constitution by a government whose warrant, electoral and military, stood unchallenged. That is the sequence's darkest implication, and the article states it without varnish: the centralisation that the Emergency codified in 1975 was not improvised in 1975. It was assembled, piece by piece and with parliamentary and public sanction, out of the mandate and the victory of 1971.

## The Opposition in the Year of the Sequence

The sequence had a witness whose testimony certifies it: the opposition. The parties that had combined in the Grand Alliance to remove Indira Gandhi in March 1971 spent December 1971 competing to felicitate her, and the competition was not hypocrisy but repricing. The Jana Sangh's Atal Bihari Vajpayee, whose party's parliamentary strength the March verdict had cut to 22 seats, praised the Prime Minister's leadership of the war in terms that entered the record of the December debates. The Congress (O), reduced to 16 seats and stripped of Kamaraj's personal seat, could offer no alternative account of the year that any audience would hear. The socialist parties were divided between support for the Bangladesh cause, which their traditions demanded, and resentment of the Prime Minister's accumulation, which their traditions also demanded, and the division paralysed them. Only the Communist Party of India (Marxist), holding its Bengal and Kerala strongholds, maintained a coherent critical position, attacking the government's economic record while supporting the war, and its coherence was purchased by abstention from the national argument.

The repricing had a mechanical cause that the opposition understood precisely. Indian oppositions recover between elections, in the state assemblies, in by-elections, in the slow corrosion of a government's promises. The sequence closed each recovery route in turn. The March verdict had taken the Lok Sabha for five years. The December victory had taken the nation's gratitude. The March 1972 assembly elections took the states, the traditional nursery of opposition comebacks, and installed chief ministers who were the Prime Minister's nominees. A national opposition requires either a parliament, a province or a public mood, and by the spring of 1972 the opposition held none of the three in usable strength. The consequence for the sequence is structural: the amendment cycle and the supersession were accomplished not against resistance overcome but against resistance that had lost its instruments, which is why the division lists of the period show margins that read as acclamations. The Emergency's later ease in suppressing opposition had its precondition here, in an opposition that had already spent four years without a platform the constitution could amplify.

One opposition figure stands outside the pattern, and his position illuminates it. Jayaprakash Narayan, retired from party politics into the Sarvodaya movement, supported the Bangladesh cause and honoured the victory, and then, from 1973 onward, rebuilt an opposition where the parties could not, in the movements against corruption and price rise in Gujarat and Bihar. The JP movement's method is the negative image of the sequence: where Indira Gandhi had centralised politics into a plebiscitary mandate, Narayan decentralised protest into a moral campaign outside Parliament, aimed at the mandate's legitimacy rather than at its arithmetic. The collision of 1974 and 1975, between a Prime Minister whose authority was a sequence of conversions and a movement whose authority was a refusal of office, was set up by the completeness of the victory of 1971. An opposition with seats argues about legislation. An opposition without seats argues about legitimacy, and legitimacy was the ground on which the Allahabad judgment would fall.

## The Second Founding: Bangladesh's Own War

The sequence is an Indian political story, and it must not be allowed to absorb the agency of the people whose state it produced. Bangladesh was not created by the Indian Army in fourteen days. It was proclaimed by the Awami League's elected leadership after the crackdown, governed in exile from Calcutta by the Mujibnagar government under Acting President Syed Nazrul Islam and Prime Minister Tajuddin Ahmad, sworn in on 17 April 1971 at Baidyanathtala, and fought for through the year by the Mukti Bahini's sectors, whose guerrilla campaign bled the Pakistani garrison, destroyed the bridges and river craft on which its logistics depended and held the population's allegiance against a campaign of terror that killed, on estimates the Bangladeshi state maintains, in the millions. The Indian intervention of December converted a war of attrition the Bengalis were not losing into a decision they could not have reached alone before Pakistan's reinforcement or international intervention froze the lines. The relationship was symbiotic, and both parties to it knew the terms: Bangladesh supplied the cause, the intelligence, the terrain and the legitimacy of an elected government resisting annihilation; India supplied the sanctuary, the training, the artillery and, in December, the divisions.

Acknowledging the second founding strengthens rather than weakens the sequence's claim, because it clarifies what the Indian mandate actually purchased. Indira Gandhi's government did not manufacture the Bangladesh cause, and the March verdict had nothing to do with its existence: the cause was manufactured in Dhaka in December 1970, when East Pakistan voted, and in March 1971, when the Pakistan Army answered the vote. What the mandate purchased was India's capacity to act on the cause at the scale and speed the cause required, and to convert another people's liberation war into a settled state before the international system could partition the outcome. The counterfactual is instructive. The Bangladesh cause confronted with an India governed by a fragile coalition, an India unable to sign the August treaty or to wait out the monsoon or to risk the American reaction, would have produced a longer war, a frozen conflict or a negotiated autonomy short of independence, on the evidence of every comparable case in the period. The sequence's Indian links are the difference between the history that occurred and those alternatives, and the difference is the article's subject.

## The Settlement: Simla, the Prisoners and the Returning Refugees

The sequence's proof lies partly in what the victory was spent on. The refugees went home: through the first months of 1972 the camps emptied as the Indian government and the new Government of Bangladesh organised the repatriation of the roughly ten million people whose arrival had made the war necessary, and by the spring of 1972 the great majority had returned to their villages. The fiscal emergency that drift would have made permanent was liquidated in a season, and the liquidation was itself a political asset in the states that had borne the burden, above all West Bengal and Tripura. A government that had promised the refugees' return as the war's object could display the emptying camps as the promise kept, in the same idiom of delivery that Garibi Hatao had established and that the amendment cycle was converting into constitutional text.

The settlement also freed the man whose election had started the crisis. Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, arrested on the night of 25 March 1971 and held in a West Pakistani prison through the war under sentence proceedings that the Bangladeshi government in exile treated as a death watch, was released by President Bhutto on 8 January 1972 and returned to Dhaka on 10 January to head the government of the state India had recognised in December. Mujib's survival and return completed the refugee logic of the war: the political settlement whose absence Indira Gandhi had cited in every capital in October and November existed at last in the person of the leader East Pakistan had elected in December 1970. The Indian government's satisfaction in the outcome was genuine and was also, in the sequence's terms, a further deposit: the war's stated object, a Bangladesh governed by its elected leadership and able to receive its people, stood accomplished in public within five weeks of the surrender, and the accomplishment was credited in India to the decisions of the Indian government.

The repatriation that Simla framed was completed in stages that the sequence's domestic audience followed as a closing ledger. The Delhi Agreement of August 1973, concluded among India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, organised the simultaneous movement of the remaining prisoners, of Bengalis stranded in Pakistan and of Biharis who had opted for Pakistan, and the process concluded in April 1974 with the return of the final 195 prisoners after the tripartite understanding under which Bangladesh set aside the projected war crimes trials. Each stage lowered the war's temperature in Indian politics, and each was administered by the government the war had made as evidence of its method: undertakings given at Simla executed on schedule, an emergency liquidated, a settlement closed. By the time the last prisoners crossed the border at Wagah, the war had been converted entirely into memory and authority, which is the form in which the sequence's later links drew on it. The authority outlasted the memory of its conditions, and the decade's tragedy is contained in that disproportion.

The prisoners and the territory were spent at Simla. The Simla Agreement of 2 July 1972, signed by Indira Gandhi and President Zulfikar Ali Bhutto of Pakistan, converted the ceasefire line in Jammu and Kashmir into the Line of Control, committed both countries to bilateral settlement of their differences and set the framework within which India returned the western territories its army held and, in the following two years, repatriated the 93,000 prisoners without the war crimes tribunal for the eastern command's senior officers that Bangladesh had demanded and India had initially endorsed. Critics of the settlement, in India and in Bangladesh, have charged that Simla surrendered the victory's leverage cheaply. The charge belongs to the foreign policy ledger. In the domestic sequence, Simla's function was closure: it wound up the war as a finished event with a signed instrument, freeing the political capital the war had generated for the uses the government had already chosen, the state elections of 1972 behind it and the constitutional programme ahead. A war left diplomatically open would have taxed the mandate's authority month by month. Simla banked it.

## The Complication: A Victory India Received and Pakistan Delivered

The strongest counter to the unified-sequence reading must be stated at its strongest, because the reading survives it only in a precise form. The counter runs as follows. The December outcome was substantially made in Pakistan: by a military regime that refused the verdict of its own December 1970 election, by a crackdown that manufactured ten million refugees and united world opinion against Islamabad, by an eastern garrison deployed for internal repression rather than defence, by a high command in Rawalpindi that opened a western war it could not win on 3 December, and by the Yahya Khan government's diplomatic isolation, which left Pakistan dependent on an American patron whose support stopped at signals. On this account, associated with the Pakistani failure literature and acknowledged in part by Ganguly's structural treatment, the victory was delivered to India more than it was achieved by India, and a legitimation built on a delivered victory rests on an attribution error: the Indian public credited Indira Gandhi with an outcome that Pakistani decisions had substantially determined.

The article accepts the counter's military half and denies that it touches the claim. The distinction is between the war's causation and the war's reception, and the sequence runs on reception. Legitimation is not a medal awarded for operational brilliance; it is a political deposit created when a public attributes an outcome to its government. The Indian public of December 1971, and the parliamentary opposition that felicitated the Prime Minister, and the press that measured Dhaka against the 1962 humiliation, attributed the outcome to the government's choices: the patience of April, the treaty of August, the recognition of 6 December, the restraint of the unilateral ceasefire. Whether military historians apportion the causation differently, and Raghavan's reconstruction apportions a large share to Pakistani failure and to the Mukti Bahini's groundwork, does not withdraw the deposit, because the deposit was made on the attribution, not on the apportionment. The amendment cycle of November 1971 to April 1972 drew on the deposit within days and weeks of its creation, which is why the sequence treats the reception, documented in the December debates and the felicitations, as the operative evidence.

The counter does discipline the claim in one respect, and the article records the discipline. The sequence cannot argue that the mandate made the victory inevitable, or that a different Indian government would have lost the war; the eastern campaign's outcome was overdetermined by Pakistan's position. What the sequence claims is narrower and stronger: that the mandate determined the war's timing, its insurance, its limited aims and its conclusion on schedule, and that the reception of the victory, whatever its causal recipe, was converted by a specific parliamentary majority into constitutional change within twenty weeks of the surrender. Separated readings cannot explain the conversion's speed. A treatment of the amendments as ordinary legislation of a majority government misses why the veto coalition of 1970, which had blocked privy purse abolition in the Rajya Sabha and in the Supreme Court, offered no comparable resistance in December 1971 and April 1972. The unified sequence explains it with one variable, the refreshed authority of a government that had just been seen to win, and the variable is documented in the division lists: 381 to 6, and 167 to 7, on the very measure that had failed a year earlier by a whisker.

## What the Mandate Could Not Buy: The Ledger After 1972

The sequence's conversions were political, and the ledger they could not settle was economic. Garibi Hatao had promised the removal of poverty, and the promise's terms came due in the drought years that followed the victory. The monsoon of 1972 failed across large parts of the country, foodgrain production fell, and prices rose through 1973 at rates that consumed the wage gains of the poorest households; the wholesale price index climbed by more than a fifth in the year to 1974, and the oil price shock of October 1973, imposed by the petroleum exporting countries after the West Asian war, multiplied the import bill of a country that had just paid for a war and a refugee emergency. The government's responses, the takeover of the wholesale wheat trade in 1973, abandoned within a year, and the wage and price measures of 1974, were administered by the same centralised machinery the sequence had built, and their failures were charged to the same account as the victories. A plebiscitary mandate personalises credit, and it personalises blame with the same efficiency.

The industrial and labour registers recorded the strain. The railway strike of May 1974, called by the railway unions under George Fernandes's leadership and suppressed by the government with mass arrests under the preventive detention laws, was the largest industrial action of the era, and its suppression demonstrated both the machinery's reach and its character: a government whose mandate had been assembled on the removal of poverty detained tens of thousands of railway workers in defence of an economic order it could not otherwise stabilise. The strike's collapse after twenty days was counted a victory of the same kind as the amendments, an institutional resistance dissolved by concentrated authority, and it deepened the pattern the sequence describes. Each successful conversion lowered the anticipated cost of the next, and the habit of meeting constitutional, judicial, federal and industrial resistance alike with concentration became the government's ordinary method rather than its emergency reserve.

The federal instrument the sequence had built was among the first casualties of the ledger. The nominee chief minister system, installed across the states in 1972, converted every state level failure into a charge against New Delhi, because a chief minister who owed office to the Prime Minister's selection could not absorb blame locally. When the Nav Nirman agitation in Gujarat, begun by students in January 1974 over hostel and food prices, widened into a movement against corruption and forced the dissolution of the Gujarat assembly and the fall of Chimanbhai Patel's ministry in March 1974, the precedent was ominous for the centre: a state government installed under the mandate's authority had been dismissed by the street, and the demand that followed in Bihar, for the dissolution of that state's assembly as well, was directed at the system the sequence had created rather than at any single ministry. The Bihar movement's adoption by Jayaprakash Narayan in 1974 gave the street a national leadership and a moral vocabulary drawn from the freedom struggle, and the vocabulary was aimed at the mandate's legitimacy itself. The centre's instruments for answering, the assembly arithmetic, the party machine, the presumption of vindicated judgment, belonged to the sequence's earlier links and had depreciated with the deposit.

None of this dissolves the sequence's achievement, and the article's adjudication does not require it to. The mandate, the war and the amendments were real, and the legitimation they generated was real. The ledger after 1972 shows only that legitimation is a wasting asset when its founding promise is economic and the economy refuses delivery: by 1974 the deposit of December 1971 had been drawn down by drought, prices and the wheat trade's failure to a balance that left the government's survival dependent on the mandate's legal shell rather than on its living authority. That is the precise condition in which the Allahabad judgment arrived. A judgment voiding the Prime Minister's election in 1973, at the deposit's height, might have been absorbed by an appeal and a by-election. The same judgment in June 1975, with the deposit spent and the movements of Gujarat and Bihar in the streets, presented the government with the choice it answered on 25 June. The sequence explains the timing of the Emergency as exactly as it explains the timing of the war: in both cases, a government acted when the political room its mandate afforded had narrowed to the decision it took.

## From the Mandate to Allahabad: How the Sequence Was Tested

The sequence's final link was forged at its first link. In April 1971, weeks after the count, Raj Narain, the socialist candidate whom Indira Gandhi had defeated in Rae Bareli, filed an election petition in the Allahabad High Court challenging her election under the Representation of the People Act, alleging corrupt practices that included the assistance of Yashpal Kapoor, a government officer, in her campaign and the use of state machinery and the Indian Air Force for her election tour. The petition travelled slowly through the early 1970s, a minor item in the political news, while the events this article has traced accumulated above it: the war, the amendments, the state elections, the Kesavananda judgment and the supersession. Its obscurity is part of the structure. The mandate that enabled everything else carried, filed in a high court registry in its first month, the instrument of its own auditing.

On 12 June 1975 Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha delivered the judgment. The court upheld the petition on two counts, holding that Yashpal Kapoor's services had been obtained while he remained a government servant and that the assistance of state officials in arranging the Prime Minister's campaign infrastructure constituted corrupt practice under the Act, and it declared Indira Gandhi's election void, disqualifying her from elective office for six years. The legal findings were narrow, technical and, in the scale of Indian electoral practice, almost delicate; the political consequence was total, because the judgment struck at the one asset on which the entire sequence rested, the personal mandate of March 1971. A majority of 352 seats could survive the loss of its leader only by ceasing to be the majority it was, since the seats had been won as a plebiscite on her name. The stay of the judgment for twenty days, the appeal to the Supreme Court, and the proclamation of Emergency on 25 June 1975 followed as moves in the endgame the judgment had opened. On 23 June 1975, Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer, sitting as the Supreme Court's vacation judge, granted a conditional stay: Indira Gandhi could remain Prime Minister and address Parliament but could not vote in the Lok Sabha until the appeal was decided. The order satisfied no one, since it left the mandate's holder in office and under judgment at once, and within two days the government's answer arrived in the form the sequence had made available: the Emergency proclamation of 25 June, the arrests of the opposition leadership under the detention statute the Fifth Lok Sabha had enacted in its first season, and the conversion of the mandate's machinery to the suspension of the politics that had created it. The appeal itself was overtaken by the Thirty-Ninth Amendment, which removed the Prime Minister's election from judicial review altogether, and by the Supreme Court's November 1975 judgment applying that amendment while striking its boldest clause. The legal endgame belongs to the Emergency's own record. Its premise was set on 12 June, when a court audited the first link of the sequence and found it defective.

The sequence's reading of that endgame is the claim on which the article closes its argument. The Emergency is conventionally narrated as a rupture, a democratic order interrupted by an authoritarian decision in June 1975. The unified sequence narrates it as the final conversion in a chain that began with the dissolution of December 1970: a plebiscitary mandate converted into wartime authority, wartime authority converted into constitutional centralisation, and centralisation, when the mandate's own legality was voided by a court, converted into the suspension of the electoral order that had created it. Each conversion used the deposit of the one before, and the chain's logic is the logic of legitimation compounding. The Allahabad judgment tested the sequence's durability by testing its origin, and the order the sequence had built could not survive the test by ordinary means, which is why extraordinary means followed within thirteen days. The durability question answers itself across the decade: the settlement of 1971 lasted exactly as long as the mandate's personal legitimacy remained unvoided, and not one day of constitutional stress longer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Garibi Hatao slogan?

Garibi Hatao, meaning remove poverty, was the Congress (R) slogan for the 1971 general election and the most consequential campaign phrase of the Indira era. It answered the opposition Grand Alliance's cry of Indira Hatao, remove Indira, by shifting the election's subject from the Prime Minister's person to the voter's condition: the opposition offered a removal, she offered a programme of removal aimed at poverty itself. The slogan named no policy, no timetable and no budget. Its work was constitutive rather than programmatic. It defined the majority being assembled as a majority for overriding the institutional vetoes that had blocked bank nationalisation's consolidation and the privy purse abolition in 1970, and after the victory it served as the standing warrant invoked for the Twenty-Fourth, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Sixth Amendments. Painted on hoardings and repeated in budget speeches, Garibi Hatao converted a campaign sentence into the Fifth Lok Sabha's title deed, and its unmet promise became, in the drought and inflation of 1973 and 1974, the charge sheet of the movement that rose against its author.

### Q: When was the 1971 Indian general election?

The 1971 Indian general election, the poll for the Fifth Lok Sabha, was held from 1 to 10 March 1971, after President V. V. Giri dissolved the Fourth Lok Sabha on 27 December 1970 on Indira Gandhi's advice, more than a year before its term expired. It was India's first midterm general election and the first in which the national poll was delinked from most state assembly elections, a change that freed the contest from the state party machines the Congress (O) controlled. Voting covered 518 constituencies before an electorate of roughly 274 million, with turnout recorded at 55.27 percent in the Election Commission of India's Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Fifth Lok Sabha, published in 1972. Counting delivered the Congress (R) 352 seats on 43.68 percent of the vote, and Indira Gandhi was sworn in for her second term as Prime Minister on 18 March 1971, eight months before the war that the mandate would enable.

### Q: What was the Indo-Soviet Treaty of 1971?

The Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation was signed in New Delhi on 9 August 1971 by External Affairs Minister Swaran Singh and Soviet Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko, for a term of twenty years. Its operative provision, Article 9, committed each side, if the other faced an attack or the threat of one, to enter immediately into mutual consultations to remove the threat and to take appropriate effective measures to ensure peace and security. The treaty stopped short of an automatic military alliance, and its drafting preserved India's formal nonalignment, but its strategic effect was insurance: it raised the cost to Pakistan of war, to the United States of pressure on India, and to China of intervention on Pakistan's behalf. In December 1971 the insurance was collected, when the Soviet Union vetoed American sponsored ceasefire resolutions in the Security Council on 4 and 5 December and Soviet naval units moved toward the Indian Ocean as the American carrier Enterprise entered the Bay of Bengal.

### Q: What was the Bangladesh Liberation War?

The Bangladesh Liberation War was the nine month struggle of 1971 in which East Pakistan became the independent state of Bangladesh. It began on the night of 25 March 1971, when the Pakistan Army launched Operation Searchlight against Dhaka and other cities, arresting Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and attacking the population that had given his Awami League an absolute majority in Pakistan's December 1970 elections. The elected leadership formed the Mujibnagar government in exile in April 1971, and the Mukti Bahini's guerrilla forces fought the Pakistani garrison through the monsoon while roughly ten million refugees fled into India. India recognised Bangladesh on 6 December 1971, three days after Pakistan's air strikes on Indian airfields opened the formal war between the two states, and Indian forces advancing with the Mukti Bahini compelled the surrender of Pakistan's Eastern Command at Dhaka on 16 December 1971. The war ended Pakistani rule in the east and created the world's eighth most populous state from a population that had been governed, since 1947, from a capital a thousand miles away.

### Q: How did Indira Gandhi handle the East Pakistan refugee crisis?

Indira Gandhi's government treated the refugee crisis of 1971, in which roughly ten million people fled East Pakistan into West Bengal, Tripura, Assam and Meghalaya, as a political emergency with a required terminus rather than as a humanitarian burden to be absorbed. The administration registered and fed the refugees through a vast camp system, mounted inoculation drives that held epidemic disease in check, and presented the costs to Parliament and to foreign governments as a charge created by Pakistan's generals. Diplomatically, the government insisted from the spring of 1971 that the refugees would return only to a political settlement guaranteeing their safety, which meant a settlement with the elected Awami League leadership, and Indira Gandhi carried that demand to Western capitals in October and November 1971 without obtaining it. Domestically, she accepted her army chief's timetable for intervention, allowed the Mukti Bahini to train and operate from Indian soil, signed the Indo-Soviet Treaty in August, and moved to open war only after Pakistan's air strikes of 3 December. The refugees returned to Bangladesh in the first months of 1972.

### Q: What were the 24th, 25th, and 26th Amendments?

The Twenty-Fourth, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Sixth Amendments were the constitutional instruments of the post 1971 amendment cycle. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment, assented to on 5 November 1971, answered the Supreme Court's Golaknath judgment of 1967 by declaring Parliament's power to amend any part of the Constitution including the fundamental rights, and by obliging the President to assent to amendment bills. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment, assented to on 28 December 1971, twelve days after the Dhaka surrender, abolished the privy purses and privileges of the former princely rulers, omitting the constitutional articles that had guaranteed payments of roughly four crore rupees a year and barring future recognition of rulers. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment, assented to on 20 April 1972, weakened judicial review of property acquisition and inserted Article 31C, shielding laws that implemented the directive principles on equitable distribution of resources from challenge under the rights to equality, freedom and property. Together the three amendments asserted parliamentary supremacy over the property order, on the authority of the 1971 mandate as refreshed by the 1971 victory.

### Q: How did the 1971 victory lead to the Emergency?

The connection runs through legitimation and its exhaustion. The December 1971 victory deposited in Indira Gandhi's government a presumption of vindicated judgment that carried the amendment cycle of 1971 and 1972, the sweep of the 1972 state elections, the installation of chief ministers as New Delhi's nominees and the 1973 supersession of three Supreme Court judges after the Kesavananda Bharati judgment. Each use of concentrated authority lowered the cost of the next, converting emergency methods into ordinary government while the deposit lasted. The deposit was spent by drought, inflation and the unmet Garibi Hatao promise through 1973 and 1974, and the movements in Gujarat and Bihar rebuilt an opposition outside a Parliament the government owned. When the Allahabad High Court voided Indira Gandhi's own 1971 election on 12 June 1975, it struck the mandate's legal shell at the moment its living authority was weakest, and the government's answer, the Emergency proclamation of 25 June 1975, applied to the constitutional order the same conversion the sequence had practised on every other institution: suspension rather than negotiation.

### Q: Why did Indira Gandhi call the 1971 election more than a year early?

The early election of 1971 was an offensive decision taken from a position of minority government. After the Congress split of November 1969, Indira Gandhi governed with outside support from the Communist Party of India, the DMK and others, a tenure that could be ended by her partners at any moment and that left her legislative programme hostage to negotiation. Through 1970 she accumulated plebiscitary assets, bank nationalisation, the privy purse attempt, a public reputation for acting against entrenched privilege, and the dissolution of the Lok Sabha on 27 December 1970 converted those assets into a bid for a majority of her own before the opposition could consolidate and before the East Pakistan crisis, already visible after the Awami League's December 1970 victory, could narrow her freedom. Inder Malhotra's biography records the calculation as deliberate timing rather than desperation: the midterm poll, delinked from state elections, would be a referendum on the Prime Minister personally, fought on terrain the Congress (O)'s state machines could not reach. The verdict of March 1971, 352 seats, vindicated the arithmetic completely.

### Q: How many seats did Congress (R) win in the 1971 election?

The Congress (R) won 352 of the 518 Lok Sabha seats contested in the general election of 1 to 10 March 1971, on 43.68 percent of the popular vote, according to the Election Commission of India's Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Fifth Lok Sabha (1972). The tally exceeded the undivided Congress party's performance in 1967, when it had taken 283 seats, and it was assembled by a formation that had existed as a separate party for barely a year since the split of November 1969. The Communist Party of India (Marxist) followed at a distance with 25 seats, the Communist Party of India and the DMK took 23 each, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh 22, and the Congress (O), the organisation that had expelled Indira Gandhi in 1969, collapsed to 16 seats on 10.43 percent of the vote. With her allies counted, the Prime Minister commanded the two thirds strength required for constitutional amendment, the arithmetic on which the amendment cycle of 1971 and 1972 and the war decision of December 1971 both rested.

### Q: What was the Grand Alliance of 1971?

The Grand Alliance was the opposition coalition assembled against Indira Gandhi for the 1971 general election, joining the Congress (O) of K. Kamaraj and Morarji Desai, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the Swatantra Party and the Samyukta Socialist Party in seat adjustments across much of the country. The alliance's members agreed on one proposition, that Indira Gandhi had to be removed, and campaigned on the slogan Indira Hatao, and they agreed on very little else: the Swatantra Party's free enterprise liberalism, the Jana Sangh's Hindu nationalism, the socialists' egalitarianism and the Congress (O)'s organisational conservatism shared no programme and no candidate for Prime Minister. The alliance's strategy assumed that anti Congress (R) votes would pool behind single challengers. The delinked, plebiscitary character of the election defeated the assumption, because voters selecting a Prime Minister chose Indira Gandhi over a committee, and the alliance's components were reduced to fragments in the result: 16 seats for the Congress (O), 22 for the Jana Sangh and 8 for Swatantra, a combined strength too small to constitute an alternative government.

### Q: What was the opposition's Indira Hatao slogan?

Indira Hatao, remove Indira, was the campaign cry of the Grand Alliance in the 1971 general election, and it became famous chiefly for the answer it provoked. The slogan stated the opposition's whole case in two words: the election was about a person, her accumulation of power since the 1969 split, her ordinances and her contempt, as the Congress (O) framed it, for institutional procedure. Indira Gandhi seized the formulation and inverted it on the campaign trail, declaring that the opposition's programme was Indira Hatao while hers was Garibi Hatao, the removal of poverty, and asking voters which removal they preferred. The exchange fixed the election's frame as a choice between a personal vendetta and a social promise, and it exposed the alliance's structural weakness: a coalition that campaigns against a person must persuade voters that the person is the country's problem, against a leader who was simultaneously distributing the assets of 1969 and 1970, nationalised banks and an assault on princely privilege, as evidence of the opposite. The verdict measured the persuasion's failure at 352 seats to 16 for its principal authors.

### Q: What was Operation Searchlight?

Operation Searchlight was the Pakistan Army's crackdown launched across East Pakistan on the night of 25 March 1971, intended to crush the Bengali autonomy movement by force before it could consolidate the Awami League's electoral mandate. Troops struck Dhaka University, where students and faculty were killed in their halls, the Rajarbagh police lines and the Hindu neighbourhoods of the old city, and operations extended to cantonment towns and district centres across the province. Sheikh Mujibur Rahman was arrested and flown to West Pakistan, and the Awami League's leadership proclaimed the independence of Bangladesh and formed a government in exile. The operation's political premise, that terror would restore the authority of a military regime that had lost the December 1970 election, inverted within weeks: the killings, which Bengali and international accounts placed in the hundreds of thousands across the following nine months, destroyed whatever consent Pakistani rule in the east retained, filled the Mukti Bahini's ranks, and drove roughly ten million refugees into India, creating the emergency that carried the subcontinent to the December war.

### Q: What was the Mukti Bahini?

The Mukti Bahini, the liberation force, was the armed resistance of Bangladesh during 1971, built from the Bengali soldiers of the East Bengal Regiment and the East Pakistan Rifles who revolted after the crackdown of 25 March 1971, and expanded through the year with Awami League volunteers, students and defecting police and civil servants. Organised under the Mujibnagar government in exile and commanded operationally by Colonel M. A. G. Osmani, the force was divided into sectors along the East Pakistan frontier and trained in camps on Indian soil with Indian instructors, weapons and logistics. Its campaign through the monsoon and autumn of 1971 ambushed Pakistani communications, destroyed bridges and river craft, attacked isolated garrisons and held the allegiance of the countryside against the occupation, at heavy cost to its own largely young cadres. On 21 November 1971 the Mukti Bahini was placed with Indian forces under a joint command for the eastern theatre, and its units entered Dhaka alongside the Indian advance in December. The force's groundwork is central to any honest account of the war: Pakistan's garrison was already besieged in its own province before the first Indian division crossed the border.

### Q: Why did Pakistan launch air strikes on India on 3 December 1971?

Pakistan's air strikes of 3 December 1971, Operation Chengiz Khan, opened the formal war with attacks on Indian airfields across the western frontier, and the decision is generally read as a pre-emptive gamble by a regime that had concluded war was inevitable and sought its advantages. By late November, Indian forces and the Mukti Bahini under joint command were pressing into East Pakistan, the eastern garrison was being compressed toward Dhaka, and President Yahya Khan's government calculated that a full war might internationalise the conflict on terms favourable to Pakistan: an American and Chinese reaction against India, a Security Council ceasefire imposed with Pakistani forces still holding the eastern cities, and relief for the east through pressure on India's western front. The strikes also answered a domestic imperative, since the regime had promised its public a defence of Pakistan's integrity that the eastern attrition was visibly failing to deliver. The gamble failed on every term: the airfields were repaired within hours, the Security Council's ceasefire efforts were vetoed by the Soviet Union, China did not move, and the war's formal opening freed India to execute the eastern campaign its autumn build up had prepared.

### Q: What role did the Soviet Union play in the 1971 war?

The Soviet Union's role in the 1971 war was diplomatic and deterrent rather than combatant, and it was decisive at both levels. The Indo-Soviet Treaty of 9 August 1971, with its Article 9 commitment to immediate mutual consultations in the event of attack or threat, had priced Soviet behaviour into every other capital's calculations before the war began. When fighting opened in December, the Soviet Union vetoed the American sponsored ceasefire resolutions in the Security Council on 4 and 5 December 1971, resolutions whose adoption would have frozen the conflict with Pakistan's army still holding East Pakistan's cities, and Soviet diplomacy defended the position that a ceasefire required a political settlement with the elected representatives of East Pakistan. As the United States moved the carrier Enterprise into the Bay of Bengal, Soviet naval units from the Pacific Fleet deployed toward the Indian Ocean and shadowed the American task force, cancelling its signal. Srinath Raghavan's 1971: A Global History of the Creation of Bangladesh (2013) reconstructs this superpower choreography from the archives and treats the Soviet performance as the treaty's intended yield: India fought its fourteen day war inside a diplomatic shelter that Moscow had contracted to provide.

### Q: How many Pakistani soldiers surrendered at Dhaka in 1971?

The surrender at Dhaka on 16 December 1971 placed some 93,000 Pakistani personnel in Indian custody, the figure carried in the subsequent diplomatic record and treated as the largest military capitulation since the Second World War. The total covered the Eastern Command's regular soldiers together with paramilitary forces, police and civilian officials taken into custody in the east, and the prisoners were held in camps in India under the Geneva Conventions while their fate became a central question of the post war diplomacy. Lieutenant General A. A. K. Niazi signed the instrument of surrender before Lieutenant General Jagjit Singh Aurora at the Ramna Race Course, in a public ceremony photographed for the world, and the image of the signing became the war's defining document in India. The prisoners were repatriated in stages after the Simla Agreement of July 1972 and the Delhi Agreement of August 1973, with the final groups, including the 195 personnel Bangladesh had designated for war crimes trial and later released under the tripartite settlement, returning to Pakistan by April 1974.

### Q: How long did the 1971 war last?

The formal war between India and Pakistan in 1971 lasted fourteen days, from Pakistan's air strikes on Indian airfields on the evening of 3 December 1971 to the ceasefire India declared unilaterally on 17 December, effective that evening, after the surrender of Pakistan's Eastern Command at Dhaka on 16 December. The brevity was a product of design and preparation rather than improvisation. India had spent the months since April 1971 stockpiling the eastern theatre, training the Mukti Bahini, signing the Indo-Soviet Treaty and waiting for the winter conditions that dried the Bengali terrain and closed the Himalayan passes to Chinese movement, so the campaign that the December opening released had been assembled across three seasons. The war's short duration also served its political object: it concluded before the international system could impose a settlement freezing the conflict short of Bangladesh's liberation. The longer war inside the shorter one, the Mukti Bahini's guerrilla campaign and the refugees' nine month exile, ran from the crackdown of 25 March 1971, a distinction the chronology of the year requires and the separated histories of the war often blur.

### Q: What was the Simla Agreement of 1972?

The Simla Agreement, signed in the early hours of 2 July 1972 by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi and President Zulfikar Ali Bhutto of Pakistan after five days of negotiation in the Himachal Pradesh capital, was the bilateral settlement that closed the 1971 war's account between the two states. The agreement committed both countries to settle their differences by peaceful bilateral means, converted the ceasefire line of 17 December 1971 in Jammu and Kashmir into the Line of Control, which both sides undertook to respect without unilateral alteration, and provided for the withdrawal of forces to their own sides of the international border. Under its framework India returned the roughly 15,000 square kilometres of Pakistani territory its army held in the west, and the repatriation of the 93,000 prisoners of war proceeded in stages completed by 1974. Critics in India charged that Simla spent the victory's leverage without settling Kashmir, and Bhutto's defenders and critics in Pakistan disputed what he had verbally promised on Kashmir's future. The agreement's Indian function in the sequence of 1971 was closure: it banked the war as a finished event and freed the mandate's authority for domestic use.

### Q: Who was Sam Manekshaw?

Sam Hormusji Framji Jamshedji Manekshaw was the Chief of Army Staff of India during the 1971 war and the professional soldier whose insistence on preparation fixed the war's timetable. A Sandhurst trained officer of the Gorkha regiments who had won the Military Cross in Burma in 1942 and served as a brigade and corps commander through the wars of 1947 and 1965, Manekshaw became army chief in 1969 with a reputation for candour toward political masters formed in the shadow of the 1962 defeat. In April 1971 he told Indira Gandhi's cabinet that an immediate invasion of East Pakistan would fail in the coming monsoon, offering his resignation if the government insisted, and the government's acceptance of his calendar, intervention deferred to winter, was the year's pivotal civil military transaction. He directed the joint planning that produced the fourteen day eastern campaign and the surrender at Dhaka. In January 1973 he became the first Indian officer promoted to Field Marshal. His partnership with the Prime Minister, her patience against his candour, is the human form of the compact between the 1971 mandate and the professional military that executed its war.

### Q: What was the voter turnout in the 1971 general election?

The Election Commission of India recorded a turnout of 55.27 percent in the general election of March 1971, from an electorate of roughly 274 million names on the rolls for the 518 constituencies of the Fifth Lok Sabha, as published in its Statistical Report on the General Elections to the Fifth Lok Sabha (1972). The figure sits within the range of India's early general elections and was achieved in a midterm poll held a year ahead of schedule, in a single national contest delinked from most state assembly elections for the first time. The Commission's constituency tables show the Congress (R) converting its 43.68 percent share of the votes cast into 352 seats, a seat share of nearly 68 percent, the amplification being the standard arithmetic of the plurality system applied to a divided opposition: the Grand Alliance's components together polled a substantial vote that their fragmentation across parties and constituencies converted into only a fraction of the seats. The turnout and its conversion matter to the year's history because the mandate's extraordinary legislative strength, its capacity to amend the Constitution, was manufactured by that amplification from a plurality of votes rather than from a majority of them.
