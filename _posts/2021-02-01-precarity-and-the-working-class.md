Precarity names a condition that millions of workers recognise from the inside: work that arrives irregularly, pays unpredictably, carries no benefits worth the name, and can be withdrawn at short notice by an employer who owes the worker nothing beyond the hours already worked. The word has gathered a literature around itself, complete with a proposed new class, a diagnosis of an age, and a quarrel about whether anything genuinely unprecedented is being described. That quarrel turns, more than its participants usually admit, on a question of comparison. To call work newly insecure is to measure it against some earlier state in which work was secure, and everything depends on which earlier state is chosen, how long it lasted, where it held, and whom it covered. Choose the baseline carelessly and the conclusion is built into the measurement before the evidence is even consulted.

![A rota board with shifting hours pinned beside a row of empty lockers, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The argument of this article can be stated plainly at the outset. The secure, permanent, full-time job, protected by law and accompanied by benefits, which serves as the silent baseline in most writing about precarity, was a historical exception. It held for about thirty years following 1945, in a handful of wealthy economies, and even there it covered mainly a subset of workers, disproportionately men in strongly unionised employment. Before that arrangement, outside it, and underneath it, insecure and irregular work was the ordinary experience of the labouring population across most of the world. Precarity, judged against the long record, is the norm to which labour reverts, and the security was the departure that requires explanation. Yet the correction, however well supported, does not end the matter, because a generation that was promised security and then watched the promise withdrawn has experienced something real, and a Marxist analysis that cannot register that loss has traded understanding for a debating point. The tradition, fortunately, is better equipped than either side of the quarrel suggests. Marx analysed irregular employment within his account of the reserve army of labour, and the question worth asking is whether that older category, honestly handled, already does the work the new class concept claims only it can do.

## Precarity and the question of the baseline

Every claim about a change in the character of work contains a hidden photograph of the past. When a commentator declares that employment has become precarious, the sentence leans on an implied contrast with employment that was not, and the implied contrast does more argumentative work than any statistic offered in support. If the past being invoked is a specific settlement, reached in particular places under particular conditions and dismantled within living memory, then the claim describes a reversal, significant and worth analysing. If the past being summoned is instead an imagined permanence, a supposed age in which security was simply what work was, then the claim describes a fall from a condition that never generally existed, and the analysis built on it will mislocate both the causes and the remedies. Sorting out which of these two claims is being made, in any given text, is the first discipline this subject demands.

The word baseline is borrowed from measurement, and the borrowing is deliberate. A baseline is the fixed point against which movement is registered, and a badly placed baseline manufactures movement that never occurred or conceals movement that did. Labour history is unusually vulnerable to badly placed baselines because its most documented decades are also its most unusual ones. The generation of scholars, officials, and trade unionists who wrote the standard accounts of the postwar labour market had themselves lived inside an arrangement of exceptional stability, and they described that arrangement in the grammar of normality, as the standard employment relationship, a phrase that quietly promotes a local and temporary settlement into the definition of employment as such. Once the promotion has happened, everything outside the settlement reads as deviation, and the whole of labour history before and beyond it becomes a long prelude or a sad decline, rather than what it was: the ordinary condition of people who sold their labour power under capitalism.

### Why does the choice of baseline decide the argument about precarity?

because the same evidence yields opposite conclusions depending on the comparison point selected. Measured against a short postwar settlement, insecure work appears as a dramatic departure requiring new categories. Measured against the longer record of labour under capitalism, the settlement itself appears as the departure, and insecurity appears as the enduring condition that the settlement briefly and partially suspended.

Thinking in baselines also disciplines the emotions that this subject reliably provokes. Nostalgia is a baseline error wearing the dress of memory: it takes the best arrangement a particular group of workers ever obtained and treats its loss as the loss of the natural order. Contempt for nostalgia can become its own error, a knowing shrug that treats every complaint about insecurity as a failure of historical perspective. Both responses skip the analytical step. The baseline question is empirical before it is anything else. How long did the secure arrangement last, in how many places, covering which workers, guaranteed by what institutions, and paid for out of what economic conditions? Those questions have answers, assembled over decades by labour historians, and the answers are not flattering to the assumption that security was ever the general rule. The sections that follow build the article's case in the order the brief of the evidence demands: first the new class argument in its strongest form, then the baseline correction that qualifies it, then the older Marxist category that competes with it, then the residue that survives both corrections, and finally the sharper argument about surplus populations that reframes the whole discussion.

A note on method belongs here, because this article observes a strict rule throughout. No country, government, policy, sector, employer, or legal case is named anywhere in what follows. The rule is not coyness. Claims about precarity decay faster than almost any other claims in this series, because they are usually tied to a particular labour market at a particular moment, and a tied claim expires with its moment. Stated structurally, in terms of mechanisms, baselines, and durations, the same claims remain testable by a reader in ten years who brings a different labour market to the page. Where a trend matters, its direction and the decades over which it ran are given, and magnitudes are left to the research literature, which is named as literature rather than mined for figures that would date and distort the argument. The reader who wants the figures for a specific place and year should consult that literature directly; the reader who wants to know what kind of claim survives the figures changing will find the argument here.

## The new class argument stated at full strength

Fairness to an argument one intends to qualify begins with stating it in the form its author would recognise as its strongest. The precariat thesis received its canonical statement in Guy Standing's book The Precariat: The New Dangerous Class, published in 2011, and any serious discussion of precarity works in the shadow of that statement whether it acknowledges the debt or not. Standing argues that globalisation and the labour flexibility pursued across the advanced economies over preceding decades have produced a new class formation, a class in the making rather than a class fully conscious of itself, whose members are defined by a distinctive relation to work, to income, and to the state. The precariat, on his account, lacks what the traditional working class at its most organised possessed: security of employment, an occupational identity built over a working life, and access to the rights-based entitlements that the postwar settlement attached to the status of employee.

The anatomy of the proposed class deserves slow unpacking, because its plausibility rests on the accumulation of distinct insecurities rather than on any single one. Labour market security, the reasonable expectation of continued employment, is absent. Employment security, protection against arbitrary dismissal, is absent. Job security in the narrower sense, a defined post with a defined ladder of progression, is absent. Work security, protection against accident and illness at work backed by regulation and compensation, is thin. Skill reproduction security, the chance to build competence through training and apprenticeship, is rarely offered. Income security, a predictable flow sufficient for a stable life, is replaced by fluctuation that defeats planning. Representation security, a collective voice through an organisation of one's own, is weakest of all where the other insecurities are deepest. A worker might suffer one or two of these absences and remain recognisably inside the old working class. Standing's claim is that their combination, concentrated in a growing stratum and reproduced across generations, marks out a population whose situation differs qualitatively from that of the securely employed, and whose interests may diverge from that stratum's interests sharply enough to constitute a separate class position.

### What does the precariat thesis claim that a description of bad jobs does not?

it claims that the insecurities combine into a distinct social position with its own relation to income, identity, and political authority. A description of bad jobs can be absorbed into the old picture as its lower tier. A class claim cannot: it asserts that the population concerned is coming to stand in a new relation to the rest of society, with grievances and temptations that the established organisations of labour neither voice nor contain.

Two further elements give the thesis its polemical edge. The first is the denial of occupational identity. The securely employed worker of the settlement years could answer the question of what he was with the name of a trade, and the answer carried a narrative: skills acquired, seniority accumulated, a pension approached, a standing in a community of practitioners. The precarious worker, by contrast, performs tasks without a narrative arc, holding a sequence of engagements that add up to no recognised identity, and Standing treats this absence as a deprivation in its own right, corrosive of dignity and of the capacity for collective self-recognition. The second element is political warning, carried in the book's subtitle. A stratum denied identity, security, and voice becomes available for political mobilisation of a resentful kind, courted by voices that offer it enemies rather than entitlements. The precariat is dangerous, on this account, not because it threatens property in the manner of the old labour movement, but because its frustration, unorganised and unrepresented, can be harvested by movements hostile to democracy itself.

Stated this way, the thesis has genuine explanatory attractions, and an honest article should enumerate them before qualifying anything. It names an experience that the vocabulary of class inherited from the settlement era struggles to phrase: the experience of working constantly while never quite having a job. It explains a political volatility that purely economic accounts leave mysterious, the readiness of insecure populations to support disruptive movements across the conventional spectrum. It directs attention to entitlements, to the dense web of rights and benefits that the settlement attached to employment status and that precarious arrangements quietly strip away, leaving the wage as the only remaining term of the contract. And it issues a challenge to labour organisations whose membership, rituals, and demands were formed around the secure worker, asking whom they represent when the secure worker is no longer the typical figure at the gate. These are real services. The question the rest of this article presses is narrower than the thesis's admirers and detractors both tend to make it. Grant the experience, grant the volatility, grant the stripping of entitlements. Does the analysis require a new class, and does the story of a fall from security survive contact with the longer record? Those are separable questions, and they have different answers.

## The baseline table and what it actually records

Arguments about baselines are won and lost on specifics, and the specifics of the standard employment relationship can be set out in a single table. The baseline table is this article's findable artifact: it states, for the arrangement against which precarity is conventionally measured, the period it covered, the places it covered, the workers it covered, and the duration it lasted, with a final column drawing the implication for the precarity claim. Read the four rows slowly, because the entire dispute is contained in them, and most popular discussion of insecure work proceeds as though none of the four rows existed.

| Dimension | What the standard employment relationship covered | Implication for the precarity claim |
|---|---|---|
| Period | The decades following 1945, roughly a generation of exceptional growth and full employment | The baseline is a bounded episode, not a standing condition of capitalism |
| Countries | A small number of wealthy economies where the settlement was institutionalised | The baseline is geographically narrow, not a world standard from which the world fell away |
| Workers covered | Mainly a subset within those economies, concentrated among men in strongly unionised employment | The baseline excludes, even at its height, much of the labour force of its own countries |
| Duration | About three decades before the arrangement was progressively dismantled | The baseline is shorter than a single working life, so its loss was witnessed by those who held it |

Each row of the table corresponds to a body of scholarship, and each deserves a paragraph of defence. The period row rests on the economic peculiarities of the postwar decades: reconstruction demand, rapid productivity growth, cheap energy, and labour scarcity that strengthened workers' bargaining position to a degree employers and governments had to accommodate rather than merely concede. The settlement was the political form of that accommodation. The countries row rests on comparison: even among wealthy economies the arrangement differed in depth and coverage, and across the greater part of the world nothing resembling it was ever generalised, casual and unprotected labour remaining the rule for the majority of humanity throughout the period in question. The workers row rests on the internal exclusions of the settlement itself, examined in a later section. The duration row rests on the chronology of dismantling, a process spread across subsequent decades, uneven by place and industry, but consistent in direction: protections narrowed, tenure weakened, risk transferred from employer to worker by increments too small to announce themselves as a single event.

### In what sequence does the baseline table arrange its comparisons?

it places the exceptional episode inside the longer record instead of allowing the episode to stand for the record. The row for period fixes the settlement between a before and an after in which insecure labour predominated, the row for duration shows the episode to be shorter than the spans on either side of it, and the remaining rows show its coverage to have been partial even while it lasted.

The table also exposes why so much of the precarity literature talks past itself. Two writers can survey the same labour market and reach opposite verdicts, one announcing an epidemic of insecurity, the other noting that standard employment remains widespread, because each is silently comparing against a different row. The first compares the present arrangement against the settlement at its height and registers loss. The second compares the distribution of job types against a recent decade and registers continuity. Neither comparison is dishonest, yet the conclusions cannot both carry the meaning their authors intend, because the word precarity smuggles the baseline in without declaring it. The discipline this article follows, and recommends to every participant in the dispute, is that every comparison must state its baseline period, the countries included, and the workers counted. A comparison that cannot state those three things has not yet become an argument; it is an impression with footnotes. Much of the apparent irresolubility of the field dissolves once the rule is enforced, since many celebrated disagreements turn out to be agreements about the facts wearing different baselines.

There is a historiographical point underneath the methodological one. The settlement's decades generated an enormous documentary residue: personnel records, collective agreements, social insurance files, official inquiries, union archives. Earlier and less formal labour left thinner traces, and the historian's craft is needed to recover it from court records, parish registers, travellers' accounts, and the archaeology of workplaces. The archive therefore flatters the settlement, making the best documented form of employment look like the most typical, when documentation and typicality are entirely different properties. Labour historians have warned against this archival illusion for generations. E. P. Thompson's reconstruction of English working class formation showed a world of casual, seasonal, and violently irregular labour long before any settlement existed, and Eric Hobsbawm's studies of labour's history repeatedly stressed how late, how local, and how incomplete the stabilisation of employment was, even in the economies where it went furthest. The correction of the precarity baseline is not a revisionist novelty. It is the settled finding of the discipline that studies work over long spans, and the precarity literature's weakness is less its politics than its historiography.

## Three decades of security set against the longer record

What did work look like before the settlement, for the ordinary person who had nothing to sell but the capacity to labour? The record assembled by labour historians is unromantic to a degree that still startles readers raised on the settlement's self-image. Hiring was commonly by the day, the week, or the task. Seasonal rhythms governed whole trades, with intense labour at harvest, at launch, at the building season, followed by slack months in which the same workers survived on credit, charity, kinship, and the pawnshop. Payment arrived in arrears, in kind, in tokens redeemable at an employer's shop, or not at all when an enterprise failed, taking the wage fund with it. Dismissal required no process and attracted no compensation. Sickness, injury, pregnancy, and old age were private catastrophes, absorbed by families or by the punitive mercies of poor relief. A working life was typically a portfolio of occupations rather than a career, assembled opportunistically as openings appeared and vanished, and the notion that an employer owed a worker continuity of any kind would have struck most employers, and many workers, as a confusion about the nature of the wage bargain.

None of this is offered as antiquarian colour. Each element of that older insecurity has a precise descendant in the condition the precarity literature describes: hiring by the task, income that fluctuates with demand, payment detached from any obligation beyond the engagement, the transfer of risk for sickness and age onto the individual, the portfolio of unrelated jobs mistaken for a biography. The resemblance is not a coincidence of vocabulary. It reflects the default settings of a labour market in which labour power is a commodity like any other, bought when needed and left unsold when not, with the costs of its maintenance during unsold intervals borne by its owner. Capital, left to its own preferences, does not volunteer continuity, because continuity is a cost, and the entire history of labour regulation can be read as the history of workers and their organisations forcing continuity into the bargain against that preference, inch by inch, protection by protection. The settlement decades were the high-water mark of that forcing, reached when labour scarcity, union density at its peak, and rapid growth briefly aligned. When the alignment dissolved, the water receded toward the older shoreline, not because of a conspiracy but because the underlying preference had never been abolished, only restrained.

### Is precarity new?

not historically. The secure full-time job with benefits existed for roughly three decades after 1945, in a small number of countries, and mainly for a subset of workers within them. For most workers in most places, insecure and irregular work has been the norm.

The snippet answer states the article's core in miniature, and its careful qualifications each carry weight. Roughly three decades: the settlement was shorter than the working life of a person who entered it young, which is why its dismantling could be experienced by the same individuals who had organised their expectations around it. A small number of countries: the arrangement was a provincial achievement even in its heyday, surrounded by a world in which it had never been attempted or had been attempted only for narrow enclaves. A subset of workers within them: the settlement had an inside and an outside in every place it existed, and the boundary between the two was one of the central facts of its labour markets. Strip any one of the three qualifications away and the sentence becomes propaganda for one side or the other. Keep all three, and the sentence becomes what this article takes it to be: the most economical true statement available about the historical shape of employment security under capitalism.

Feminist scholarship adds a dimension that the standard telling, focused on the male industrial worker, systematically misses. The settlement's security rested on a household model in which a male wage was expected to support dependants, and the unpaid labour of wives, in cooking, cleaning, nursing, and child rearing, subsidised the whole arrangement by reproducing labour power at no cost to the employer. Women's paid work, meanwhile, was concentrated in the settlement's insecure margins: part-time, seasonal, casual, and home-based labour, frequently excluded from the protections attached to full employment status, and treated in law and in union practice alike as secondary earnings rather than as a livelihood. To measure precarity against the settlement without this correction is to measure women's work against a security most women workers never had. The fall into insecurity, for a very large share of the labour force, is a fall that never happened, because the height was never reached. Postcolonial scholarship makes the parallel correction at world scale. In colonised and formerly colonised economies, the standard employment relationship governed, at its most extensive, a small formal enclave surrounded by vast informal economies in which insecure work was not a deviation but the organising principle, a point this series develops in its treatment of informality and to which a later section returns.

## Who the settlement covered and whom it left standing outside

The subset clause of the baseline deserves its own examination, because the internal exclusions of the secure decades are the strongest single evidence that security was an enclave rather than a condition. Even within the economies where the standard employment relationship was most fully institutionalised, large populations worked outside its protections throughout its entire duration. Agricultural labourers, domestic servants, casual dock and building workers, homeworkers, seasonal migrants, and the staff of small enterprises typically stood beyond the reach of the collective agreements and insurance schemes that defined security for the core workforce. Their exclusion was not an oversight awaiting correction. It was structural: the settlement's costs were contained by limiting its coverage, and the boundary of coverage was policed by distinctions of status, of firm size, of hours thresholds, and of contribution records that sorted workers into those whose insecurity society would underwrite and those whose insecurity remained a private problem.

Gender organised the boundary as powerfully as any legal distinction. The protected core was imagined, in the design of its institutions, as male: a breadwinner whose continuous full-time employment would accumulate the contributions and seniority from which benefits flowed. Women entered the core on terms that penalised interruption, and interruption was built into the lives the household model assigned them. Part-time work, which might have been a bridge, was instead designed as a separate and lesser status, carrying reduced protections in exact proportion to its convenience for employers seeking labour without commitment. The result is a fact that any honest baseline must record: for roughly half the labour force of the settlement economies, the celebrated security was experienced, when it was experienced at all, at one remove, as a dependant's claim on someone else's protected wage, a claim that dissolved with marriage breakdown, widowhood, or abandonment. Feminist labour historians have insisted, correctly, that a baseline drawn from the male core workforce is not a baseline for the working class but for a privileged fraction of it, and that the precarity literature inherits the distortion whenever it generalises from that fraction's biography.

Migration organised the boundary along a second axis. The settlement economies, during their decades of labour scarcity, drew in workers from poorer regions and colonies on terms that deliberately withheld the settlement's securities: temporary permits, tied housing, restricted rights of movement between employers, and deportability as the sanction behind discipline. Such workers built the infrastructure and staffed the least attractive shifts of the golden age while standing formally outside its protections, a arrangement whose convenience to the host economies was precisely that it supplied labour without extending the settlement. Their descendants frequently inherited a marginal position within labour markets that their parents' labour had enriched. A baseline that averages across these populations describes a country that never existed. The settlement was real, and so was its outside, and the two were functionally connected: the security of the core was partly purchased by the insecurity of the periphery, within the same borders and often within the same enterprise, where a protected permanent staff and an unprotected casual pool performed adjacent tasks under the same roof.

### What separates the settlement's inside from its outside?

the boundary was drawn by status rules rather than by the nature of the work performed. Contribution records, hours thresholds, firm size exemptions, marital assumptions, and migration permits sorted workers performing similar labour into protected and unprotected populations. The distinction therefore tells us about the political design of the settlement, not about any technical difference between secure and insecure kinds of work as such.

Race and colonial history drew the boundary in its starkest forms, in economies where the settlement coexisted with legally enforced hierarchies of labour. Whole populations were confined by law or by administrative practice to the insecure tiers, recruited for the hardest labour, paid at rates the protected workforce would not accept, and denied the organisational rights through which the core had won its protections. To speak of those decades as an age of security, without qualification, is to adopt the vantage point of the protected. The point matters for the precarity debate because the debate's emotional register, the sense of a civilisation losing its footing, sounds very different from positions that never had the footing. A Marxist treatment has to hold both registers at once: the loss experienced by the formerly protected is genuine, and the universality claimed for their former condition is false. Holding both is not equivocation. It is the baseline discipline applied to the politics of memory, and it will matter again when this article weighs what the reversal of the settlement does and does not establish.

## Why the security lasted only about three decades

Explaining the exception is the analytical heart of the matter, because an exception explained is a claim about the rule. The settlement rested on a conjunction of conditions that had never previously coincided and showed no tendency to persist once they separated. Reconstruction and pent-up demand gave employers full order books. Productivity rose rapidly as techniques developed between the wars were generalised, allowing real wages to climb without squeezing profits to the point of revolt. Labour was scarce relative to demand, partly through the demographic consequences of war, partly through the absorption of workers into expanding public provision, and scarcity is the one condition under which the individual seller of labour power bargains from strength. Unions stood at their historical maximum of membership and legal recognition. Governments of the settlement economies, fearing both unrest and the electoral consequences of mass unemployment within living memory of its horrors, accepted full employment as a responsibility of state. Each condition reinforced the others. Remove several, and the settlement's terms became, from capital's side, an expensive indulgence to be renegotiated.

The renegotiation, when the conjunction dissolved, proceeded by mechanisms rather than by a single dramatic repeal, which is why dating the reversal is so difficult and why this article describes it by direction and duration rather than by endpoints. Slowing productivity growth squeezed the surplus out of which rising wages and expanding protections had both been paid. Intensified international competition exposed the settlement's high-cost producers to rivals whose labour regimes had never carried its obligations. Employers rediscovered the virtues of flexibility, a word whose career deserves suspicion, since in labour market usage it almost always denotes the transfer of variability from the enterprise, which is equipped to absorb it, to the worker, who is not. Legal frameworks were adjusted incrementally: thresholds redefined, categories of engagement multiplied, enforcement thinned. Each adjustment was defended as a marginal modernisation. Their cumulative effect was the reconstruction, inside the settlement economies themselves, of the older labour market's essential features: engagement without commitment, income without predictability, risk without insurance. The shoreline metaphor used earlier should not be pressed into fatalism. The water level was set by struggle and can be set by struggle again. But the direction of the decades in question is not in serious dispute in the research literature, whatever disputes persist about magnitudes.

### Why was the era of security so brief?

because the settlement depended on a conjunction of rapid productivity growth, labour scarcity, strong unions, and governments committed to full employment, and the conjunction dissolved. As growth slowed and competition intensified, employers pressed successfully to transfer risk back onto workers. Security had been a political achievement resting on favourable conditions, not a permanent feature of mature capitalism.

A further cause deserves separate mention because it is so often omitted from elegies for the settlement: the settlement was never extended to the whole world, and its partial character generated the pressure that undermined it. Production could migrate toward the vast insecure labour reserves beyond the settlement's borders, and the credible threat of migration disciplined workers inside them. The exception, in other words, contained the geography of its own undoing. A security that covers a subset of the world's workers, in a system where capital moves globally, is a security under permanent siege from the insecurity it excludes. This is one reason the surplus population argument, treated later in this article, matters so much: it directs attention from the defended enclave to the global mass of labour that capital cannot profitably employ, which is the background condition against which every local settlement is negotiated. The baseline table's rows, which can look like antiquarian corrections to a lively debate, turn out to be load-bearing. Period, place, coverage, and duration are exactly the variables that explain both why the exception arose and why it could not hold.

## The reserve army already contains the precarious worker

Turn from history to theory, and the precarity debate changes character. The question stops being whether insecure work is new, which the record answers, and becomes whether the Marxist tradition possesses a category under which insecure work was already analysed, which is a question about texts and concepts. The candidate category is the reserve army of labour, Marx's name for the population of workers surplus to capital's immediate requirements, whose existence disciplines the wages and conditions of those in employment. The full exposition of that category, its forms, and its laws of motion belongs to the companion article on the [the reserve army of labour](/2010/12/15/reserve-army-of-labour-explained/), and this article links to that exposition rather than restating it, in line with the series rule that each concept has one canonical home. What belongs here is only the adjudication: does the reserve army analysis, as Marx left it, already accommodate the phenomenon that the precariat thesis presents as requiring a new class?

Within the reserve army, Marx distinguishes forms according to the regularity with which their members are drawn into employment. One form hovers at the edge of the active workforce, expelled and reabsorbed with the cycle. Another waits in employments being dissolved by capitalist development, released slowly as old forms of production decay. A third form, the one that matters here, is characterised by extremely irregular employment: its members work when work can be found, in whatever occupation offers, for whatever duration the engagement lasts, and their standard of life sinks below the average of the working class precisely because the irregularity prevents the accumulation of anything, savings, seniority, skill premiums, or claims. Marx calls this the stagnant form of the relative surplus population, stagnant not because its members are idle, since they may labour desperately and continuously in aggregate, but because their relation to employment never stabilises into the regular wage relation around which the rest of the analysis is built. A reader who places the description of the stagnant form beside a description of precarious work will struggle to find daylight between them. Irregular engagement, depressed and fluctuating income, absence of the protections attached to regular employment, a life organised around the search for the next engagement: the correspondence is not approximate but structural.

### How far does the reserve army framework reach into this subject?

substantially, yes. The stagnant form of that surplus population is defined by extremely irregular employment and a standard of life below the working class average, which matches the precarity literature's description point for point in its material content. What the older analysis does not supply is an account of lost expectations and vanished occupational identity among workers who once held protected status.

The existence of the category reframes the burden of proof in the debate. A proponent of the precariat as a new class must show not merely that insecure work is widespread and miserable, which nobody in the tradition disputes, but that the phenomenon escapes the analytical net Marx already cast: that the stagnant form, together with the floating and latent forms and the general law connecting surplus population to accumulation, fails to explain some feature of the condition that a new class concept would explain. That is a demanding brief, and most presentations of the precariat thesis never attempt it, preferring to write as though the tradition offered only the image of a uniform factory proletariat against which any complication counts as novelty. The uniform proletariat is a figure of textbooks and polemics. In the analysis itself, the working class was always internally differentiated by the regularity of its employment, and the differentiation was not decorative: the irregular strata performed functions in the system, supplying labour at peaks, absorbing the shock of contractions, and pressing down the claims of the regularly employed by their visible availability. Precarious workers, on the older analysis, are not an anomaly at the edge of the working class. They are one of its standing layers, produced by the same accumulation that produces the protected core, and related to that core by mechanisms the theory names.

Precision about what the category does not do is equally necessary, and a later section devoted to the strongest objection will develop the concession fully. The reserve army analysis was built to explain the function of surplus labour for accumulation: its effects on wages, on the bargaining position of the employed, on the rhythm of expansion and crisis. It was not built to describe the subjective and political experience of downward mobility, of having held a protected status and lost it, because the population Marx described had never held that status. A category can cover a phenomenon functionally while missing it experientially, and the precariat thesis, whatever its defects as class theory, is an attempt to name the experiential remainder. Acknowledging the remainder costs the Marxist position nothing and buys it honesty. Denying the remainder, by contrast, turns a strong analytical claim into a brittle one, since any reader who has lived the loss of an expected security will know the denial to be false to experience, and will discount the analysis that contains it.

## Class by relation to production or class by condition of employment

Underneath the empirical quarrel sits a definitional one, and the series has met it before in other territory. In the Marxist framework, class is defined by relation to the means of production: by whether one owns productive property and lives from the labour of others, or owns nothing but labour power and lives by selling it, or occupies one of the intermediate and contradictory positions that real economies generate between those poles. The framework is set out in the [marxist theory of class guide](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/), which this article takes as given. The precariat thesis defines its class differently: by conditions of employment, by the insecurity, irregularity, and entitlementlessness of the work its members perform. Two workers on the same line, producing the same goods for the same employer, fall into different classes on the employment-condition definition if one holds a permanent contract and the other an engagement that can lapse at a week's end. On the relational definition they are members of one class differently situated, and their difference is a difference of stratum, of bargaining power, and of immediate interest within a shared fundamental position.

The objection to defining class by employment conditions is not pedantry about a word. Definitions in this tradition are load-bearing: the concept of class is asked to explain the deep alignments of interest that persist beneath the shifting surface of politics, and a definition keyed to contract type ties the concept to the most administratively mutable feature of the labour relation. Contracts are redesigned by employers and legislatures continuously; a class structure that reshuffles with each redesign explains little, because it merely redescribes the surface it was meant to explain. The same objection has been pressed, in this series and elsewhere, against other attempts to multiply classes by occupational or lifestyle criteria, and the parallel treatment of [erik olin wright class analysis](/2020/02/15/erik-olin-wright-class-analysis/) shows how even a rigorous scholar working inside the tradition had to discipline such multiplications with relational anchors. Erik Olin Wright's contradictory locations earned their keep by being derived from relations of ownership and authority, not from the felt quality of jobs. The precariat, defined by felt and contractual quality, lacks that derivation, and the lack shows whenever the thesis is asked the hard questions: where exactly the boundary of the class runs, why its members' interests should be expected to unify, and against whom.

### Where does a genuinely new class part from a renamed old condition?

a new class requires a distinct relation to the means of production, generating interests that systematically diverge from those of existing classes. An old condition given a fresh label redescribes a stratum within a class: its members share the fundamental position of selling labour power, however irregular the sale, and their grievances, however bitter, arise inside that shared position rather than from a new one.

Standing's own formulation concedes more than his subtitle advertises. The precariat is described as a class in the making, a phrase borrowed from the historiography of class formation, where it denotes a population that shares a position but has not yet acquired the consciousness, organisation, and self-recognition of a class for itself. The concession matters because it relocates the thesis's claim. If the precariat is a class in the making in the weak sense, a stratum becoming aware of shared grievances, then the claim is about political formation, and it can be assessed politically: do insecure workers organise as insecure workers, under that banner, against identifiable opponents, with a programme expressing the stratum's specific condition? The evidence of organisation among precarious workers, in unions of casual and platform workers, in movements of the unemployed and the informally employed, is real and growing in the literature, yet the banners under which such organisation proceeds are overwhelmingly the old banners of labour, demanding exactly the securities the settlement embodied: regular contracts, living incomes, social protection, representation. The stratum, when it speaks, asks to be admitted to the condition of the working class as historically organised, not to be recognised as a new class beyond it. That is not a refutation of every element in Standing's account, but it is a serious embarrassment for the class claim at its centre, and it aligns with the relational definition's prediction: shared position generates shared demands, however varied the contracts through which the position is occupied.

A defender might reply that classes in the relational sense are too coarse for political analysis, that the permanent worker and the casual worker plainly do not experience the same world, and that a theory refusing to register the difference at the level of its central concept has chosen elegance over truth. The reply underestimates the framework it criticises. Stratification within classes is native to the analysis: the reserve army's forms, the distinction between skilled and unskilled, the labour aristocracy debates, the contradictory locations literature, all exist because the tradition has always needed finer instruments than the two great poles. The question is never whether differences within the working class matter. They demonstrably do, for wages, for politics, for the prospects of organisation. The question is whether a given difference is a difference of class or a difference within class, and that question is settled by the relational test, not by the intensity of the difference. A gulf in lived experience can yawn inside a single class position, as it yawns between the protected core and the stagnant stratum, without either population ceasing to confront capital as sellers of labour power with nothing else to sell.

## What the reversal of the settlement genuinely establishes

Having corrected the baseline and refused the new class, the article must now resist its own momentum, because the corrections, true as they are, do not dispose of the phenomenon that gave the precariat thesis its audience. Within particular economies, within living memory, a settlement that delivered real security to real people was dismantled, and the dismantling was experienced as loss by the people who had organised their lives around its promises. Expectation is a social fact with causal powers. A worker who entered employment expecting continuity, who borrowed against future earnings, formed a household, and planned a retirement on the strength of that expectation, and who then watched the expectation withdrawn, has undergone something that a worker who never held the expectation has not, even if their eventual contracts are identical. The difference lies not in the present condition but in the biography, and politics is made of biographies as much as of conditions. Movements of resentment draw their energy precisely from the gap between the life that was promised and the life that arrived, and an analysis that waves the gap away as a baseline error will understand nothing about the political weather of the economies where the settlement rose and fell.

The reversal establishes several further things that a careful Marxist should state without flinching. It establishes that the securities of the settlement were never rights in the strong sense, inherent in the status of worker, but concessions held on condition, revocable when the conditions that extracted them weakened; the speed and completeness of the revocation is itself evidence about the nature of the original grant. It establishes that the institutional memory of security survives its material basis, in the form of expectations, entitlements claimed as of right, and standards against which new arrangements are judged and found wanting, and that this survival gives labour movements in the former settlement economies a resource their counterparts elsewhere lack: a remembered standard, embodied in living witnesses, that can be pointed to rather than merely imagined. And it establishes that the direction of change, for the populations concerned, was downward, a deterioration in the terms on which labour power is sold, and that describing deterioration accurately is not nostalgia but reportage. The baseline correction disciplines the interpretation of the reversal; it does not annul the reversal, and any use of the correction to annul it is an abuse that this article explicitly refuses.

### Does the reversal of secure employment matter if security was never the norm?

yes, because political life runs on expectations formed within living memory, not on long-run averages. A population that held security and lost it possesses a remembered standard, a grievance with a biography, and a susceptibility to movements promising restoration. The historical correction explains why the loss feels like a fall from normality; it does not make the loss, or its consequences, any less real.

There is also a generational mechanism worth isolating, because it explains how the reversal reproduces itself as a durable political fact rather than a passing complaint. The first generation to lose the settlement experiences the loss as theft, comparing its own trajectory against the trajectory it was promised. The second generation, raised inside the insecure regime, experiences no theft but absorbs the insecurity as the water in which it swims, and its political expressions differ accordingly: less nostalgia, more diffuse anger, weaker attachment to institutions that visibly failed to protect its parents. The third generation may never encounter the settlement even as a family story, at which point the baseline correction becomes, for them, a simple description of the only world they know. Analysts who treat precarity politics as a single phenomenon miss this succession of stances toward the same material condition. The condition persists; the meaning of the condition rotates with the memory available to those living it. Standing's dangerous class warning belongs mostly to the first and second of these stances, where expectation and reality diverge most painfully, and the warning deserves to be heard in that register even by readers who reject the class theory wrapped around it.

The composition question lurks beneath the reversal as well. The working class of the settlement economies was never the homogeneous body of later imagination, and its transformation involves migration, deindustrialisation of some regions and the growth of service employment in others, rising female participation under insecure terms, and the ageing of the protected core into retirement on the settlement's pensions. The companion article asking [whether the working class still exists](/2011/11/01/does-the-working-class-still-exist/) treats that composition question in full. For the purposes of the precarity argument, the relevant point is that the reversal and the recomposition are entangled: many of the workers counted as newly precarious are workers whose categories, women entering paid labour in large numbers, migrants, the young, were always precarious, and their growing weight in the labour force changes the average experience of work without any individual biography of loss. Aggregate deterioration and individual loss are different claims, each requiring its own evidence, and the literature's habit of sliding between them is another form of the baseline sloppiness this article has been resisting throughout.

## The surplus population argument is the sharper version

A distinct body of work, developed largely outside the precariat debate and rarely brought into it, makes a claim that is simultaneously bleaker and more precise. On this account, the significant development is not that employment has grown insecure but that capital increasingly cannot absorb the labour available to it at all. Accumulation, driven by rising productivity, expels labour from production faster than expanding branches reabsorb it, and the result is a growing population that is surplus not in the cyclical sense of a reserve awaiting recall, but in the structural sense of a mass for whom the wage relation offers, at best, intermittent and marginal purchase. Such populations survive through informal economies, subsistence strategies, casual and illegalised work, remittances, and the charity or coercion of states, assembling livelihoods from fragments in which wage labour is one strand among several and often not the largest. The cultural theorist Michael Denning gave this condition its most cited formulation in his account of wageless life, arguing that the wageless, not the wage labourer, are the typical figures of global capitalism when the world rather than the settlement enclave is taken as the frame.

The surplus population argument cuts across the precariat debate in an uncomfortable direction for both parties. Against the precariat thesis, it denies that the defining experience of the insecure is a degraded form of employment from which a new class might crystallise; for the surplus population, employment in any form is the exception in a biography, and the political problem is not the quality of jobs but the absence of a structural place in production from which claims could be pressed. Against complacent uses of the baseline correction, it denies that the present merely restores an old normality. The older irregular worker operated in economies where labour was, in aggregate, scarce and needed; the casual labourer of the nineteenth century port or building site stood a real chance of absorption into expanding industry across a working life, and his grandchildren often achieved it. A surplus population produced by productivity growth confronts a different arithmetic, in which the system's need for its labour declines secularly even as output rises. Irregularity as a station on the way to absorption and irregularity as a permanent condition are materially similar from inside a given week and historically opposite in their trajectories. The baseline discipline, properly applied, distinguishes them, because the baseline for the surplus population is not the settlement but the whole prior history of labour absorption, and against that baseline the development is genuinely new.

### What is the surplus population argument in relation to precarity?

it holds that the decisive trend is not the degradation of jobs but the declining capacity of accumulation to absorb labour at all, producing populations for whom wage work is intermittent or absent and survival depends on informal strategies. Precarity, on this view, is the mildest, most employment-centred region of a much larger territory whose centre is wagelessness rather than insecure wages.

The argument also reconnects the advanced economy debate to the global informality that this series treats in its own right, and the connection changes the moral geography of the subject. Informal survival economies are not a distant backdrop against which the settlement economies conduct their argument about contracts. They are the destination toward which surplus populations inside the wealthy economies are themselves pushed, as formal employment sheds them and as the state's provision thins: the unregistered letting, the unlicensed trade, the cash engagement, the kin network as welfare system. Observers of wealthy cities have documented the growth of such strategies among populations the formal economy cannot place, and the literature on informality, developed for the economies of the global South, increasingly supplies the concepts through which the wealthy economies' own margins are described. That reversal of the usual direction of theory, concepts travelling from the informal economies toward the formal ones, is among the most telling symptoms that the surplus population argument identifies something real. The precariat debate, conducted as an argument about the fate of a European-style working class, misses the scale of its own subject. The surplus population argument restores the scale, at the price of a conclusion nobody finds comfortable: that the central labour question of the coming order may not be the defence of the job but the organisation of life where jobs, in the settlement's sense, are not coming back for a growing share of humanity.

## Occupational identity and the story a working life tells

Among the elements of Standing's account, the loss of occupational identity is the one that survives translation into Marxist terms with the least damage, and it deserves treatment at a length its importance warrants. A trade, in the world the settlement inherited and partly preserved, was not merely a way of earning. It was a narrative device. It told its holder where he had come from, an apprenticeship served and skills certified by the judgement of peers; where he stood, in a recognised rank of competence that commanded a recognised rate; and where he was going, along a path whose later stages, seniority, mastery, the training of others, a pension, were visible from its beginning. The narrative was often confining, and the romance of craft should not be exaggerated, since trades excluded as energetically as they included, and the identity they conferred was purchased with obedience to their hierarchies. But a confining story is still a story, and a person located in a story can plan, can defer, can endure a bad year as an episode rather than experiencing it as the whole text. Much of what the settlement's defenders valued in it, beneath the material securities, was this narratability of a working life: the possibility of treating one's labour as a biography rather than as a sequence of emergencies.

Precarious work dissolves the narrative device without replacing it. Engagements arrive through intermediaries, platforms, agencies, and word of mouth, each disconnected from the last, demanding generic availability rather than accumulated mastery. Skills acquired in one engagement rarely compound in the next, because the next engagement was allocated by an algorithm or a roster indifferent to the worker's history. The question what do you do receives, from the precarious worker, an answer in the present tense and the plural: shifts, tasks, gigs, a list rather than a name. Standing's insight is that this grammatical difference registers a real deprivation. A population that cannot say what it is, in the occupational sense, struggles to say what it is in the political sense either, since class consciousness in the historical record grew substantially out of occupational communities, their lodges, chapels, clubs, and unions, the dense associational life that turned a shared position into a shared identity. Where the occupational substrate thins, the associational superstructure thins with it, and the result is not merely weaker unions but a weaker sense, among the insecure themselves, that their condition is a condition, shared and therefore arguable about, rather than a personal run of bad luck to be privately managed.

The Marxist framework has resources for this analysis that its custodians do not always deploy. The young Marx's account of alienation, whatever its distance from the mature critique of political economy, was precisely an account of labour stripped of narrative and self-recognition, of activity experienced as external and hostile rather than as the worker's own life expressing itself. The reserve army analysis, by contrast, is silent about interior experience, being concerned with functions. A complete Marxist treatment of precarity needs both registers: the functional analysis that locates irregular labour in the mechanics of accumulation, and the experiential analysis that describes what irregularity does to the worker's relation to time, to skill, and to other workers. The precariat literature, at its best, supplies raw material for the second register while botching the first. The tradition's defenders, at their worst, supply the first register and dismiss the second as sentimentality. The synthesis is available to any writer willing to take Standing's phenomenology and Marx's mechanics together, discarding Standing's class theory and the defenders' complacency in the same motion. That synthesis, and not a verdict for either camp, is what this article has been constructing section by section.

Time deserves a paragraph of its own within the phenomenology, because insecurity is at bottom a relation to time. The secure worker sells labour in large blocks and receives, in exchange, not only income but predictability: the right to make promises, to sign a lease, to plan a holiday, to commit to a mortgage or a course of study, to say yes to the future in small enforceable ways. The precarious worker's time is colonised differently. Hours not worked are not leisure but availability, held in readiness for engagements that may or may not materialise, so that the working day expands to fill the week while the paid hours remain few and scattered. Waiting becomes the characteristic activity, waiting for the roster, the message, the call, the season, and waiting of this kind is labour in everything but payment and recognition. Sociologists of insecure work return repeatedly to this temporal expropriation, and it explains a paradox that purely income-based measures miss: two workers with identical annual earnings, one secure and one precarious, do not have identical lives, because the second has paid for the same income with a far larger tribute of uncommitted, unusable, anxious time. Any measure of precarity that counts only money will understate the condition, and any politics that promises only money will fail to relieve it.

## Risk, its transfer, and the mechanics of insecurity

Strip the vocabulary away and precarity names a transfer. Every employment relation allocates a set of risks: the risk that demand falls and labour is not needed, the risk that the worker falls sick or grows old, the risk that skills obsolesce, the risk that income fluctuates beyond the household's capacity to smooth it. The settlement allocated an unusual share of those risks to employers and to collective insurance, which is to say it socialised them across enterprises and across the working population. Precarious arrangements allocate the same risks to the individual worker, who is the party least able to bear them, having neither reserves nor diversification nor any capacity to pool. The transfer, and not any change in the total quantum of risk in the economy, is the material core of the phenomenon. Demand for labour was always variable; what changed, in the economies where the settlement was dismantled, is who holds the variability when it arrives. An enterprise that engages labour by the hour and dismisses it by the hour has not eliminated the cost of slack demand. It has relocated the cost to the household budgets of its workers, where it appears as arrears, debt, deferred medical care, and the slow consumption of savings and kinship credit, invisible in the enterprise's accounts and therefore available to be celebrated as efficiency.

Debt is the hinge between the transfer of risk and the discipline of the workforce, and the precarity literature is at its weakest when it treats debt as a separate topic. Irregular income in a society whose obligations, rent, utilities, food, transport, are regular and unforgiving generates borrowing as surely as a leaking hull generates bailing. The precarious worker borrows to smooth, borrows to survive the gap between engagements, borrows at the punitive rates reserved for borrowers without security, and thereby acquires a second master alongside the labour market: the repayment schedule, which does not negotiate. Indebtedness then performs the disciplinary function that the reserve army performed in Marx's account, and performs it more intimately. The worker in debt cannot refuse a bad engagement, cannot risk a gap for the sake of a better one, cannot withdraw labour in a dispute without courting default, and experiences each of these constraints not as the visible pressure of an employer's threat but as the private arithmetic of obligation. Analysts who ask why insecure workers so rarely organise receive, in the debt relation, a large part of their answer. Organisation requires slack: time to attend, money to risk, a horizon beyond the month. Precarity systematically consumes slack, and debt consumes what precarity leaves. The condition is self-reinforcing in a way that a merely contractual description cannot capture, which is why the better writers on the subject, across traditions, keep returning to the household and its balance sheet as the true site where insecurity is lived.

The household, indeed, is where the transfer finally lands, and feminist political economy has mapped the landing in detail. When income fluctuates and public provision thins, the work of absorption falls to unpaid domestic labour: the labour of stretching food, of negotiating with creditors, of nursing without services, of caring for children and elders whom purchased care no longer covers, of holding together the emotional fabric that insecurity frays. That labour is overwhelmingly performed by women, and it expands exactly as the wage relation contracts, so that a full accounting of precarity would measure not only the insecurity of the employed but the intensification of the unpaid work that subsidises it. The settlement's household model at least acknowledged the arrangement, building a wage norm around a supported dependant. The precarious regime enjoys the subsidy while denying the acknowledgment, treating each worker as an unencumbered individual whose dependants, debts, and caring obligations are private externalities. A Marxist analysis recognises the pattern instantly, because the reproduction of labour power has always been the hidden foundation of the wage relation in this tradition's account; precarity is, among other things, a crisis of reproduction, in which the costs of renewing the workforce are pushed ever more completely onto the workforce itself, until the strain appears as demographic and social pathologies that commentators then discuss as though they were cultural novelties unrelated to the labour market.

### How does insecurity discipline workers who are never threatened directly?

through the arithmetic of irregular income and regular obligation. Rent, food, and repayments fall due on schedule while engagements arrive unscheduled, so the worker internalises the employer's discipline as private necessity. Refusing bad work, waiting for better work, or risking a dispute all threaten default, and the threat operates continuously without any manager needing to utter it.

Against this mechanics, the cheerful counter-literature of flexibility deserves its moment of scrutiny. In that literature, irregular engagement figures as freedom: freedom from the tyranny of the single employer, freedom to compose a portfolio of activities, freedom to work when one chooses and rest when one chooses, the entrepreneurial self realising at last the autonomy that the factory and the office denied. The description is not false of everyone. A minority of the insecure, those with scarce skills, savings, and strong demand for their services, do experience contractual freedom as genuine autonomy, and their testimony is honestly given. The error is generalisation. Autonomy in the labour market is a function of bargaining power, and bargaining power is a function of alternatives, and for the great mass of the insecure the alternative to a bad engagement is not a better engagement but no engagement, which converts the celebrated freedom to refuse into a freedom exercised mainly in speeches. The same contractual form, the short engagement, the project contract, the zero-commitment roster, is liberation at the top of the skill distribution and compulsion at the bottom, and a literature that averages the two experiences into a celebration of the gig economy class has committed the baseline error in a new key: it has taken the experience of a subset, the subset with options, as the character of the whole. The subset clause, which corrected the settlement's mythology, corrects the mythology of its dismantling as well.

## Platform work as the emblem and the distraction

No discussion of precarity escapes the platform for long, and this article must handle the emblem without being captured by it. Work mediated by digital platforms, summoned by application, priced by algorithm, rated by customers, and stripped of the legal status of employment by contractual design, presents the features of precarity in a concentrated and photogenic form. The platform worker has no workplace to organise in, no visible employer to confront, no colleagues in the flesh, and no continuity beyond the next allocation, while the platform itself accumulates the asset that matters, the network and its data, from the labour of people it declines to employ. The full Marxist treatment of that business model is given in the companion piece on [platform capitalism marxist analysis](/2020/11/15/platform-capitalism-marxist-analysis/), and the disambiguation discipline of this series assigns the model's economics there. What belongs here is the platform's function in the precarity debate, which is double: it is genuinely a laboratory in which the transfer of risk reaches a kind of perfection, and it is also a distraction, whose novelty and visibility draw attention away from the far larger territories of insecure work that involve no application and no algorithm at all.

The distraction effect deserves emphasis because it distorts both scholarship and politics. Insecure work in cleaning, care, hospitality, agriculture's modern successors, warehousing, delivery by conventional fleets, construction's long casual traditions, and the vast informal economies of the world dwarfs platform work in headcount, yet the debate's centre of gravity sits on the platform, because the platform is new, is legible to journalists, and implicates the professional classes as consumers in a way that a casualised cleaning contract does not. The result is a literature whose emblem is unrepresentative of its subject, rather as though a literature on poverty took the lottery winner as its emblem because his story was vivid. A baseline discipline helps here too. Platform work is best understood not as a rupture but as a recombination: the piece rate, the casual hiring hall, the putting-out system, and the employer's evasion of responsibility for the worker's maintenance are all old devices, and the platform's contribution is to automate their administration and to scale them beyond the local labour market. Marx's analysis of piece wages, as a form that disguises the wage relation and intensifies self-exploitation, reads across to the platform's payment systems with embarrassing ease, embarrassing, that is, for claims of unprecedented novelty. The worker paid by the task, competing against an invisible pool, driven by the rate to lengthen and intensify the working day without any overseer's command, would have been recognised by any nineteenth century observer of sweated labour. The telephone in the worker's pocket is new. The relation it mediates is not.

### Is the platform worker the typical precarious worker?

no. Platform work is the most visible and most discussed form of insecure work, but the larger part of precarious employment sits in cleaning, care, warehousing, hospitality, casual building work, and the informal economies, where no algorithm administers the insecurity. Treating the platform as typical mistakes the emblem for the territory and directs political attention toward a minority of the affected population.

Yet the laboratory function is real, and dismissing platforms as mere recombination would repeat the error of complacency in a new form. What the platform perfects is the separation of control from responsibility. Earlier casual employers exercised crude control and evaded responsibility imperfectly, constrained by the visibility of the hiring hall and the physical presence of the workforce. The platform exercises control of unprecedented granularity, over pace, route, price, and conduct, monitored continuously and enforced automatically, while its contractual architecture disclaims the responsibilities that control historically attracted. That combination, total direction without reciprocal obligation, is a genuine development in the form of the labour relation, even though every component has a pedigree. It matters for the precarity argument because it demonstrates the direction of institutional innovation under the pressure to transfer risk: not toward the abolition of control, as the rhetoric of independent contracting suggests, but toward its refinement, control becoming more continuous as commitment becomes more completely absent. A Marxist reading fastens on exactly this asymmetry. The capital relation was always, at its core, the purchase of labour power combined with the direction of its use; the platform strips away the incidental solidarities that the settlement attached to that core and exhibits the core itself, polished. In that sense the platform is less the future of work than the truth of work under the wage relation, with the settlement's wrappings removed, which is why it fascinates and why it frightens in proportions beyond its headcount.

## Measuring the condition without inventing its scale

A word about evidence, since this article has deliberately withheld figures for the quantities readers most expect to be given. The protocol governing this treatment forbids figures for contract types, self-employment, union density, and employment shares, and the prohibition is not an eccentricity. Such figures are the most perishable objects in labour market writing: they are revised, redefined, and overtaken by events, and an article built on them becomes a museum of expired magnitudes within a few years, its argument hostage to numbers its author can no longer defend. Worse, the figures in this field are unusually sensitive to definition. Whether a worker counts as temporary, as self-employed, as marginally attached, or as underemployed depends on survey instruments and legal categories that differ across jurisdictions and shift across time, so that a figure quoted without its instrument is not a fact but a rumour with decimals. The research literature on insecure work, the comparative labour market studies and the long-running sociological investigations of employment change, handles these instruments with appropriate caution, and this article's practice is to name that literature as the place where magnitudes live, while stating its own claims in the directional and structural terms that magnitudes would illustrate but cannot overturn.

Directionally, then, and with the literature's authority behind the direction: across the decades in which the settlement was dismantled, the incidence of engagements lacking the standard protections grew within the economies concerned, the share of workers able to treat employment as a predictable foundation for a household shrank, and the exposure of household income to fluctuation increased, while at the same time, and this is the point the alarmist wing of the literature omits, standard employment remained the experience of a large part, on most careful readings the greater part, of the workforce in the wealthy economies. Both halves of that sentence must be carried together. The first half refutes the complacent claim that precarity is a journalistic invention. The second half refutes the claim, implicit in much precariat rhetoric, that standard employment has already collapsed into a minority condition and that the politics of the future is the politics of a universally insecure mass. The research literature associated with comparative employment studies, and the careful national investigations that feed it, supports the conjunction: a substantial and growing insecure stratum alongside a still extensive standard sector, with the boundary between them porous, contested, and politically charged. That conjunction is less thrilling than either collapse or denial. It has the compensating virtue of being what the evidence, handled carefully, shows.

### Has insecure work become the typical experience in wealthy economies?

on the careful readings in the comparative research literature, no. Standard employment, with its protections attenuated but its form intact, remains the condition of a large share of the workforce, while the insecure stratum is substantial, growing over the decades of dismantling, and concentrated among the young, among women returning to paid work, among migrants, and among workers in the least organised employments.

The majority question matters politically out of proportion to its analytical difficulty, because the two answers license opposite strategies. If precarity were already the universal condition, then the politics appropriate to it would be the politics of a new universal class, and Standing's framework would be the natural vehicle, whatever its theoretical defects. If precarity is instead a large and growing stratum within a working class whose majority retains some form of standard employment, then the fitting politics is the older and harder politics of unification: the construction of demands and organisations that bind the strata together, extending the protections of the core outward rather than abandoning the core's standards as a lost world's relics. The history of labour movements offers examples of both strategies and a clear verdict on their relative success. Movements that organised the unorganised by extending established standards, the great organising drives that brought casual and marginal workers inside the settlement's protections during its expansion, enlarged both the stratum's security and the movement's power. Movements that proclaimed the established standards dead and the established organisations obsolete have generally succeeded mainly in weakening the only instruments through which standards have ever been extended. The majority question, which looks like a statistician's quarrel, is therefore the strategic question of the whole debate in disguise, and the care this article has taken with baselines and definitions is justified by nothing so much as by the consequences that flow from getting it wrong.

## The critics of the precariat concept and what their criticisms establish

The precariat concept has attracted a critical literature substantial enough to constitute a position in its own right, and an honest survey must give that literature its due before this article's verdict is pronounced. The critics are not a chorus; they arrive from different directions, with different stakes, and their objections do not all coexist comfortably. What they share is a refusal to grant the concept the status its author claims for it, and a suspicion that the concept's popularity owes more to the experience of a particular stratum of commentators, educated workers who encountered insecurity as a shocking novelty in employments that had sheltered their parents, than to the condition of the global working class, for whom the shock is unintelligible because the shelter never existed.

The historical objection has been this article's centre and need only be summarised here: the precariat's defining condition is labour's historical norm under capitalism, and a concept that presents the norm as an emergent class mistakes the end of an exception for the birth of a formation. The sociological objection cuts differently. The population gathered under the precariat label is, on inspection, extraordinarily heterogeneous: it includes the downwardly mobile graduate and the lifelong casual labourer, the migrant without papers and the citizen with a mortgage, the young worker in a first engagement and the older worker expelled from a protected post, and these populations share little beyond the absence of security, which is a negative property, a lack, and lacks make poor foundations for class formation, since a class must be held together by a positive common relation and not merely by a common deprivation. Critics working in the Marxist tradition press the relational objection developed earlier in this article: class defined by employment condition confuses the stratification of the working class with its division into classes, and multiplies entities beyond analytical necessity. Critics from the feminist and postcolonial directions press the coverage objection: the concept generalises the biography of the formerly protected, treating as a world-historical emergence what is, from the standpoint of women workers and the colonised world, the arrival of the metropolitan core at a condition its margins never left. Each objection has force. Their combined effect is not to show that nothing has changed, a conclusion none of the serious critics holds, but to show that the concept of a new class is the wrong instrument for registering the change, an instrument that misdates it, mislocates it, and misdescribes the population it claims to name.

### From which directions do the concept's critics attack it?

they argue from several directions that converge on one point: insecurity is historically normal, internally heterogeneous, and defined by a lack rather than by a shared relation to production, so it cannot constitute a class. The critics generally accept that the dismantling of the postwar settlement was real and consequential; their quarrel is with the concept built on it, not with the experience the concept was built to honour.

Standing's replies to his critics deserve recording, because a debate in which only one side is summarised has not been surveyed but staged. He responds to the historical objection by granting the long record and insisting that the conjunction is new: insecurity combined with the loss of entitlements that had been won, experienced by an educated population with expectations formed by the settlement and by the promises of meritocracy, in economies rich enough that the insecurity appears as a political choice rather than a fate. He responds to the heterogeneity objection by embracing it, arguing that classes in formation are always heterogeneous, that the English working class of Thompson's account was a congeries of trades and conditions before it was a class, and that shared grievances against a common structure of denial can weld a formation out of disparate materials. He responds to the relational objection by questioning the sufficiency of the production relation for an era in which distribution, through entitlements, transfers, and access to the commons of public provision, shapes life chances as powerfully as the wage. These replies are not negligible, and the first of them, the conjunction reply, identifies the genuine residue that this article has also identified under the heading of reversal and lost expectation. Where the replies fail, in this article's judgement, is at the final step: nothing in the conjunction, the heterogeneity, or the distributional emphasis converts a stratum's shared deprivation into a class relation, and the political programme Standing builds on the concept, centred on a basic income detached from employment, follows from the misdiagnosis, proposing to compensate a population for its exclusion from secure work rather than to contest the organisation of work itself. Readers who want the argument between the hub of this cluster and the present article can pursue it through [marxism and modern capitalism](/2020/11/01/marxism-and-modern-capitalism/), where the series frames the modern capitalism debates as a whole.

The debate also has a sociology of its own, which a candid article should not suppress. Concepts in the social sciences succeed partly by fitting the experience of the audiences positioned to amplify them, and the precariat fitted, with uncanny precision, the experience of the educated metropolitan strata who write, publish, teach, and broadcast: people whose parents held the settlement's protected posts, whose own employments in universities, media, and the cultural industries were casualised within a generation, and who encountered in their own biographies the fall the concept describes. For such an audience the concept did not need to argue; it needed only to name. The global working class, whose experience the concept fits far less well, does not review books. This asymmetry of amplification does not refute the concept, since a concept's popularity among the literate is evidence about the literate and not about the concept's truth. But it explains the otherwise puzzling disproportion between the concept's fame and its analytical yield, and it supplies one more reason for the discipline this article has practised: when a theory flatters its audience's biography, the baseline checks must be run twice.

## Misreadings that the baseline correction removes

A series article earns its place partly by the errors it retires, and the precarity field offers a generous stock. The first misreading is the golden age assumption itself: that secure employment was the historical norm from which the present has fallen. The baseline table retires it. Security was a bounded achievement, and every argument that treats its loss as a fall from the natural order must be rewritten as an argument about a specific settlement, its conditions, and its dismantling, which is a better argument in every way, being arguable. The second misreading is the class claim: that the precariat constitutes a class in the Marxist sense. The relational test retires it. The insecure are a stratum of the working class, internally varied, whose condition the tradition's categories already reach. The third misreading inverts the second and is common among the tradition's own defenders: that Marxism, having a category for irregular work, has nothing to learn from the precarity literature and no gap in its account. The concession about lost expectation and occupational identity retires that complacency. A category built for populations that never held security does not describe the experience of populations that held it and lost it, and the experience is politically operative whether the theory names it or not.

The fourth misreading concerns scale: that precarity has already replaced standard employment across the advanced economies, leaving a universally insecure workforce. The research literature, read carefully and stated directionally, retires it. Standard employment persists for a large share of the workforce, and the political task is unification across the boundary, not the proclamation of a new universal subject. The fifth misreading is the mirror of the fourth: that because standard employment persists, precarity is a marginal or invented concern. The growth of the insecure stratum across the decades of dismantling, the transfer of risk it embodies, and the surplus population dynamics at the system's margins retire that comfort. The sixth misreading is subtler and infects even careful writers: that the historical correction settles the political question, that once the golden age is exposed as myth, the grievances built on its memory evaporate. Expectations do not evaporate when their genealogy is exposed. They persist as claims, and a politics that cannot address claims rooted in a partly mythical past will lose to a politics that addresses them dishonestly. The correction is an analytical instrument. Wielded as a sedative, it becomes the seventh misreading's accomplice: the belief that being right about the baseline is a substitute for organising the people whose lives the baseline describes.

### Which misreading does the most damage in public discussion of insecure work?

the golden age assumption, because it silently organises all the others. Once a brief and partial settlement is installed as the natural order, the insecure past disappears, the insecure present looks like an aberration requiring a new class to explain it, and political argument degenerates into rival nostalgias instead of addressing the mechanisms that allocate risk between employers and workers.

There is a pedagogical point to be drawn from the catalogue, and it generalises beyond this subject to the pattern this article instantiates for the series. Claims about change are only ever as good as their account of the prior state, and the prior state is where advocacy does its quiet work, because a prior state described loosely cannot be checked. The baseline-correction pattern therefore recommends a habit of reading: whenever a text announces that something has become, has turned, has been lost, has emerged, the reader's first question should be comparative and concrete. Compared with when, exactly, and where, and for whom? A text that can answer has earned its claim of change. A text that cannot has located its conclusion in its premise, and the reader who has learned to ask will watch a surprising fraction of celebrated theses, in this field and others, dissolve into their baselines. The habit is teachable, it transfers across subjects, and it is arguably worth more than any single verdict this article delivers, since verdicts expire with their literatures and habits of reading do not.

## The strongest objection, stated fairly and answered

The objection that this article must face at full strength is not the weak claim that insecurity is unpleasant, which nobody denies, but the strong claim that the older categories, whatever their functional coverage, fail to name a distinct social situation that has come into being. Put in its most formidable form, the objection runs as follows. Workers in specific economies experienced, within a single working lifetime, a settlement that delivered security and then its removal. The resulting condition is not simply the old irregularity restored, because it is lived by people formed for a different world: educated for occupations that no longer offer continuity, promised that credentials would purchase stability, and then delivered into a labour market that honours neither the promise nor the credential. The condition includes the loss of occupational identity and of the entitlements that once accompanied employment, and it is accompanied by a distinctive political psychology, a sense of betrayal by institutions that older irregular workers, who expected nothing, could not feel. A category designed for a surplus population that never held expectations cannot name betrayal, and a theory that cannot name the operative political emotion of a population will be routed around by movements that can. Therefore, the objection concludes, something like the precariat concept is necessary, whatever its theoretical untidiness, because politics is conducted in names and the old names do not fit the new experience.

The answer begins by conceding the objection's factual core in full. The loss of an expectation is a real phenomenon, distinct from the absence of an expectation, and the reserve army analysis, built in a period when the stagnant stratum had never been promised anything, does not capture it. The phenomenology of betrayal, the collapse of occupational narrative, the educated worker's discovery that the credential was a lottery ticket rather than a deed, all of this is data that a Marxist account of the period must register, and the precarity literature has registered it more vividly than the tradition's own writing has. Where the answer parts from the objection is at the inference from experience to class. A distinct experience, even a distinct and politically potent one, establishes a distinct stratum, a distinct mood, a distinct political problem. It does not establish a distinct position within the relations of production, and class in this framework names the relation, not the mood. The population of the betrayed sells labour power, owns no productive property, and confronts employers whose power over it derives from that asymmetry, exactly as the protected worker does and exactly as the lifelong casual worker does. Its interests, when traced to their material root, align with theirs: security, income, protection, voice, the very goods whose loss it mourns. The objection proves that the working class contains a stratum with a biography unlike any the nineteenth century produced. The framework absorbs that proof without strain, because the framework never claimed that the class's strata share biographies. It claimed they share a position, and the objection, for all its force, leaves the position untouched.

### What would a Marxist have to give up to accept the precariat as a class?

the relational definition of class itself. Accepting employment condition as a class criterion means allowing administrative categories, which employers and legislatures redesign continuously, to redraw the class map with each redesign. The tradition would gain a fashionable label and lose the explanatory anchor that connects class to the ownership of productive property and the extraction of surplus value.

A second answer addresses the objection's political sting, the claim that movements will route around a theory that cannot name betrayal. The premise is sound and the conclusion does not follow. Naming is not the monopoly of class concepts. The tradition names strata, moods, and moments constantly: it has a vocabulary of betrayal, of broken compacts, of generations formed and cheated, in its historical writing, and nothing prevents its political writing from naming the lost settlement and the population orphaned by it with all the vividness the subject deserves. What the framework refuses is only the promotion of the name into a class, with the theoretical commitments the promotion smuggles. The refusal is not a failure of empathy but a defence of the instrument panel: a movement that misidentifies its own composition will misidentify its opponents and its programme, demanding recognition for a new class where it should be demanding the extension of security to a whole class, and building institutions for a stratum where it should be rebuilding institutions for all who sell their labour. Precision about what the insecure are is, in the end, a form of respect for what they need.

## Organisation among workers who share no workplace

If precarity is a stratum within the working class rather than a class apart, the practical question becomes organisational: how do workers who share no workplace, no shift pattern, no occupational identity, and frequently no legal status as employees come to act together at all? The historical record of casual labour organisation, usually omitted from the precarity debate, is more encouraging than the debate's pessimism suggests, precisely because casual workers have faced the problem before. The hiring hall, in the industries where it was won, was an organisational technology for exactly this condition: it converted a daily scramble at a gate, in which workers competed by underbidding and favour, into a collective allocation of engagements governed by rules the workers' organisation administered. The technology mattered less than the principle it embodied, that the allocation of insecure work is itself a terrain of struggle, and that workers who cannot yet win security can win a say in how insecurity is distributed, which is a foothold from which security itself becomes arguable. Modern equivalents recur wherever insecure workers organise: demands for guaranteed minimum engagements, for rosters published in advance, for the conversion of repeated casual engagements into continuing ones, for portable entitlements that travel with the worker between employers rather than dying with each contract. Each demand accepts the terrain of irregularity and contests its terms, and the lineage from the hiring hall to the portable entitlement is direct, a continuity the new class framework, with its insistence on novelty, is structurally unable to see.

The obstacles are formidable and should not be sentimentalised. Turnover dissolves organisation as fast as it forms, since the membership of a casual workforce changes faster than any structure of dues and meetings can stabilise. Fear is rational where the next engagement is discretionary, and the worker who is visible as an organiser can be punished by simple non-selection, a sanction that leaves no documentary trace and against which legal remedies, designed for dismissal from continuing employment, barely bite. Competition is engineered into the allocation systems themselves, whose ratings and rankings set worker against worker in a continuous audition that makes solidarity feel like a competitive disadvantage. And the employer's legal form, interposed agencies, franchises, platforms, and contracting chains, diffuses the target of organisation until responsibility for any given worker's condition can be passed along the chain indefinitely. Against these obstacles the organising that has succeeded among insecure workers shares recognisable features in the literature: it organises around the allocation point rather than the workplace, it recruits through community and kinship networks that survive turnover, it aims its early demands at the rules of allocation and the floor of income rather than at the full settlement programme, and it builds portable institutions, benefit funds, training schemes, and legal services that belong to the worker and persist across engagements, giving membership a value that no single employer's displeasure can cancel.

### Why is the allocation of work itself a terrain of struggle for insecure workers?

because where employment is irregular, the rules deciding who receives engagements, how often, and on what notice determine income as directly as the wage rate does. Workers who cannot immediately win continuing employment can still contest allocation: its fairness, its transparency, its freedom from favour and punishment. Historical hiring halls and modern roster campaigns are the same struggle in different dress.

There is a lesson in this organisational record for the theoretical dispute, and it cuts against the new class framework from an unexpected side. The insecure workers who have organised most effectively have done so, overwhelmingly, by joining or building the general institutions of labour and bending them toward the allocation problem, not by founding institutions of a separate class. Their demands have been the classical demands, security, protection, representation, extended to populations the classical institutions had neglected, and their rhetoric has claimed membership of the working class rather than exemption from it. Practice, in other words, has voted for the relational analysis. The stratum knows itself to be part of the class, ill-served by the class's institutions in their settlement-era form, and bent on reforming those institutions rather than replacing the class with a more fashionable category. Theorists who announce a new class on the stratum's behalf might attend to the stratum's own verdict, delivered in the only poll that matters, the choice of banners under which it is willing to fight.

## Entitlements, status, and the quiet architecture of security

Security, examined closely, is rarely a property of the job itself. It is a property of the statuses that surround the job and of the entitlements those statuses unlock. The settlement's achievement was less the permanent contract, considered as a piece of paper, than the dense architecture that the contract anchored: insurance against sickness funded by contributions the employment generated, a pension accumulating invisibly across the working life, protection of income during unemployment that treated joblessness as a social risk rather than a personal failure, housing and credit markets that lent against the predictable wage, and a legal presumption, in disputes, that the employment relationship was continuing unless ended for cause. Strip the architecture away and the contract alone would have delivered a much thinner security, as workers in formally permanent but institutionally bare employments have always known. Precarity, correspondingly, is best defined not by the shortness of engagements but by exclusion from the architecture: the precarious worker is the one whose engagements generate no accumulating claims, whose sickness is uninsured, whose old age is unprovided, whose unemployment is unindemnified, and whose income, however adequate in a good month, cannot be borrowed against or planned upon because no institution will certify its continuation.

This architectural view clarifies several puzzles in the literature. It explains why two workers with identical contracts can inhabit different worlds of security, one inside a web of contribution records, recognised status, and accumulated claims, the other outside it, because an interruption, a migration, a period of informal work, broke the chain of contributions on which the architecture runs. It explains why the loss of entitlements features so centrally in Standing's account, out of proportion, on a contractual view, to its importance: he perceived, correctly, that the settlement's real substance was its architecture, and that the dismantling proceeded as much by reclassifying workers out of the statuses that carried entitlements as by shortening their contracts. Reclassification is the quiet mechanism of the whole reversal. A worker need not be dismissed to be precaritised; it suffices that the engagement be relabelled, as self-employment, as agency supply, as a service contract between businesses, and the architecture detaches from the worker while the work itself continues unchanged at the same bench, on the same roster, under the same supervision. The legal form migrates; the labour stands still; the security evaporates. Any analysis that tracks contracts rather than statuses will systematically undercount this process, which is one reason official magnitudes in this field so often lag the lived experience they purport to measure.

### Why does reclassification precaritise a worker whose tasks never change?

because security resides in the entitlements attached to employment status, not in the tasks performed. Relabel an engagement as self-employment or agency supply and the contributions, insurance, pension accumulation, and legal presumptions detach from the worker, while the work, the supervision, and the workplace continue exactly as before. The labour stands still; only its legal shadow moves.

The architectural view also indicates where a politics of security could be rebuilt without pretending that the settlement's exact form can be restored. Entitlements can, in principle, be attached to the worker rather than to the job: contribution records that aggregate across engagements, insurance that follows the worker, pension accumulation indifferent to the number of employers, and income-smoothing mechanisms that treat fluctuation as the normal case for a growing stratum rather than as an anomaly. Such designs are discussed across the research and policy literature in generic terms, and this article, bound by its rule against naming policies, states only the structural point: the settlement attached its architecture to a particular employment form, the standard relationship, and the architecture's dependence on that form is what made the dismantling of the form so destructive. A security rebuilt on the person would be harder to dismantle by reclassification, since there would be no status boundary left to push workers across. Whether such a reconstruction is compatible with the deeper dynamics of accumulation is a harder question, and the Marxist tradition's scepticism has its grounds: entitlements conceded under one balance of forces are eroded under another, whatever their legal anchoring, and the history rehearsed in this article, of a settlement granted and revoked within living memory, is the standing illustration. Architecture matters, and so does the balance of forces that decides whether architecture stands.

## Credentials, youth, and the queue for the shrinking core

The queue is the image that organises the experience of the young in the labour markets this article describes. Where the settlement offered school leavers a broad gate into standard employment, the dismantled regime offers a narrow one, guarded by credentials, internships, probationary engagements, and successive filters, with a lengthening queue of qualified applicants competing for each protected place. The queue's length is the mechanism by which insecurity disciplines even those who will eventually escape it: years spent in irregular engagements, in unpaid or underpaid trials, in the accumulation of certificates whose value inflates away as the queue behind acquires them too, are years in which the worker's bargaining power is nil and the worker's expectations are retrained downward. Credential inflation, the process by which the qualification required for a given post rises as the supply of qualified applicants grows, converts education from a ladder into a toll, paid by all, decisive for few. The graduate who emerges, indebted, into casual work for which the degree was formally unnecessary has lived the central irony of the precarity literature's educated stratum, and the sense of fraudulence, of a contract between effort and reward that society has broken, is the emotional fuel of that stratum's politics.

A Marxist analysis places the queue inside the reserve army framework without difficulty, and the fit is instructive. The queue is the reserve army in its educated form: a surplus of qualified labour power relative to the posts that would employ it at the expected standard, pressing down the wages and conditions of those in the posts and reconciling those outside them to engagements beneath their qualifications. What the framework adds to the precarity literature's description is the causal direction. The queue is not a malfunction of an otherwise sound matching process between education and employment. It is the predictable product of an accumulation regime that raises productivity faster than it expands protected employment, while the education system, driven by families' entirely rational pursuit of advantage, expands the supply of credentials into the narrowing gate. Each family's decision is sensible; the aggregate is a queue in which the credential's scarcity value dissolves. The literature on overeducation and graduate underemployment documents the result across the settlement's former territories, and its findings, stated directionally, are consistent: a substantial stratum of the highly educated now passes its youth, and in many cases its whole working life, in employments that neither require its education nor deliver the security the education was purchased to obtain.

### How does the queue for protected jobs discipline workers who eventually obtain one?

by consuming the years in which resistance might have formed and by retraining expectations downward before the protected post is reached. A worker who has spent a decade in irregular engagements, grateful for each, arrives at security with habits of compliance and a fear of return to the queue that employers can rely on more surely than on any rule in the handbook.

Age draws the second map of the condition, complementary to youth's. At the far end of the working life, precarity appears as expulsion and non-return: the older worker displaced from a protected post discovers that the queue for re-entry is ordered by assumptions about adaptability and remaining years that no accumulation of experience can overcome, and descends into the irregular economy for the remainder of a working life that pension arrangements, designed for continuous contribution, will reward with inadequacy. Between the young queueing to enter and the old expelled from re-entry, the protected core shrinks toward a mid-life enclave, and the biography of security contracts from a working life to a phase within it. This contraction is among the least discussed and most consequential features of the dismantling, because it dissolves the settlement's implicit life-course promise, that insecurity was a brief apprenticeship of youth preceding a long plateau of stability. Where insecurity brackets the working life at both ends and seeps inward from each, the plateau itself is redefined, and the populations experiencing precarity cease to be a stage through which workers pass and become, for a growing share, the condition in which whole lives are spent. The surplus population argument returns here with renewed force, since a life spent bracketing a core one never enters is wagelessness by instalments, and the boundary between the precarious worker and the surplus population, so important to the theorists, is from the standpoint of the life lived a boundary within a single biography.

## Housing, place, and the geography of the insecure

Insecurity has an address. The precarious worker's relation to place is governed by the same transfer of risk that governs the relation to time: where the secure wage underwrote a mortgage, a long tenancy, a settled presence in a community whose institutions could be joined and whose future could be invested in, the irregular income underwrites only arrangements that can be abandoned or endured at short notice. Short tenancies, shared and subdivided dwellings, dependence on kin for shelter, migration in pursuit of engagements, and the acceptance of distance between home and work that consumes the unpaid hours around the paid ones, these are the residential forms of the condition, and they compound its other features with mechanical reliability. A worker who may have to move cannot build the local ties through which casual work is often found and through which collective organisation has historically recruited. A worker whose tenancy is insecure experiences the landlord as a second employer, with the same discretionary power over the continuity of life and the same absence of reciprocal obligation. A worker priced out of the districts where engagements concentrate pays for access in commuting time, the unpaid tribute that the geography of insecure labour exacts before the working day begins.

Place also sorts the condition politically in ways the national aggregates conceal. Insecure work concentrates where land and housing are cheapest or where demand for casual services is densest, and the two geographies are opposite: the declining district whose protected industries vanished, leaving a population stranded among the ruins of the settlement's physical plant, and the booming metropolitan core whose wealth generates an archipelago of insecure service labour amid general prosperity. The politics of the two places differ, as the literature on left-behind regions and on metropolitan precarity respectively records, and a national precarity politics must speak to both: to the place where insecurity arrived as collapse, remembered against a local golden age that was real for that place whatever its limits elsewhere, and to the place where insecurity is the permanent underside of success, invisible to the prosperous at the distance of a delivered meal. The baseline discipline travels well into this geography. Each place has its own baseline, its own remembered settlement or absence of one, and arguments that average across places commit the same error as arguments that average across periods: they describe a location that exists nowhere and persuade no one who lives anywhere.

### Why does insecure work so often mean insecure housing as well?

because landlords and lenders price continuity as employers once provided it. Irregular income cannot certify itself to a mortgage market or command a long tenancy on favourable terms, so the worker pays more for worse shelter, accepts shorter and more terminable arrangements, and holds a home on the same discretionary basis as the engagements that pay for it.

The household forms that grow under these pressures deserve the same unsentimental attention the article has given the labour forms. Multigenerational living extended into adult life, the return of grown children to parental homes, the doubling up of families within dwellings built for one, the postponement of household formation and of childbearing into years the settlement's biography reserved for consolidation, all are recorded across the literature of the dismantling economies, and all represent the private absorption of a public transfer: the housing and care that wages and entitlements once funded are supplied instead by the unpaid capacities of kin networks, until those networks themselves are exhausted. Demographers register the postponements as cultural change, a shift in the values of the young. The materialist reading is less flattering to the culture: a generation that cannot certify an income cannot sign the contracts, of tenancy, of credit, of marriage's economic partnership, on which the older biography was built, and its values are the rationalisation of its constraints. Here again the precarity literature's contribution is descriptive vividness, and here again the Marxist framework supplies the mechanism the description lacks, locating the postponed lives in the same transfer of risk that postpones everything else the insecure might otherwise begin.

## Health, exhaustion, and the body as the final insurer

Every transfer of risk terminates somewhere, and where institutions refuse it and households exhaust their capacity to absorb it, the terminus is the worker's body. Insecurity is a physiological condition before it is a statistic. The irregular worker sleeps worse, in housing that is noisier and more crowded, on schedules that rotate against the body's rhythms, with the low continuous arousal of anticipated emergency that the literature on stress physiology associates with cumulative damage to cardiovascular and immune function. Engagements are accepted when the body protests, because the engagement refused in sickness may not be offered in health, and the insurance that would convert sickness from catastrophe into inconvenience is precisely what the precarious status excludes. Injuries are worked through, symptoms are deferred, and the deferral compounds, so that the health gap between secure and insecure workers, documented across the epidemiological literature in directional terms consistent enough to survive every methodological quarrel, widens with age into a gap in life expectancy itself. The settlement, whatever its limits, had recognised the body as a collective charge: sickness insurance, industrial injury compensation, and limits on hours were among its earliest components, won by movements that understood exhaustion as a political fact. The dismantling re-privatised the body along with everything else, and the worker's health became the final insurer of the enterprise's flexibility, absorbing shocks that no balance sheet records.

The mental economy of the condition follows the same gradient. Anxiety in the insecure worker is not a disorder to be treated in isolation from its object; it is an accurate registration of a situation in which the next month's income, housing, and standing are genuinely undecided, and the clinical literature's finding that job insecurity corrodes mental health as reliably as unemployment itself, and sometimes more insidiously because the sufferer retains the obligations of employment without its consolations, should be read as a measurement of the condition rather than as a commentary on the sufferer's resilience. A politics that responds to this corrosion with programmes of individual resilience, teaching the insecure to breathe through the emergency that their labour market renews weekly, has mistaken the location of the pathology with a precision that would be comic if the stakes were lower. The Marxist tradition's insistence that consciousness reflects material circumstance is, in this territory, simply good medicine: the circumstance is the diagnosis, and remedies addressed to the reflection leave the circumstance, and the corrosion, exactly where they were.

### Why can insecurity damage health as much as unemployment does?

because the body responds to anticipated emergency as well as to realised loss. The insecure worker keeps the obligations and expenditures of employment while facing continuous uncertainty about income, hours, and continuity, a combination that sustains stress without resolution. Unemployment is a blow; insecurity is a pressure, and pressure applied for years leaves marks that a single blow does not.

Fatigue, finally, is the condition's most democratic product and its least organised grievance. The secure worker's tiredness had a rhythm and a remedy: the shift ended, the weekend restored, the holiday accumulated as a right. The insecure worker's tiredness is arrhythmic, compounded of broken sleep, multiple engagements with travel between them, availability that forbids true rest on days without paid work, and the administrative labour of insecurity itself, the applications, the chasing of payments, the negotiation of rosters and creditors, which is work in every respect except remuneration. Movements of the insecure that have named rest as a demand, the right to disconnect, to predictable rosters, to hours that permit a life, have understood something the wage-centred tradition sometimes missed: that what is being expropriated in precarity is not only labour but recuperation, the worker's capacity to return to the next engagement restored. A labour power that cannot reproduce itself is being consumed rather than employed, and the distinction, which Marx drew for the factory system's consumption of its workers, applies with undiminished force to a regime that consumes them more politely, by roster and by notification, across a working life of fragments.

## Casual labour before the settlement: the world the exception interrupted

A deeper pass through the pre-settlement record is warranted, because the precarity debate's participants frequently concede the point about the past in a sentence and then proceed as though the concession had no weight. Weight is exactly what the record has. Consider the ordinary mechanisms by which labour was engaged in the centuries before any standard relationship existed. Engagement was personal, verbal, and terminable at will on both sides, which in practice meant at the employer's will, since the worker's freedom to leave was the freedom to starve in a different parish. Payment was frequently in arrears by design, the arrears functioning as a bond against departure, and forfeiture of arrears on dismissal was a standard instrument of discipline. Whole categories of workers were engaged by the piece, by the load, by the tide, by the season, with earnings that swung between feast and famine according to rhythms no worker controlled. Combination among workers to stabilise any of this was, for long stretches, a criminal conspiracy in the eyes of the law, and the friendly societies and burial clubs through which workers insured themselves against sickness and death were tolerated precisely to the extent that they stopped short of touching the wage. This was not a labour market with imperfections. It was a labour market whose imperfections were its constitution, and the generations that fought their way out of it into the settlement understood, as their descendants sometimes do not, that they were fighting against the default condition of the wage relation and not defending a birthright.

The nineteenth century's own observers, whose testimony the surplus population literature mines, recorded the human product of that constitution with a clarity later sociology rarely matches. The casual wards, the lodging houses, the dawn crowds at the points of hiring, the systematic preference of employers for a surplus of applicants over any obligation to the applicants chosen, all appear in the record not as scandals at the margin of an otherwise orderly system but as the system's ordinary operation in its greatest cities. Marx drew directly on this record in constructing the category of the stagnant stratum, and the officials and journalists whose reports he quoted were describing the population that the precariat literature would later rediscover and rename. The continuity matters for a reason beyond scholarly priority. If the condition has been produced continuously by the wage relation wherever the relation operates unrestrained, then its causes are located in the relation itself and in the restraints placed upon it, and the analytical task is to study the restraints: their construction, their coverage, their dismantling. If the condition is instead a novel product of a novel economy, its causes are located in the novelty, in technology or globalisation or the spirit of the age, and the analytical task dissolves into commentary on the times. The first location yields politics; the second yields only mood. The precarity debate's drift toward mood, its elegiac tone, its fascination with the spirit of flexibility, is the price of its weak historiography, and the baseline correction is the toll that returns the debate to politics.

### What did the fight for the settlement understand that later memory forgot?

that security was a conquest over the default condition of the wage relation, not the relation's natural state. The generations that built the settlement treated every protection as a restraint imposed on an employer preference for unrestricted casual engagement, which is why they built institutions to hold the restraints rather than trusting prosperity or goodwill to maintain them.

## Informality as the world's majority condition

Zoom out from the settlement economies entirely, and the proportions of the subject transform. Across most of the world, the standard employment relationship never governed more than an enclave, and the majority of the world's workers have always earned their living in arrangements that the vocabulary of the settlement cannot describe: family labour, petty trade, casual engagement, subsistence production combined with wage work in shifting proportions, and the dense improvisation of livelihoods that the development literature gathers under the name of the informal economy. This series treats the informal sector's specific histories in their own articles and in the relevant national clusters, and the disambiguation rule assigns those histories there. The bearing on the present argument is a matter of scale and of standpoint. From the standpoint of the world's working population taken as a whole, the question that organises the precarity debate, why has work become insecure, is unintelligible, because work, for that population, never became secure in the first place. The intelligible question is the inverted one this article has pressed throughout: how did a minority of the world's workers obtain, for a few decades, an exemption from the general condition, and what has become of the exemption?

The inversion is more than a rhetorical flourish, because it relocates the precarity phenomenon in the world economy's structure. The settlement enclave and the informal ocean were never independent. The enclave's industries drew raw materials, cheapened by informal and coerced labour, from the ocean; the enclave's consumers enjoyed prices that the ocean's insecurity subsidised; and when the enclave's own protections came under pressure, the ocean's existence supplied both the mechanism, production's migration toward unprotected labour, and the ideology, the claim that protection itself was a luxury the world market no longer permitted. Precarity in the wealthy economies and informality in the poor ones are therefore not two subjects but one: the general condition of labour under a world market, and its locally varying restraints. The surplus population argument earns its centrality here, because it alone among the frameworks surveyed begins from the world scale, treating the wageless and the informally employed not as the settlement's others but as the system's typical products, and the protected worker of the enclave as the anomaly requiring explanation. A reader who absorbs that reversal of figure and ground will find the precarity debate permanently reframed, its parochialism visible at a glance, and its genuine insights, about expectation and entitlement and loss, relocated to their proper scale: as the experience of the anomaly's dissolution, significant precisely because the anomaly was real for those who lived inside it.

## The state in the background of every contract

No employment relation is a private arrangement between two parties, however fiercely the ideology of contract insists otherwise. Behind every engagement stands a public architecture that decides what the engagement means: which statuses the law recognises, which obligations attach to each status, what happens when the engagement ends, what happens when the worker sickens or ages, and how the costs of each eventuality are distributed among worker, employer, and the collective. The settlement was, in this light, a particular configuration of that architecture, and its dismantling was equally architectural, a reconfiguration carried out through thousands of adjustments to statuses, thresholds, and enforcement practices, few of which announced themselves as the reversal they collectively composed. This article's rule against naming governments or policies protects the analysis from expiring with any administration, but the structural point must be stated with full clarity: precarity is not a weather event. It is the output of an architecture, maintained by decisions, and describable, at the level this article occupies, as the systematic relocation of risk from collective and employer shoulders to individual ones through the redesign of the statuses that surround work.

The relocation metaphor should be handled precisely, because it can mislead by suggesting a fixed stock of security that was simply moved. Security is not a substance; it is a set of guarantees, and guarantees can be created and destroyed, not only transferred. What the dismantling destroyed, in many instances, was the guarantee itself, replacing an insured risk with an uninsured one, so that the total of protection in the system fell rather than merely changing hands. The worker who lost sickness insurance did not receive its value in wages; the enterprise that shed the obligation did not pay it out in prices. The guarantee evaporated, and the risk it had covered remained, now borne in the only currency left, the worker's health, savings, and family. Economists of the dismantling sometimes describe this evaporation as the removal of a distortion, restoring the labour market to its natural pricing. The baseline discipline supplies the reply: the market's natural pricing, unobstructed, is the pricing of the pre-settlement world, in which the costs of sickness, age, and slack demand were paid by the poor in the coin of destitution, and the settlement's guarantees were built because that pricing was judged, by the societies that experienced it, to be intolerable. Judgements of intolerability are political, and they can be remade, but they cannot be refuted by the observation that the market, left alone, prices as it always priced.

### Why is precarity an architectural product rather than a market accident?

because every feature of the condition corresponds to a decision about status and entitlement: which engagements count as employment, which obligations follow from the classification, which risks are insured collectively and which are left with the individual. Architectures are built and can be rebuilt; describing the output as weather conceals the builders and forecloses the politics that the description should inform.

## What a student should separate: the norm, the memory, and the mechanism

Three objects circulate in this debate under one another's names, and the single most valuable service an article can perform for a student reader is to prise them apart. The first object is the historical norm: the condition of labour across the long record, established by historiography, in which insecurity predominates. The second object is the remembered golden age: the settlement as carried in family memory and political rhetoric, burnished by contrast with what followed, its exclusions forgotten, its securities enlarged in retrospect. The third object is the mechanism: the set of processes, risk transfer, reclassification, allocation control, credential inflation, surplus population growth, by which insecurity is produced and distributed in any given period. Confusion among the three generates most of the debate's sterility. Arguments against the golden age are heard as denials of the mechanism. Descriptions of the mechanism are heard as claims about the norm. The norm is invoked to sedate grievances that belong to the mechanism's operation within living memory. A student who learns to ask, of any sentence in this literature, which of the three objects the sentence is about, will find the literature rearranging itself into sense, its authors' agreements and disagreements suddenly legible, since many who appear to contradict one another are simply describing different objects with the same vocabulary.

The separation also disciplines the student's own writing, which is where this series habitually aims its advice. An essay on precarity that begins from the norm will reach the baseline correction and stop, satisfied, having refuted a claim about novelty that the serious literature barely makes. An essay beginning from the memory will reproduce the elegy, moving and unarguable. An essay built on the mechanism, holding the norm in one hand as the corrective to false novelty and the memory in the other as the datum of political experience, can do what neither of the others can: explain how a condition that is historically normal came to be experienced, in specific places, as a shocking fall, and why that experience, without being historically accurate as a claim about the past, is politically accurate as a description of the present's relation to its own promises. That is the essay the examiners of this subject reward, because it is the essay that demonstrates the discipline's actual skill, the simultaneous handling of structure and experience, rather than the recitation of either alone.

## Grading the precariat concept against the finding

Assessment requires a rubric, and the rubric this article applies to the precariat concept follows from the finding that the exception was the security. A concept earns high marks on this rubric insofar as it respects the baseline, locates its phenomenon in a mechanism, and survives the relational test for any class claim it advances. Graded so, the precariat concept divides into components with very different fates. As a description of a condition, the transfer of risk, the stripping of entitlements, the dissolution of occupational narrative, it grades well: vivid, substantially accurate for the populations it was drawn from, and a genuine contribution to the phenomenology of insecure work. As a history, it grades poorly: its implicit baseline is the settlement mistaken for the norm, and the mistake is not incidental but load-bearing, since the concept's claim to name an emergence depends on it. As a class theory, it fails the relational test outright, for the reasons the definitional sections developed. As a political prognosis, the warning about resentful mobilisation among the betrayed, it grades better than its critics admit, because the mechanism of betrayal it identifies has operated visibly wherever the settlement's promises were withdrawn from populations educated to expect their fulfilment. A single grade for the whole concept would conceal this distribution, which is why the finding about the exception is best used as a grading instrument rather than as a slogan: it separates the concept's durable contributions from its structural errors and shows exactly where each lies.

### What marks does the precariat concept earn under the baseline finding?

it splits the concept into components and marks them separately. The description of the condition passes, the warning about betrayed expectations passes with credit, the implied history fails on its baseline, and the claim to have identified a new class fails the relational test. The finding thus preserves what the concept knows while refusing what it asserts.

## The gig economy class and the synonyms that mislead

Vocabulary is never innocent in this field, and the synonyms through which precarity is discussed deserve an audit of their own. The phrase gig economy class, which circulates as a casual equivalent for the precariat, smuggles two errors in four words: it names the whole condition after its most visible and least typical form, the platform-mediated engagement, and it presupposes the class status that the entire debate exists to test. Insecure work, the plainer synonym, is analytically cleaner, naming a property of engagements without prejudging the taxonomy of those who hold them, and this article has preferred it wherever the argument is doing analytical rather than reportorial work. The precariat itself, as a word, performs the thesis it names: a portmanteau fusing precarious with proletariat, it asserts in its very morphology that a new proletariat-like formation exists, so that a writer who adopts the term has conceded the argument before the evidence is presented. Such loaded coinages are common in the social sciences and not always dishonest, but the reader's defence is to notice the loading. Where a term asserts what its literature should be proving, the baseline habit applies in linguistic form: ask what the word assumes, and whether the assumption has been earned.

Synonym capture matters for a practical reason as well, which the series' method makes explicit. Readers arrive at this subject through search phrases formed by the public debate, and the public debate's phrases carry its confusions. An article that answers the query is the precariat a class must handle the phrase precariat while declining its presupposition, which is the manoeuvre this article has performed throughout: meeting the reader's vocabulary at the door and then, politely and with reasons, replacing it for analytical purposes. The alternative strategies both fail. Refusing the vocabulary abandons the readers who need the correction most, since they will simply find a less scrupulous article that speaks their language. Adopting the vocabulary without correction propagates the errors the article exists to retire. The discipline of translation, using the popular term as the address and the precise term as the content, is the series' standing solution, and precarity, a subject whose public vocabulary is unusually loaded, is where the solution earns its keep most visibly.

## Competition inside the stratum and the politics it produces

A stratum defined by a shared lack does not automatically share a politics, and the internal competitions of the insecure deserve analysis as unsparing as any directed at employers. Insecure workers compete with one another continuously and by design: for the next engagement, for the allocator's favour, for the rating that keeps the allocations coming, for the scarce protected posts at the core's edge. The competition is not a failure of solidarity to be lamented from outside but a structured feature of the condition, engineered by allocation systems that rank workers against one another and experienced by workers as the daily texture of the labour market. Its political products are correspondingly divided. Resentment within the stratum seeks targets, and the targets nearest to hand are other insecure workers: the newcomer whose willingness to accept worse terms undercuts the established casual, the migrant whose presence is narrated as the cause of a scarcity that the allocation system manufactures, the younger worker whose credentials crowd the queue. Standing's dangerous class warning derives much of its force from exactly this intra-stratum dynamic, the readiness of a population denied security to purchase a simulacrum of it by excluding others from the little that remains. The warning should be credited where the mechanism is real, and the mechanism is real wherever allocation is scarce and competitive by design.

Against that dynamic, the historical record of casual labour organisation offers the counter-principle already encountered in the hiring hall: solidarity among the insecure has always been built by socialising the allocation, by replacing competitive allocation with rule-governed allocation administered collectively, so that the worker's competitor is converted into the worker's colleague by an institutional act. The principle generalises. Every successful organisation of insecure workers has had to solve the allocation problem before it could solve any other, because a workforce whose members experience one another as rivals for survival cannot be organised on any programme, however correct, until the rivalry is institutionally blunted. This is a harder organisational teaching than the settlement era's unions, recruiting among workers whom the employer's own structure had already gathered and stabilised, ever had to learn. It suggests that the organisational future of the stratum lies less in replicating the settlement's union form than in inventing allocation institutions for a workforce the employer refuses to gather: portable membership, collective administration of rosters and registries, and funds that pool the stratum's risks across engagements and employers. Such inventions exist in embryo across the organising literature. Their theoretical interest is that they amount to rebuilding, from below and within the stratum, fragments of the settlement's architecture, which suggests that the stratum's own practical judgement about what it needs agrees with this article's analysis of what it lost.

### Why does competition among insecure workers block organisation more than employer hostility does?

because hostility can unify its targets while competition divides them at the point of survival. Where the next engagement is allocated competitively, each worker experiences colleagues as rivals for income itself, and appeals to solidarity ask the worker to subsidise the rival. Organisation becomes possible when allocation is collectivised, converting rivals into colleagues by rule rather than by exhortation.

## Wages, hours, and the arithmetic that never balances

Follow a single household budget through a month of irregular income and the abstractions of this article acquire arithmetic. Obligations arrive in fixed amounts on fixed dates. Income arrives in variable amounts on variable dates, with gaps that no diligence can schedule around, since the gaps are the employer's flexibility expressed in the worker's calendar. The mismatch is bridged, in sequence, by savings where any exist, by the deferral of whichever obligation can be deferred at least immediate cost, by borrowing from kin whose own budgets run the same mismatch a week out of phase, and by commercial credit priced for exactly this desperation. Each bridge has a toll, and the tolls accumulate into a surcharge on poverty that the literature on the costs of being poor has documented in many forms: the insecure pay more for credit, more for housing held on short terms, more for food bought in small quantities at nearby prices, more for transport arranged at short notice, and more in fees and penalties for every failed payment that the mismatch eventually forces. The surcharge is not a market failure incidental to the condition. It is the condition's continuation by other means, the transfer of risk expressing itself as a transfer of income from the insecure to every institution positioned to charge for their insecurity.

Hours complete the arithmetic's cruelty. The insecure worker's problem is rarely a simple shortage of hours; it is the distribution of hours across the calendar in patterns that defeat every remedy. Hours cluster, so that in one week the worker is worked past exhaustion across overlapping engagements while dependants and creditors are neglected, and in the next week the hours vanish while the availability must still be maintained, the phone answered, the district not left, in case the allocation comes. Annualised, the hours might resemble a standard job's. Lived, they are nothing like it, because the standard job's defining gift was never the quantity of hours but their predictability, the property that lets a life be built around work instead of work's contingencies consuming the life entire. Measures of underemployment that count hours alone therefore miss the condition's essence, and the better research literature supplements them with measures of schedule control and advance notice, which capture the variable that actually governs the worker's existence: not how much work, but how much of the worker's time the work's uncertainty commands. Commanded time, paid and unpaid together, is the true measure of precarity's expropriation, and by that measure the condition is more extensive than any count of contracts suggests, reaching into every hour the insecure worker holds in readiness for a market that may not call.

## The working class recomposed around the insecure stratum

Return, with the analysis assembled, to the composition question that the companion article on the working class's continued existence treats in full. The working class that the precarity debate argues over is not the class of the settlement's imagination, and the debate's confusions are partly the imagination's fault. That imagination pictured the class as male, as gathered in large workplaces, as employed continuously across a life, as organised in unions that spoke for it, and as culturally homogeneous enough to recognise itself in a single mirror. Every element of the picture described the protected core of a particular period and mistook the core for the class. The class itself, in the settlement decades as much as in any other, included the casual, the seasonal, the domestic, the migrant, the homeworker, and the informally employed, in numbers that rivalled and by world scale dwarfed the core. The dismantling did not create a new class beneath the old one. It shifted the proportions among the class's long-standing strata, shrinking the protected core and swelling the insecure layers that had always existed at its edges and beyond its borders, until the edges became too large to be treated as edges and the imagination's picture collapsed under the weight of its own exclusions.

Proportions, however, are not a detail, and a Marxist who celebrates the correction too loudly will miss what the shift in proportions does to the class's capacities. A working class whose protected core is large possesses institutions, funds, press, parties, and habits of solidarity built over generations, from which the insecure strata can be organised and into which they can be drawn. A working class whose core shrinks toward a privileged remnant finds those institutions ageing with their membership, their funds committed to the pensions of the protected, their habits formed around a workforce that is disappearing, while the growing insecure strata stand outside, unorganised, and increasingly unable to see the institutions as theirs. The danger the precarity literature gestures at, beneath its theoretical errors, is this institutional hollowing: not the birth of a new class but the decay of the old class's organs faster than new organs form. The remedy implied by the analysis is neither nostalgia for the organs' settlement form nor proclamation of a new subject, but the deliberate reconstruction of the organs on the stratum's own terrain, allocation, portability, and income floors, the programme the organising record already sketches. Theory's contribution is to keep the programme's aim clear: the extension of security across the class, not the recognition of insecurity as an identity.

### Does a shrinking protected core change what the insecure stratum is?

it changes the stratum's prospects rather than its class position. The insecure remain sellers of labour power, but the institutions through which their class historically converted numbers into power were built by and for the protected core. As that core shrinks, the stratum must rebuild organisational capacity on its own terrain or remain politically weightless despite its growth.

## Precarity and the reserve army's laws of motion

The reserve army framework contributes more than a matching description of irregular work; it contributes a dynamics, an account of why the irregular stratum grows and shrinks and of what its size does to the whole labour market. In Marx's construction, the relative surplus population is not a residue but a product, generated continuously by accumulation itself as rising productivity expels workers from established processes faster than new processes absorb them, and functioning continuously as the lever by which the wages of the employed are regulated. The stagnant stratum sits at the bottom of that construction as its most depressed layer, the layer whose availability at any terms sets the floor beneath the whole wage structure and whose expansion signals that accumulation is failing to absorb the population it has set in motion. Read through this lens, the growth of precarious work in the dismantling decades is not a puzzle requiring a new theory of the age. It is the relative surplus population expanding upward into employments that the settlement had temporarily withdrawn from its reach, as the protections that fenced those employments off were dismantled one status at a time. The framework even predicts the sequence the literature records: insecurity colonises first the employments nearest the surplus population, the young, the migrant, the returning mother, the unskilled, and advances toward the core as each fence falls, so that the experience of precarity arrives at each stratum as a shocking novelty while being, in the system's terms, the same tide reaching a higher mark on the same shore.

The dynamics also explain the political temperature of the condition better than the class framework can. A reserve army disciplines by existing; its members need do nothing, and their mere availability moderates the claims of the employed. But a reserve army drawn from workers formerly employed at protected standards disciplines with an admixture of humiliation, because its members measure their availability against their own biographies, and humiliation is a politically volatile substance in a way that mere poverty is not. The movements that have mobilised the insecure most successfully have been those that converted humiliation into indictment, naming the dismantling as a decision and its beneficiaries as identifiable, rather than those that offered the insecure an identity to inhabit. Identity consoles; indictment organises. The distinction, which runs through the whole history of labour politics, is the practical form of this article's theoretical verdict: the insecure do not need a new name for what they are so much as a true account of what was done, by what mechanisms, in whose interest, and the tradition's categories, reserve army, risk transfer, surplus population, supply that account in a connected form that the new class concept, for all its vividness, interrupts.

## Reading the literature: a field guide to its recurring moves

Students entering this literature benefit from a map of its recurring moves, since the same arguments recur across decades and authors with only their examples refreshed. The first move is the announcement of novelty, in which a condition long documented is presented as a discovery, usually by ignoring the historiography; the defence is the baseline question, asked early and without embarrassment. The second move is the synecdoche, in which the experience of one stratum, usually the educated and formerly protected, is presented as the experience of the whole; the defence is the coverage question, asking of every general claim which workers it actually describes. The third move is the definitional slide, in which class, stratum, condition, and generation are used interchangeably until the argument's conclusion can be reached by whichever definition suits the paragraph; the defence is the relational test, applied each time the word class appears. The fourth move is the elegy, in which the settlement is mourned in terms that erase its exclusions; the defence is the subset clause, the insistence that the baseline table's third row be read aloud whenever the golden age is invoked. The fifth move is the dismissal, in which the correction of the elegy is used to deny the loss; the defence is the concession about expectation, stated as firmly as the correction itself. A reader armed with the five defences can process the literature at speed without being processed by it, which is the most this article, or any article, can finally do for the reader's independence.

### Which single question most improves a reader's handling of this literature?

the baseline question in its full form: compared with when, where, and for whom? It exposes false novelty, detects the synecdoche of the formerly protected stratum, forces baselines into the open where they can be checked, and converts the literature's grand disagreements into specific, decidable disputes about periods, places, and populations.

## The verdict and the condition that would reverse it

Verdicts in this series are required to carry their defeaters, and this article's verdict is now ready to be stated with its defeater attached. The verdict: precarity is the historical norm of labour under capitalism; the secure standard employment relationship was the exception, bounded in period, place, and coverage; the precariat is not a class in the Marxist sense but a stratum of the working class whose condition the reserve army analysis already reaches, most precisely in its stagnant form; the dismantling of the settlement was nevertheless a real reversal with real political consequences, rooted in lost expectation and stripped entitlement, which the older categories describe functionally but do not exhaust experientially; and the sharpest available framework for the condition's future is the surplus population argument, which asks whether accumulation can absorb the labour it sets free, and finds the question increasingly open. The exception was the security, and analysis should spend itself explaining the exception, its construction, its coverage, and its dismantling, rather than treating the return to type as the mystery.

The defeater is empirical and specific. The judgement that precarity is the norm would be defeated by evidence that secure employment relationships were general across a wide range of economies and periods before the twentieth century: that the typical worker of the earlier record held continuing employment, predictable income, and institutional protection as a matter of course. That is the form the opposing claim would have to take to overturn the verdict, and it is the form the labour history record, as assembled by the discipline's historians across generations of archival work, does not support. Should such evidence emerge, from records not yet excavated or regions not yet studied, the verdict would require reversal, and the precarity literature's narrative of a fall would be vindicated at this article's expense. A verdict that names its defeater in this way is not weakened but completed by the naming, since the reader is told exactly what the verdict is worth and exactly what would change it, which is the most that any judgement in a contested field can honestly offer.

## Skill, craft, and the deskilling that insecurity accelerates

The fate of skill under insecure employment is a subplot of the main argument with its own melancholy logic. Skill, in the strong sense the settlement's trades embodied, is accumulated slowly, through supervised practice over years, and it is accumulated by institutions: the apprenticeship, the training levy, the employer who invests in a workforce expected to stay. Insecurity dissolves each condition of accumulation. An employer engaging labour by the week has no rational interest in training it, since the trained worker's next engagement may serve a rival; a worker moving between unrelated engagements cannot deepen any single competence; and the collective mechanisms that once socialised training costs across an industry decay when the industry's employment relation fragments. The result is a slow deskilling that proceeds independently of technology, driven purely by the time horizons the employment relation permits. Sociologists of work have documented the pattern in trade after trade: the substitution of narrow, quickly learned task competence for broad craft knowledge, the migration of judgement from the worker to the procedure and the checklist, the thinning of the experienced layer from which the next generation's teachers would have come. The precarity literature registers this as one insecurity among others, the absence of skill reproduction security in Standing's list. The Marxist framework gives it a sharper edge, because deskilling in this tradition is not a side effect but a tendency of capital itself, the drive to cheapen labour power by simplifying the labour it must buy, and insecurity appears, in that light, as deskilling's accelerant: the employment form that makes skill formation nobody's interest arrives at the cheapened workforce by the shortest route.

The worker's own relation to skill changes with the form. The craftsman's skill was property of a kind, a possession that travelled with its holder, commanded a rate, and grounded a claim to respect that no single employer could revoke. The insecure worker's competences are increasingly of the opposite kind: certified familiarity with particular systems, valid for particular employers, expiring with each change of software, roster, or contractor, and worthless as a general claim on the labour market. Skill becomes a subscription rather than a freehold, renewed engagement by engagement, and the worker experiences competence itself as precarious, a wasting asset whose maintenance consumes unpaid time. Educational policy that responds to insecurity by exhorting workers to reskill mistakes the direction of causation in a way this article's mechanism analysis exposes: the obstacle is not the worker's stock of skill but the employment relation's refusal to reward its accumulation, and a worker who reskills into the same insecurity has merely raised the level at which the queue forms. The credential inflation examined earlier and the deskilling examined here are the same process viewed from opposite ends of the labour market, the overproduction of certified labour at the top and the destruction of craft labour at the bottom, both driven by an accumulation regime that wants labour cheap, flexible, and interchangeable, and that experiences a skilled, secure, self-confident workforce as a cost and a threat in equal measure.

### Why does insecure employment destroy skill even when the work itself is unchanged?

because skill formation requires time horizons that insecure engagement abolishes. Employers who hire by the week will not fund years of training, workers rotated between unrelated tasks cannot deepen a craft, and the industry-wide mechanisms that once shared training costs decay. The work may look identical while the workforce's accumulated competence quietly drains away.

## The family wage, its critics, and what replaced it

No honest treatment of the settlement can avoid the family wage, the norm, embedded in the settlement's design, that a male worker's wage should suffice to maintain a household. The norm was always partly fictive, since working class households had always required multiple earners, and it was always partly an instrument of exclusion, used to argue women out of employments and out of equal pay. Feminist critics inside and outside the Marxist tradition dismantled its pretensions generations ago. Yet the norm also encoded a real standard, that a single full-time wage should be capable of founding a household, against which the subsequent transformation stands in damning contrast. The dismantling economies did not replace the family wage with equal individual wages for all adults, the feminist alternative the critics had proposed. They replaced it with a regime in which two insecure incomes, patched across a household's adults, frequently fail to purchase what the settlement's single protected wage was normed to buy, while the unpaid labour that the old norm at least acknowledged as the wage's counterpart continues unacknowledged and intensified. The sequence deserves the student's attention as a model of how emancipatory criticism can be absorbed by the order it criticised: the critique of the family wage was correct, its victory was real, and the form of its victory, the universalisation of insecure individual employment rather than of security, was chosen by the side that profited from it.

Household structure registers the transformation with demographic precision. The settlement's biography, leaving the parental home, forming an independent household, partnering, bearing children, in the decade after schooling, has stretched across the dismantling economies into a prolonged and often incomplete transition, with household formation deferred toward middle life and childbearing deferred or forgone in proportions that demographers record without always naming the labour market among the causes. The materialist reading offered throughout this article applies without strain: the contracts of adult life are underwritten by income continuity, and a generation denied continuity defers the contracts. What cultural commentary describes as a change in values, a generation's supposed reluctance to commit, is in large part the settlement's dismantling viewed from inside the lives it reorganised, and the precarity literature's educated stratum, whose biographies exhibit the deferrals most visibly, has mistaken its own deferred adulthood for a universal spiritual condition. The baseline discipline, applied to demography, returns the phenomenon to its mechanism: not a crisis of commitment but a shortage of the material predicate of commitment, a wage on which a future can be signed.

## Migration and the labour market's inner frontier

Migration is the point where the labour market's strata are manufactured most visibly, and no account of precarity is complete without it. Every labour market that contains a protected core maintains, alongside it, mechanisms for admitting labour on terms inferior to the core's, and migration regimes are the most powerful of those mechanisms, since they can attach insecurity to the person rather than to the job: the permit that expires, the status tied to a single employer, the ever-present possibility of removal, which together produce a worker whose precarity is juridical before it is economic. Such workers are not merely cheaper. They are differently disciplined, unable to refuse, to organise, or to exit at the thresholds where citizens bargain, and their presence in a labour market exerts, through the reserve army mechanism, a downward pressure on the terms of all who compete near them. The employers who benefit from the arrangement are adept at narrating it as a natural fact of global wage differences. The Marxist analysis insists on the manufacture: the frontier between protected and unprotected labour is drawn by law, administered by bureaucracies, and policed at borders, and it runs through the interior of every wealthy labour market, sorting its workers into statuses whose differences no account of technology or globalisation can explain.

The politics generated at this frontier are the most dangerous in the whole field, because the frontier's victims are positioned to blame one another with a plausibility that the system's actual authors exploit with relief. The citizen casual worker who experiences the migrant as the cause of scarce engagements is not hallucinating the competition; the competition is real at the level of the allocation, and only the analysis that rises to the level of the allocation's designers can redirect the grievance accurately. Standing's dangerous class warning finds its sharpest application here, since the betrayed stratum's resentment, denied a structural account, defaults to the frontier's own categories and demands a harder frontier as its programme: security for us, purchased by exclusion of them, which is the settlement's original sin repeated as farce, the enclave defended by shrinking it. The alternative politics, the extension of status and security across the frontier until the frontier ceases to sort workers into castes of vulnerability, is harder to build and easier to state, and it is the one programme in the field addressed to the mechanism rather than to a redistribution of its victims. Labour history supplies both precedents in abundance, movements that organised across the frontier and movements that policed it, and the correlation between the choice and the movement's subsequent strength is one of the best-attested regularities the historiography contains.

### Why is the migrant worker's insecurity different in kind from the citizen's?

because it is attached to legal status rather than to the engagement alone. A permit that depends on one employer, or a right to remain that employment must continuously sponsor, converts every workplace dispute into an existential risk. The citizen casual worker risks an income; the migrant on a tied status risks presence in the country of employment itself.

## The professions and the hollowing of the salariat

Standing's scheme reserves a place, above the precariat and below the elite, for the salariat, the securely employed professionals and managers whose protected status the settlement extended upward into the middle reaches of the class structure. The fate of that stratum under the dismantling is the precarity debate's most quietly dramatic subplot, because the mechanisms this article has catalogued, reclassification, allocation control, credential inflation, the transfer of risk, have not stopped at the boundary of the professions but have advanced through them, converting salaried posts into contracted engagements, replacing tenure with review, and subjecting professional judgement to metrics administered with a granularity the factory inspector would have envied. The university teacher engaged by the course, the journalist retained by the piece, the clinician whose sessions are allocated and timed by a platform-like system, the lawyer whose partnership track dissolves into contract review work, each inhabits a professional shell whose interior has been precaritised while its exterior, the title, the credential, the social sign, remains. The literature calls this the hollowing of the salariat, and its political significance is considerable, since the stratum thus hollowed supplies the writers, teachers, and broadcasters through whom the precarity debate is conducted, which returns us, by a new route, to the sociology of the concept's success: the precariat was named at the moment its condition reached the naming class.

The hollowing also performs an unintended service for the analysis, because it furnishes a controlled comparison that tests the class thesis. The hollowed professional and the lifelong casual worker now share contractual insecurity, fluctuating income, and thinning entitlements, the full syndrome of the condition. If the syndrome constituted a class, the two should be converging into a single formation with common interests and a common politics. What the record shows instead is divergence along the lines the relational framework predicts: the professional's insecurity is buffered by assets, credentials, and networks accumulated under the old regime, is experienced as a fall from a known height, and generates a politics of restoration; the casual worker's insecurity is unbuffered, lifelong, and generates, where it generates politics at all, a politics of first admission to goods never held. Same contracts, different classes of experience, because class in the operative sense tracks the whole relation to property, credentials, and reserves, not the contract's current term. The comparison is as close as social analysis comes to an experiment, and its result is the one this article's verdict records: the condition is real, the class is not.

## Automation's shadow and the fear that misnames itself

Public discussion of insecure work habitually merges with public discussion of automation, and the merge deserves resistance, because the two phenomena obey different mechanisms and the merge serves identifiable interests. Automation displaces labour from tasks; precarity degrades the terms on which labour is engaged. A machine that performs a task abolishes an engagement; a reclassification that converts the engagement's holder into a contractor abolishes nothing but the holder's security, while the task, and the worker performing it, continue exactly as before. The greater part of what populations experience as the new insecurity belongs to the second category, the degradation of terms, which is a political and legal process with authors and beneficiaries, yet the first category, technological displacement, dominates the imagery through which the whole is discussed, because machinery photographs better than contract law and because the technological story absolves every author: no one decided, the robots came. The series treats automation and machinery in their own articles, and the Fragment on Machines tradition within Marx's notebooks supplies the classical framework for thinking about machinery and labour's displacement. For the precarity argument, the necessary discipline is separation. Each claim of insecurity should be audited for its mechanism: was the work abolished, or was its status abolished? The audit, performed across the dismantling decades, returns a consistent answer in the research literature, that status abolition accounts for the larger share, which is why this article has organised itself around statuses, entitlements, and allocations rather than around machines.

The separation also clarifies the surplus population argument's proper scope. Genuine displacement, where accumulation's rising productivity expels labour faster than new branches absorb it, produces surplus population in the strict sense, and its scale is the deepest open question in the field. Degradation of terms, by contrast, produces precarity among the employed, a phenomenon fully compatible with labour being absorbed, even eagerly absorbed, on worsening terms. Conflating the two produces the muddled picture, common in popular writing, of a world simultaneously running out of work and working its workers to exhaustion. The muddle dissolves once the mechanisms are separated: an economy can absorb nearly everyone and still precaritise nearly everyone, by engaging them all insecurely, and it can expel a growing surplus while the remaining employed enjoy a protected enclave's terms. The two processes have proceeded together in varying proportions across the dismantling decades, and the proportions matter for prognosis, since precarity among the employed is contestable through the classical instruments of labour politics, while a structurally surplus population poses the harder question of what claims on the social product can be grounded outside the wage relation altogether. Standing's signature policy proposal, a basic income, is best understood as an answer to the second question wearing the clothing of the first, which is why it attracts the surplus population's theorists and irritates the labour tradition in equal measure: it concedes, as the tradition does not, that the wage relation will not reabsorb those it has expelled, and proposes to pension the concession.

### How can an economy run out of security without running out of work?

by separating the abolition of jobs from the abolition of statuses. Most insecurity in the dismantling decades came from engagements reclassified, protections detached, and risks transferred, while the underlying work continued and its performers kept performing it. Full employment and full precarity are compatible, because they describe different variables: the quantity of work and the terms of its engagement.

## The emotions of the condition: shame, anger, and anomie

A political economy of precarity that omits its emotional register will fail to explain the condition's politics, since the emotions are the medium through which the mechanisms become movements. Shame is the first of them, and the most privatising. Insecure work in a culture that reads employment as the certificate of worth delivers its failures as personal verdicts: the unanswered application, the roster without one's name, the engagement that went to another, each is experienced, against all structural knowledge, as evidence about the self. The shame is functional for the order that produces it, because a population ashamed of its condition conceals the condition, declines to organise around it, and experiences the similarly placed as reminders rather than as allies. The historical labour movement's first cultural achievement, before any of its economic victories, was the conversion of shame into grievance, the teaching that the casual worker's condition was a fact about the labour market and not about the worker, and every successful organisation of the insecure since has had to repeat that conversion as its founding act. Anger is the second emotion, shame's outward turning, and its direction is the whole political question of the field: toward the allocation's designers or toward the allocation's other victims, toward the architects of the dismantling or toward the frontier's far side. Anomie, the third, is anger and shame exhausted into drift, the condition Standing's account captures best, in which the future's disappearance as a plannable territory dissolves the disciplines, of saving, of training, of association, that the settlement's biography presupposed, leaving a present tense lived at the tempo of the notification. A theory of the condition that cannot hold these three emotions in view will mistake the politics of the insecure for irrationality whenever it erupts, and the eruptions will keep arriving as surprises to everyone except those who studied the emotions as data.

## What security would have to mean now

Articles in this series are not programmes, but an analysis that has dismantled a concept owes the reader a sketch of the standard against which programmes in the field should be measured, stated structurally and without the naming of policies that this article's discipline forbids. Security, generalised from the settlement's experience and corrected by its exclusions, would mean at minimum the following, each element corresponding to a mechanism this article has examined. Income continuity sufficient for a household to sign the contracts of adult life, which addresses the arithmetic of mismatch. Entitlements attached to the person and accumulating across engagements, which addresses reclassification. Allocation governed by rules the allocated can inspect and contest, which addresses the competitive audition and its poisons. Time sovereignty, rosters known in advance and availability bounded, which addresses the colonisation of unpaid time. Skill formation funded collectively and rewarded in the wage, which addresses the deskilling dynamic. And a floor beneath the whole, provision for those whom accumulation cannot absorb, which addresses the surplus population honestly instead of abandoning it to informal survival. No element is utopian in the sense of lacking historical precedent; each existed, in partial and exclusionary form, inside the settlement. The programme the elements compose differs from the settlement in exactly one respect, the respect the baseline table's third row dictates: coverage. Security confined to a subset is an enclave awaiting its dismantling, since the excluded will always be usable against the included. Universality is therefore not the programme's ornament but its structural condition, the lesson the exception's history teaches at the cost of the exception itself.

Whether such a programme is achievable within the relations the Marxist framework describes is the question on which the tradition's internal debates turn, and this article will not pretend to settle what generations have contested. The reformist reading holds that the settlement proved security compatible with capitalism, and that its dismantling proved only that security must be defended politically, as all gains must. The revolutionary reading holds that the settlement's conditions were unrepeatable, a conjunction of reconstruction, scarcity, and fear that will not recur, and that security generalised and secured against revocation requires the socialisation of the investment decisions from which insecurity flows. The precarity debate, conducted at its usual level, rarely reaches this underlying fork, because its categories, the new class, the golden age, the spirit of flexibility, are too coarse to state it. One measure of the baseline framework's superiority is that it states the fork precisely: if security was an exception produced by a conjunction, the strategic question is whether the conjunction can be rebuilt, substituted, or transcended, and each answer implies a different politics, a different organisation, and a different attitude toward the insecure stratum's own emerging institutions. The stratum, meanwhile, does not wait for the theory to settle. It builds its hiring halls and portable funds and roster campaigns out of the materials at hand, as its predecessors built theirs, and the theory that hopes to be of use will attend to the building with the respect this article has tried to practise: correcting the stratum's would-be champions where they misname it, and learning from the stratum where it names its own needs correctly.

### Why is universality the structural lesson of the settlement's failure?

because every exclusion from the settlement became a lever against the included. Workers outside the protections could be played against workers inside them, production could migrate toward the unprotected, and the enclave's defence consumed the movement's strength. A security covering only a subset manufactures the instrument of its own dismantling; coverage is a condition of durability, not a decoration.

## The long view: insecurity and the shape of a life

Step back from mechanisms and literatures, and the subject of this article is the shape of a human life under a labour regime. The settlement, for all its exclusions, proposed a shape: a legible progression from training through mastery to a protected old age, in which the sacrifices of each stage were redeemed by the securities of the next, and a person could locate the present difficulty within a story whose ending was underwritten. The shape was a discipline as much as a comfort, and many chafed under it, yet its dissolution has not liberated the lives it governed into improvisation's joys for any but a resourceful minority. For the majority of the insecure, the dissolved shape has been replaced not by freedom but by formlessness, a life whose stages no longer redeem one another, in which training does not ripen into mastery, mastery does not ripen into security, and old age approaches as the final and least forgivable insecurity, the one for which no next engagement can compensate. The literature of the condition returns, in its most honest passages, to this biographical register, because the statistics of contracts and statuses cannot state what the condition costs: it costs the intelligibility of a life to the person living it. Marxism began, in one of its sources, as a protest against exactly this cost, against labour that confronts the labourer as an alien sequence rather than as the medium of a self. The protest's vocabulary has changed across two centuries; the cost has proved more durable than any of the vocabularies, and it will outlast the precariat concept as it outlasted the vocabularies before it, until the relations that impose it are themselves transformed. That durability, and not any novelty, is the deepest fact this article has to record, and the reason its verdict, properly understood, is not a counsel of despair but a refusal of amnesia: the condition is old, its conquest was real and partial and brief, and the conquest's brevity is the measure of the task, not of its impossibility.

## The evidence of the senses: what fieldwork among the insecure finds

Ethnography, the patient observation of work as it is lived, supplies the precarity field with its most durable evidence, and a survey that confined itself to theories would miss the texture that makes the theories matter. Fieldworkers among casual and insecure workers record, with monotonous consistency across continents and decades, a set of signature experiences that no survey instrument captures whole. The telephone as an instrument of suspense, checked compulsively, silenced at the cost of terror, since the missed call is the missed engagement. The performance of cheerfulness under allocation, the cultivation of the supervisor's or the algorithm's favour as a second, unpaid job performed inside the first. The micro-economies of the workforce, loans between workers at terms set by desperation, the pooling of transport, of food, of information about which allocator is hiring and which is to be avoided, an entire shadow welfare system built from the wages it supplements. The elaborate dignity work by which workers narrate their condition to themselves and their children, the insistence on the temporary, the strategic, the chosen, even where every observable fact contradicts the narration, because the alternative narration, that the market has discarded them, is unliveable as a daily belief. And the calendar of small catastrophes, the boiler, the tooth, the fine, the broken vehicle, each trivial in a secured budget and each capable, in an unsecured one, of triggering the cascade into arrears from which recovery takes a season. Ethnography's lesson for the theorist is humility about categories: the condition is not a status but a weather system inside a life, and the workers inside it are not a class in waiting but people conducting, with ingenuity the literature rarely credits, a continuous emergency that the secured mistake for a lifestyle.

Fieldwork also corrects a sentimentalism to which the precarity debate is prone, the portrait of the insecure worker as pure victim awaiting theoretical rescue. The same studies that document the emergency document the stratagems: the cultivation of multiple allocators so that no single refusal is fatal, the maintenance of skills and tools against engagements that may return, the conversion of kinship into infrastructure, the sly management of availability, present for the good allocator, unreachable for the bad, by which workers recover fragments of the control the employment relation denies them. None of this ingenuity refutes the structural analysis; a prisoner's ingenuity does not refute the prison. But it disciplines the analysis's tone. The stratum is not the passive raw material of either capital's flexibility or theory's categories, and the politics that eventually organises it will be built from its own stratagems generalised, the pooling, the mutual insurance, the information networks, rather than imported from the settlement's institutional museum. Observers who have spent time inside the condition tend to emerge with less interest in naming it than in the stratagems, and that shift of interest, from taxonomy to practice, is the fieldwork tradition's standing rebuke to the precariat debate's more scholastic stretches.

### What does fieldwork add that surveys of contract types cannot?

the texture of the condition as a continuous emergency managed with ingenuity: the suspense of allocation, the unpaid labour of staying employable, the shadow welfare of mutual loans and pooled information, and the dignity work through which workers narrate insecurity to themselves. Surveys count statuses; fieldwork shows what the statuses do to a week, a household, and a self.

## Generations inside one workforce

Stand at any point of allocation, physical or digital, and the stratum's internal generations are visible side by side, experiencing the same condition as different historical events. The oldest present were formed under the settlement and carry its standards as personal memory; their insecurity is a demotion, and their conversation is comparative, organised around the distance between the terms they hold and the terms they held. The middle generation entered work as the dismantling proceeded and holds a divided consciousness, promised the settlement by its parents' example and delivered the dismantling by its own experience, fluent in both vocabularies and at home in neither. The youngest know no other condition and exhibit the adaptation that alarms their elders: expectations calibrated to insecurity from the start, a fluency in the allocation systems, a portfolio conception of the self, and a politics, where it surfaces, that owes little to the labour tradition's categories and much to the immediate solidarities of network and neighbourhood. Analysts who address the stratum as one audience fail all three generations at once. The demoted require acknowledgement of loss; the divided require an account of the fork at which their biography turned; the adapted require, most difficult of all, a reason to want securities they have never tasted, which can only be given by making the securities imaginable as a common inheritance rather than as their grandparents' nostalgia. The baseline table, in this light, is a diplomatic instrument among generations as much as an analytical one: it validates the elders' memory as fact, corrects its scope, and offers the young a past that is neither golden age nor fable but a documented, bounded, buildable precedent.

## The employer's side of the ledger

An analysis of precarity that never reconstructs the employer's reasoning will mistake a strategy for a fate, and the reasoning is neither mysterious nor, within its own premises, irrational. Labour engaged flexibly converts a fixed cost into a variable one, aligning the wage bill with revenue hour by hour and transferring the risk of slack demand, as established, to the worker. It dissolves the employer's obligations as trainer, as insurer, as pension contributor, and as the party responsible for the workforce's continuity, each dissolution registering as a saving. It weakens collective organisation by dissolving the stable workforce that organisation requires, a benefit employers have historically valued beyond its cash equivalent. And it intensifies the effort of the workers retained, since the visible pool of the available, waiting at the allocation point, disciplines effort more cheaply than supervision can. Against these advantages stand real costs that the flexibility literature understates: the loss of accumulated skill and local knowledge, the quality failures of a workforce without commitment to the product, the recruitment churn that consumes managerial time, and the reputational and political risks of a visibly casualised operation. The balance between the advantages and the costs is struck differently in different activities, which is why casualisation advances unevenly, conquering where labour is substitutable and quality insensitive, halting where skill and trust are the product. The unevenness is itself evidence for the article's framework: a fate would fall evenly, while a strategy advances exactly where its ledger profits, and the map of precarity across employments is the map of that ledger's arithmetic.

Capital's fractions disagree about the strategy, and the disagreement is a resource for analysis and politics alike. Enterprises whose model rests on skill, continuity, and reputation experience the casualised labour market as a degradation of their own inputs, a thinning of the trained workforce from which they recruit and a coarsening of the demand their customers bring. Enterprises built on labour arbitrage experience the same market as their element. The settlement, in its day, was partly the political victory of the first fraction's preferences, codified as the general interest; the dismantling was partly the second fraction's revenge. A Marxist treatment notes the disagreement without mistaking either fraction for an ally of labour: the first fraction's preference for a stable workforce is a preference for a more productive instrument, not for the worker's security as an end, and it evaporates wherever a cheaper instrument appears. The point of reconstructing the employer's ledger is not sympathy but accuracy about the terrain: insecurity is chosen, enterprise by enterprise and rule by rule, by actors responding to incentives that public architecture sets, and what is chosen under one architecture can be made costly under another, which is the entire practical content of the claim that precarity is made rather than fated.

### Why does casualisation advance unevenly across different kinds of work?

because the employer's ledger balances differently where labour is substitutable than where skill, trust, and continuity are the product. Flexibility saves most where any worker will do and costs most where the accumulated knowledge of a stable workforce is the enterprise's real asset. A fate would fall evenly; the observed pattern is a strategy's map.

## Security's price and the question of who paid it

The settlement's elegists rarely ask what the settlement cost or who paid, and the question, asked fairly, neither damns the settlement nor canonises it, but completes the baseline. The securities of the protected core were financed from the surplus of economies growing rapidly, and the growth itself rested partly on the unprotected: on colonial and postcolonial labour regimes whose products entered the settlement economies cheaply, on the unpaid domestic labour that reproduced the protected workforce, on migrant labour admitted without the settlement's rights, and on the insecure domestic strata whose exclusion contained the scheme's fiscal cost. The settlement was, in the strict sense, subsidised by insecurity, its own outside paying for its inside. This is not an argument against the settlement, whose securities were real goods in the lives they covered, and the workers who won them are not answerable for the world economy's structure. It is an argument about what a successor settlement would have to be: financed without an outside, or not financed at all. The surplus population framework poses the resulting problem in its hardest form, since a world economy that generates a growing population it cannot employ cannot obviously fund universal security from the wages of the employed, and the funding question drives analysis toward the ownership of the surplus itself, toward who appropriates the productivity gains from which alone a universal floor could be paid. At that depth, the precarity question and the oldest question of the Marxist tradition become the same question, which is perhaps the strongest evidence that the tradition's categories were the right ones all along: follow any of the debate's threads far enough, the baseline, the stratum, the surplus population, and the thread arrives at the ownership of the means of production, where the tradition always said it would.

## Precarity in the mirror of earlier debates

The precariat controversy is not the first time the tradition has confronted a claim that a new formation has superseded its categories, and the precedents instruct. Claims that a new middle class, a salariat, a class of managers or of knowledge workers had displaced the old poles of capital and labour recur across the twentieth century's sociology, each announced with the confidence of discovery and each absorbed, on inspection, into the framework it claimed to replace, as a stratum, a fraction, or a contradictory location within relations that had not changed their fundamental shape. The pattern of absorption is not evidence of the framework's dogmatism, though dogmatists have certainly wielded it as one. It is evidence of the framework's level of abstraction: a theory of relations to productive property is not refuted by changes in the occupational scenery, any more than a theory of gravitation is refuted by new architecture, and the burden on each successive claimant is to show a change at the level of relations, not merely a rearrangement of the furniture. The precariat claim, examined under that burden, shows rearrangement in abundance: contracts, statuses, and experiences transformed, while the relation, the sale of labour power by those without productive property to those with it, persists untouched at the centre of every case its literature presents. The claimant's own best material keeps proving the framework's point, since page after page of the precarity literature describes people whose entire predicament derives from having nothing to sell but their capacity to work, and whose insecurity is the insecurity of that sale's terms. A new class whose members are defined, in every concrete instance, by the classical predicament of the proletariat is a class the framework has already named.

Precedent also instructs about the fate of such concepts. The successful ones survive as stratum names, useful shorthand for a layer's condition, long after their class claims are forgotten; the unsuccessful ones survive as period pieces, indexing the anxieties of the decade that coined them. The precariat shows every sign of the first fate, and this article's verdict should be read in that light: not as an execution but as a demotion to the rank the concept can actually hold. As the name of a stratum and a condition, the word will likely persist in the literature and in journalism, and its persistence will do more good than harm provided the demotion is understood, provided, that is, that readers who meet the word supply the corrections this article has assembled: the baseline, the relational test, the surplus population's wider frame. Concepts in the social sciences are not true or false as wholes; they are instruments with rated capacities, and the critic's task is the rating. The precariat is rated, on the evidence assembled here, for description and for warning, and not for class analysis. Used within its rating, it illuminates. Used beyond it, it misleads its most sympathetic readers into a politics addressed to a subject that does not exist, while the subject that does exist, the working class in its insecure strata, waits for its champions to finish arguing about its name.

### Why do claims of a new class keep recurring in the sociology of work?

because occupational scenery changes faster than the relations beneath it, and each generation of observers mistakes the new scenery for a new structure. The claims are absorbed, one after another, as strata or fractions, once the relational test is applied. The recurrence itself is evidence about the level at which the underlying relations persist.

## The exam room and the seminar room

This subject is examined as much as it is debated, and the two settings reward the same discipline from opposite directions. In the exam room, the precarity question is a trap for the candidate who has memorised a side. The question's wording almost always conceals a baseline, and the candidate who exposes the baseline, dates it, bounds it, and only then adjudicates the claim of novelty, has answered at the level the examiner is testing, whatever verdict follows. The candidate who recites the precariat's features, or who recites the dismissal of them, has described a literature rather than analysed a problem. In the seminar room, the subject's pedagogical gift is its capacity to make method visible: a single seminar can move from the table's four rows through the relational test to the surplus population's reframing, and students feel the ground of the discussion shift at each stage, which is the sensation of an argument actually progressing rather than positions being exchanged. Teachers report, in the literature on teaching this material, that the baseline table is the device that changes the room, because it converts an exchange of impressions about the modern world into an inquiry with checkable rows. The persona sections that follow this article's main argument develop the guidance for students and teachers in the forms this series reserves for them, and the reader whose interest is practical should treat the sections so far as the analysis those practical sections presuppose.

## The working day reassembled from fragments

Consider the working day as the insecure worker actually assembles it, since the assembly is where the condition's distinctiveness from older casual labour both appears and dissolves. The day begins before any engagement, with the checking of allocations, the maintenance of availability, the arrangement of transport and care against engagements that may shift or vanish by midday. It proceeds through fragments: an engagement here, a journey, a second engagement under a different allocator's rules, gaps between them that belong neither to work nor to rest, since rest requires the certainty the gaps deny. It ends with administration, the logging of hours, the chasing of discrepancies, the application for the next fragments, work that the settlement's worker delegated to the employer's payroll office and that the insecure worker performs unpaid, nightly, as the price of remaining employable. Summed across a week, the fragments and their administration may exceed the standard working week in hours while yielding a fraction of its income and none of its securities. The assembly also explains the condition's invisibility to the statistics of employment: the worker is employed, multiply and exhaustingly employed, in every hour the survey counts and in many it does not, and the gap between the counted employment and the lived exhaustion is precisely the territory the word precarity was coined to name. Older casual labour assembled its day similarly, at the gate and the hiring fair, which is the continuity; the difference is administrative, the gate replaced by the notification, the foreman's nod by the allocator's score, a difference in the technology of allocation that leaves the relation, and the exhaustion, intact.

The fragmentation of the day has a cognitive price that the literature on attention and poverty helps to price. Decisions multiply as security falls: every day of irregular work is a day of budgeting under uncertainty, of sequencing obligations against guesses, of maintaining contingency plans for contingencies that mostly do not occur but cannot be ignored. The secured worker's great unacknowledged luxury is the smallness of the decision load that subsistence imposes, the rent that pays itself, the income that arrives as a premise rather than as a problem. Scarcity of money, the research on scarcity's psychology shows, consumes the cognitive bandwidth that planning, learning, and organising require, taxing exactly the capacities whose exercise might escape the condition. Precarity is therefore self-sealing at the level of the mind as it is at the level of the household budget: it consumes the slack, temporal, financial, and cognitive, from which all exits are built. Programmes addressed to the insecure that ignore the bandwidth tax, demanding attendance, application, and retraining as though the insecure disposed of a secretary's calendar, fail predictably and blame the failing on the population. The design lesson generalises the article's organisational one: institutions for the insecure must supply slack before they demand anything, meeting the worker at the allocation point, in the fragment's interstices, with the entitlement already attached, or they will recruit only the least precarious of the precarious and mistake the recruitment for success.

### Why does insecurity consume the very capacities needed to escape it?

because subsistence under uncertainty is cognitively expensive. Budgeting against guesses, sequencing obligations, maintaining contingency plans, and administering one's own employability consume attention and time that security would free for training, organising, and planning. The condition taxes the bandwidth its own remedy requires, which is why effective institutions supply slack before demanding effort.

## Care work and the insecurity that dare not speak its hours

Among the employments where insecurity concentrates, the work of care occupies a singular place, because its insecurity is compounded by invisibility and by a cultural script that misdescribes labour as love. Care is performed in private homes and in institutions, by workers whose engagements are fragmented by the needs of the cared for, whose hours include the night and the interval, whose emotional exertion is the substance of the product, and whose claim to the standard protections has historically been defeated by the argument, rarely stated and always operative, that the work is a natural expression of the worker's sex rather than an employment. The settlement largely excluded domestic and care labour from its architecture, an exclusion whose gendered and racialised character the feminist historiography has established beyond serious dispute. The dismantling era then generalised the care model outward, as other employments adopted its features: fragmentation, availability, the misdescription of commitment as vocation, the wage discounted by the worker's supposed intrinsic reward. To study care work's long insecurity is therefore to study the template from which the wider precarity was partly cut, and the precarity literature's failure to centre it is the coverage error in its most consequential form, the formerly protected discovering, as a novelty, a condition that the women who cleaned and nursed through the golden age could have described to them in detail at any point during it.

## Rural echoes: the oldest insecurity of all

The countryside is the precarity debate's forgotten origin, since agricultural labour was, for most of human history, the majority occupation and the paradigm of insecurity: employment by the season and the weather, payment in kind and in arrears, the cottage tied to the job so that dismissal meant eviction, and a calendar alternating between the frenzy of harvest and the starvation of the winter gap. The standard employment relationship never conquered the countryside even in the settlement's heartlands; it merely shrank the countryside's share of the workforce until its insecurity became demographically negligible and therefore theoretically invisible. The shrinkage itself was one of the great movements of labour in the period, the latent reserve army of Marx's construction, the population held in declining rural employments and released, generation by generation, into the towns, where its arrival replenished the urban surplus and disciplined urban wages. The parallel with the present argument is exact and rarely drawn. A form of insecurity can dominate a labour market's history and vanish from its theory simply by shrinking as a share of employment, while persisting intact for those who remain within it. Urban precarity's rediscovery in the dismantling decades is the same event with the sign reversed: a form of insecurity growing as a share of employment until its persistence can no longer be ignored. The lesson the countryside teaches is that the visibility of insecurity in theory tracks its share of the metropolitan workforce, not its existence in the world, and that a theory which aspires to completeness must be built, as the reserve army framework was built, to hold the town and the country, the enclave and the ocean, in a single account of labour's varying distance from security.

Harvest's calendar also bequeathed the conceptual template for understanding all seasonal and cyclical insecurity: the alternation of overwork and idleness within a single year, the impossibility of annualising income from weekly rates, the dependence on credit across the gap, and the employer's preference for a labour force that maintains itself through the idle season at its own cost and presents itself, fed and ready, at the season's turn. Every element recurs in the modern forms, in the festival peaks and January silences of service employments, in the project cycles of the contracted professions, in the tourist season and the academic term. The mechanisms differ in their administrative dress and are identical in their allocation of the gap's cost to the worker. A student who grasps the harvest calendar has grasped the deep grammar of insecure work in all its dialects, and the historiography of rural labour, unfashionable as it may be beside the literature of platforms, remains the cheapest education in the subject available.

### What does the countryside's history teach the precarity debate?

that the visibility of insecurity in theory follows its share of the metropolitan workforce rather than its existence in the world. Agricultural labour was the paradigm of insecure work for centuries and vanished from theory mainly by shrinking as a proportion of employment. Urban precarity's rediscovery is the same accounting event with the direction reversed.

## The pension horizon and old age as the final audit

Every employment regime is finally audited in old age, when the working life's terms are converted, irreversibly, into the terms of retirement. The settlement's audit was its proudest exhibit: the continuous contributor retired on a pension that, however modest, converted the biography of security into a secured ending, and the visibility of that ending, in every street, disciplined the young into the contributory virtues. Precarity's audit is already legible in projection, and the literature on pension coverage among the insecure, stated directionally, is among the most alarming in the field: interrupted contributions, years outside the recognised statuses, low and fluctuating earnings on which little can accumulate, and private provision that presupposes exactly the surplus and continuity the condition denies. The result, arriving on a lag of decades, will be the return of mass old-age poverty in economies that congratulated themselves on abolishing it, arriving precisely as the surplus population question sharpens, since the aged insecure are the surplus population in its final form, beyond even the theoretical possibility of absorption. Societies register such arrivals as fiscal crises and demographic burdens. The framework of this article registers them earlier and more accurately, as the deferred invoice of the dismantling, the moment at which the transfer of risk completes its journey through a life and presents itself, undeniable at last, as a collective charge that was never abolished but only postponed and privatised until privatisation's term expired.

## Solidarity's arithmetic: why the strata need one another

The strategic conclusion of the whole analysis can be stated as an arithmetic of mutual need. The protected core, shrinking, cannot defend its protections alone, because each defence is outflanked by the growth of the unprotected labour that employers can substitute and by the political isolation of a stratum increasingly describable, and described, as privileged. The insecure strata, growing, cannot win security alone, because they lack the accumulated institutions, funds, and organisational memory that winning requires, and because their condition systematically consumes the slack from which organisation is built. Each stratum holds what the other lacks: the core holds the institutions and the remembered standard; the insecure hold the numbers and the future. The politics that unites them, the extension of the core's securities outward under the core's institutional shelter, is the only programme in the field that answers the mechanism, since it attacks the boundary on which the whole system of differentiated insecurity depends. Every alternative programme leaves the boundary intact and argues about which side of it deserves better treatment, which is why such programmes, however passionate, reproduce the condition they protest. The boundary is the mechanism. Abolish the boundary's consequences, by attaching security to the person instead of the post, and the employer's ledger that this article reconstructed loses its central entry, the saving that reclassification and casualisation exist to capture.

The arithmetic has a temporal dimension that urgency should not obscure. Institutions decay at the speed of their membership's retirement, while the insecure strata's self-organisation matures at the speed of trust accumulated through small victories. The window in which the core's institutions remain substantial enough to shelter an extension, and the stratum's organisations remain oriented toward the general institutions rather than toward separatist or resentful alternatives, is not permanent. Standing's warning about the dangerous class is, at its most defensible, a warning about that window: a stratum denied admission to the old house will eventually build or burn according to the offers it receives, and the offers from the merchants of resentment arrive faster than the offers from the institutions of labour. The warning does not require the class theory to be worth heeding. It requires only the observation, which the whole of this article's evidence supports, that expectation betrayed and organisation absent is the classic recipe for political catastrophe, and that the recipe's ingredients are assembled wherever a settlement is dismantled faster than its successor is imagined.

### Why do the protected and the insecure need each other strategically?

because each possesses what the other is missing. The protected core holds institutions, funds, and a remembered standard but shrinks toward isolation; the insecure strata hold numbers and the future but lack accumulated organisation. Extension of security across the boundary is the only programme that attacks the mechanism, since the boundary itself is what makes differentiated insecurity profitable.

## A note on the sources and their limits

Scholarly hygiene requires a closing note on the materials from which this article's argument is built, and on the limits the reader should bear in mind. The argument rests on four bodies of material, each with a characteristic strength and a characteristic blindness. The historiography of labour, from Thompson and Hobsbawm forward, supplies the baseline, and its blindness is the archive's: it knows most about the workers who left records, which is to say the organised and the literate, and must infer the casual majority from hostile or incidental sources. The precarity literature, centred on Standing's 2011 statement and its critics, supplies the phenomenology and the debate, and its blindness is the metropolitan and educated standpoint from which much of it is written. The research literature on employment change, comparative and sociological, supplies the directional findings about the dismantling decades, and its blindness is definitional, since its categories shift beneath it as the statuses it counts are redesigned. The Marxist framework supplies the relational test and the dynamics, and its blindness is experiential, the silence about lost expectation that this article has had to correct from the literature it otherwise criticises. An argument woven from four materials, each correcting another's blindness, is stronger than any of its sources and weaker than certainty. The reader who wants to go further should read across the four in the same corrective spirit, and should treat this article's verdict as what its defeater clause declares it to be: the best judgement the assembled record supports, reversible by the specified evidence and by nothing weaker.

## The vocabulary of flexibility and its one-way direction

Words in this field should be audited like accounts, and flexibility is the entry most in need of audit. In the dismantling decades' vocabulary, flexibility described a virtue, modern, adaptive, liberating, and its application to labour markets carried the virtue with it, so that to oppose flexible working was to oppose adaptability itself. The audit begins by asking the baseline question of the word: flexible for whom, in which direction, at whose cost? An engagement flexible for the employer, variable in hours, terminable at will, uncommitted in duration, is rigid for the worker in every dimension that matters to a life: the worker must be inflexibly available, inflexibly solvent against the income's variation, inflexibly ready to rearrange care, transport, and rest around allocations announced at the allocator's convenience. The flexibility of the system as a whole has not increased; it has been concentrated, gathered from the many who once held small shares of it, the right to a predictable week, to a refused shift, to a planned holiday, and deposited with the few who allocate. The word's career is a case study in the ideological use of abstraction: by naming a property of the system while suppressing its distribution, the vocabulary made a transfer of power sound like a gain in efficiency. The precarity literature, to its credit, saw through the word early, and the tradition's older vocabulary saw through it earlier still, since the demand for labour exactly when wanted and its dismissal exactly when not is the demand Marx's reserve army analysis placed at the centre of the wage relation's normal operation. Flexibility is the reserve army's public relations name, and recognising the identity restores the debate's continuity with the analysis that named the thing first.

A companion word deserves the same treatment: choice. The insecure engagement is persistently described, in the literature of celebration, as chosen, and the description trades on an equivocation between choosing among options and choosing the option set. Workers choose engagements as diners choose from a menu written elsewhere, and the freedom of the choice is real exactly as far as the menu extends and not one dish further. Where the menu contains only insecure engagements, the universality of insecure choices proves nothing about preferences, a point so elementary that its persistent neglect in the celebratory literature must be counted a rhetorical achievement rather than an oversight. The revealed preference of insecure workers, wherever a secure engagement is genuinely on the menu, is recorded in the length of the queues for protected posts examined earlier in this article: the queue is the preference, revealed at scale, and it points in one direction only.

### Why does the word flexibility mislead in labour market debate?

because it names a system-wide property while hiding how that property is distributed. Hours variable for the employer require the worker to be rigidly available, rigidly solvent, and rigidly ready to rearrange a life around short notice. The flexibility has not grown; it has been transferred from the many who held small shares of it to the few who allocate work.

## Precarity and the meaning of a fair day's work

Beneath the analytical disputes runs a moral intuition that the analysis should acknowledge and clarify rather than dismiss: the intuition that there is something indecent in a labour relation that takes a worker's best hours and years while accepting no responsibility for the worker's continuity, that consumes labour power as a pure input while treating its maintenance as an externality to be borne by the worker, the family, and the shrinking commons of public provision. The Marxist framework translates the intuition into its own terms without remainder: the wage, in the classical analysis, was supposed to cover the reproduction of labour power, the cost of maintaining the worker and raising the next generation of workers, and a labour regime in which the wage, aggregated across its fragments, systematically fails to cover reproduction is a regime consuming its own foundation, drawing down the stock of healthy, skilled, housed, and hopeful labour power that previous arrangements accumulated. The drawdown can proceed for a long time before it appears in the accounts, because the stock is large and its depletion is registered first in the private ledgers of households, in deferred care and deferred children and exhausted kin, rather than in any statistic the system's managers watch. When it does appear, as demographic decline, as skills famine, as the politics of despair, it is discussed as an exogenous misfortune, a cultural turn or a demographic fate, by the same commentary that celebrated the flexibility whose invoice it is. The framework's cold satisfaction in such moments is not a pleasure this article takes. It is, however, a reason to keep the framework: it connects the invoice to the purchase, across the decades that separate them, when every other vocabulary in the field has lost the thread.

## Comparisons across the stratum's own borders

The stratum's internal variety has been invoked repeatedly as an objection to its class status, and the variety deserves one direct inspection, because it also refutes a subtler error, the assumption that the condition's unity of mechanism implies a unity of experience that politics can simply address. Place the cases side by side. A young graduate in casual professional work, indebted, queueing for a protected post, whose insecurity is a tunnel with a plausible exit. A middle-aged worker pushed out of a protected post into agency engagements, whose insecurity is a slope with no exit short of retirement's inadequacy. A migrant worker on a tied status, whose insecurity is a legal condition layered under the economic one. A mother reassembling paid work around care in fragments the labour market prices at its lowest, whose insecurity is a permanent negotiation with time itself. A lifelong casual worker for whom the condition is not an event but an element, whose insecurity has no biography of loss because it has no contrast. The mechanism is one: the transfer of risk, the detachment of entitlements, the competitive allocation. The experiences are five, and the politics adequate to each differs, in its emotional address, its time horizon, and its plausible demands, as much as the experiences do. A movement that speaks to the tunnel will bewilder the slope; a programme built for the element will sound like surrender to the tunnel's hopeful queue. The craft of organising the stratum is the craft of composing these differences into demands that each case can recognise as its own, security's universal grammar declined in five biographical cases, and the craft is rare precisely because it requires the analysis this article has attempted: one mechanism, many experiences, no shortcut through a class name that pretends the differences away.

The comparison also returns a final verdict on the heterogeneity objection to the precariat concept, refining it. Heterogeneity does not by itself refute a class claim, since classes are always internally various; the English working class of the classic historiography contained more various trades and conditions than the precariat does. What refutes the claim is that the variety in this case is not variety within a shared relation but variety of relation to the settlement's memory, to legal status, to property and credentials, which is to say variety at the level the class concept actually measures. The five cases share a mechanism's output and nothing deeper, and a politics honest about that will build a coalition and call it one, rather than proclaiming a class and waiting for it to behave like one. Coalitions are not lesser things than classes; they are the form in which most historical change by the labouring majority has actually been accomplished, the settlement itself being a coalition's monument. The precariat literature's deepest error may be its contempt, implicit in its central concept, for the coalition form: its insistence that the insecure must be a class to matter, when the record shows that mattering has always been the coalition's work.

### What does comparing cases inside the stratum finally show?

that one mechanism produces many experiences, differing by biography, legal status, and relation to the settlement's memory. The variety refutes not solidarity but the class shortcut: what can be built from such material is a coalition around security's universal demands, and coalitions, not proclaimed classes, are the form in which labour's historical victories were actually won.

## The durability of the question

Why does this subject refuse to settle, returning decade after decade in new vocabulary to the same seminar rooms and the same front pages? Because the condition it names is not a problem the system has failed to solve but a tendency the system continuously regenerates, and each generation meets the tendency in the dress of its own labour market and takes the dress for a discovery. The vocabulary will change again; the gig economy class will date, as earlier coinages dated, and a successor term will announce the condition anew to a readership that believes itself the first to feel it. Against that cycle of rediscovery, the defences are the ones this article has practised until they should by now be reflexes: the baseline question, the relational test, the coverage clause, the concession to lost expectation, and the surplus population's widening of the frame. A reader who carries those five instruments into the next iteration of the debate will be equipped to extract its genuine news, for there is always genuine news in the mechanisms and the proportions, while declining its perennial falsehood, the claim that the ground itself has shifted beneath a working class that stands, in its relation to capital, exactly where the tradition located it. The ground has not shifted. The tide has moved across it, up and down the shore of security, and the task of analysis is to chart the tide without mistaking it for an earthquake.

## Organising the argument: a synthesis in one movement

Gather the threads, since an article of this length owes its reader a single passage in which the whole can be held at once. A word, precarity, names the condition of work stripped of security's architecture. A thesis, Standing's, promotes the condition into a class and dates it as an emergence. A table corrects the dating: the architecture stood for three decades, in few places, for a subset, and the condition it briefly displaced is the labour market's historical default. A category from the tradition's founder already contains the condition's description, in the stagnant form of the surplus population, and a test of relations refuses the promotion to class, since the condition's sufferers sell labour power like the rest of their class and differ in stratum, not in relation. A concession preserves the thesis's truth: the architecture's dismantling within living memory inflicted a loss that the old categories do not name, the loss of an expected life, and that loss is a political force of the first order. A sharper thesis widens the frame: beyond the insecure employed stretches the population accumulation cannot absorb, for whom the wage itself is intermittent, and whose growth is the period's deepest labour question. And a defeater disciplines the whole: the verdict stands unless the historiography of the earlier record is overturned, which is the only evidence that could overturn it. The synthesis is not a compromise between the debate's camps, splitting differences at their midpoints. It is an ordering, in which each camp's strongest material is assigned its proper level, description, history, relation, experience, and prognosis kept distinct, and the confusions that made the camps appear irreconcilable are retired as level errors. That ordering is the article's contribution, and the reader who disputes its verdicts can locate the dispute exactly, at one level, against specified evidence, which is all that scholarly disagreement requires to be fruitful.

## The night, the week, and the year's shape under insecurity

Time's texture under insecure employment deserves one more examination at the scale of the calendar, because the settlement's deepest gift was calendrical: it gave the worker a year with a shape. The week had its boundary, the year its holidays accumulated as of right, the life its seasons of training, earning, and rest in legible succession. Insecurity dissolves the calendar at every scale simultaneously. The week loses its boundary when availability is continuous and allocations arrive without respect for rest days. The year loses its holidays when absence is unpaid and therefore unaffordable, so that the insecure worker's year becomes a flat expanse of readiness punctuated by engagements, with rest taken only in the involuntary form of gaps that anxiety poisons. The life loses its seasons when training does not ripen and mastery does not protect. Sociologists who study working time under flexibility describe the result as temporal anarchy, and the phrase is exact: not the absence of time's discipline but its fragmentation into disciplines imposed by others at unpredictable intervals, which is a more complete subjection than the factory bell ever achieved, since the bell at least announced itself and fell silent. The bell's successor, the notification, neither announces a term nor permits an aftermath, and the worker governed by it is never entirely at work and never entirely away, a condition the older labour movement would have recognised instantly as the abolition of the distinction its first campaigns were fought to establish, the distinction between the hours sold and the hours that remain one's own.

Against that abolition, the demands that recur in the stratum's own organising acquire a calendrical unity: notice of rosters, payment for availability, the right to refuse without penalty, holidays that survive the change of employer. Each demand is a small restoration of the calendar, an attempt to reimpose shape on time from below. The tradition's oldest demand, the limitation of the working day, was the same attempt at a larger scale, and the continuity should reassure anyone tempted to see the stratum's struggles as a new politics for a new class. The struggle over time is the labour movement's founding struggle, and the insecure are fighting it on the terrain to which the dismantling drove it, the roster and the notification rather than the factory gate, with the same adversary and the same stake: whether the worker's life is the worker's own outside the hours purchased, and whether the hours purchased have edges at all.

### How is the notification a more complete discipline than the factory bell?

because the bell announced a bounded term and then fell silent, while allocation by notification claims continuous availability without continuous employment. The worker is never fully summoned and never fully released, so the distinction between sold hours and one's own hours, the distinction the earliest labour campaigns were fought to establish, dissolves into permanent readiness.

## Trust, reputation, and the scored worker

A quieter transformation accompanies the loud ones: the conversion of the worker's reputation into a scored and portable asset, owned in practice by the allocating systems rather than by the worker. Under the settlement, reputation travelled by word of mouth within a trade and belonged, in effect, to its subject, who could carry it between employers and appeal to it in disputes. Under scored allocation, reputation is a number computed from ratings, completion records, and acceptance rates, legible to the allocator, opaque to the scored, and forfeitable without hearing when the number dips. The scored worker thus labours under a double insecurity: the engagement is insecure, and the record on which future engagements depend is held by the counterparty. The arrangement generalises beyond platforms wherever employers share blacklists, reference databases, and informal warnings, and its disciplinary efficiency exceeds anything the settlement's employers commanded, since the sanction it threatens is not dismissal from a job but erasure from a market. Marx's analysis of the wage relation assumed the worker's freedom to sell labour power to any buyer as the formal premise of the whole system, the freedom that distinguished the wage labourer from the serf. A regime in which access to buyers is mediated by scores the buyers control qualifies that freedom in substance while preserving it in form, and the qualification deserves the tradition's closest attention, because it suggests that the frontier of the labour relation's development lies not in the contract's length but in the governance of access to contracts altogether.

## Children of the insecure and the inheritance of expectation

Expectation is inherited, and the dismantling's longest shadow falls across the children of the insecure, whose formation proceeds inside the condition their parents fell into or never left. The effects are material before they are psychological: housing that moves, schooling interrupted by moves, the household's emergencies absorbed by children as a normal atmosphere, the parent's availability consumed by the allocator's demands at exactly the hours family life requires. The effects are also formative in the strict sense, since a child raised inside insecurity learns the labour market's lessons before encountering the market: that promises are provisional, that institutions fail, that planning is a luxury of other people's parents, and that one's own future is a sequence to be improvised rather than a structure to be built. Schools register the formation as disengagement or as its opposite, a frantic credentialism, and both registers misread a rational adaptation as a trait of character. The intergenerational transmission of insecurity is the mechanism by which a labour market condition becomes a social structure, reproducing itself in the expectations of people who have not yet sold an hour of labour, and it is the strongest reason for refusing the consoling thought that the dismantling was an episode. Episodes end. Transmissions continue, and the population whose expectations were formed by the fall will staff the labour market, and its politics, long after the settlement's last beneficiaries have left both.

The transmission also runs in the other direction, and the reverse flow is the stratum's hidden asset. Children of the insecure who reach education carry into the seminar room and the profession a knowledge of the condition that no fieldwork can purchase, and the literature of the dismantling is increasingly written by the condition's own graduates, whose accounts correct the metropolitan discovery narrative from inside. The precarity concept's career will be decided partly by this authorship: whether the generation that inherited the condition finds the concept adequate to a life that never contained the fall the concept presupposes. Early signs, in the writing of that generation, suggest a cooler reception, a preference for the vocabularies of rent, debt, and surplus, and an impatience with the elegy for a settlement their families never inhabited. Concepts, like settlements, are audited by their successors, and the audit now beginning has the baseline table's own spirit: it asks of the famous concept what this article asked of the famous settlement, whom it covered, and for how long, and from where it spoke.

### How does insecurity reproduce itself in the next generation before that generation works?

through the household's atmosphere and the expectations it teaches: that promises are provisional, institutions unreliable, and planning a luxury. Children absorb the labour market's lessons from moves, emergencies, and consumed parental time, and carry the formation into school and work as disengagement or frantic credentialism, both misread as character rather than adaptation.

## The small employer and the chain of insecurity

Insecurity is transmitted down chains of contracting as faithfully as it is generated at their heads, and the small employer, so often cast as the condition's fellow victim, occupies an ambiguous middle position that honest analysis should neither excuse nor caricature. The small enterprise engaged by a large one on punishing terms, paid late, squeezed on price, terminable at convenience, passes its own insecurity downward in the only currency it commands, the terms of its workers: shorter engagements, thinner protections, wages that wait on the client's payment. The chain thus converts the bargaining power of its head into a gradient of precarity along its length, with each link's workers bearing the accumulated risk of every link above. The political consequence is a fractured landscape in which the immediate author of a worker's insecurity is frequently another insecure actor, and the system's true beneficiaries sit several removes away, insulated by the chain's length from both view and liability. Labour law's traditional instruments, built for the two-party employment relation, struggle with the chain, and the struggle is one of the genuine novelties of the field, a place where the precarity literature's attention to fissured workplaces has contributed analysis the older framework lacked and has had to absorb. Absorption, here as elsewhere, is the framework's method: the chain is a mechanism for distributing the reserve army's discipline through contractual layers, and naming it so connects the new observation to the old dynamics without denying the observation's freshness.

## Public services and the insecurity of those who staff them

The dismantling reached, in its later stages, the employments that the settlement itself had built as its own infrastructure: the public services, whose staffs had embodied the standard relationship in its most complete form, with tenure, pensions, and vocational identity fused into a single status. The conversion of that status, by contracting out, by agency substitution, by the fragmentation of services into purchasable units with workforces attached on market terms, is the dismantling's most symbolically complete episode, because it demonstrates that no employment is protected by its social usefulness once the architecture of its status is redesigned. The nurse's aide engaged through an agency to work the same ward, the teacher supplied by the day to the same classroom, the clerk whose office became a contract and whose contract became a roster, each enacts the general law this article has traced: the work persists, the worker persists, the status evaporates, and the security that the status carried is discovered, in the evaporation, to have been the substance all along. The episode also dissolves a comforting boundary in the public mind, between a precarious private economy and a secure public remnant to which the displaced might retreat. The remnant's own precaritisation closes the retreat, and with it closes the last geography in which the settlement's biography could be lived unchanged, which is one reason the condition's politics have generalised in the dismantling's later decades from a grievance of particular trades into a premise of public life.

There is a fiscal irony inside the episode that the literature on contracting has documented and that the framework predicts. The savings recorded when a tenured post becomes an agency engagement are partly illusory, since the state that sheds the employer's obligations acquires, in exchange, the costs of the insecurity it has manufactured: the in-work poverty it must supplement, the health consequences it must treat, the old age it must pension at the floor, and the administrative machinery of perpetual re-tendering. The transfer of risk, examined at the level of the whole society, turns out to be partly a transfer between budgets and partly a deferral with interest, and the interest is paid in the currency this article has tracked throughout, the consumed health, deferred lives, and corroded expectations of the workforce concerned. Austerity's arithmetic and flexibility's arithmetic are the same arithmetic, and both fail in the same place: they price the guarantee's removal as a saving without pricing the guarantee's absence as a cost, because the cost falls due in other ledgers and later years.

### Why is the precaritisation of public employment the dismantling's most complete episode?

because it proves that social usefulness confers no protection once a status is redesigned. The work and the worker persist while the tenure, pension, and vocational identity evaporate, demonstrating in the settlement's own house the general law that security lived in the architecture of status rather than in the nature of the job.

## What remains of the argument after the argument

An article should end by acknowledging the distance between its subject as analysed and its subject as lived, a distance no verdict closes. The worker checking a telephone in the hour before dawn is not waiting for a taxonomy, and the analysis offered here, the baseline, the stagnant form, the surplus population, will be judged by that worker, fairly, on whether it returns anything usable: an explanation that converts shame into grievance, a history that converts isolation into membership, a programme that converts the stratum's stratagems into institutions. The tradition this article has defended earned its authority, where it earned it, by passing exactly that test for earlier generations of the insecure, and it will keep or lose that authority on the same terms now. The precariat concept, whatever its rating as analysis, was an attempt to pass the test by naming, and the attempt's popularity measures the need it addressed. Let the last word therefore be neither the concept's nor the framework's but the condition's own, stated in the terms the whole article has laboured to make precise. Insecurity is the old condition of labour under capital, interrupted once, briefly and partially, by a security that had to be built and was not defended in its building's own terms, universality. The interruption proves the condition is not fate. The brevity proves the building is not enough without the defence. And the population living the condition's return, enlarged now by the surplus that accumulation cannot absorb, is the same class that built the interruption, under a worse sky and with a clearer map, this article among the attempts to draw it, of where the walls stood and why they fell.

## The interview, the trial shift, and unpaid auditioning

The gateway into insecure work has itself been precaritised, and the gateway's transformation is a compact illustration of every mechanism in this article. Where the settlement's hiring was a brief formality preceding a durable relation, hiring into insecurity is a prolonged audition: the application assembled from unpaid hours, the trial shift worked without certainty of engagement or, in the grey zones the literature documents, without pay, the probationary engagement at reduced terms, the succession of assessments whose real function is to keep a pool of the auditioning permanently available and permanently hopeful. Auditioning is labour, skilled and effortful, and its unpaid character marks a further transfer, of recruitment's costs from the employer onto the applicants themselves, each of whom subsidises the employer's selection with hours the employer's own accounts never record. The pool's hope is the system's fuel, and hope, unlike wages, costs the allocator nothing to issue and nothing to disappoint. A Marxist reading recognises the audition as the reserve army's discipline refined to the level of the individual biography: the reserve no longer merely waits at the gate but performs at the gate, continuously, in competition with itself, and the performance's prize is admission to the insecurity beyond. The older casual labourer at least knew the shape of his subjection, the foreman's glance over the morning crowd. The auditioning worker's subjection is gamified, scored, and narrated as opportunity, which does not make it lighter. It makes it harder to name, and the naming, as this article has argued throughout, is where politics begins.

Closely allied is the phenomenon of the perpetual intern and the volunteer of necessity, the worker who labours without wages at all on the promise of a future claim, a reference, an experience, a place in a queue whose head is never quite reached. Such arrangements were once confined to the threshold of the professions; they have generalised wherever the queue is long and the gate narrow, and they represent the transfer of risk at its logical limit, the point where the worker pays, in labour, for the privilege of remaining a candidate for labour's sale. The framework's categories accommodate the limit case without strain: a reserve army so deep that its members bid the wage down to zero and below, purchasing candidacy with unpaid work, is the relative surplus population operating under conditions of extreme surplus, and the phenomenon's spread among the educated is the credential queue's arithmetic arriving at its conclusion. The precarity literature deserves credit for documenting the limit case vividly; the framework deserves credit for explaining why the limit case is not an aberration but a tendency, visible wherever the surplus is deep enough and the gate narrow enough, and destined to advance until the surplus or the gate is addressed.

### Why is unpaid auditioning a transfer and not merely a hurdle?

because the costs of selection have been moved from the employer to the applicant pool. Hours of application, trial labour, and probationary discount are real work consumed by the employer's recruitment, recorded nowhere in the employer's accounts. The pool subsidises its own selection, and the hope it is fed costs the allocator nothing either to offer or to betray.

## Weather, season, and the new calendars of demand

Demand's rhythms have always governed insecure work, but the rhythms themselves have been reorganised in ways the stratum feels as an intensification of the calendar's tyranny. The older seasonalities were collective and predictable: the harvest, the building season, the holiday peak arrived for everyone together, could be planned around communally, and supported institutions, the seasonal migration, the winter's alternative trades, the hiring fair's fixed date, that converted rhythm into a rough security of expectation. The newer rhythms are fragmented and privatised: peaks that arrive by promotion, by weather event, by algorithmic surge, announced to the individual worker at short notice and experienced as personal summonses rather than as the season's shared turning. The difference matters because predictability, even of hardship, is a form of wealth: the community that knows the slack season can organise against it, pooling, migrating, and enduring together, while the worker summoned and dismissed by privatised rhythms faces each fluctuation alone and unprepared, the fluctuation's very unpredictability preventing the formation of any collective rhythm in response. Flexibility's deepest victory is this atomisation of time itself, the replacement of a shared calendar, which workers could organise around and eventually against, by a private weather of demand that each worker weathers separately. Recovering a shared calendar, through notice periods, guaranteed patterns, and collective scheduling rights, is correspondingly among the stratum's most concrete and least utopian demands, a demand for the precondition of every other solidarity: a common time in which to meet.

## The double day of the insecure carer of others

Return once more to the household, where this article's mechanisms converge on a figure who concentrates them: the worker, statistically and culturally a woman, who sells insecure labour in the market and performs insecure labour's unpaid counterpart at home, absorbing in her own person the fluctuations of two systems at once. Her market work is fragmented because her availability is fragmented by care; her care work is intensified because her market income is insufficient and unreliable; and each fragmentation is used to justify the other, the employer discounting her commitment, the household depending on her discounting. The settlement's architecture at least priced her position, however inadequately and however patriarchally, through the breadwinner wage and the dependant's claims. The dismantled regime prices it not at all, treating her double day as two private arrangements that happen to coincide in one exhausted person. Feminist political economy's central correction to the whole precarity field applies here with undiminished force: any account of the condition that begins at the factory gate or the allocation point, rather than at the household where labour power is daily and generationally reproduced, has begun in the middle of the story, and the part it missed is the part where the condition's costs are finally paid. The surplus population framework, to its credit, can be extended to her: she is the insurer of the surplus population, the one whose unpaid labour keeps the wageless and the precarious alive at standards the market would not fund, and her exhaustion is the system's deepest subsidy and its least recorded one.

Her figure also completes the article's answer to the question of who the settlement's baseline excluded. It excluded her twice, as a worker confined to its margins and as a dependant whose security was derivative, and the precarity literature's discovery narrative excludes her a third time, by treating the condition she always inhabited as the new experience of the men the settlement protected. A historiography that centred her would write the whole period differently: the golden age as a brief subsidy of one household form, the dismantling as the subsidy's withdrawal and the generalisation of her condition to the population at large. That rewriting is under way in the scholarship, and this article's framework is compatible with it at every point, since the baseline, the coverage clause, and the reproduction of labour power are precisely the instruments the rewriting uses. The tradition's own house required the correction, and acknowledging the requirement is part of the honesty the verdict's defeater clause is meant to institutionalise: frameworks, like verdicts, should state what would change them, and feminist political economy has already changed this one for the better.

### Why does the analysis of precarity have to begin at the household rather than the workplace?

because the household is where labour power is reproduced and where insecurity's costs are finally absorbed. The unpaid labour, overwhelmingly women's, that stretches food, absorbs emergencies, and cares for those the market cannot place is the subsidy on which the whole regime of cheap, flexible labour rests, and an account beginning at the gate never sees the subsidy.

## The argument's edge: where this article's verdict could be wrong

Fairness to the reader requires a section that presses this article's own verdict at its weakest points, since a verdict that has survived only friendly questioning has not been tested. Three pressure points deserve naming. First, the baseline rests on historiography, and historiography revises: if the archival record of earlier centuries were shown to contain far more continuing, protected employment than the discipline finds in the record as it stands, the exception's boundedness would shrink and the verdict with it. The article judges the probability low, given the record's consistency across independent national historiographies, but low is not zero, and the defeater clause exists precisely for this contingency. Second, the relational test for class is a theoretical commitment, not a fact, and a reader who rejects the Marxist definition of class will reject the verdict's central refusal while accepting everything else in the article; the article's response is that definitions must be judged by their explanatory yield, and the relational definition's yield, across this subject and the series, is the ground of its retention, but the commitment should be visible rather than smuggled. Third, the surplus population argument, the article's chosen prognosis, rests on a judgement about accumulation's absorptive capacity that honest analysts dispute, pointing to the new employments that successive waves of displacement eventually generated. If absorption reasserts itself at scale, the surplus population shrinks back toward a reserve, and the precarity question returns to the classical terrain of terms and protections. The article's structure survives that outcome, since its verdict on the precariat does not depend on the prognosis, but the prognosis is flagged here as the verdict's most revisable component, so that the reader's confidence can be apportioned with the precision the subject deserves.

## The reference, the record, and the worker's paper shadow

Every worker carries a paper shadow, the accumulated record of engagements, contributions, certifications, and judgements that institutions consult before admitting the worker to the next engagement, and the shadow's governance is an under-examined province of the condition. Under the settlement, the shadow was thick and largely benign: a contribution record that grew with employment, a service history that testified to continuity, references that spoke within a trade's small world where exaggeration was checked by reputation. Under insecurity, the shadow thins and darkens at once. Engagements too short to register leave gaps that read as idleness; statuses misclassified by employers leave records that follow the worker rather than the misclassifier; contribution gaps created by the allocator's convenience become the worker's pension inadequacy decades later, at an audit no appeal can reach. The worker, meanwhile, is made responsible for the shadow's maintenance, assembling proof of a working life from fragments the system's own design scattered, and the administrative burden falls, as every burden in this article has fallen, on the party least equipped to carry it. Bureaucratic literacy becomes a survival skill of the stratum, and its uneven distribution, by education, by language, by the sheer time that form-filling consumes, sorts the insecure into those who can work the record and those whom the record works over. A full political economy of precarity would include a chapter on the shadow: on who keeps it, who may read it, who profits from its gaps, and what a worker's right to a legible, portable, and contestable record would mean as a demand. The demand is modest in form and radical in implication, since a record the worker owned and carried would dissolve one of the allocator's quietest powers, the power to make a working life disappear by declining to document it.

The shadow has a temporal reach that completes the point. Contribution records are the pension system's memory, and a regime that fragments employment fragments the memory, so that the worker arrives at old age accompanied by a shadow full of holes that the system's rules interpret as a biography of insufficient effort. The interpretation is the final injustice of the transfer: risks imposed by the employment relation are recorded as choices made by the worker, and the record's authority, its status as mere fact, conceals the imposition more completely than any ideology could. Historians of earlier record regimes, of the parish register and the poor law file, will recognise the pattern, since the documentation of the poor has always served the documenting institutions first. The stratum's demand for its own record is therefore also a demand about the future's historiography: that the dismantling decades not bequeath to their historians an archive in which the insecure appear, once again, as gaps.

### Why does the worker's record become a site of injustice under insecure employment?

because the record converts imposed risks into apparent choices. Gaps left by short engagements, misclassification by employers, and interrupted contributions are stored as the worker's biography and read back, at pension age and at every hiring gate, as evidence of the worker's insufficiency, while the decisions that produced the gaps leave no trace in the file.

## Language learning, accent, and the market for the self

The insecure worker sells not only labour power but a presentation, and the market for the presentation has its own cruelties that the fieldwork literature records and the theory should absorb. Where allocation is discretionary and competition continuous, the worker's manner, accent, appearance, and fluency in the allocator's codes become part of the commodity, assessed at every encounter and impossible to separate from the labour itself. The settlement's protected worker could be graceless within wide limits, since the post was his by contract and seniority; the insecure worker's post is renewed by impression, and impression management becomes unpaid labour of a peculiarly intimate kind, the continuous manufacture of an employable self. Sociologists of emotional and aesthetic labour have mapped this manufacture in the service employments; its generalisation across the stratum is one of the dismantling's quieter cultural products, a population trained to experience its own personality as a business asset under perpetual review. The Marxist framework has a phrase waiting for the phenomenon, the sale of labour power extended to the seller's self-presentation, alienation wearing a smile, and the phrase's availability should not dull the phenomenon's specific ache: the worker who must be liked to be fed lives a different unfreedom from the worker who must merely be strong, and the difference, multiplied across a stratum, shapes a culture's texture, its deference, its irony, its exhaustion with performance, in ways the political literature is only beginning to register.

## The strike that cannot be called and the leverage that remains

Classical labour leverage rested on a simple geometry: workers gathered at a point of production whose stoppage cost the employer more than concession, and the withdrawal of labour, coordinated and timed, converted the geometry into power. Insecurity dissolves the geometry at every point. The workforce is dispersed, its stoppage is the allocator's ordinary Tuesday, since non-selection is indistinguishable from sanction, and the employer's dependence on any given worker approaches zero where the reserve at the gate is deep. Analysts who conclude that the stratum is therefore powerless have, however, mistaken the old geometry for the only one. The stratum's labour, aggregated, remains indispensable: the city that the insecure clean, feed, nurse, deliver to, and guard does not run a day without them, and the indispensability is a geometry of a different kind, distributed rather than concentrated, whose stoppage would be felt not at one gate but across a whole urban metabolism. Coordinating a distributed stoppage is harder than coordinating a concentrated one by exactly the margin that the allocation systems were designed to produce, which is why the organisational inventions catalogued earlier, the portable institutions, the collectivised allocation, the community-based recruitment, matter as leverage's preconditions rather than as welfare accessories. Where such inventions have matured, the stratum has discovered actions proportioned to its geometry: the coordinated refusal of allocations at peak, the public naming of allocators, the alliance with the service's users, whose dependence on the work is the stratum's strongest card. None of this restores the factory gate. All of it refutes the obituary for labour leverage that the flexibility literature pronounced, prematurely and with suspicious satisfaction, at the dismantling's height.

The state of the question, then, is neither the old movement's confidence nor the new literature's despair. It is a race between the maturation of the stratum's own institutions and the decay of the inherited ones, run against the clock of the surplus population's growth, and its outcome will decide whether the condition this article has analysed is remembered as a passage, through which the working class travelled toward new forms of security, or as a destination, the form in which labour's subordination was finally perfected by being made too fragmented to resist. Scholarship cannot run the race for the runners. It can refuse the false consolations, the new class that exists only in books, the golden age that exists only in elegy, and it can keep the map honest, which is the service this article has attempted from its first paragraph to its verdict.

### Where does leverage live for workers whose stoppage looks like an ordinary day?

in the aggregate indispensability of their labour to the systems they staff. Individually replaceable, the stratum collectively operates the cleaning, feeding, nursing, delivering, and guarding on which urban life depends. Actions proportioned to that distributed geometry, coordinated refusals at peak, alliances with service users, become possible once portable institutions solve the coordination problem.

## Coda: the exception's lesson

The exception was the security. Four words have carried this article's argument, and they can carry its ending too, provided their full weight is felt. They mean that the condition now rediscovered with alarm is the condition from which a few, for a while, escaped; that the escape was built by organisation out of a favourable hour; that its builders knew it for a conquest and garrisoned it with institutions; that the garrison was allowed to thin while the condition waited, patient as water, at every gate the institutions did not guard; and that the return of the condition is therefore not a mystery of the spirit of the age but a lesson in the arithmetic of defence, which every generation of labour must relearn at the price its predecessors paid for the same knowledge. The precariat will be renamed. The reserve army will be rediscovered. The surplus population will grow or shrink with accumulation's appetite. Through every renaming, the question the baseline table poses will remain the first question of the subject, and the workers who learn to ask it, of every claim and every elegy and every programme including this article's, will have taken from these pages the one thing worth taking: not a verdict to repeat, but a way of checking verdicts, beginning with the comparison on which every verdict silently stands.

## The comparison the reader can perform unaided

The baseline method is portable, and this article's penultimate service is to hand it over in a form the reader can apply without the article. Take any claim that work, or anything else, has become worse, newer, larger, or more dangerous than it was. Refuse, for one minute, to evaluate the claim. Instead, interrogate its comparison. What period supplies the before? Demand its dates, and notice whether the dates describe a generation or a civilisation. What places supply it? Notice whether the map is a world or a neighbourhood with good archives. What population supplies it? Notice whether the before was drawn from the protected, the documented, and the remembered, while the after is drawn from everyone. Then ask what the claim becomes when the comparison is corrected: sometimes it collapses, sometimes it shrinks to a true and smaller claim, and sometimes, the interesting case, it survives correction and emerges stronger, because a claim that withstands its own baseline audit has been separated from the rhetoric that usually inflates it. The precarity debate contains examples of all three outcomes, and this article has sorted them accordingly: the new class collapsed, the epidemic shrank to a substantial stratum, and the reversal, the surplus population, and the transfer of risk survived, strengthened by having their coordinates fixed. A reader who performs the audit habitually will find that it works far beyond this subject, on every declension narrative and every golden age that public argument offers, which is why the series treats the baseline-correction pattern as a general instrument and this article as its demonstration piece.

There is a final refinement worth stating, because it protects the method from its own misuse. Correcting a baseline is not the same as choosing the longest possible one. A comparison against all of recorded history can be as evasive as a comparison against a single decade, since it dissolves every specific event into the permanence of the human condition and licenses the shrug that this article has repeatedly refused. The right baseline is the one the claim itself presupposes, made explicit and then tested: if a writer mourns the settlement, the settlement is the baseline, and the honest questions are about its coverage and duration; if a writer announces a new class, the baseline is the whole prior organisation of classes, and the honest question is relational. Method, in other words, is not a machine for winning arguments by extending timelines. It is the discipline of making comparisons own their coordinates, at whatever scale the claim itself has chosen, and its power comes from that ownership rather than from any trick of scale.

### How should a reader choose the right baseline rather than merely the longest one?

by recovering the comparison the claim itself presupposes and making it explicit. A lament for the settlement takes the settlement as its baseline and must answer for its coverage and duration; a claim of a new class takes the prior class structure as its baseline and must answer the relational test. Extending the timeline indefinitely is its own evasion, dissolving events into permanence.

## The settlement's defenders and the uses of an honest elegy

Something should be said, before the personas and the questions, in defence of the elegy this article has spent its length correcting, because the correction can be misheard as a verdict on the elegists. The men and women who mourn the settlement are mourning something real: workplaces that held communities, securities that underwrote lives, institutions that embodied a generation's achievement and its self-respect. Their elegy is evidence, of the loss's depth and of the standard's survival as a measure, and a politics that cannot honour the elegy will never recruit the elegists, who are, not incidentally, among the few constituencies in the dismantling economies with living memory of organisation's victories. The baseline correction is addressed to the elegy's historiography, not to its grief. Delivered as the article has tried to deliver it, as an enlargement rather than a refutation, the correction offers the elegist a larger story in which the mourned world was not the natural order stolen but a fortress built, exceptional and therefore buildable again in principle, by people no better equipped than the present generation and considerably worse informed. Elegy corrected becomes genealogy, and genealogy is the form in which a movement's past becomes usable: not a home to return to, but a set of instructions, written in the only ink that does not fade, the ink of achieved and lost things, for those who intend to build again with the exclusions repaired and the garrison kept.

## The archive of the dismantling and who will write its history

Every period of labour history is known through the records its institutions kept, and the dismantling decades pose a problem their historians will inherit in an acute form: the insecure generate almost no institutional paper. The settlement's worker accumulated a file in every year of employment, contributions, assessments, agreements, grievances, a documentary density that lets the historian reconstruct a working life in detail. The insecure worker's engagements are recorded, where they are recorded at all, in the proprietary systems of allocators, in formats designed for operational use and scheduled for deletion, behind commercial walls that no archive law reaches. The risk is a historiography of the period written from the records of the secure, in which the stratum appears as a statistical shadow at the edge of other people's files, exactly as the casual poor appear in the settlement's own archives and exactly as the pre-settlement casual appears in the parish and the court record, visible mainly when accused, relieved, or counted. Against that risk, the preservation of the stratum's own materials, its messages, rosters, testimonies, and organisational records, is a scholarly task of the present, and the oral history of the dismantling, gathered while its witnesses' memories are fresh, will be to the period what the great interview archives were to the settlement's generation. The point connects to this article's method at a deep level. The baseline table was built from an archive that flattered the secure; the table's successor, for the decades now unfolding, will be built from an archive that erases the insecure unless deliberate rescue intervenes. Historiography's politics begin in the filing cabinet, and the filing cabinet of precarity is being emptied nightly by design.

The rescue has begun, in the work of labour historians and sociologists who treat the stratum's ephemera as sources and its testimony as evidence, and their findings already complicate the debate in the ways this article has drawn on: the stratagems, the shadow welfare, the dignity work, the generational stances. What their work cannot supply is scale, and scale is where the period's official statistics will mislead the future most seductively, since the statistics were built to count the statuses the period was busy abolishing. A future historian who reads the dismantling through its own categories will conclude that standard employment held up remarkably, because the categories went on registering engagements as standard long after the entitlements that made them standard had been detached. The corrective archive, the testimony and the ephemera, exists to prevent that conclusion, and its keepers deserve the same recognition this article has given the settlement's historians: they are building the baseline from which the period will one day be judged, and the judgement will be only as honest as the archive they manage to save.

### Why will the dismantling decades be hard for future historians to reconstruct?

because insecure work generates almost no institutional paper. Engagements live in proprietary allocation systems, designed for operations and scheduled for deletion, while official statistics keep counting statuses whose substance has been detached. The period risks being written solely from the files of the secure unless testimony and ephemera are deliberately preserved.

## The moral economy of the queue

Crowds at a gate develop a moral economy, a shared sense of what allocations are fair and what conduct is shameful, and the queue for insecure work is no exception, however atomised its members appear. Fieldworkers record the norms consistently: contempt for the worker who underbids the going rate and thereby lowers it for all, respect for the worker who shares information about allocators and openings, sanctions against favouritism's beneficiaries, and a rough ethic of rotation, the belief that engagements should circulate so that everyone eats. These norms are the stratum's constitution in embryo, the customary law from which formal organisation has always grown, and their existence refutes the portrait of the insecure as an anomic mass awaiting either a theorist's name or a demagogue's call. The moral economy is already there, enforced by gossip and exclusion at every allocation point, and the organiser's task is not to create solidarity from nothing but to give the existing norms an institution durable enough to bargain with. Standing's account, focused on the stratum's anger and anomie, underweights this customary order, perhaps because anomie photographs better than etiquette. The older historiography, Thompson's above all, teaches the corrective: the crowd that historians once dismissed as a mob turned out, on inspection, to be governed by a legitimising notion of fairness more coherent than the market doctrine arrayed against it. The queue's moral economy is that notion's descendant, and a politics that learns to speak its language, rotation, fairness, the shame of the underbidder, will find the stratum already organised in everything but name and scale.

## The word itself: a short history of a label

Labels have biographies, and the biography of this one explains part of its charge. The adjective behind the noun travelled from legal and philosophical usage, where it marked whatever is held on sufferance, revocable at another's pleasure, into the vocabulary of labour through the study of informal economies, where it described livelihoods conducted outside every protection the formal order afforded. From there it migrated into the metropolitan debate, gathering its class claim on the journey, and arrived carrying both its older sense of juridical exposure and its newer sense of epochal diagnosis. The migration matters because the word's force depends on the older sense: to call a worker precarious is to say that the worker's hold on livelihood is a tenancy at will, whatever the contract's length, and that single juridical image organises the whole phenomenology this article has surveyed, the suspense, the audition, the revoked expectation. Critics of the class thesis can therefore keep the adjective with a clear conscience. It names a quality of tenure in the broadest sense, the quality of being held by another's leave, and qualities of tenure, unlike classes, can be predicated of strata without theoretical embarrassment. The noun built from it, naming a population, is where the trouble starts, since populations named by a quality tend to be treated as unified by it, and the slide from a shared quality to a shared interest to a shared class is the slide this article has resisted at each step. Vocabulary discipline is not pedantry here. It is the whole argument conducted at the level of grammar, which is why the series insists on auditing synonyms and why this article has preferred the adjective and the plain phrase insecure work wherever analysis, rather than reportage, was under way.

## A last comparison: the fortress and the tide

The governing image of this article has been tidal, security as a shoreline briefly advanced and then surrendered, and a closing word about the image's limits is owed, since images argue in their own way and can smuggle conclusions past the reader's guard. Tides are natural, cyclical, and blameless, and nothing in the history this article has told is any of the three. The shoreline moved because people built, defended, neglected, and dismantled, and it will move again only through the same verbs. If a single image must stand for the whole, let it be the fortress rather than the tide: a work of deliberate construction, raised in a favourable season by builders who understood the surrounding country, garrisoned unevenly, its walls covering some districts and never reaching others, and lost in the end less to assault than to the slow withdrawal of the will and the means to man it. Fortresses can be rebuilt, on better plans, with the old exclusions surveyed and corrected, by builders who study why the first one fell. Tides cannot be rebuilt at all. The choice between the images is therefore the choice between the two politics this article has weighed throughout: a politics of mourning for a natural order that never existed, and a politics of construction toward a security that existed once, partially, and can exist again, universally, if the arithmetic of its defence is learned in time. The article's verdict, its table, its categories, and its concession all serve the second politics, and they are offered to the reader, and above all to the stratum whose condition occasioned them, in that service and no other.

## The reader's own baseline: a closing exercise

Every reader arrives at this subject carrying a private baseline, assembled from family history, from a first job's terms, from the stories a household tells about the years when work was otherwise. The article's method can be turned, profitably and a little uncomfortably, on that private material before the book is closed. Ask of the family story the table's four questions. Which years does it actually describe, and how many are they? Which place, and how representative was that place of the wider economy around it? Which workers does the story silently centre, and which members of the same family, the grandmother whose paid work never carried a pension, the uncle whose engagements were always casual, does it leave in the margin of the photograph? And how long did the arrangement the story celebrates in fact endure, measured against the working lives that surrounded it? Readers who perform the exercise honestly usually find their family story surviving the audit in a reduced and more valuable form: not as the portrait of a lost world, but as the record of one household's passage through a bounded and partial settlement, precious exactly because it was partial, and instructive exactly because its partiality can now be seen. That is the destination toward which the whole article has been travelling, from the first paragraph's hidden photograph to the verdict's defeater. The baseline is not a scholar's toy. It is the instrument by which private memory becomes public history without lying, and by which public argument about work, in this decade and in every successor debate conducted under fresher names, can be held to the standard the subject deserves: comparisons that declare themselves, claims that state their coordinates, and verdicts that carry, openly, the evidence that would bring them down.

## For students and exam candidates

For students and exam candidates, this subject should be approached as an exercise in comparison discipline before it is approached as a body of content. The content itself is compact: a definition of precarity, Standing's precariat thesis with its seven insecurities and its class in the making formulation, the baseline correction with the table's four rows, the stagnant form of the reserve army, the relational definition of class, the concession about lost expectation, and the surplus population argument. A candidate can memorise that inventory in an hour, and the memorisation will earn little, because the questions set on this material test the handling, not the inventory. The handling consists of three moves, which should be practised until they are automatic. First, state the baseline of every claim of change before evaluating the claim, naming the period, the place, and the population. Second, apply the relational test to every class claim, asking what relation to productive property the alleged class occupies that existing classes do not. Third, concede precisely: identify what the opposing position registers that your own framework does not, and show that the concession is absorbed rather than evaded. An answer built from those three moves can defend any of the positions in this debate at the highest level, because the examiner is marking the moves. Candidates should also handle the surplus population argument as the standard extension: when a question's scope widens beyond the wealthy economies, the argument that accumulation may not absorb the labour it frees is the extension that distinguishes the strongest answers, and it should be deployed with Denning's formulation of wageless life attached, since named formulations are what separate citation from gesture. Finally, no figure should be cited for the prevalence of any contract type, for self-employment, for union density, or for shares of employment; the literature's magnitudes are instrument-dependent, and an answer that states directions and names literatures will outscore an answer that recites numbers it cannot defend.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

For teachers and seminar leaders, the baseline table is the session's engine, and it should be put on the board before any position is discussed. Ask the group to fill its four rows from their own assumptions first: what period do they imagine when they picture normal employment, which places, which workers, how long? The assumptions, once visible, become the seminar's real subject, and the historiography can then be introduced as the correction of the room's own table rather than as an external authority. A productive exercise follows: distribute short extracts, one from the precarity literature's elegiac mode, one from a dismissal of it, and require each group to identify, sentence by sentence, which of the trio, the norm, the memory, or the mechanism, each sentence describes. The exercise reliably produces the seminar's best hour, because students discover that the extracts' apparent contradiction is mostly a difference of object. The relational test should be taught as a question rather than as a doctrine: ask what would have to be true of the insecure population's relation to production for the class claim to hold, and let the group discover that the answer requires a different economy than the one described. Close with the defeater discipline: require every student verdict to be submitted with its defeater attached, the evidence that would reverse it, since the habit generalises to every topic the course will touch and is the single most transferable skill the subject offers. Teachers should be ready for the session's emotional undertow, since many students are themselves in the queue this material describes, and the baseline correction, delivered without the accompanying concession on lost expectation, will be heard as a dismissal of their own biographies. Teach the concession as prominently as the correction; the material's fairness is its pedagogy.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, historians, and journalists, the working rules that emerge from this article's analysis are few and strict. Researchers should treat every magnitude in this field as instrument-dependent, reporting the survey or legal category that generated a figure alongside the figure, and should prefer the directional and structural claims that survive redefinition. The open research frontier, on this article's analysis, is the boundary work: how statuses are designed and redesigned, how allocation systems govern the stratum's interior life, and how the surplus population's scale should be measured when its members cycle through wage work, informal work, and wagelessness within a single year. Historians should note that the dismantling decades are acquiring an archive problem inverse to the settlement's: the insecure leave thin records by design, since their engagements generate no personnel files, and the historiography of the period will depend on materials, platform data, allocation records, and oral testimony, whose preservation cannot be assumed and whose collection is a present task. Journalists should apply the baseline rule to every story the subject generates: a report that work has become insecure should be required to state insecure relative to which period, which place, and which population, and a report that quotes a new class should be required to state the class's relation to production or to label the term as its author's coinage rather than as a finding. The journalist's version of the concession matters as much as the scholar's: the betrayed expectation of a specific workforce is a story with names and dates, and it is not refuted by the baseline, only framed by it. Reporting that holds the frame and the story together is rarer than either alone, and it is the standard this subject, of all subjects, deserves, since its readers are so often its subjects.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is precarity new?

Judged against the long history of wage labour, no. Hiring by the day, payment in arrears, seasonal idleness, and the absence of any protection against sickness or age were the ordinary texture of working lives for centuries. What was new, briefly, was the opposite condition: a bounded period in which a protected form of employment was extended to a substantial minority of the world's workers. The sense of novelty arises because the protected period sits within the memory of families and institutions, while the insecure centuries left fewer advocates and thinner archives to speak for them.

### Q: What is the precariat?

The term was given currency by Guy Standing's 2011 book, which gathers workers whose engagements lack security of tenure, predictable income, occupational identity, and the entitlements attached to standard employment into a single named formation. Standing presents this population as a class still in the process of forming, marked by anger at blocked expectations and by vulnerability to political movements that promise recognition. Whether the label describes a class or a stratum is the contested question; the underlying population, and the insecurities that define its working life, are not in dispute among serious observers.

### Q: Is the precariat a class in Marxist terms?

No, on the framework's own test. Marxist class analysis sorts populations by their relation to productive property: those who own it and live from others' labour, those who own none and live by selling their capacity to work, and the intermediate positions between. Insecure workers fall squarely in the second group, alongside the securely employed. Their contracts differ, their incomes fluctuate more, and their lives are harder, but none of that alters the relation that defines the class. They are best described as a layer within the working class, distinguished by the irregularity of their employment.

### Q: What was the standard employment relationship?

It was the template of protected work that a few wealthy economies generalised for roughly a generation after the Second World War: a permanent and full-time engagement, a predictable wage, contributions accumulating toward a pension, insurance against sickness and unemployment, and legal limits on dismissal. Even where it flourished, it was modelled on a male breadwinner and reached mainly men in strongly organised employments. Women, migrants, casual trades, and domestic workers stood largely outside it throughout its existence, which is why treating it as the universal past of work distorts every comparison built on it.

### Q: Does Marxism already have a category for irregular work?

Yes. Marx's account of the population surplus to capital's immediate needs divides that population into forms distinguished by how regularly its members find employment. The form at the bottom is defined precisely by extreme irregularity: work taken whenever it offers, in whatever trade offers it, at standards below the working class average. The resemblance to the condition described by the precarity literature is not loose but detailed, covering irregular engagement, depressed income, and the absence of accumulating claims. The category was elaborated in the nineteenth century, long before the modern debate began.

### Q: Does the reserve army analysis cover precarity?

It covers the condition's material mechanics: why a pool of irregularly employed workers exists, how its existence presses down what the employed can claim, and why accumulation continually replenishes it. What it does not cover is the experience of falling into that pool from protected status, with expectations formed by the protection and a biography organised around its promises. That experience is historically distinctive, politically potent, and the strongest element in the case for new vocabulary. The analysis therefore needs supplementing at the level of experience, not replacing at the level of mechanism.

### Q: What is the surplus population argument?

It is the claim that the central labour problem is shifting from the quality of employment to its availability. As productivity rises, production sheds workers faster than expanding activities reabsorb them, and a growing population finds that the wage relation offers it, at best, occasional and marginal purchase. Such populations assemble livelihoods from informal trade, casual engagements, subsistence activity, remittances, and kin support, with wage work as one strand among several. The argument reframes precarity as the mildest province of a larger territory whose governing condition is not insecure wages but the absence of wages altogether.

### Q: Does the reversal of secure employment matter?

It matters greatly, even though the security reversed was never universal. Populations that held protected status within living memory, and organised households, debts, and retirements around its continuation, experienced its withdrawal as a breach of an understood bargain. That experience generates political energies, resentment, nostalgia, and openness to movements promising restoration, which no amount of historical correction can dissolve. A settlement's loss is a fact about the present as well as about the past, and any politics that hopes to address the populations concerned must address the loss directly rather than lecturing it away.

### Q: Is precarity the majority experience in advanced economies?

The careful comparative literature suggests not, though the insecure share is large and grew across the decades in which protected employment was dismantled. Standard engagements, their protections thinned, still employ a large part of the workforce in wealthy economies. The insecure population is concentrated where organisation is weakest and where biography is most exposed: among the young entering work, among carers returning to it, among migrants, and among those leaving protected posts late in life. Claims that standard employment has already shrunk to a small remnant outrun the evidence and should be treated as rhetoric rather than measurement.

### Q: What do critics of the precariat concept argue?

Critics converge from several directions. Historians object that the concept mistakes a brief exception for the norm it supposedly departs from. Sociologists object that the population it names is too heterogeneous, spanning graduates and lifelong casual workers, to form a class. Marxists object that defining class by contract type abandons the relational test that gives class analysis its explanatory power. Feminist and postcolonial scholars object that it takes the biography of formerly protected metropolitan men as the general case. Most critics accept that insecurity grew and that its growth matters; they dispute the conceptual frame built over those facts.

### Q: How does the finding that the exception was the security grade the precariat concept?

It allows the concept to be marked component by component instead of accepted or rejected whole. The description of insecure work's texture, the catalogue of missing securities, and the warning about betrayed expectations all survive the finding and earn their place in the literature. The implied history fails, because it rests on the mistaken baseline the finding corrects. The claim to have discovered a new class fails separately, on relational grounds. Grading in this split fashion is more useful than a single verdict, since it tells readers exactly which parts of a famous argument they may rely on.

### Q: What should a student separate between a historical norm and a remembered golden age?

Three things that public discussion constantly fuses. The norm is what the long record shows: insecurity as labour's default condition. The golden age is the settlement as memory edits it: real securities, enlarged by hindsight, with the exclusions forgotten. The mechanism is what produces insecurity in any given period: risk transfer, reclassification, competitive allocation, and the growth of populations surplus to accumulation's needs. An answer that keeps the three apart can concede the memory's emotional truth, affirm the norm's documentary truth, and analyse the mechanism, without allowing any one of them to impersonate the others.

### Q: Why did secure employment last only about three decades?

Because it rested on conditions that were never guaranteed to persist: rapid productivity growth that let wages and protections rise together, scarce labour that strengthened workers' bargaining position, unions at their peak of membership and recognition, and governments that treated full employment as their responsibility. As growth slowed, as production became mobile across borders, and as employers pressed to recover flexibility, the conjunction came apart piece by piece. The brevity is the lesson. Security was a political construction maintained by a balance of forces, and constructions of that kind last exactly as long as the balance that built them.

### Q: How does the baseline table order the periods compared?

The table disciplines comparison by forcing every claim to declare its coordinates. Its rows fix the protected arrangement's period, the places where it held, the workers it actually covered, and the span it lasted, and a final column draws out what each coordinate implies for claims about insecurity. With those rows in view, the longer record falls into place around the protected episode: insecure centuries before it, a partial and bounded security within it, and a partial dismantling after it. Arguments that skip the table typically smuggle one row in as the whole picture.

### Q: What separates a new class from an old condition with a new name?

A new class would occupy a relation to productive property that existing classes do not, and would develop interests systematically opposed to theirs. An old condition with a new name leaves the relations untouched and redescribes a layer within them. The insecure sell their labour power, own no productive property, and want what the rest of their class wants: continuity, protection, a living income, and a voice. Their condition is older than the name coined for it, and the name's novelty is a property of the vocabulary, not of the social structure it labels.
