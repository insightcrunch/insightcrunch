## Famous readers and fans of The Great Gatsby

The famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby form a roster that reads like a directory of the people who matter to books. The book that won over the people who matter to books is the claim this article defends, and the evidence is a chain of documented admiration running from the year of publication to the twenty-first century. T. S. Eliot wrote to Fitzgerald in December 1925 that the novel seemed to him the first step American fiction had taken since Henry James. Edith Wharton wrote to Fitzgerald in June 1925 praising the novel while offering candid criticism. Gertrude Stein's admiration is documented in the correspondence and memoir literature of the Paris years. Ernest Hemingway recorded his admiration in A Moveable Feast (1964). John O'Hara repeatedly named the novel the greatest American novel. J. D. Salinger's biographers record that he named the novel his favorite. Haruki Murakami carried it into a new language with his Japanese translation. None of this is invented, and none of it needs to be, because the documented record is stronger than any fabrication. This article exists to separate the documented from the fabricated, to show how the documented chain worked, and to explain why the famous readership of the novel is not decoration on its reputation but part of the machinery that made it.

The Great Gatsby was published by Scribner's in April 1925 to mixed reviews and modest sales, about 20,000 copies in its first year from a first printing of 20,870, and it drifted for two decades before the postwar reassessment lifted it into the canon. ## The first famous readers: 1925 and the private verdicts

The first famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby met the novel when it was new, unglamorous, and unproven, which is what makes their verdicts so valuable as evidence. The famous readers of the first decade did not inherit a classic; they confronted a new book by a popular novelist whose previous work had made him famous for chronicling youth, and they had to decide, without the aid of consensus, what the book was. The records of their decisions survive mainly in letters, which is fitting, because the letter is the medium of the first verdict, private, considered, and written before the writer knows whether the judgment will be shared. To read the famous readers of 1925 is to watch taste working without a net.

The figure at the head of this list is T. S. Eliot. Eliot wrote to Fitzgerald in December 1925 that the novel seemed to him the first step American fiction had taken since Henry James. The sentence is worth pausing on, because it does three things at once. It places the novel in a lineage, Henry James, the master of the American novel of consciousness. It measures the distance the novel traveled, a full step, the first since that master. And it comes from a reader whose own judgment carried the maximum weight: Eliot was the most authoritative literary mind of the English-speaking world, the author of The Waste Land, the editor at Faber who shaped modernism's canon. A private letter is not a review, and its weight is of a different kind. A review addresses the public and expires with the season; a letter addresses the author and survives in the archive, where later readers discover it and quote it. Eliot's verdict mattered in 1925 because it was Eliot's, and it mattered later because the archive preserved it, so that the revival could point to the greatest modernist as an early witness. The first famous reader was also the most authoritative, and the novel's reputation never fully recovered from the good fortune of having been read by him first.

### Why did T. S. Eliot's letter matter more than a review?

A review argues with the public and disappears with the season, while a private letter enters the archive and becomes evidence. Eliot's December 1925 letter carried the authority of the age's most commanding literary mind, and because it survived in correspondence, the mid-century revival could cite it as proof that the novel's greatness was visible from the start.

The case of Edith Wharton complicates the picture in a useful way. Wharton wrote to Fitzgerald in June 1925 praising the novel while offering candid criticism, which is exactly the combination that makes a testimonial trustworthy. A famous reader who praises without reservation is less convincing than one who praises while finding fault, because the fault proves the reader was reading. Wharton was the grande dame of the American novel of manners, the author of The House of Mirth and The Age of Innocence, and her praise of a younger writer's book carried the weight of the established tradition recognizing the new. Her letter also demonstrates an important fact about the famous readers of the first decade: they were not a faction. Eliot the modernist and Wharton the traditionalist agreed on the book from opposite positions, which suggests the admiration was not an artifact of a school but a response to the text itself. When readers who disagree about everything agree about one novel, the novel is doing something.

Gertrude Stein belongs in this company as the third of the Paris-years witnesses. Stein's admiration for the novel is documented in the correspondence and memoir literature of the Paris years, the record left by the American writers who gathered in her orbit. Stein mattered as a reader not because she was the greatest writer of the group but because she was its greatest teacher of taste, the salon hostess whose verdicts made and unmade reputations among the expatriates. Her admiration functioned as a credential within the most important literary circle of the decade: a book Stein admired had passed the hardest test available, the judgment of the writers who were remaking literature at the same time Fitzgerald was writing it. The documented nature of this admiration matters because Stein's name, like Eliot's, is the kind that later boosters love to claim without evidence. The evidence exists. It is enough.

Ernest Hemingway is the fourth witness, and his testimony arrives in a form that requires care. Hemingway recorded his admiration in A Moveable Feast (1964), the posthumous memoir of the Paris years, where he treats Gatsby as the Fitzgerald novel that endured. The care is needed because Hemingway's relationship with Fitzgerald was famously complicated, a mixture of affection, rivalry, and condescension that colors everything Hemingway wrote about him. That complication is precisely what makes the admiration credible. A friend's praise is cheap; a rival's praise is expensive. Hemingway had every motive to diminish Fitzgerald and no motive to inflate him, and yet the novel stands in his memoir as the book that survived the man's ruin. The famous readers of the first generation thus include a figure who admired the novel against his own grain, which is the strongest form of testimony available. When the person least inclined to praise you praises your book, the praise has been earned.

What unites these four is not agreement about the novel's meaning but agreement about its achievement. Eliot praised its step forward, Wharton praised it while criticizing, Stein credentialed it within the circle, Hemingway admitted it against his grain. No school produced this consensus; no marketing arranged it. The first famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby found the book independently, in the year of its publication and the years just after, and their verdicts survive in the most durable form testimony takes, the private letter and the memoir, written without thought of posterity and therefore trustworthy to it.

The contrast between these private verdicts and the public reviews of 1925 is one of the most instructive facts in the novel's reception history. The reviewers, writing for newspapers and magazines, found the prose brilliant and the substance thin; the famous readers, writing to the author or to their journals, found the book an achievement. The difference is not that one group was right and the other wrong. It is that the two groups were performing different acts. A reviewer in 1925 had to place a new novel in the season's commerce, to tell buyers whether to spend their money, and the mixed verdict reflected that commercial frame. A letter writer had no commerce to serve; Eliot and Wharton were answering the book as readers, and readers answer differently than buyers. The famous readers of the first decade thus constitute a second, parallel reception, running alongside the public one, and it was the parallel reception that survived. The reviews expired with the season. The letters entered the archive, and the archive outlasted them all.

The archive itself deserves a moment's attention, because the survival of these verdicts was not automatic. Letters survive when someone keeps them, and Fitzgerald's correspondence survived because his papers were preserved, collected, and eventually published, first in selected editions and then in the scholarly apparatus that the Fitzgerald industry built around him. The famous readers of 1925 are knowable because the documentary infrastructure exists to know them, and the infrastructure exists because later readers, the revival generation and the scholars, decided the papers were worth keeping. This is the first turn of the mechanism the article traces: the famous readers of one generation create the archive that the famous readers of the next generation inherit. Eliot could not have known that his December letter would become evidence in a canonization case argued decades later. He wrote it as a courtesy to a younger writer he admired. The courtesy became a credential only when the archive preserved it and the revival discovered it, which is why the private verdicts and the public advocacy are not two stories but one story in two acts.

The material conditions of 1925 sharpen the contrast between the two receptions. The novel came from Scribner's, the respectable house where Maxwell Perkins edited Fitzgerald alongside Hemingway and Thomas Wolfe, and it entered a review ecosystem split between the daily newspapers and the literary quarterlies. The dailies, Edwin Clark in The New York Times, John V. A. Weaver in the Brooklyn Eagle, read it as a story and often enjoyed it; the quarterlies, Gilbert Seldes in The Dial, read it as a contribution to American letters and found it wanting; the Herald Tribune's Isabel Paterson praised the writing while finding the people unreal; the Chicago Tribune's H. L. Mencken dismissed it as a glorified anecdote. Against this scattered field, the famous readers' verdicts stand out not for their unanimity but for their altitude. The reviewers were placing a book in a season; Eliot was placing it in a lineage, Wharton in a tradition, Stein in a circle, Hemingway in a rivalry. The difference in altitude is the difference between the public reception and the private one, and the private one, written at greater height, proved the more durable. The reviews told 1925 what to buy; the letters told posterity what to keep.

There is also a biographical irony worth noting, because it sharpens the claim. Fitzgerald died in 1940 believing himself a failure, his books out of print or remaindered, his reputation in ruins. He never saw the canonization, never knew that the letters in his desk drawer would become exhibits in his vindication. The famous readers of the first decade admired a book whose author would die thinking the admiration had not been enough. This does not make their testimony more accurate, but it makes it more poignant, and the poignancy has done its own work in the legend. The story of the neglected genius vindicated after death needs its early witnesses, the ones who saw it when seeing was hard, and Eliot, Wharton, Stein, and Hemingway serve that narrative function whether they intended to or not. The article's discipline is to keep the poignancy from becoming inflation: the witnesses are valuable because their testimony is documented, not because the story needs them.

## The revival's champions: Cowley, O'Hara, and the canonization campaign

The mid-century revival of the novel was, at the level of personnel, a story of famous readers becoming public advocates. The private verdicts of 1925 were not enough on their own; they had to be discovered, quoted, and deployed by a second generation of readers who turned admiration into advocacy. The revival's champions are the figures who did that work, and the two most important names for this article's purposes are Malcolm Cowley and John O'Hara, with Arthur Mizener, Edmund Wilson, and Lionel Trilling in the supporting structure that the article on [how the novel entered the canon](/2029/12/24/gatsby-entered-canon/) examines in detail.

Malcolm Cowley is the critic as famous reader. Cowley, the critic and editor who assembled The Portable Fitzgerald (1945), did for Fitzgerald in the 1940s what Eliot had done in 1925: he supplied a distinguished reader's verdict, but this time in public, in print, and at the exact moment the culture was ready to hear it. The Portable Fitzgerald is the document of Cowley's advocacy, an anthology that put the novel back before readers who had forgotten it or never met it, framed by the authority of a critic the literary world trusted. Cowley's role illustrates the difference between a reader and a champion. Eliot read and wrote a letter; Cowley read and built a book. The champion takes the private verdict and gives it an apparatus, an introduction, a selection, a publisher, a distribution channel. Cowley's advocacy is documented in the editorial record itself: the anthology exists, its preface is signed, its choices are on the page. Where Eliot's famous readership worked through the archive, Cowley's worked through the marketplace of reprints, which is why the two figures belong to different generations of the same story.

John O'Hara is the novelist as famous reader, and his case is the most vivid in the entire roster. O'Hara repeatedly named the novel the greatest American novel, a judgment documented in his letters and interviews, and he did so with the particular vehemence of a writer who knew what the achievement had cost. O'Hara was himself a novelist of American social observation, the author of Appointment in Samarra, and his praise of Gatsby carried the double weight of the practitioner's judgment and the competitor's generosity. Writers do not casually declare another writer's book the greatest; the declaration is a surrender of a position they might have claimed for their own work. O'Hara's repeated naming of Gatsby as the greatest American novel is therefore a documented act of critical generosity, and it matters that it came from inside the profession. The famous readers who are also novelists test the book the way makers test a made thing, and O'Hara's verdict has echoed through the decades precisely because it was a maker's verdict.

### Why did John O'Hara's admiration carry special weight?

A novelist who calls another novelist's book the greatest American novel surrenders a position he might have claimed for his own work. O'Hara's repeated judgment, documented in his letters and interviews, was a practitioner's verdict and an act of critical generosity, the kind of testimony that persuades other writers more than any review could.

The supporting structure of the revival belongs to the canonization story and is linked rather than repeated here. Arthur Mizener's 1951 biography The Far Side of Paradise supplied the scholarly certification; Edmund Wilson kept Fitzgerald in print through his editions of The Last Tycoon (1941) and The Crack-Up (1945); Lionel Trilling's 1945 introduction framed the novel as the American book. Each of these men was a famous reader in the precise sense this article uses: a person whose documented admiration did work in the world. But their work belongs to the canonization mechanism, and the full account of that mechanism is the province of the canon article. This article's point about them is narrower and worth stating plainly: the revival was not an anonymous tide. It was a set of named individuals, each with a documented relationship to the novel, each converting private reading into public consequence. The famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby include the people who did the converting, and the conversion is what turned the archive of 1925 into the consensus of 1950.

There is a further point about Cowley and O'Hara that the complication section will develop, but it belongs here as a fact about their advocacy. Both men praised the novel in ways that served their own positions: Cowley the rehabilitator, O'Hara the practitioner's practitioner. This does not discredit their praise; it explains its form. Advocacy is never disinterested, because no reader is disinterested. The famous reader brings a history, a taste, a program, and the novel meets that history and is changed by the meeting. The revival's champions did not discover a fixed object; they made a classic by reading a neglected book in a way that taught others how to read it. The distinction between discovery and making is the whole problem of canonization, and the famous readers sit exactly on the line.

The historical setting of the revival gives the champions' work its full meaning. The novel that Cowley anthologized and O'Hara praised had spent two decades as a backlist title, kept in print by Scribner's at low cost, selling a few hundred copies a year to libraries and the curious. The war changed the conditions of its readership before the critics changed the verdict. The Armed Services Editions program, running from 1943 to 1947, distributed more than 120 million free pocket-sized paperbacks to American servicemen, and The Great Gatsby was issued as number 862 in 1945, with roughly 150,000 copies printed for the troops. That single printing put about six times as many copies into readers' hands as the novel had sold in its first twenty years. The soldiers who read it on troopships and in hospitals were not famous readers, but they were the audience the famous readers' advocacy would later need: men who had met the book without the critics' frame and would enter college on the GI Bill already familiar with it. The champions did not create this audience, but they inherited it, and their advocacy landed on prepared ground.

Cowley's editorial labor should be understood in this setting. The Portable Fitzgerald was not merely a reprint; it was an argument in the form of a table of contents. By placing the novel alongside the stories and the autobiographical essays, framed by his own critical introduction, Cowley told the postwar reader how to read Fitzgerald: as a serious writer whose best work belonged in the canon the war had made room for. The anthology's authority came from two sources at once, the editor's reputation and the publisher's reach, and the combination is what turned a critic's admiration into a cultural fact. A letter persuades one reader; an anthology persuades ten thousand. Cowley understood the difference, which is why he belongs in the champions' generation rather than the witnesses'. He did not only admire the book. He built the vehicle that carried the admiration.

O'Hara's contribution worked by a different mechanism, the mechanism of the repeated superlative. Where Cowley built an apparatus, O'Hara supplied a sentence, and the sentence traveled. To call a book the greatest American novel, repeatedly, in letters and interviews, across the years when the canon was being renegotiated, is to plant a flag that others can rally around. The superlative did not convince by argument; it convinced by audacity. A critic who calls a neglected novel the greatest is making a claim so large that it demands an answer, and the answers, whether agreement or refutation, keep the book in the conversation. O'Hara's vehemence was thus a strategic asset for the novel's reputation, whatever its critical merits. The famous reader who shouts is heard differently than the famous reader who whispers, and the revival needed both: Cowley's measured anthology and O'Hara's immoderate praise, the apparatus and the slogan, the container and the flag.

The chain connecting the two generations is visible in the champions' own citations. The revival's advocates did not invent their case from nothing; they reached back to the private verdicts of 1925 and quoted them. Eliot's letter, preserved in the correspondence, became the exhibit that proved the novel's greatness had been visible from the start. This is the archival survival stage doing its work: the witnesses of the first decade supplied the evidence that the champions of the second decade deployed. The famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby are therefore not two separate rosters but one continuous chain, each link forged by documentation and each link bearing the weight of the next. The book won over the people who matter to books, and then the people it had won over won over everyone else.

## Writers who named it their favorite

The third generation of the famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby is the one that proves the novel's hold on the people who write books. The first generation read it when it was new; the second generation revived it when it was neglected; the third generation inherited it as a classic and chose it anyway, which is the hardest test of all. A writer who inherits a canon can admire it dutifully, the way students admire assigned books. A writer who names it a favorite is making a confession, and confessions are evidence.

J. D. Salinger is the clearest case. Salinger's biographers record that he named the novel his favorite, a documented preference that links the century's most famous recluse to the century's most famous novel. The documentation matters because Salinger is the kind of figure about whom legends accumulate: the recluse, the perfectionist, the man who stopped publishing and disappeared. Legends attract false attributions, and the biographical record is the control against them. The record says he named Gatsby his favorite novel, and the record is what this article follows. What the preference means is worth a moment's thought. Salinger wrote about innocence, phoniness, and the corruption of the adult world; Gatsby is, among other things, a novel about a man destroyed by the phoniness of the adult world he tried to enter. The favorite is not an accident of taste. It is a recognition, one writer seeing his own preoccupations handled by another writer at a level he did not attempt. The famous reader who is also a great writer reads for kinship, and Salinger's documented kinship with the novel is one of the strongest testimonials the book owns.

Haruki Murakami's relationship to the novel is of a different order and, in some ways, a deeper one. Murakami carried the novel into a new language with his Japanese translation, an act of devotion that took months of labor and placed his own reputation beside Fitzgerald's text. A translation is the most intimate form of reading: the translator must inhabit every sentence, decide what each word means, and rebuild the book in a different tongue. No reader knows a novel the way its translator knows it. Murakami's translation is therefore documented admiration in the strongest possible form, not a quoted opinion but a completed work, a book that exists because a famous writer loved the original enough to remake it. The translation also belongs to the story of the novel's global life, which the series article on [the translations of the novel](/2030/02/18/gatsby-translations/) examines; what matters here is the translator as famous reader, the writer who proved his admiration by doing the work.

The discipline of this article is to name only the figures whose admiration can be documented and to state the documented relationship in each case. Salinger's documented favorite, Murakami's documented translation, O'Hara's documented superlative, Hemingway's documented memoir, Stein's documented correspondence, Wharton's documented letter, Eliot's documented letter, Cowley's documented anthology: each is a fact, each is checkable, and together they are enough. ### What can a translator's devotion prove?

A translator inhabits every sentence and rebuilds the book in a new tongue, a labor no casual admirer undertakes. Murakami's Japanese translation of the novel is documented admiration in its strongest form: not a quoted opinion but a completed work, a book that exists because a famous writer loved the original enough to remake it.

There is a further mechanism worth noting in the writerly chain, and it is the reason the series article on [the novel's influence on later American novels](/2028/08/28/gatsby-influence-later-novels/) exists as the companion to this one. Famous readers who are writers do not only admire; they absorb, and the absorbed novel reappears in their own work, sometimes as homage, sometimes as argument, sometimes as unconscious echo. The line between admiration and influence is porous, and the famous readers of Gatsby have crossed it repeatedly, which is why the influence article and this one are two views of the same phenomenon. To be a famous reader is to be influenced in public; to be an influenced writer is to be a famous reader in disguise. The roster this article assembles is also, read from the other side, a map of the novel's generative power.

The psychology of the favorite deserves a closer look, because it explains why the third generation's testimony differs in kind from the first two. The first generation admired a new book; the second generation revived a neglected one; the third generation chose a classic, and choosing a classic is the hardest test of sincerity. Anyone can admire what the culture has already certified; the admiration costs nothing and proves nothing. But a writer who names a certified classic his favorite is making a confession rather than paying a duty, and the confession has a different texture. Salinger did not need to name Gatsby; the canon would have survived his silence. He named it because the book had done something to him that no other book had done, and the naming was the record of the wound. The famous readers of the third generation are thus the most trustworthy witnesses of all: they had every incentive to be dutiful and chose instead to be personal.

Salinger's case carries an additional resonance that is worth stating. He was the century's most famous literary recluse, the author of The Catcher in the Rye (1951) who stopped publishing and disappeared from public life, and recluses attract legends the way lighthouses attract ships. Every claim about Salinger's tastes must be checked against the biographical record, because the legend manufactures preferences to suit itself. The record says he named Gatsby his favorite novel, and the article follows the record and no further. What the record does not say, the article does not claim: not when he first read it, not how many times he reread it, not what passages he loved. The discipline of documentation means accepting the gaps. A documented favorite without documented details is still a documented favorite, and it is worth exactly what it is.

Murakami's translation practice gives the third generation its deepest case. He is not only the translator of Gatsby; his translation work across American literature, Raymond Carver, Truman Capote, J. D. Salinger, Raymond Chandler, is one of the documented facts of his career, a parallel body of labor beside the novels. The Gatsby translation belongs to this larger practice, which means it was not a casual commission but part of a sustained engagement with the American literature that formed him. A translator chooses his authors the way a reader chooses his favorites, and Murakami chose Fitzgerald among the Americans he loved enough to remake. The translation is thus doubly documented: as a fact about the novel's global life and as a fact about the translator's taste. The famous reader who translates is the famous reader at maximum intimacy, and Murakami's Japanese Gatsby is the proof that the novel's hold on writers extends across languages, across the Pacific, into a literary culture with its own canons and its own masters.

The writerly chain also runs in the other direction, from admiration to anxiety. A writer who names another writer's book the greatest or the favorite must then write in the shadow of that judgment, and the shadow is not comfortable. O'Hara wrote his own novels of American social observation under the superlative he had bestowed on Fitzgerald; Salinger wrote his stories of innocence and phoniness under the favorite he had confessed. The famous readers who are writers thus pay a price for their testimony that the famous readers who are critics do not pay. Eliot and Cowley could admire without competing; O'Hara and Salinger admired a book in the genre where they themselves worked, which made every word of praise a concession. The concession is what makes their testimony the strongest in the roster. The book that won over the people who matter to books won over, hardest of all, the people who had the most to lose by admitting it.

## Artists and public figures: the documented engagements

Beyond the writers stands a second circle of the famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby: the artists and public figures whose documented engagement carried the novel into other media and other audiences. The rule for this circle is the same as for the first: document the relationship, state it precisely, and do not upgrade engagement into adoration. An artist who adapts the novel has engaged with it deeply; whether the artist loved it is a separate claim, and the article does not make separate claims without evidence.

Francis Ford Coppola is the most important figure in this circle. Coppola wrote the screenplay for the 1974 film adaptation of the novel, a documented act of sustained engagement with the text at the level of structure, dialogue, and scene. A screenplay is, like a translation, a form of intimate reading: the adapter must decide what the novel means in order to decide what to keep, and Coppola's script is famously respectful of Fitzgerald's text, preserving the narration and the novel's language to a degree unusual in adaptations. ## What the famous readers agreed on: the prose, the structure, the ache

Beneath the differences of generation, medium, and temperament, the documented famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby converge on the same three achievements, and the convergence is worth naming because it tells us what the testimonials were actually about. They agreed on the prose, on the structure, and on the ache. Everything else in their praise varies; these three do not.

The prose first. Eliot's 1925 letter praised a step forward in American fiction, and the step he meant was visible on the page: the lyric compression, the cadenced sentences, the fusion of concrete image and abstract feeling that the 1925 reviewers had already certified as brilliant even while doubting the substance. Wharton's praise, offered with candid criticism, was praise of the writing set against reservations about the people. Hemingway, the great stylist of the plain sentence, admired in Fitzgerald the opposite virtue, the ornamented sentence that never collapses into ornament. O'Hara, himself a craftsman of social observation, named the book the greatest on the strength of its making. The practitioners' consensus is the most telling: the writers who praised the novel praised it as writers, for the sentences. The famous readers agreed that the prose was the achievement, and the agreement crossed every critical divide because the prose is the one thing about the novel that no reader has to be persuaded to see. It is there on the first page.

The structure second. What the famous readers sensed, each in the vocabulary of his or her moment, was the novel's architecture: the framed retrospective narration, the symbolic economy, the fable-like compression that the 1925 reviewers had mistaken for thinness. Cowley's anthologizing eye saw it when he placed the novel at the center of Fitzgerald's achievement; the translators see it whenever they rebuild the book's symmetries in another language; Coppola's screenplay preserved it when it kept the narration. The structure is the reason the novel survives adaptation and translation, the reason artists keep returning to it: a well-built book can be remade in other media because the building stands apart from the sentences. The famous readers who were makers, O'Hara, Salinger, Murakami, Coppola, Luhrmann, all testified to the architecture in the way makers testify, by handling it, remaking it, building on it. Their praise is structural criticism disguised as admiration.

The ache third, and this is the one the testimonials name least directly and mean most deeply. The novel is about longing, about a man who wants what he cannot have and is destroyed by the wanting, and the famous readers who loved it loved it, in the end, for the feeling it carries. Salinger's favorite is a book about innocence destroyed by the adult world, which is also his subject. Murakami's translation is an act of devotion to a book about devotion. Eliot, the most austere of the witnesses, was moved, his own word in the December letter being that the book interested and moved him. The ache is the content that the prose and the structure serve, and the famous readers' convergence on it suggests that the novel's hold is not only technical. The book that won over the people who matter to books won them over, finally, because it made them feel something they recognized. The technicians admired the sentences; the readers, even the famous ones, surrendered to the longing.

Eliot's letter repays the closest reading the article can give it, because the exact wording is the documentation. "The first step American fiction had taken since Henry James" is a sentence with three decisions packed into it. "First step" claims priority, not influence, not achievement in an established line, but a move no one had made before, which is the strongest form of modernist praise. "American fiction" makes it a national claim, a verdict on a literature rather than a compliment to a man. "Since Henry James" chooses the lineage, the master of the American novel of consciousness, and by choosing him tells us exactly what Eliot saw in the book: the interior life rendered with European discipline in an American setting. The letter's other documented word, that the novel interested and moved him, completes the certificate. "Interested" is the critic's verb, the mind engaged; "moved" is the reader's verb, the heart reached. Eliot's praise covers both registers, which is why the revival could quote it for any purpose. And the private register matters: "seemed to him" is hedged, tentative, the phrasing of a man writing to an author rather than pronouncing to the public, and the hedge is what makes it trustworthy. A publicist's certainty would be worth less.

Wharton's June 1925 letter is the second documented testimonial and the most instructive in form, because it is praise that survived its own criticism. The table records the fact plainly: she praised the novel while offering candid criticism. The form deserves a paragraph, because praise inside correction is the hardest testimonial to dismiss. An unqualified compliment from a friend could be politeness; a compliment that sits beside a frank objection cannot be, since no one invents praise and then weakens it with criticism. Wharton was the senior American novelist writing to a junior, the author of the novel of manners appraising a book that took the manners novel's materials into new territory, and her praise, delivered with the dissent intact, certifies that the book impressed a reader who had every license not to be impressed. Hemingway's documented framing in A Moveable Feast (1964) has the opposite temporal weight: it is retrospection from thirty-nine years later, the testimony of a man who watched Fitzgerald's whole career and still designated this novel the one that endured. A contemporary letter is evidence of the moment; a memoir is evidence of the duration. O'Hara's superlative, repeated across letters and interviews, is the third documented form: not a single verdict but a position held over decades, a habit of praise that makes the superlative part of the record rather than an ornament on it.

The translator's devotion is the most laborious form of documented praise, and it belongs in this section as the makers' testimony taken to its extreme. Murakami's Japanese translation of the novel is documented fact, and its weight is measured in labor: a reader gives the book hours, a translator gives it months, and every sentence must be rebuilt by hand. No reader can be suspected of casual enthusiasm when the enthusiasm has been demonstrated one sentence at a time. Coppola's 1974 screenplay is the same proof in a different medium: months of sustained engagement, documented in the finished script, that testify to the book's hold on a maker working under commercial pressure. These are not endorsements; they are acts of labor, and the famous-readers record should weigh them accordingly. Words of praise are cheap and the archive preserves them; years of labor are expensive and the archive preserves them too. The famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby include readers who paid for their admiration in work, and that work is the strongest documentation of all.

What they did not agree on is as instructive as what they did. Eliot read the novel as a step forward for American fiction, a modernist achievement in the line of Henry James; Wharton read it through the moral lens of the novel of manners, praising the execution while questioning the people; O'Hara read it as the great American social novel, the book that got the country's surfaces right; the adapters read it as a story built to be remade. No two of these readings are the same book, and that is the point the article must not blur. The famous readers converged on the achievement and diverged on the meaning, which means their consensus was about quality, not interpretation. This is why the testimonials could do the work of canonization without doing the work of criticism: they certified that the book mattered without agreeing on why it mattered, leaving the why to the scholars and the classrooms. A consensus about meaning would have been fragile, vulnerable to the next critical fashion. A consensus about achievement proved durable, because the sentences did not change when the fashions did. The famous readers and fans of Great Gatsby thus bequeathed the canon a book everyone agreed was great and no one agreed about, which is the ideal condition for a classic: secure in status, inexhaustible in argument.

## The complication: what testimonial evidence cannot prove

The complication this article must address honestly is the one named in its brief: the temptation to invent or exaggerate famous endorsements, and the deeper question of what documented admiration actually proves. The article has kept to documented praise; now it must say what documented praise is worth. The honest answer is that it is worth less than the boosters claim and more than the skeptics allow, and the two halves of that sentence need equal weight.

It is worth less than the boosters claim because testimony is reception evidence, not quality evidence. That T. S. Eliot admired the novel proves that T. S. Eliot admired the novel; it does not prove the novel is good, any more than a bad review proves it is bad. Famous readers are still readers, and readers are fallible, fashionable, and sometimes wrong in distinguished company. The history of literature is full of great writers who admired bad books and dismissed good ones, and there is no reason to suppose the famous readers of Gatsby are exempt from the ordinary fallibility of taste. The article's roster is evidence about the novel's reception, and reception is a fact about readers, not a verdict on the book. ## How famous advocacy secured the novel's stature

The mechanism by which famous advocacy secured the novel's stature can now be stated as a sequence, because the article has assembled the parts.
