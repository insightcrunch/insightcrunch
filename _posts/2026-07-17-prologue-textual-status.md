## The Prologue's Strange Textual Status: A Sonnet with Three Different Lives

The Prologue to Romeo and Juliet, the fourteen-line sonnet in which the Chorus announces "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1) and gives away the ending before the action starts, is the most familiar threshold in English drama. Its textual status is anything but familiar. The speech does not descend through the early editions as a single stable document. It survives complete in exactly one early text, the second quarto of 1599. It survives damaged in the first quarto of 1597, where it shrinks to twelve lines, loses two of its lines outright, mislays its rhymes, and renames the households. And it does not survive at all in the First Folio of 1623, the prestige collected edition through which the play reached posterity, even though the Folio's printers worked from a quarto copy in which the sonnet stood complete. The Prologue's companion piece, the second Chorus sonnet that opens the second act with "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie" (2.0.1), traces the mirror image of the same journey: missing from the first quarto, present in the second, and present in the Folio only as a bare, unattributed sonnet stripped of its speaker. Between them, the two speeches were never transmitted whole together except by the second quarto.

![The Chorus sonnets of Romeo and Juliet in the early quartos and the Folio - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-67.webp)

The verdict of this study is stated at the outset, because the evidence supports one conclusion more strongly than its rivals. The two Chorus sonnets are the author's frame for the play, designed as a matched pair, and their strange transmission is the record of what the early modern theatre did to frame material: performance cut it, memory garbled it, and a collected edition regularized the play around it. Neither speech was lost because anyone doubted who wrote it. The Prologue was lost, damaged, and orphaned because a speech that stands outside the action, speaks no character's lines, and advances no event is the most detachable object in a playbook, and every hand that carried the play from stage to print treated detachable objects as expendable. What looks like a puzzle about authorship is a lesson about transmission. The most quoted sonnet in the play owes its survival, intact and attributed, to a single edition printed from the author's own draft papers, and to the editors who have taken that edition as their copy-text ever since.

A word on method, since the case turns on it. The six early editions are not six votes. Three of them, the third, fourth, and fifth quartos, are reprints that add no independent testimony, and the case therefore rests on three independent witnesses whose evidence must be weighed rather than counted. Weighing asks of each witness two questions: how close does its ancestry run to the author's papers, and through what kind of hands did it pass on the way to print? A witness copied mechanically from a good ancestor preserves wording and loses nothing but freshness. A witness that passed through performance carries the stage's priorities in its very substance, its cuts where the stage cut, its paraphrases where memory served. A witness whose copy was prepared for a monument carries the monument's priorities, regularity, completeness of action, indifference to ceremony. The Chorus speeches are the ideal test of the method, because they sit exactly where the three kinds of hands differ most in their grip. What each hand did to them is legible, and the legibility is why a fourteen-line sonnet and its twin can sustain a study of this length: they are the play's most sensitive recording instrument for the forces that transmitted everything else in the book, forces that acted on the great scenes too, but left their fingerprints there under layers of dialogue that many channels agreed on. At the frame, the channels disagree in the open, and their disagreement is the evidence.

## Where Do the Chorus Speeches Appear? The Witness-by-Witness Record

Six early editions carry the play between 1597 and 1637: the first quarto (Q1, 1597), the second quarto (Q2, 1599), the third quarto (Q3, 1609), the undated fourth quarto (Q4, assigned to 1622), the fifth quarto (Q5, 1637), and the First Folio (F, 1623). Their testimony about the Chorus speeches divides cleanly. Q2 prints both sonnets complete, each under the Chorus name. Q3, Q4, and Q5, as reprints in the second quarto's line, print both as well, so the quarto tradition read by the seventeenth century contained the frame entire. Q1 prints the Prologue in a shortened and corrupted form and omits the second Chorus altogether. The Folio prints the second Chorus but omits the Prologue, so the collected Works of 1623 opened the tragedy with the street brawl, the Chorus's sonnet nowhere in the volume. No other early text mixes the pattern. Each of the two performance-adjacent witnesses, the reported first quarto and the playhouse-annotated Folio copy, sacrificed one speech, and the two sacrifices were different speeches.

### Does the second quarto print the Prologue?

Yes, and the point matters because the second quarto is the one complete witness. Printed by Thomas Creede for Cuthbert Burby in 1599, the second quarto opens the play with a page headed "The Prologue," assigns the sonnet to a speaker named Chorus, and prints all fourteen lines in their familiar form.

Its title page advertises the text as newly corrected, augmented, and amended, and the sonnet runs from "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1) to "What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue.14). Every modern text of the speech descends from this page.

The shape of the record is the first clue to its meaning. The speeches are safest in the line of transmission that runs through print shops: manuscript draft to Q2, then Q2 copied by Q3, Q3 by Q4, Q4 by Q5. They are damaged or missing precisely where the line of transmission runs through the playhouse: in a text shaped by performance and report, and in a text whose copy was annotated against theatrical documents. A purely bibliographical accident, a compositor skipping a page, would not sort the witnesses so neatly by their distance from the stage. The frame survived where the play was treated as a book and suffered where the play was treated as a show. That distribution frames every question the rest of this study takes up: what happened to the Prologue inside Q1, why the Folio dropped a speech its own copy contained, why the second Chorus fared inversely in the same two witnesses, and what editors and directors, faced with speeches of questioned credentials, have done about it.

The quarto line deserves one more precision before the witnesses are examined singly. Q3 of 1609 is a straightforward reprint of Q2, page for page in substance, and Q4 and Q5 continue the same descent, so the presence of both Chorus speeches in those books proves nothing new about authority; it proves only that the reprint trade found the frame worth its paper for four decades. The independent witnesses are three: Q1, Q2, and the Folio. On the Prologue, Q1 and Q2 agree in substance while differing in form, and the Folio dissents by silence. On the second Chorus, Q2 and the Folio agree in substance while differing in dress, and Q1 dissents by silence. The sonnets' whole early history is written in those two patterns of agreement and silence, and the patterns point in opposite directions, which is why the case has to be argued witness by witness rather than settled by counting books.

## The Prologue in the Second Quarto: The One Complete Copy

The second quarto's authority over the Chorus speeches rests on what kind of manuscript stood behind it. The consensus of editors from W. W. Greg to René Weis holds that Q2 was printed from the author's foul papers, the working draft, because the book preserves the draft's fingerprints throughout: speech prefixes that waver between a character's name and description, stage directions that describe action in the author's provisional wording, and at least one passage, the aubade in 3.5, where an earlier and a later version of the same lines both reached print because the deletion in the copy was not plain enough to stop the compositor. A text set from the author's draft stands closer to the desk than any playhouse document, and for frame material the distinction is decisive. A promptbook might omit a prologue that the company had stopped speaking; the author's draft contains what the author wrote for the play's opening, whether or not the company went on speaking it. The full study of [the second quarto of 1599 and its standing as the play's foundation text](/2026/04/03/second-quarto-1599-good-quarto/) sets out that case in detail; the Chorus speeches are among its clearest exhibits.

What Q2 prints, under the heading "The Prologue" and the speech prefix "Chorus," is the sonnet in its canonical shape. The first quatrain sets the feud: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue.1-4). The second turns to the lovers: "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life; / Whose misadventured piteous overthrows / Doth with their death bury their parents' strife" (Prologue.5-8). The third measures the cost: "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love / And the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, nought could remove, / Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.9-12). The couplet contracts with the audience: "The which, if you with patient ears attend, / What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue.13-14). Fourteen lines, three quatrains and a couplet, rhyming in the English sonnet pattern. As a reading of the speech as poetry, with its foreknowledge and its contract, the sonnet has its own study in [the Prologue sonnet that spoils the ending](/2022/03/18/romeo-and-juliet-prologue-sonnet-analysis/); what matters here is the documentary fact that this complete, regular, attributed text exists in one early edition only.

Even Q2's completeness has a texture worth noting, because it separates the two speeches' credentials. The Prologue stands before the play proper, on its own page territory, headed as a piece: "The Prologue." The second Chorus stands inside the play's flow, where Q2 gives it the speech prefix "Chorus" and lets it do the work of closing the feast and opening the orchard scenes. Modern editions supply an entrance direction for both speeches, since the quarto's directions are sparse at exactly these joints, but the attribution itself, the name Chorus over both sonnets, is Q2's own. The book thus presents the frame as one voice speaking twice, a pair of matching sonnets by a single functionary. That presentation is itself evidence in the status debate, because a printer's makeshift or a playhouse filler would be unlikely to arrive in the copy as a designed pair, matched in form, matched in speaker, and placed at the two joints where the action changes state, before the feud's first brawl and before the lovers' first meeting in the orchard.

The counterfactual sharpens the point. Suppose the Chorus speeches were late additions, tacked on for a revival or supplied by a hired pen for the print shop. The foul papers behind Q2 would then have to contain them as part of the author's own draft package, pasted into the very manuscript whose untidiness everywhere else betrays the writing desk rather than the playhouse. The simpler account is that the draft always carried its frame. Q2's Prologue is not a puzzle in itself; it becomes one only when set beside the witnesses that damaged or dropped it, and the damage begins with the earliest book of all.

## Twelve Lines for Fourteen: The First Quarto's Prologue

The first quarto, printed in 1597 by John Danter with Edward Allde completing the volume, bills itself on its title page as the play "as it hath been often (with great applause) played publicly" by the Lord Hunsdon's servants. Its Prologue keeps the promise of that billing in an unintended way: the speech reads like something carried away from a performance rather than copied from a page. Q1 prints the sonnet with twelve lines. Set out in modern spelling, its version runs: "Two household friends alike in dignity, / (In fair Verona, where we lay our scene) / From civil broils broke into enmity, / Whose civil war makes civil hands unclean. / From forth the fatal loins of these two foes, / A pair of star-cross'd lovers took their life: / Whose misadventures, piteous overthrows, / (Through the continuing of their fathers' strife, / And death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage) / Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage. / The which if you with patient ears attend, / What here we want we'll study to amend." The skeleton is the same speech. The flesh has slipped.

### How many lines does the first quarto's Prologue have?

Twelve, two short of the sonnet form the second quarto preserves. The missing matter comes from the second and third quatrains, where the first quarto compresses five lines into three and patches the join with a parenthesis, leaving a poem that no longer answers to the sonnet's count or design.

The lines it drops are "Doth with their death bury their parents' strife" (Prologue.8) and "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love" (Prologue.9). A sonnet reduced to twelve lines is no longer a sonnet, and the reduction is the single most telling physical fact about the speech's transmission through the playhouse.

The damage is diagnostic because of where it falls and what survives it. The first quatrain survives almost whole, altered in wording but intact in shape: "household friends" for "households, both," "civil broils broke into enmity" for "ancient grudge break to new mutiny." The sense persists; the words approximate. Then the texture changes. The line that carries the play's whole tragic mechanism, the death that buries the parents' strife, vanishes. The famous "death-mark'd" survives, but dislodged: in Q2 it belongs to a poised, self-contained line, "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love" (Prologue.9), while in Q1 the word resurfaces inside a bracketed aside, attached to "passage" instead of "love," in a line that has to serve as connective tissue: "And death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage." A word remembered, a line forgotten, a patch built from the debris: the sequence is exactly what a text looks like when it has been carried in memory rather than copied from paper, because memory retains the striking word and the general drift while losing the syntax that held them in place.

The rhymes tell the same story in a stricter court. In Q2 the sonnet rhymes cleanly through all three quatrains: dignity, mutiny, scene, unclean; foes, overthrows, life, strife; love, rage, remove, stage. In Q1 the first quatrain holds its pattern by slant rhymes, dignity against enmity, scene against unclean. Then the pattern collapses. "Foes" waits for a partner that never comes, since its answering rhyme "overthrows" has been displaced two lines down and spent on a line that needed a different mate. "Life" finds "strife" only across the wreckage of the lost lines. "Rage" and "stage" slant toward each other at a distance. The couplet closes on the near rhyme of "attend" and "amend," where Q2 has the firmer "attend" and "mend." A writer revising a sonnet repairs its rhymes; a memory losing a sonnet sheds them. The first quarto's Prologue is the play's most famous set piece in the condition of a ballad half recalled by a good listener, gist faithful, architecture gone.

The couplet's wording seals the reading. Q2's Chorus promises, "What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue.14): the company's labour will supply whatever the summary omitted, a confident bargain. Q1's version promises, "What here we want we'll study to amend": what is lacking, and the word "want" carries its older sense of lack, will be studied and amended. The Q1 couplet apologizes for deficiencies the Q2 couplet does not admit. Whether the apology belongs to the reporting actor, to the playhouse adapting its frame for a particular audience, or to a compositor tidying a defective copy, its posture matches the document it closes, a text conscious of falling short of a fuller original that its makers had heard but did not hold.

## Line by Line: The Two Prologues Set Side by Side

The first quarto's Prologue rewards a slow collation, because its variants are not random noise but a profile of the medium that carried it. Line 1 in Q2 addresses the subject as "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1): two institutions, matched in rank, the sentence balanced on the doubled "both." Q1 reads "Two household friends alike in dignity." The institutional noun has become a social one, the matched pair a pair of friends, and the line's chill formality has warmed into something nearer gossip. The sense survives; the register, the part of the line that carries the play's civic meaning, does not. Line 3 in Q2, "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue.3), sets old against new in a single bound, the grudge ancient, the mutiny fresh. Q1 reads "From civil broils broke into enmity": the temporal architecture vanishes, the verb slips into the past tense, and "civil," borrowed from the next line, does duty early. Line 4 completes the borrowing: where Q2 has "Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue.4), its "civil" sounding twice as the city's sickness, Q1 has "Whose civil war makes civil hands unclean," a third "civil" in four lines, the word retained because it was memorable and redeployed because its original placements were not.

The middle of the sonnet shows the method at its most exposed. Q2's lines 5 and 6 pass intact in substance, "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes" and the star-cross'd lovers, though Q1's lovers "took their life" where Q2's "take their life," the past tense again flattening the proclamation into report. Then the losses begin. Q2's line 7, "Whose misadventured piteous overthrows" (Prologue.7), survives in Q1 with its grammar loosened, "misadventures" pluralized into a noun. Q2's line 8, the tragedy's thesis in nine words, "Doth with their death bury their parents' strife" (Prologue.8), disappears. Q2's line 9, "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love" (Prologue.9), disappears as a line, leaving its strongest word behind. Q2's lines 10 and 11, "And the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, nought could remove" (Prologue.10-11), collapse into Q1's parenthesis, "(Through the continuing of their fathers' strife, / And death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage)," in which the dropped line's "strife" and the dropped line's "death-mark'd" are preserved like furniture saved from a fire and stored in the wrong room. Line 12, "Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.12), survives whole, the advertisement being the part of the speech a playhouse remembers best. The couplet, as noted, trades confidence for apology.

Read as a whole, the collation describes a mind, or a chain of minds, that retained the speech's programme and its striking words while losing its joinery. Nothing in the profile suggests a scribe copying badly from a page, since a copyist's errors cluster in letterforms and eyeskips, not in the systematic conversion of present-tense proclamation into past-tense summary. Nothing suggests a reviser improving the piece, since no reviser trades "ancient grudge break to new mutiny" for "civil broils broke into enmity" on purpose. The profile is a hearer's. It is the single most concrete exhibit in the case for the memorial character of Q1, and it has the further virtue of being checkable by any reader against the two printed pages, which is why the Prologue, of all the play's passages, became the exhibit on which the quarrel over the first quarto was so often fought.

The chart exposes the pattern the prose witness statements can blur. Presence alone would score the Folio and the first quarto as equals, one speech apiece. Condition separates them and separates both from the quarto line. Only Q2 and its reprints present the frame as the frame presents itself: two sonnets, one named speaker, stations at the play's two thresholds. Q1 keeps the first threshold and loses the second, and what it keeps it has half forgotten. The Folio keeps the second threshold and loses the first, and what it keeps it has anonymized, printing the sonnet as an unattached block of verse between the feast and the orchard. The two defective witnesses are defective in mirror image, and the mirror is the finding: there was no single catastrophe, no one fire or theft, but a steady pressure, exerted independently in different places, against speeches that belonged to no character and advanced no plot. ## The Reprint Line: How the Cheap Quartos Kept the Frame

Between the second quarto and the civil wars, the play lived in the book trade as a quarto, and the quarto trade kept its frame. Q3 in 1609 reprinted Q2 entire, Prologue and second Chorus in place, and the volume's descent through Q4 and Q5 carried both speeches to readers of 1622 and 1637. The reprint line was not mindless. The Folger editors note that the fourth quarto's printer appears to have consulted Q1 for some corrections and word choices elsewhere in the play, a reminder that the trade's memory was long and its copy eclectic; yet neither Q4's eclecticism nor any reprint's economy disturbed the Chorus speeches, which stood in the copy the printers actually used and were set as a matter of course. For four decades the frame was simply part of the book that stationers sold and readers bought, its presence in the cheap format as unremarkable as its absence from the expensive one would prove to be.

The distribution carries a social fact worth stating plainly. The readers most likely to know the Prologue in the seventeenth century were the readers of the least prestigious editions, the playgoers and pamphlet buyers of the quarto trade, while the readers of the great collected folio, the edition that defined the canon for institutions, read a tragedy that began in a street fight. Textual prestige and textual completeness had already parted company, and the Prologue stood on the completeness side. When the theatres closed in 1642 the quarto line ended with them, and the frame's survival passed wholly into the hands of the editors who would shortly begin rebuilding the text from all the witnesses at once. The reprint line had done its work by then. It had kept both sonnets in continuous print for the entire period during which the folio tradition lacked one of them, and it had kept them in the form, Q2's form, that the restoration would canonize.

## The Folio's Missing Prologue: Dropped from a Copy That Contained It

The Folio's omission is the stranger of the two losses, because it cannot be blamed on a defective ancestor. The bibliographical work of Charlton Hinman on the printing of the First Folio, and the Folger editors Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine in their introduction to the play, converge on the derivation: the Folio text of the tragedy was set from a copy of the third quarto, and that copy had been annotated by a hand whose changes run beyond anything compositors invent for themselves. Q3 contains the Prologue. A compositor setting type from an annotated Q3 does not skip a whole sonnet on the first page of his copy by eyeskip, and the Folio's two compositors for this play, the workmen Hinman identified as B and E, show no comparable lapse anywhere else in their stints. The Prologue was not lost in the Folio's printing house. It was deleted before or during the preparation of the copy, by the same agency that made the Folio's other considered departures from Q3, and the deleting hand had the speech in front of it and chose, or was instructed, to let it go.

### Did the Folio's printers have the Prologue in their copy?

Yes. The Folio text was set from the third quarto of 1609 with manuscript annotation, and the third quarto, a reprint of the second, prints the Prologue complete on its opening page. The omission is therefore a deletion, not an inheritance, made somewhere between the annotated copy and the Folio's first page of the play.

The volume went to press opening with the act and scene heading and the serving-men's brawl, the most famous fourteen lines in the tragedy set aside before composition began.

Why would the annotator strike the Prologue and keep the second Chorus? Three explanations circulate, and they are not equally strong. The weakest is printing-house economy: a folio page saved. Economy explains cuts measured in pages across a volume of nine hundred pages, and the Prologue is half a page; it also fails to explain why the second Chorus, identical in length and kind, was worth its paper. The second explanation is architectural. The Folio regularized the play into the act and scene divisions the quartos lack, printing "Actus Primus. Scoena Prima" at the head of the tragedy. The second Chorus stands inside that architecture, between the first act's close and the second act's opening scene, and it survived, demoted to an anonymous insertion but legible in place. The Prologue stands outside the architecture altogether, before the first act begins, the one element of the copy that belonged to no act and no scene. A copy prepared toward act-and-scene regularity had no shelf for it. The third explanation is theatrical, and it is the one the Folger introduction's annotated copy most supports: the annotation drew on playhouse documents, and in the playhouse the Prologue was the first casualty of a play's maturity. A prologue advertises novelty, begs patience, and promises two hours' traffic; a company reviving a proven success needs none of the three. What the promptbook no longer spoke, the annotated copy no longer insisted on, and the Folio printed the play as its later performances had come to begin it, in medias res, with drawn swords.

The architectural and theatrical accounts reinforce each other, and between them they answer the chart's riddle. The Folio did not lose the Prologue the way Q1 lost lines, by frailty. It shed the Prologue the way institutions shed ceremony, deliberately and without record, because the speech's work, introducing an unknown play to a first audience, was work the collected edition no longer needed done. The irony is exact: the edition that monumentalized the play for readers discarded the one speech that exists to mediate between a play and its audience, while keeping, in the second Chorus, a speech of the same kind that happened to sit inside the numbered structure. Survival, in the Folio, was a matter of filing.

## Set in Type: The Printing Houses Behind the Witnesses

The witnesses differ as physical products before they differ as texts, and their manufacture bears on the frame's fate at every stage. Q1 came off two presses, John Danter's and Edward Allde's, in the hurried conditions of a small playbook rushed to market on a playing success, and its Prologue stands where such books put their prologues, immediately after the title page, the first page of the text proper. Nothing in its manufacture suggests difficulty at that page; the shortened sonnet was in the copy, not made in the shop. Q2 came from Thomas Creede's shop for Cuthbert Burby, a more deliberate production, and its Prologue stands in the same position, complete. Creede's shop, though, supplies the case's most delicate piece of evidence. The Folger editors record that for one stretch of the play, the pages covering 1.2.55 to 1.3.37, Q2 appears to be no more than a reprint of Q1, the printer having turned to the earlier book where his manuscript copy failed or was illegible. The shop therefore had both documents in reach during production, the foul papers and the reported quarto, and set the play from each in turn. At the Prologue it set the manuscript's version, fourteen lines, not the quarto's twelve. The choice, if choice it was, shows the manuscript legible and trusted exactly where the frame stood, and it anchors the Prologue's full text to the author's papers by the shop's own practice.

The Folio's manufacture completes the triangle. Hinman's analysis of the Folio's printing assigned the tragedy to compositors B and E, workmen whose habits across the volume are documented closely enough that a whole-sonnet eyeskip at the very start of their copy would stand out as an anomaly without parallel in their stints. The compositors set what their copy gave them, including an Act 2 Chorus without a prefix, a detail no compositor invents, since setting a speech without its prefix saves no labour worth the irregularity. The prefix was absent in the copy, as the Prologue was absent in the copy, and both absences trace back to the annotation that prepared a third quarto for the Folio's press. The printing houses, examined closely, acquit themselves at every point. The frame's fortunes were decided upstream of the presses, in the documents the presses were handed, and the documents were decided by the playhouse and the study, between them.

## The Second Chorus: Lost by the First Quarto, Orphaned by the Folio

The second Chorus sonnet, "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.0.1-2), performs the play's first act of summary from inside the action. Rosaline is dismissed in its opening quatrain, "That fair for which love groaned for and would die, / With tender Juliet matched, is now not fair" (2.0.3-4); the lovers' new peril is stated in its second, Romeo bound to "his foe supposed" (2.0.7) and Juliet stealing "love's sweet bait from fearful hooks" (2.0.8); and its couplet promises the engine of the rest, "But passion lends them power, time means, to meet, / Tempering extremities with extreme sweet" (2.0.13-14). The speech is load-bearing commentary, the hinge between the comedy of the first act and the tragedy of the remaining four. Its transmission is the Prologue's inverted: where the Prologue is whole in the quartos and absent in the Folio, the second Chorus is whole in the quarto line, absent in Q1, and present in the Folio in a state that needs a second look.

Q1's omission is the easier case, and it matches the condition of Q1's Prologue. A version of the play shortened for performance and carried into print by report sheds commentary before it sheds story, since commentary is what a playing text can spare and what a reporter's memory holds worst. The first quarto keeps every dramatic movement of the longer text in sequence, the feast, the meeting, the parting, but drops the speech in which no character speaks and nothing happens. Under the memorial account of Q1, the omission needs no separate explanation: the same frailty that cost the Prologue two lines and its rhymes cost the second Chorus its existence. Under the performance account, the omission is a cut of the same kind the Folio's annotator made at the other end of the play's frame. Either way, Q1's silence about the second Chorus is of a piece with its treatment of the first, and the pair of facts belongs in one explanation.

The Folio's treatment is the subtler document. The Folio prints all fourteen lines of the speech, standing between Juliet's "Come let's away, the strangers all are gone" at the feast's end and "Enter Romeo alone" at the orchard wall, but it prints them bare. There is no heading naming the piece, no entrance for a speaker, and no speech prefix assigning the lines to Chorus; in the Folio's through-line numbering the sonnet occupies lines 733 to 746 as an unattributed block. The words survive with their authority papers removed. A reader of the Folio alone would meet a sonnet floating between the acts, spoken by nobody the book identifies, and only the quarto tradition supplies the name that makes it a speech. The demotion is consistent with the Prologue's deletion in one respect: the Folio's copy preparation had no investment in the Chorus as a personage. It kept the second sonnet, plausibly, because it sat inside the act structure the edition was imposing and because its summary bridged a join the bare scenes left abrupt; it stripped the attribution because the annotator's documents, theatrical in origin, treated the frame as apparatus rather than as a role.

### Does the Folio print the Act 2 Chorus?

Yes, entire, but on reduced terms. The Folio prints all fourteen lines of "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie" (2.0.1) between the close of the feast and Romeo's entrance at the orchard wall, yet gives the speech no heading, no entrance direction, and no Chorus prefix.

The sonnet stands in the text as an anonymous insertion. The speech survives in the Folio; its speaker does not, and the attribution in modern editions comes from the second quarto.

Set the two Folio facts together and a coherent editorial temperament appears. The collected edition wanted the play's action complete and its architecture regular. It had no corresponding want for the play's frame as a voiced institution. One Chorus speech fell outside the architecture and was removed; the other fell inside it and was retained as text while its voicing was discarded. Neither decision looks like censorship or doubt about authorship. Both look like the priorities of a book being made from playhouse papers by men for whom the Chorus had stopped being a colleague and become a feature of the copy, to be kept where convenient and dropped where not.

## The Second Chorus Collated: Fourteen Lines in Two Dresses

The second Chorus survives in two early states, and collating them shows a channel under different strain from the one that wrecked Q1's Prologue. The Folio's fourteen lines agree with the second quarto's in substance at nearly every point: the dismissal of Rosaline, the foe supposed, the sweet bait and fearful hooks, the couplet's promise of power and time. The differences are of surface and dress. The Folio prints "death bed" open where the modern text hyphenates the compound, spells "yong" for "young" in "young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.0.2), and levels the quarto's "Alike bewitchèd" into "A like bewitched" in "Now Romeo is beloved and loves again" (2.0.5-6), a small unmetrical smoothing of the kind compositors and annotators make without noticing. Nothing is lost, nothing transposed, no line patched from debris. The channel between Q2 and the Folio, print shop to print shop with an annotator between, transmitted verse the way print transmits, with accidentals drifting and substantives holding. Set beside Q1's Prologue, where substantives themselves dissolve, the Folio's Chorus marks the opposite pole of the frame's fortunes: a speech can cross three decades of print with its argument intact and still arrive diminished, because what the channel stripped was not wording but standing, the prefix that made the lines a person's speech.

The collation also settles a question the chart raises. If the Folio's copy preparation struck the Prologue deliberately, why did the same preparation spare the second Chorus and then bother to strip its prefix? The sparing and the stripping are one gesture read twice. The speech stood inside the copy's sequence of scenes, embedded between the feast and the orchard, and the preparation's habit was conservative toward embedded matter: adjust, regularize, occasionally correct, but retain. The prefix, by contrast, was frame apparatus, of the same kind as the Prologue itself, and the preparation's habit toward apparatus was deletion. The two habits together produce exactly the Folio the chart records, a text that keeps the play and thins its frame at every point where the frame can be thinned, deleting the speech outside the structure, retaining and anonymizing the speech inside it. No theory of compositorial accident produces that pattern, and no theory of ideological hostility to choruses explains why the hostility stopped at anonymization. The pattern is bureaucratic, and bureaucracy, in the transmission of plays, is what the playhouse's paperwork always was.

## Star-Crossed or Death-Marked: The Variant Inside the Sonnet

One celebrated variant sits inside the Prologue, and the brief of this study assigns it a passing examination, since the variant study proper belongs to a companion article. The passing examination yields a surprise. The sixth line, "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue.6), is the most quoted phrase the Chorus speaks, and it is stable: Q1 agrees with Q2, reading "star-cross'd" in its own spelling, so the phrase that named a cultural commonplace was never in textual danger. The instability gathers instead around "death-mark'd." In Q2 the word stands in the sonnet's most deliberate line, "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love" (Prologue.9), where it brands the love itself as fated for death. In Q1 that line is gone, and the word has migrated into the parenthetical patch, "death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage," where it brands the parents' rage instead and sits inside brackets as part of a repair. The full adjudication of [the star-crossed or death-marked variant and what each reading commits the play to](/2030/10/11/star-crossed-or-death-marked/) is the companion study's work; the transmission point is this study's. The variant is not a coin toss between two authorial wordings at one point in the line. It is the debris pattern of a lost line, a strong word surviving the wreck of its context and washing up in a neighbouring sentence, which is what happens to language carried by memory and what does not happen to language carried by a compositor's copy. The variant, examined in passing, testifies for the memorial character of Q1 more compactly than any speech in the book.

## The Heading and the Prefix: How Each Early Book Presents the Speech

A speech's textual status is written in its presentation as much as in its wording, and the Chorus speeches are presented differently by every class of witness. The quartos print the play as a continuous text without the act and scene divisions modern readers navigate by, and the Prologue stands at the head of that continuum under its own heading. Q1 gives the piece the heading "The Prologue" and nothing else: no entrance, no prefix, no exit, the twelve lines following the heading as a titled poem follows a title. Q2 gives the same heading and adds the speech prefix "Chorus," converting the titled poem into a spoken part, and gives the second sonnet the same prefix where it stands. The reprint quartos inherit Q2's presentation unchanged. In the quarto tradition, then, the frame is a headed, and in the better line an attributed, component of the book, as much a part of the volume's furniture as the title page that advertises the play above it.

The title pages themselves frame the contrast between the two early quartos. Q1's book calls the play "An Excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet" and advertises performance, the play "as it hath been often (with great applause) played publicly." Q2's book calls it "The Most Excellent and lamentable Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet" and advertises textual authority, the play "newly corrected, augmented, and amended." The books' self-presentations predict their treatment of the frame with uncomfortable fidelity. The performance book carries the performance's damaged Prologue. The corrected book carries the corrected, complete one. A reader choosing between the quartos in 1599 was choosing, among other things, between two states of the most famous sonnet in the language, and the title pages, without knowing it, said so.

The Folio's presentation abolishes the heading along with the speech. Where the quartos begin with a titled piece, the Folio begins with a structural label, "Actus Primus. Scoena Prima," and the serving-men. The substitution is the edition's whole programme in one gesture: the play is to be presented as literature organized by architecture, acts and scenes numbered like chapters, and the apparatus of performance, the titled prologue that once stood at the door of the playhouse text, has no place in the architecture. The second Chorus, which could be filed inside the architecture as the second act's threshold, was filed and kept. The Prologue, which preceded the architecture, was not. Presentation, in the Folio, was destiny.

### Do the quartos divide the play into acts and scenes around the Chorus speeches?

No. Neither quarto prints act or scene divisions anywhere in the play, so in the early quartos the Prologue stands before an undivided text and the second Chorus stands where the feast ends and the orchard action begins. The numbered framework behind modern citation is an editorial inheritance from the Folio's divisions, refined by later editors.

The afterlife of the presentations is a quiet irony. Modern editions print Q2's words under headings and directions assembled from all three traditions: the speech from Q2, the act and scene scaffolding from the Folio, the entrance and exit directions from editorial convention. The page a student reads is thus a composite no early reader ever saw, and the Prologue at its head, complete, attributed, and correctly placed, is the most composite element of all, a speech that had to be reassembled from the wreckage of its own transmission before it could stand where the design put it.

## A Speaker Named Only Chorus: The Identity the Texts Never Give

Every witness that attributes the speeches attributes them to the same bare word. Q2 heads the first "The Prologue" and prefixes both sonnets "Chorus." Q1 heads its shortened version "The Prologue" and gives it no prefix at all, so in the earliest book the speech is a titled piece without a named speaker. The Folio, at the other extreme, prints the second sonnet with neither heading nor prefix. No early text names an actor, assigns the part to a character in the fiction, or explains the figure's standing. The Chorus of this play is a function wearing a label, and the label is all the documentation there is. The larger question of the figure's identity, its ancestry in the classical and early English chorus, and its use of "we" and "our stage" as a company's voice is the subject of [the Chorus in Romeo and Juliet and the question of who is speaking](/2023/03/17/chorus-in-romeo-and-juliet-analysis/); the textual point is narrower and stranger. Attribution itself decays along the same gradient as the text. The witness printed from the author's papers attributes both speeches fully. The performance witnesses attribute partially or not at all. The frame's named existence, like its wording, is a property of the literary transmission, and thins wherever the play passed through hands that used it rather than read it.

The decay matters for a practical reason. A speech with a named speaker inside the fiction, a Friar or a Prince, cannot be mislaid by an editor or a company without leaving a hole in the plot. A speech assigned to a function can be reassigned, divided among a company, spoken by a character borrowed from the dramatis personae, or cut, and the plot closes over the gap without a scar. The whole later history of the Chorus, in editions that must decide how to label it and productions that must decide who speaks it, is contained in that label's thinness. The texts transmit the words with unusual care in one line of descent and the identity with no care in any, and the combination is the Prologue's status in miniature: a canonical text with an anonymous voice, famous lines in search of a speaker the documents never supply.

## The Sonnet Form as Evidence: Why the Shape of the Speech Testifies

The Chorus speeches are sonnets, and their form is not decoration on the evidence; it is evidence. A sonnet is the most formally accountable utterance in the play, fourteen lines locked into a rhyme scheme that either holds or visibly fails, and that accountability makes the sonnet a measuring instrument for the channels that carried it. Prose can be paraphrased invisibly and blank verse can lose a line without breaking stride, but a sonnet transmits its own damage report. When Q1's Prologue arrives with twelve lines and collapsed rhymes, the form itself certifies that the text has passed through a medium that could not hold formal structure, whatever that medium was. When Q2's Prologue arrives with its scheme intact through three quatrains and a couplet, the form certifies a channel that copied rather than recalled. The status debate has always turned on this certification, because the sonnet leaves its examiners nowhere to hide: the damage is countable.

The pairing of the speeches extends the instrument's range. The second Chorus is also a sonnet, in the same form, rhyming as strictly in Q2 as the Prologue does, and the two speeches stand at the two points where the play changes its state, from peace threatened to feud declared, from desire fixed on Rosaline to desire fixed on Juliet. Around them the play thinks in sonnets at its joints: the lovers' first exchange at the feast is itself a shared sonnet, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand" (1.5.92), built by two speakers line about line. A play that composes its thresholds in sonnets is a play whose frame sonnets belong to its load-bearing design, and the design argument matters textually because it raises the cost of the non-authorial hypothesis. A hired prologue-writer supplying a door piece for one occasion might produce one sonnet. The matched pair, integrated with the play's sonnet practice at its centre, asks to be explained as a system, and systems of that kind are what authors, rather than occasions, produce.

Form also disciplines the performance explanations. The company's playing text, on any account, treated the frame as negotiable, and the sonnet form explains why the negotiation so often ended in loss. A sonnet cannot be shortened gracefully in performance the way a scene can, because its argument is distributed across a fixed count of lines; cutting two lines from the middle of a sonnet does not abbreviate it but deforms it, as Q1's page shows. A company that needed fourteen lines' worth of time back from the Prologue had no clean way to take four and keep the piece, and the surviving options were the ones the witnesses record: keep it whole, as the reading text does, garble it by carrying it in memory, or drop it, as the Folio's copy did. The form that makes the speech fragile in transmission is the same form that makes its survival in Q2 so probative. Nobody's memory, and no playhouse's convenience, produces an intact English sonnet by accident. Its intactness is the signature of the page it was copied from, and that page was the author's.

## The Mirror Case: What Another Play's Chorus Shows

The Prologue's fortunes look less eccentric when set beside the nearest comparable case in the canon, the Chorus of Henry V. That play's elaborate choruses, five speeches and an epilogue that frame the action act by act, survive only in the First Folio. The first quarto of Henry V, printed in 1600 from a performance-derived text, omits the choruses entire, every one, and presents the history as unmediated action. The parallel with the tragedy is exact in structure and opposite in outcome. In both plays the performance-derived quarto sheds choral frame material. In Henry V the literary witness that preserves the frame is the Folio; in the tragedy it is Q2, and the Folio is the shedder. The comparison cuts two ways, both useful. It confirms that quarto transmission through performance was hard on choruses as a class, which strengthens every account of Q1 that runs through the playhouse. And it warns against Folio fundamentalism in either direction: the collected edition is a witness with habits, generous to frame material in one play and careless of it in another, its treatment governed by the copy it happened to receive rather than by any policy toward choruses. The tragedy's Prologue was not singled out for destruction. It was processed, and the processing differed by document, which is the whole argument in one comparison.

A second comparison, inside the author's own practice, fixes the Prologue's kind. Troilus and Cressida has an armed Prologue that survives in both its quarto and the Folio, a reminder that the Folio's printers printed prologues readily when the copy carried them into the text block. Henry VIII opens with a Prologue that warns the audience against laughter, preserved in the Folio, its only early text. Pericles entrusts its frame to Gower, a chorus figure woven through the action rather than stationed at its thresholds, and Gower survives in that play's single quarto because he is load-bearing in every scene he touches. The tragedy's Chorus, by contrast, is stationed only at thresholds, pure frame with no intrusion into the action, and therein lies its vulnerability in every performance channel. What a chorus does inside the story cannot be cut without tearing the story. What it does at the door can be cut by anyone who controls the door, and the witnesses record a queue of door-keepers, reporters, annotators, and companies, each taking the same liberty for local reasons.

## Memory, Adaptation, or Loose Paper: Three Explanations Tested

Three explanations of the frame's transmission have serious advocates, and the evidence assembled witness by witness allows an adjudication rather than a survey. The first is the memorial account. Harry R. Hoppe's The Bad Quarto of Romeo and Juliet (1948) made the case that Q1 is a memorial reconstruction, a text assembled from the memories of actors in the company, and the Chorus speeches are among his strongest exhibits. A reporter carries away dialogue he spoke or heard nightly in cue with his own part; he carries frame material worst of all, because the Chorus spoke to the house, not to him, and because a sonnet's argument is its form, which memory cannot paraphrase without loss. Q1's Prologue, gist faithful and form collapsed, its second Chorus vanished entire, fits the prediction line by line.

The second explanation is the adaptation account. Jay L. Halio, editing the two quartos in parallel texts (1994) and arguing the case in his essay "Handy-Dandy," held that Q1 is no piracy but a version of the play adapted, plausibly by the author and the company, for performance under touring or chamber conditions, its shortenings deliberate and its variants revisions. On this reading the frame's state in Q1 is design: a playing version keeps the advertisement at the door, trimmed to fit the company's measure, and drops the internal summary a swift performance does not need. The third explanation is the documentary account. Tiffany Stern, in Documents of Performance in Early Modern England (2009), showed that prologues in the period were discrete documents, written for an occasion, sometimes by a hand other than the playwright's, circulated, posted, and preserved separately from the playbook they introduced. On this reading a prologue's irregular survival is its native condition, and no piracy or revision is required to explain why the frame and the play travelled by different roads and arrived in different states.

Lukas Erne's work binds the second and third accounts into the strongest composite. In Shakespeare as Literary Dramatist (2003) and in his edition of Q1 for the New Cambridge Early Quartos (2007), Erne argues that the play existed in two legitimate early states, a long literary text, preserved in Q2, meant for readers as well as players, and a short performance text, preserved in Q1, adapted to the stage's two hours. The Chorus sonnets, on Erne's showing, are literary architecture, part of the design by which the long text addresses a reader, and their damage in the performance line is the predictable cost of the adaptation, whoever performed it.

### Were the two Chorus speeches written as a pair?

The internal evidence says yes, and the pairing is the strongest single argument for the frame's authorial design. The speeches are both sonnets in the same English form, both assigned by the second quarto to the same speaker, and they divide the play's exposition symmetrically at its two thresholds.

The Prologue announces the feud and the deaths before the action; the second Chorus announces the transfer of desire and the lovers' peril at the action's first turn. Additions and fillers do not arrive in matched pairs at symmetrical stations.

The play itself supplies a footnote to the debate, and it survives only in the performance text. On the way to the feast, Romeo asks whether the maskers' entrance should be spoken for with an apology, and Benvolio dismisses the whole apparatus of presentation: "Nor no without-book prologue, faintly spoke / After the prompter, for our entrance" (1.4.7-8). The lines are Q1's alone, absent from Q2, preserved by the very channel that garbled the Prologue, and editors import them into the standard text from the first quarto. Their testimony is double. They prove that the company thought about prologues as equipment, a prepared speech carried by a presenter and fed by the prompter, equipment that could be declined when the occasion did not want it. And they prove that the declining was itself a fashionable gesture, the date being out of such prolixity. A play whose characters joke about skipping the prologue is a play whose prologue lived under the same dispensation as every other prologue of the period, spoken when useful, dropped when not, its text thereafter at the mercy of the channels that happened to carry it. The joke's survival in Q1, the witness that mangled the thing joked about, is the transmission history's neatest irony, and its importation into Q2-based editions is a standing reminder that the standard text of this play is a confederation of witnesses, each strongest where the others are weakest.

The adjudication follows from weighing what each account explains best. On the wording of Q1's Prologue, Hoppe defeats Halio. A reviser shortening a sonnet for performance cuts whole lines and keeps the rhyme scheme standing in what remains, because the rhyme is the speech's music and its mnemonic for the speaker; Q1's version keeps damaged matter and loses the scheme, shedding lines from inside quatrains and leaving partners unrhymed, which is the signature of recall, not of revision. Halio's account explains Q1's large-scale shortenings well, and the performance text Erne describes would indeed drop the second Chorus as Q1 does, but the twelve-line Prologue is not an adaptation of the sonnet. It is the sonnet remembered. On the Folio, Stern defeats any theory of loss. A prologue that lived as a separate document, spoken at first performances and dispensable thereafter, is exactly the object an annotated playhouse copy drops without comment two decades on, and the Folio's contrary care for the second Chorus, which had never been a separate document but a page inside the playbook, confirms the distinction in the Folio's own behaviour. The composite verdict stands: the speeches are the author's designed frame; performance and report garbled and cut them, each in its own way; and the Prologue's detachment, native to its kind, let the collected edition discard it while monumentalizing everything around it.

## How Editors Print a Speech the Folio Lacks

The editorial history of the Chorus speeches is a history of the quarto tradition quietly overruling the Folio. The early eighteenth-century editors who rebuilt the text after the folio line, beginning with Nicholas Rowe's edition of the Works in 1709, printed the Prologue as the play's opening, taking it on quarto authority, since the folios alone could not supply it. The restoration attracted no controversy, and that absence of controversy is itself a datum: no editor of stature proposed that the play should open as the Folio opens, with the brawl, although the Folio was the period's prestige text. The speech's absence from the Folio was treated from the start as the Folio's defect, not as evidence about the speech. Editorial instinct, in this instance, ran ahead of editorial theory, and theory later caught up and explained why the instinct was right.

Modern practice codified the instinct. The standard scholarly editions of the play, Brian Gibbons's Arden second series edition (1980), G. Blakemore Evans's New Cambridge edition (1984), Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition (2000), and René Weis's Arden third series edition (2012), all take Q2 as copy-text and print both Chorus speeches from it, recording in their collations the Folio's omission of the Prologue and Q1's omission of the second Chorus and its variants in the first. The editorial labour is not in deciding whether to print the speeches; that decision has been unanimous for three centuries. The labour is in deciding what the apparatus should say about the witnesses that lack them, and in resisting two symmetrical temptations. The first temptation is to treat the Folio's silence as a reading, as though omission were a variant with authority, and to bracket or demote the Prologue accordingly. No major edition yields to it. The second temptation is to treat Q1's shorter Prologue as a rival version deserving conflation, importing its lines into the copy-text. No major edition yields to that either. Q1's Prologue is edited where it belongs, in Erne's edition of Q1 itself and in Halio's parallel texts, as a document of the performance line, printed entire in its own twelve-line state for readers who want the play as the first audiences plausibly heard it.

### Where does a modern edition get its Prologue?

From the second quarto of 1599, in every case. The copy-text tradition that governs modern editing of the play takes the Prologue from Q2, the edition printed from the author's draft papers, and supplies the speech's heading and attribution from the same page.

The Folio cannot be the source, since it omits the speech, and the first quarto offers only the shortened, corrupted form, valuable as evidence and unusable as copy-text.

One refinement completes the editorial picture. Editors print the Prologue from Q2 while knowing that Q2's own presentation is spare: a heading, a prefix, and minimal direction. The furniture of performance that modern texts display at the speech, the entrance of the Chorus, the exit after the couplet, is editorial supplementation, standardized across editions until it looks documentary. The discipline is honest about the supplementation in its apparatuses and silent about it on the page, a division of labour the Prologue shares with much of the play's staging text. What editors will not supplement is the speech itself. Faced with a Folio that lacks it and a first quarto that garbles it, the profession's settled answer is that the second quarto's page is the Prologue, and the other witnesses are evidence about its fortunes.

## Reading the Apparatus: What the Collation Notes Record

The whole case can be read off the foot of a modern edition's page, for readers who know how to parse the apparatus, and the Chorus speeches make an unusually instructive specimen. A collation entry for the Prologue as a whole records, in the compressed grammar of textual scholarship, that the speech stands in the second quarto and in the first quarto's shortened form and is omitted from the Folio. An entry for its sixth line records that "star-cross'd" is the reading of both quartos against no Folio testimony at all, the Folio being silent for the entire speech. An entry for its ninth line records a substantive variant, Q2's "death-mark'd love" against Q1's reshaped parenthetical line, where a reader must choose between a reading and a wreck. The second Chorus generates the mirror set: entries recording its presence in the second quarto and the Folio, its absence from the first quarto, and, in the Folio's case, the absence of any speech prefix to collate against, a silence the apparatus can register only in a note.

Two lessons hide in that grammar. The first is that omission is recorded in the same notation as variation, and the equality is a trap for the unwary. A collation line that reads as though three witnesses had voted, two for the speech and one against, flatters the Folio's silence into a textual opinion. The Folio did not vote against the Prologue; it never reached the question, its copy having been prepared by a process in which the speech was set aside before composition began. Editors know this and discount the silence accordingly, but the discounting is judgment, not mechanics, and it is the clearest case in the play of why copy-text editing is a discipline of weighed evidence rather than counted witnesses. The second lesson concerns the first quarto. Q1's Prologue generates the densest cluster of substantive variants in the frame's apparatus, line after line of divergence, and the density is the memorial case in tabular form. Set beside the near-absence of variants between Q2 and its reprints, where the channel was mechanical copying, Q1's swarm of small differences marks the channel that ran through minds rather than presses. The apparatus, read attentively, does not merely record the speeches' strange status. It reproduces the evidence for every explanation of it, in the order the explanations need.

## Scene Zero: The Citation System's Confession

Modern scholarship cites the two speeches in a notation that confesses their status every time it is used. The Prologue is cited by its own name, "Prologue.6" for the star-cross'd line, because it belongs to no act and no scene. The second Chorus is cited as 2.0, act 2, scene zero, the zero marking a speech that opens an act without being a scene within it. The notation descends from the reference system the Arden tradition standardized, in which a zero in the scene position denotes the prologue to the act, and its necessity is the point. The play's architecture, inherited from the Folio's divisions and refined by editors, has numbers for everything the characters do and no number for what the Chorus does, so the frame is filed under zero, the architecture's admission that the speech is both indispensable to citation and external to the structure being cited. Students meet the oddity in their first week with the play, a prologue with a name instead of a number and a chorus at coordinate zero, and the oddity is the textual history in miniature. The speeches stand where the Folio's architecture could not file them, and the reference system, four centuries on, still registers the misfit.

The Folio's own numbering makes the same confession from inside the monument. Folio scholarship cites the volume by through lines, counting every line of type from the play's first page, and in that count the second Chorus occupies lines 733 to 746, filed between the feast and the orchard with no speech heading to interrupt the numeration. The Prologue, being absent, has no through lines at all: the Folio's arithmetic begins at the brawl, and its first 730 lines unfold in a play that, by its own count, was never introduced. A citation system built on the Folio has no way to point at the Prologue. It can only note the place where the speech would stand, before line 1, in the zero territory the Arden notation later formalized.

The misfit has a practical consequence for anyone quoting the play. A quotation from the Prologue carries no act and scene to check it against the volume on the desk, and volumes differ precisely here: a Folio-based reprint of the early text omits the speech, a school edition prints it from the quarto line, and the words quoted are identical in every modern book while their documentary warrant differs by edition. Citation of the frame is therefore a small exercise in the discipline this study has followed throughout, knowing which witness stands behind the page. The zero in 2.0 is the honest digit in the system. It says that the play's most systematic feature, its frame, is the one its systems cannot number, and that the sonnet at the threshold has always been, in the strictest documentary sense, an outsider at its own play.

## The Director's Problem: A Frame Every Age Keeps Cutting

The stage history of the Chorus speeches repeats the transmission history in modern dress. The Prologue survives in production more often than the second Chorus, because it has become the play's trademark, but both speeches are cut, reassigned, or redistributed with a freedom no character's lines would be granted, and the freedom is licensed by exactly the feature the early texts display: the speeches belong to nobody in the story. A production that gives the Prologue to the full company in unison, or to the Prince as the city's voice, or to a pair of lovers not yet in character, is solving the same problem the early books pose, a famous text with an unattributed voice. A production that cuts the second Chorus is repeating, for pace, the decision the first quarto's makers and reporters embodied, and a production that cuts the Prologue is repeating the Folio's. The early witnesses are not merely evidence about the speeches' status. They are the first recorded instances of the choices every subsequent handler of the play has gone on making.

Two famous screen solutions mark the poles of the modern practice, and both are instructive because both keep the words while abolishing the speaker. Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film retains the Prologue as narration spoken over the opening images, the Chorus dissolved into a voice without a body. Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film turns the sonnet into a television news bulletin, the Chorus dissolved into a medium. In each case the speech's authority is trusted completely and its personage discarded entirely, which is the Folio's own settlement restated in the grammar of film: the text is canonical, the Chorus is furniture. Against that settlement stands the contrary instinct of productions that restore the single robed figure at the front of the stage, an instinct with no support in the early texts beyond Q2's bare prefix. The stage, like the printing house, keeps deciding that the frame is indispensable and its keeper is not, and the textual record suggests the decision was being made within a year of the play's first performances.

For the director the practical consequence of the scholarship is a clarification rather than a command. Cutting the second Chorus has the first quarto's precedent and costs the play its hinge of summary, the one moment where the action pauses to be understood. Cutting the Prologue has the Folio's precedent and costs the play its contract, the foreknowledge that turns suspense into irony. Keeping both has only the second quarto's precedent, but the second quarto is the author's draft, and its precedent is the one that carries the design. The speeches' questioned credentials, examined closely, question nothing about their authorship. They record how consistently the theatre, beginning with the theatre that first played the piece, has valued the frame below the picture it frames.

## Two Hours' Traffic: The Line the Theatre Historians Borrow

One line of the Prologue has had a second career outside the textual debate, and its fortunes there confirm the speech's documentary weight. "Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.12) is the period's most quoted statement of a playing time, cited wherever the length of an early modern performance is argued, in reconstructions of the public theatres' afternoons and in estimates of the speed at which companies spoke verse. The line survives in both quartos, a point of rare stability in Q1's version, and its stability is itself instructive: the advertisement was the part of the frame a playhouse had reason to keep accurate, since it promised the audience a duration and the audience held the company to it. Theatre historians lean on the line because it is the play speaking about its own conditions in its own voice, and the textual record lets them lean with confidence about the wording while warning them, through Q1's surrounding damage, that the voice reached print by a damaged road.

The warning cuts deeper in one direction. The first quarto's whole text, at some 2,232 lines against the second quarto's 3,007, could plausibly be spoken inside the two hours the Prologue promises, while the full text strains the promise at any natural pace. The discrepancy has been used to argue that the short text represents the play as actually performed and the long text the play as written for the page, Erne's two versions in numerical form. The couplet that follows the advertisement sharpens the irony for the Folio's readers. The Chorus contracts with its hearers: give the summary patient ears, and the company's toil will mend what the summary missed. The Folio's audience never received the contract, since the speech containing it was the one the edition dropped, and the folio play accordingly makes no promises and keeps none, its spectators left to discover unaided what the quarto's spectators were told at the door. A frame is a covenant before it is an ornament, and the witnesses can be ranked by whether the covenant was ever offered. The Chorus's own advertisement thus becomes evidence about the Chorus's own fate: a company performing the short text kept the promise of brevity by cutting, among other things, the second Chorus and half the Prologue's argument, and the speech that announced the two hours was itself audited by them. No other fourteen lines in the play testify about so many of the play's conditions at once, which is a final reason the speech's textual status repays the attention this study has given it. It is not only a document with a strange history. It is a document about the very machinery that made its history strange.

## Three Centuries Without the Prologue: What the Folio's Silence Did

Between 1623 and the editorial restorations of the early eighteenth century, the most prestigious form of the play in circulation was a form that began with a street brawl. The folio line descended from the First Folio through the Second (1632), Third (1663), and Fourth (1685) Folios, each reprinting its predecessor's text of the tragedy, and none restored what the first had dropped. Readers whose Shakespeare was the folio Shakespeare, the Shakespeare of collected volumes in libraries, met the tragedy without its Chorus, and the play they met was measurably different in kind: a story whose ending arrived unannounced, whose irony had to be generated by the scenes alone, whose design lacked the frame that tells an audience how to hold what it watches. The quarto line, cheaper and less monumental, carried the frame to its own readers throughout the same period, so the seventeenth century read two plays under one title, divided not by interpretation but by the presence of fourteen lines.

The stage of the same century makes the restoration's meaning plainer by contrast. The theatrical culture whose scholars were reassembling the text with quarto help was simultaneously content to perform the play in versions rewritten at will, most famously in David Garrick's alteration, which held the stage for decades while reshaping the tragedy wholesale, adding a spectacular funeral procession for Juliet and a dying conversation between the lovers in the tomb. Print was growing scrupulous as performance grew free, and the Prologue lived on opposite sides of the divide: restored to the page by editors who weighed its witnesses, absent from stages that had never needed its permission to begin with a brawl. The divergence is the early transmission repeating itself at a distance of a century, the book tradition conserving the frame and the play tradition spending it.

The restoration, when editors working across both traditions put the Prologue back at the head of the collected text, settled more than a textual point. It decided which kind of book the play would be: a framed tragedy, whose audience is told the ending and set to watch the machinery, rather than a chronicle of surprises. Every subsequent century has read the framed play. The Prologue escaped the play altogether and circulated as a freestanding sonnet, anthologized, memorized, and quoted by readers who could not have named its witnesses, and its detachment in the culture replays its detachment in the documents: a speech that keeps slipping its moorings and surviving anyway. The Folio's silence, measured across three centuries, turns out to have been the loudest testimony to the speech's standing. The edition that dropped it could not drop it from the play, because the play the world wanted was the one the quarto had preserved, frame and all, and the monument had to be rebuilt from the pamphlet.

## Verdict: What the Prologue's Strange Status Actually Is

The strangeness of the Prologue's textual status dissolves, on inspection, into a simpler and more interesting fact. The speech is not of doubtful authorship, doubtful date, or doubtful place in the play's design. It is a designed element of the author's draft, one of a matched pair of Chorus sonnets, and its textual history is strange only because every channel that carried the play except one was a channel that handled frame material carelessly. The channel of performance and report kept the speech's gist and lost its form, halving its argument, breaking its rhymes, and dropping its companion entire. The channel of the collected edition, working from annotated playhouse copy, deleted the speech while preserving its companion in anonymous form, because the one stood outside the architecture being imposed and the other stood inside it. The channel of the print shop, running from the author's papers through Q2 and its reprints, preserved both speeches entire for forty years without fuss. Fame has inverted the channels' reputations. The Folio, the monument, is the witness that opens the play wrong. The bad quarto, the byword, is a witness that at least opens it with the Chorus on stage, however imperfectly recalled. And the good quarto, long treated as a convenient source for a text whose frame everyone took for granted, turns out to be the frame's sole complete custodian.

The verdict is corrigible, and its exposures can be named. A promptbook or part containing the Prologue in a fuller state than Q1's would weaken the memorial reading of the first quarto's damage. A demonstration that the Folio's annotated copy never contained the speech, that the annotation replaced the third quarto with a Prologue-less manuscript at the play's opening, would move the deletion from the playhouse to the study without changing the verdict's substance, since a manuscript that lacked the frame in 1623 is itself a performance-line document. What no new evidence could easily do is promote the Folio's silence into an authorial judgment, because the author's draft, the one document that speaks for the study rather than the stage, contains the speech entire and paired. The burden of proof sits permanently on any account that would make the Prologue an intruder in its own play, and after four centuries of scrutiny the burden has not been met.

That custodianship is the answer to the status question the speech keeps provoking. A text that survives in one authoritative copy, damaged in a second, and absent from a third has the status of a reading preserved by editorial choice, and the choice, renewed by every editor from Rowe to Weis, is the reason the sonnet stands at the head of the play in the form the world knows. The Prologue's strangeness is therefore not a blemish on its credentials but a measure of them: only a speech secure in the design could be cut so often, by so many hands, for so many practical reasons, and be restored so unanimously whenever the text was made with care. The Chorus asks its audience, in the second quarto's couplet, to let the company's toil mend what the summary missed. The textual history reverses the bargain. It is the summary, the frail fourteen lines at the threshold, that the toil of editors has had to mend, and the mending is the most reliable evidence of what the speech was worth.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Prologue chart, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Prologue chart is the table in this study that sets the two Chorus speeches against the six early editions of the play and records the condition of each survival. Its pattern is a division by channel. The second quarto of 1599 and its reprints present both sonnets complete and attributed to the Chorus. The first quarto keeps the Prologue in damaged form and loses the second Chorus. The First Folio loses the Prologue and keeps the second Chorus without its speaker. The chart's lesson is that presence alone misleads: the witnesses must be scored by condition, and by condition only the line of transmission that ran through print shops, from the author's draft to the second quarto and its reprints, carried the frame intact. Every channel that ran through the playhouse damaged one speech or the other.

### Q: Which early text prints both Chorus speeches complete?

The second quarto of 1599 prints both Chorus speeches complete, and no other independent early witness does. Printed by Thomas Creede for Cuthbert Burby from what editors judge to be the author's draft papers, the second quarto gives the Prologue all fourteen lines under the heading "The Prologue" and the second Chorus all fourteen lines at the opening of the second act, each assigned to the speaker named Chorus. The third, fourth, and fifth quartos also print both speeches, but as reprints in the second quarto's line they add no independent authority. The first quarto prints a shortened Prologue and omits the second Chorus, while the First Folio omits the Prologue and prints the second Chorus unattributed. Modern editions therefore take both speeches from the second quarto, and the completeness of that one book is the foundation of the frame's survival.

### Q: Why does the first quarto's Prologue end two lines short of a sonnet?

The first quarto's Prologue has twelve lines because the channel that carried it lost two lines from the sonnet's middle and patched the gap rather than restoring them. The missing lines are the eighth, "Doth with their death bury their parents' strife" (Prologue.8), and the ninth, "The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love" (Prologue.9), the two lines that state the deaths' purpose and brand the love as death-marked. In their place the first quarto runs a parenthetical pair of lines built from their debris, preserving the words "strife" and "death-mark'd" in new positions. The loss is characteristic of transmission by memory or performance report, in which a speech's gist and striking words survive while its exact sequence fails. A copyist working from a page does not misplace whole lines from the middle of a sonnet and then improvise a bracketed repair; a rememberer does exactly that.

### Q: What does the first quarto call the two households in the Prologue's first line?

The first quarto calls them "household friends": its first line reads "Two household friends alike in dignity," where the second quarto reads "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1). The variant is small and diagnostic. The second quarto's wording presents two institutions, matched in rank, whose feud is a civic condition; the first quarto's wording presents two friend groups, a social picture, and drops the balancing "both" that makes the line a formal equation. Sense survives and register changes, the pattern found throughout the first quarto's Prologue, where memorable words persist in loosened constructions. Editors record the reading as a substantive variant and print the second quarto's line, but the variant earns its place in the apparatus as evidence: it shows a text recalled by someone who had heard the line's drift and rhythm and reproduced both without the line itself.

### Q: How does the Prologue's closing couplet differ between the first and second quartos?

The couplets differ in posture as much as in wording. The second quarto closes, "What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue.14), a confident bargain in which the company's labour promises to supply whatever the fourteen-line summary left out. The first quarto closes, "What here we want we'll study to amend," in which "want" carries its older sense of lack, and the promise shrinks from the company's toil to a resolve to study and repair. The first quarto's rhyme is also weaker, "attend" against "amend" where the second quarto pairs "attend" with the firmer "mend." Editors and textual scholars read the first quarto's couplet as belonging with its whole Prologue: a text conscious of deficiency, apologizing in advance for gaps it knows are there, where the authoritative text knows of none.

### Q: From what printed copy did the Folio's compositors set the play?

The Folio's compositors set the play from a copy of the third quarto of 1609 that had been annotated by a hand drawing on theatrical documents. The derivation, established by Charlton Hinman's study of the Folio's printing and endorsed in the Folger editors' introduction to the play, explains the Folio text's mixed character: a quarto base, carrying both Chorus speeches, overlaid with considered departures too purposeful for typesetters to have invented. The annotation is the hinge of the Prologue's history. Because the third quarto prints the Prologue complete, the speech stood in the compositors' copy, and its absence from the Folio must be a deletion made in preparing that copy rather than an accident of the printing house. The same preparation stripped the second Chorus of its speech prefix while retaining its lines, a paired decision that points to playhouse priorities, in which frame apparatus was expendable and embedded verse was kept.

### Q: Which later quartos reprinted both Chorus speeches?

The third quarto of 1609, the fourth quarto, assigned to 1622, and the fifth quarto of 1637 all reprinted both Chorus speeches, carrying the Prologue and the second Chorus in the second quarto's form to readers across four decades. The reprints add no independent authority, since each descends from the second quarto's text, but their commercial testimony matters: the stationers who sold the play in its cheap format found the frame worth its paper in edition after edition. The fourth quarto's printer even consulted the first quarto for some corrections elsewhere in the play, without disturbing either Chorus speech. The reprint line is the reason the frame survived in continuous print throughout the period when the folio tradition lacked the Prologue, and the reason editors of the early eighteenth century, rebuilding the text from all witnesses, had complete quarto copies at hand when they restored the speech to the collected play.

### Q: How does the Folio print the Act 2 Chorus without its speaker?

The Folio prints the speech as fourteen lines of unattributed verse standing between the end of the feast and the entrance of Romeo at the orchard wall. In the Folio's through-line numbering the sonnet occupies lines 733 to 746, beginning "Now old desire doth in his death bed lie" (2.0.1), but the book gives it no heading, no entrance direction, and no speech prefix naming the Chorus. The words are present with their authority papers removed. A reader confined to the Folio would meet a sonnet spoken by nobody the edition identifies, summarizing a transfer of desire the reader has just watched, and only the quarto tradition supplies the speaker's name. Modern editions restore the attribution from the second quarto, where the speech stands under the Chorus prefix. The Folio's treatment is not loss but demotion: the speech was worth keeping as text and not worth crediting as a role, a distinction that captures the collected edition's priorities exactly.

### Q: Why does the first quarto drop the Act 2 Chorus but keep the Prologue?

The pattern fits both leading accounts of the first quarto, and the agreement is part of the evidence. On the memorial account, a text assembled from actors' recollection keeps what performance made memorable: the Prologue, spoken at every performance as the play's advertisement, lodged in memory well enough to survive in damaged form, while the second Chorus, a single internal summary heard less often and spoken by no character the reporters played, fell away entirely. On the performance account, a version shortened for the stage sheds commentary before story, and the internal Chorus, which advances no event, is the natural first cut, while the Prologue earns its place by selling the play at the door. The two accounts divide over agency, deliberate cutting against imperfect recall, but converge on the underlying fact: in the playhouse channel, frame material was the most perishable cargo a text could carry.

### Q: What happens to the sonnet's rhymes in the first quarto's Prologue?

The rhymes hold in the first quatrain and collapse thereafter, which is the formal signature of the speech's damage. The first quarto manages slant rhymes for its opening four lines, dignity against enmity and scene against unclean, close enough to show the pattern was remembered. In the second and third quatrains the scheme disintegrates: "foes" never receives its answering rhyme, since "overthrows" has been displaced from its answering position, "life" finds "strife" only across the wreck of the lost lines, and "rage" and "stage" reach each other at a slant over the parenthetical patch. The couplet closes on the near rhyme of "attend" and "amend" rather than the second quarto's firm "attend" and "mend." A reviser shortening a sonnet protects its rhymes, because the rhymes carry the music and the mnemonic; a memory losing a sonnet sheds them in exactly this order, landmarks first and architecture last.

### Q: What does the parenthesis in the middle of the first quarto's Prologue mark?

The parenthesis marks the spot where the first quarto's text was repaired from its own debris. After the line "Whose misadventures, piteous overthrows," the quarto encloses two lines, "(Through the continuing of their fathers' strife, / And death-mark'd passage of their parents' rage)," that have no counterpart in that form in the second quarto. The bracketed lines gather material from the passage the text has lost: the parents' strife from the missing eighth line, the word "death-mark'd" from the missing ninth, the parents' rage from the tenth, compressed into a subordinate aside that lets the sentence reach "Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.12) with its grammar intact. Whether the patch was made by a reporting actor, a playhouse adapter, or the printing house, the parenthesis is the visible scar of the sonnet's worst injury, and editors point to it as the place where the first quarto's method, preservation by reconstruction, can be watched in the act.

### Q: How does Lukas Erne's first-quarto edition treat its shortened Prologue?

Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto, published in the New Cambridge Early Quartos series in 2007, prints the shortened Prologue entire and on its own terms, as the opening of a legitimate performance version of the play rather than as a corruption to be corrected against the second quarto. The treatment follows from Erne's larger argument, set out in Shakespeare as Literary Dramatist (2003), that the play circulated in two early states: a long literary text preserved in the second quarto and a short theatrical text preserved in the first. In the edition's presentation, the twelve-line Prologue is evidence to be read, its compressions and its missing second Chorus registering the adaptation's priorities, and the text is annotated rather than emended toward the fuller version. The edition gives scholars and actors a documentary basis for studying the frame as the first audiences plausibly encountered it, while leaving the second quarto's complete sonnet in possession of the standard text.

### Q: What did Harry Hoppe take the first quarto's damaged Prologue to prove?

Harry R. Hoppe, in The Bad Quarto of Romeo and Juliet (1948), took the damaged Prologue as prime evidence that the first quarto is a memorial reconstruction, a text assembled from the recollections of actors who had performed in the play. The Prologue suited his case because its damage has the profile of memory rather than of copying or revision: the gist faithful, the striking words retained, the syntax and rhyme scheme lost, whole lines gone from the middle and patched with their own fragments. A reporter, in Hoppe's account, carried away frame material worst of all, since the Chorus addressed the audience rather than the other actors and offered no cues tied to the reporter's own part. The missing second Chorus completed the exhibit, a whole speech of pure commentary vanished from a text that keeps every movement of the plot. Textual scholars have disputed Hoppe's theory in its general form, but the Prologue remains its most frequently cited specimen.

### Q: How does Jay Halio's reading of the first quarto differ from the memorial account?

Jay L. Halio read the first quarto as an authorial adaptation rather than a stolen report, and the difference rearranges the meaning of every variant in its Prologue. Editing the two quartos in parallel texts (1994) and arguing the case in his essay "Handy-Dandy," Halio proposed that the short text represents the play as Shakespeare and the company revised it for performance, its shortenings deliberate and its alternative readings bearing the authority of second thoughts. On that reading the twelve-line Prologue is a cut made by someone entitled to cut, and the missing second Chorus an excision rather than a forgetting. The present study adjudicates against the adaptation reading on the narrow evidence of the Prologue itself: a reviser trimming a sonnet protects its rhyme scheme in what remains, while the first quarto's version breaks the scheme and patches the break with debris, damage that bears the signature of recall. Halio's larger service, restoring the first quarto to serious study, stands regardless of the verdict on its origin.

### Q: What does Tiffany Stern's account of prologues as separate documents explain?

Tiffany Stern's account explains how a prologue could go missing without theft, error, or doubt about its author. In Documents of Performance in Early Modern England (2009), Stern demonstrated that prologues and epilogues in the period were discrete documents: composed for particular occasions, sometimes by hands other than the playwright's, copied separately, and preserved apart from the playbooks they introduced, which might themselves circulate without them. A speech with that documentary life is detachable by design, present when the occasion and the papers align, absent when either fails. Applied to the tragedy, the account dissolves the assumption that the Folio's omission needs a culprit. The Prologue stood outside the playbook's main line of descent because prologues stood outside playbooks as a class, and the annotated copy behind the Folio simply reflects a documentary world in which the frame was a loose sheet, kept or dropped as the sheet survived. The explanation complements the playhouse account: the theatre cut what the paperwork had never firmly attached.

### Q: Why do editors since Rowe print a Prologue the Folio omitted?

Editors print the Prologue because its credentials, weighed rather than counted, are stronger than the Folio's silence. Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709 set the pattern by opening the play with the sonnet on quarto authority, and no editor of stature since has proposed opening with the street brawl, although the folio line that omits the speech was for centuries the prestige text. The reasoning, made explicit in the modern copy-text tradition, is that the Folio's omission is explained by its copy's preparation and testifies about playhouse practice, while the second quarto's complete text descends from the author's draft and testifies about the play's design. An omission with a mechanical and theatrical explanation carries no authority against a presence with a documentary one. The unanimity matters in itself: three centuries of editors, working from every theoretical position the discipline has held, have treated the Folio's silence as the Folio's defect, and the apparatus of a modern edition records the omission as a fact about 1623 rather than as a doubt about the speech.

### Q: What is the strongest evidence that the two Chorus speeches were designed as a pair?

The strongest evidence is the matching of form, speaker, and station. Both speeches are sonnets in the same English pattern of three quatrains and a couplet. The second quarto assigns both to the same speaker, the Chorus, and no other figure in the play is given a sonnet to speak alone, while the lovers' one sonnet, at the feast, is shared between two voices as if to mark the difference. The two Chorus sonnets stand at the play's two great thresholds, before the feud's first outbreak and before the orchard meeting, and they divide the exposition symmetrically: the first announces the houses, the lovers, and the deaths; the second announces the displacement of Rosaline and the peril of the new love. Occasional pieces hired for single performances do not arrive in matched pairs at symmetrical stations inside a design that elsewhere thinks in sonnets. The pairing is the design's signature, and it anchors the verdict that the frame is the author's architecture rather than the playhouse's furniture.

### Q: Where in the play's architecture does the second Chorus stand, and why did that help it survive in the Folio?

The second Chorus stands inside the play's architecture, at the head of the second act, between the close of the feast and Romeo's entrance at the orchard wall. That position protected it when the Folio's copy was prepared. The preparation regularized the play into the act and scene divisions the quartos lack, and material embedded in the sequence of action had a place in the scheme being imposed, a slot between the acts where the sonnet could be filed and kept. The Prologue stood outside the architecture altogether, before the first act begins, the one component of the copy that belonged to no act and no scene, and a preparation working toward structural regularity had no shelf for it. The Folio's contrary treatment of the two speeches, deleting the external one and retaining the internal one while stripping its prefix, is the strongest internal evidence that the omissions were acts of arrangement rather than judgments on authorship. Position, in the Folio, decided survival.

### Q: How did the later folios treat the Prologue the First Folio had dropped?

The later folios inherited the omission without examining it. The Second Folio of 1632 reprinted the First Folio's text of the play with corrections of detail, and the Third Folio of 1663 and the Fourth Folio of 1685 continued the descent, none restoring the Prologue that the folio line had lacked since 1623. For a century and a half after the First Folio, the collected Works in their folio form opened the tragedy with the serving-men's brawl, and the frame survived only in the quarto tradition and in the memories of the stage. The restoration came from editing rather than from reprinting: when Nicholas Rowe prepared his edition of 1709 from across the witnesses, the Prologue returned to the collected text on quarto authority, and every collected edition since has kept it. The episode is a compact lesson in the difference between a text's descent and its authority. The folio line descended in unbroken succession and was wrong at the play's first page for four generations of volumes.

### Q: How does the Prologue's transmission change what the text of the play means?

The transmission shows that the text of the play is not a single object the early editions copied with varying fidelity, but a settlement among documents that handled different parts of the play by different rules. For the dialogue of the scenes, the witnesses broadly agree, and editing is a matter of choosing among variants. For the frame, the witnesses disagree in kind: one prints the speeches whole, one prints them damaged and partial, one omits and anonymizes. A reader who knows the record reads the standard text differently, aware that its first fourteen lines stand there by an editorial judgment renewed across three centuries, that its second Chorus keeps a speaker's name supplied by one quarto, and that the most prestigious early edition opened the play somewhere else entirely. The meaning of the text widens accordingly. The play includes its own survival story, and the Chorus, which promised to mend whatever the summary missed, turns out to have needed the mending most.
