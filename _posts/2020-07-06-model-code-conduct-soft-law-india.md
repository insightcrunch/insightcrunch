The model code of conduct india candidates argue about in every election began not as law but as a promise parties made to each other in Kerala in 1960, and that origin explains the instrument India still uses. The Model Code of Conduct, called the MCC in the Commission's handbooks, is a written code of election behaviour that no Parliament enacted, no court can directly enforce as a statute, and no candidate may safely ignore. This article defends one namable claim. The MCC operates as soft-law architecture; the limits of its enforcement derive from the soft-law architectural design itself rather than from failures by the officials who administer it. Every controversy over a speech left unpunished, a ministerial announcement left untouched, or a campaign ban that arrived after the damage was done returns to three design choices taken between 1960 and 1991: the Code was codified without being made statutory, it was entrusted to the Election Commission without being made judicially enforceable, and it was made binding through political recognition rather than through legal obligation. Read the document through those three choices and its powers and its frustrations fall into a single pattern.

## A Code Born in Kerala in 1960: The Model Code of Conduct Before the Commission Owned It

The first version of the Code was a Kerala experiment. Ahead of the state assembly election held in February 1960, the Kerala administration convened the parties contesting the poll and secured their agreement to a short code governing how the campaign would be conducted. The setting mattered. Kerala had passed through the dismissal of its elected Communist ministry in July 1959, an event that left campaigning in the state charged with grievance, and the parties that sat down together in 1960 were agreeing, in effect, that the contest for the assembly would be fought inside shared restraints on speeches, processions, and the use of official position. The document they accepted was modest beside the eight part text the Election Commission publishes for a modern general election, yet it contained the germ of the whole later architecture. Restraint was to be mutual. The standard was to be written down in advance. Compliance was to rest on the parties' own assent rather than on a penal clause, because no penal clause existed.

Kerala's experiment travelled because the institution that watched it had reasons of its own to want it. The Election Commission of India, which had conducted the first general election of 1951 to 1952 under Sukumar Sen with an electorate largely new to the ballot, understood that the fairness of an election depended on conduct in the weeks before polling as much as on the count itself. The founding generation of election administration, reconstructed in the account of [the founding-template architecture](/2020/06/08/sukumar-sen-1951-1952-election/), had built procedures for rolls, symbols, and polling stations. Conduct by parties between the announcement of an election and the declaration of results was a different problem, harder to reach through the Representation of the People Act 1951, whose corrupt practices were defined for election petitions after the event. A code agreed by parties in advance, policed by the Commission during the campaign, filled a space the statute book left open. By the general election of 1962 the Commission was circulating a code of conduct to recognised parties and state governments, and the Kerala form had begun its migration into national practice.

The migration was gradual, and the gradualness is part of the evidence for the soft law reading. No single moment converted the Kerala agreement into a national instrument. The Commission issued and reissued the code across the elections of the 1960s, parties were consulted, and the text gathered provisions as experience exposed gaps. In 1968 the Commission formalised the accumulated material into a comprehensive document, the version preserved in the Commission's archives from which the later text descends. Formalisation in 1968 changed the Code's physical character without changing its legal character. It became a single citable document with ordered parts, the ancestor of the eight part structure, yet it remained what the Kerala agreement had been: a set of undertakings administered by an election authority, resting on consent and on the authority of Article 324 of the Constitution rather than on any section of any Act.

### Why did the Code begin as an agreement among parties rather than as a statute?

The Code began as an agreement because the problem it answered was campaign conduct, which required a standard parties would accept before polling rather than a charge contested after results. Kerala's parties in 1960 supplied the consent, and the Commission administered a text whose authority rested on that assent and on its constitutional charge over elections.

The second consolidation came in 1979, and it addressed the actor every election code finds hardest to bind: the government of the day. Revisions issued in that period added and sharpened the restraints on the party in power, the line of provisions that matured into Part VII of the modern text. The timing followed the experience of the Emergency of 1975 to 1977 and the Janata interlude, years that taught election administrators how completely an incumbent ministry could bend official machinery, transfers, announcements, and publicity toward its own re-election. A code that regulated candidates' speeches while leaving ministers free to announce projects, tour at public expense, and combine official duty with campaign travel would have regulated the weaker half of the contest. The 1979 consolidation therefore deserves to be read as the moment the Code's authors identified incumbency itself as an electoral resource to be neutralised, a judgment the document has never since abandoned.

The third consolidation, in 1991, was not a revision of the text so much as a revolution in its administration, and it cannot be separated from T. N. Seshan, who took office as Chief Election Commissioner in December 1990. Seshan read the Commission's powers under Article 324 at their widest, treated the Code as an operative instrument from the moment elections were announced, and enforced its restraints on ministers and parties with a publicity and a severity his predecessors had not attempted. Elections were postponed when the Commission judged that conditions for a free poll did not exist. Officials who bent toward incumbents faced transfer. The period is treated in full in the account of [the assertive-commissioner-architectural-shift](/2020/06/14/tn-seshan-eci-reforms-india/), and its lesson for the Code is precise. Seshan changed what enforcement felt like without changing what the Code was. The document he wielded in 1991 and after was recognisably the document of 1968 and 1979, codified but not statutory, administered by the Commission but not enforceable in court, binding on parties that recognised it. The assertive commissioner worked inside the soft law design. He did not replace it, and the fact that his revolution required no amendment to any statute is the strongest early proof that the design itself, rather than the temperament of its administrator, sets the ceiling on what enforcement can achieve.

## Three Components of a Soft Law Design

The soft law reading organises the Code's character into three components, and each component pairs a power with a limit so tightly that the two cannot be separated. The first component is codification without statutory status. The MCC is a comprehensive written document, organised in eight parts, revised across decades, published by the Commission, and quoted clause by clause in its orders. A candidate in a constituency town can be shown the paragraph a speech is said to breach. That written precision is real power, since an unwritten convention could not be invoked against a minister within hours of an announcement. Yet the document is not a statute. No Act of Parliament contains it, no section creates an offence of breaching it, and no penalty schedule attaches to its clauses. Codification supplies the rule content; the absent statute withholds the legal consequence, and both halves of that sentence describe the same design decision.

The second component is enforcement by the Commission without judicial enforceability. The MCC is no dead letter, because the Election Commission enforces it through an armoury of institutional responses described in a later section of this article: notices demanding explanation, advisories, public censure, orders barring a campaigner from speaking for a fixed period, transfers of officials, postponement of a poll, and in grave cases countermanding an election altogether. Those responses are swift, and swiftness is the quality election enforcement most needs, since a remedy delivered after counting rarely repairs a campaign. The limit sits inside the same component. A breach of the Code, standing alone, is not an offence a court will try and not a corrupt practice a defeated candidate can plead in an election petition unless the same conduct independently violates the Representation of the People Act. The Commission's enforcement is therefore administrative and moral in character even at its most severe, powerful over the campaign calendar and weak in the courtroom, by design rather than by accident.

The third component is binding through political recognition. Parties obey the Code, in the main, because parties accept it. They were consulted in its making from Kerala onward, they receive it afresh at each election, their leaders invoke it against opponents, and the electorate is invited by every Commission order to judge violators. Recognition does the work that legal obligation does in a statutory regime, and for actors who value reputation it works well. The asymmetry is structural. A party or candidate willing to absorb public criticism, calculating that a communally charged speech or a last week inducement will gain more votes than a censure will cost, faces a sanction regime whose heaviest weapons are publicity and delay. The design binds the compliant tightly and the defiant loosely, and no administrator, however assertive, can alter that asymmetry from inside the design, because the asymmetry is the design.

The map below, labelled the Three-Component Soft Law Design Map, sets the three components beside the power each supplies, the limit each carries, and the episodes in which the limit became visible. It is the article's findable artifact, and readers citing the argument may cite the map by that name.

| Component | Power the design supplies | Limit built into the same component | Episode where the limit showed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Codified but not statutory | Written clauses citable against any candidate or minister within hours | No offence, no penalty schedule, no statutory consequence | Manifesto promises before 2013 carried no fiscal discipline clause |
| Enforced by the Commission but not judicially enforceable | Swift notices, censure, campaign bans, postponement, countermand | Breach alone is no ground for a court trial or an election petition | Balaji in 2013 routed manifesto discipline back to the Commission |
| Binding through political recognition | Consent of parties from 1960 onward gives the text accepted authority | The defiant actor can price a censure against expected votes | Campaign bans of April 2019 were absorbed as a cost by senior leaders |

The table repays a slow reading, because each row performs the article's central adjudication in miniature. A descriptive reading of the Code, the kind offered by the standard public administration treatments and by the encyclopaedia entry that dominates search results for the primary query, would list the eight parts and stop. An enforcement effectiveness reading, the kind that organises journalism around the Seshan years and around individual controversies after 2014, would ask whether the Commission acted firmly in a given episode and grade the answer. The design map asks a prior question. It asks what kind of instrument could produce, from the same clauses, both the swift silencing of a chief minister's campaign voice for seventy two hours in April 2019 and the inability of any court to treat the speech that provoked the silence as an offence. The answer is an instrument built exactly as the map describes, and the controversies that fill the newspapers are the design operating, not the design failing.

Scholarship on the Commission's place in the republic supports reading the Code at the level of design. The institution that administers it is the same institution whose wider stresses, from appointment to tenure, are examined in [the institution-under-stress framework](/2020/06/03/election-commission-institution-under-stress/), and the Code is best understood as the point where that institution meets the campaign most directly. E. Sridharan, writing on the institutional architecture of Indian elections across the post-2014 years, treated the Commission's authority as resting on constitutional text, accumulated precedent, and public standing in combination. The Code sits at the junction of all three. Its text is the Commission's own, its precedent runs from 1960, and its standing depends on parties and voters continuing to treat a non-statutory document as the campaign's constitution. A design held together by those three materials will behave differently from a statute, and it does.

## Inside the Eight Parts of the Code

Part I states the general conduct expected of every party and candidate, and its clauses reach further back into the republic's anxieties than their plain wording suggests. No party or candidate may indulge in activity that aggravates differences or creates mutual hatred between castes, communities, religions, or linguistic groups. Criticism of opponents must be confined to policies, programmes, past record, and public work; criticism founded on unverified allegations must be avoided, and the private life of leaders and workers, where it bears no relation to public activity, is placed outside the permitted field of attack. Votes may not be canvassed on grounds of caste or communal feeling. Mosques, churches, temples, and other places of worship may not be used as forums for election propaganda. Each prohibition answers a documented pathology of Indian campaigning, and the first of them, the bar on appeals that inflame communal feeling, carries the heaviest enforcement history of any clause in the document, from the notices of the Seshan years to the campaign bans of April 2019 described below.

Part II governs election meetings, and its method is characteristic of the whole Code. Parties must inform the local police of the venue and time of a meeting in advance, must ascertain whether restrictive orders are in force in the area, and must cooperate with the authorities so that meetings proceed without disorder and traffic moves. Processions receive the same treatment in Part III: organisers must settle routes and timings with the police in advance, keep to one side of the road, obey traffic directions, and ensure that a procession does not obstruct the ordinary movement of the town through which it passes. These parts read as administrative housekeeping, and administrators will say that housekeeping is where elections are quietly won or lost, since a campaign that cannot hold a meeting without a clash is a campaign already departing from the conditions of a free poll. The Code's genius in these parts is to convert courtesy into obligation by the simple device of writing courtesy down and having every party sign up to the page.

Part IV regulates polling day itself. Canvassing within one hundred metres of a polling station is forbidden. No food or drink may be served to voters as an inducement in the station's vicinity, no person may display party symbols at the booth, and the use of vehicles to ferry voters is restricted. Part V governs the polling booth: entry belongs to the voter, to polling personnel, to candidates and their authorised agents, and to persons the law and the Commission's directions admit, and no one else. Part VI provides for observers, the Commission's own eyes inside the process, officers deputed to watch the conduct of the poll and the campaign in a constituency and to report breaches upward through a chain that ends at the Commission in New Delhi. The observer system converts the Code from a text parties promise to honour into a text someone is paid to watch them honour, and the reports observers file are frequently the factual foundation on which notices and censures rest.

Part VII, addressed to the party in power, is the Code's most politically charged section and the one the 1979 consolidation built. Its governing rule is that the party in power, at the centre or in a state, must not use its official position for electioneering. Ministers must not combine official visits with campaign work, government transport and aircraft must not carry campaigners on partisan errands, public money must not fund advertisements that burnish the ruling party's achievements, and rest houses and public grounds must be open to opponents on equal terms. Announcements carry the sharpest edge: after the Code operates, governments must not announce new projects, lay foundation stones, make ad hoc appointments, or grant largesse designed to influence voters, and transfers of officials engaged in election work fall under the Commission's control. The rationale, stated plainly in Commission guidance across decades, is that an election in which the incumbent campaigns with the state's machinery in hand is not a contest between parties but a contest between a party and a government, and the Code's seventh part exists to narrow that gap for the weeks of the poll.

### What does the bar on ministerial announcements actually prevent?

The bar prevents a government, once elections are announced, from unveiling new projects, laying foundation stones, promising financial grants, or making ad hoc appointments that could sway voters. Routine administration and work already begun continue, and the line the Commission polices is the line between governing a state and purchasing an advantage in its election.

Part VIII, on election manifestos, is the youngest part of the document and the only one a court directly caused to be written. Its origin lies in the Supreme Court judgment of 5 July 2013 in S. Subramaniam Balaji v State of Tamil Nadu, examined by holding in the next section. Acting on the Court's direction that guidelines be framed for manifesto content, the Commission consulted the parties and added Part VIII, which requires that a manifesto not promise anything repugnant to the Constitution or the Code, that it indicate broadly the rationale for each promise, and that it show the ways and means of financing what it pledges. The part's arrival in the document is a perfect specimen of the soft law mechanism at work. A court identified a gap, declined to fill it by declaring promises illegal, and routed the task to the Commission, which filled it by amending a non-statutory code after consulting the very parties the amendment would bind. Every stage of that sequence is explicable by the design map and by nothing else.

The eight parts, taken together, describe a complete campaign from the first speech to the last promise, and their completeness matters to the argument. Critics of the Code sometimes write as though its limits flowed from gaps in coverage, clauses that a diligent reviser could add. The document's history refuses that diagnosis. Coverage has widened at every consolidation, from Kerala's short agreement to the 1968 text, the party in power provisions of 1979, and the manifesto part of 2013. Fresh clauses were added each time a fresh abuse appeared, and still the enforcement controversies recurred in the same shapes, because the constraint was never the inventory of clauses. The constraint was the kind of instrument the clauses lived in. A code can forbid everything and still bind only through recognition, still punish only through administrative response, still stand outside the statute book. The eight parts are therefore best read not as an unfinished statute but as a finished specimen of a different species of rule.


## What the Courts Have Held: Authority Without Enforceability

The Code's legal status has been stated by the courts with a consistency that leaves little room for honest confusion, and the holdings repay statement one by one. The foundation is Article 324 of the Constitution, which vests the superintendence, direction, and control of elections in the Election Commission. In Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, decided in 1978, the Supreme Court read Article 324 as a reservoir of power, holding that the Commission may act in areas the enacted law leaves unoccupied, provided its action does not contradict statute and respects natural justice. That holding is the constitutional ground on which Code enforcement stands. When the Commission issues a notice under the Code, it does not point to a section creating the offence. It points to Article 324, to its charge over the conduct of elections, and to the parties' own acceptance of the document, a chain of authority the Gill judgment made respectable and which no later bench has broken.

The same body of case law fixes the other boundary. A violation of the Code is not, by that fact alone, a corrupt practice under section 123 of the Representation of the People Act 1951, and it cannot by itself sustain an election petition under section 100 of that Act. Conduct that breaches the Code may also breach the statute, as when an appeal to religion in a campaign speech violates both Part I of the Code and section 123(3) of the Act, and in such a case the statutory consequence follows from the statute, not from the Code. Where conduct breaches the Code alone, the remedy lies with the Commission during the campaign and with the voter's judgment at the poll. Courts asked to convert Code breaches into legal invalidity have declined the conversion, and the declinature is not judicial timidity. It is the judicial recognition of what the instrument is. The parties made a compact, the Commission administers the compact, and the compact's sanctions are the sanctions its design contains.

The Balaji judgment of 5 July 2013 is the richest single authority, because the Supreme Court there confronted the Code's character while deciding a case about free gifts promised in manifestos. The petitioner had challenged the distribution of freebies by governments in Tamil Nadu and the promises that preceded distribution, arguing that such promises corrupted the electoral process. The Court held, first, that a promise in an election manifesto does not constitute a corrupt practice under section 123 of the Representation of the People Act, since the section's language addresses the conduct of candidates and agents and a manifesto promise, without more, falls outside it. The Court held, second, that it could not legislate a new disqualification or a new offence from the bench, however strongly the practice might be regretted. It held, third, that the Election Commission should frame guidelines on manifesto content, acting in consultation with recognised parties, and it is that third holding which produced Part VIII of the Code. A judgment that refused to make law thus enlarged a document that is not law, and the sequence only makes sense inside the soft law frame. The Court treated the Code as the constitutionally appropriate place for a standard that needed speed, consent, and revision more than it needed a penalty.

### Is a breach of the Code by itself enough to unseat an elected candidate?

A breach by itself is not enough. An election can be voided only on grounds the Representation of the People Act supplies, and a Code violation enters that list only when the conduct independently amounts to a corrupt practice under the Act. The Code's consequences run through the Commission during the campaign: censure, campaign bans, and the judgment of voters.

The manifesto guidelines episode also exposed the design's seams to anyone watching closely. Parties consulted on Part VIII accepted language requiring a manifesto to indicate the rationale of promises and the means of financing them, language of real discipline if honoured. Yet the part carries no audit, no verification, and no consequence beyond Commission comment, because none could be attached without statute. M. R. Madhavan, analysing the Code's architecture for PRS Legislative Research across several election cycles, returned repeatedly to this junction: the standards the Code states are frequently the standards reformers want, while the machinery for giving those standards legal effect is the machinery the Code was built to do without. Madhavan's analyses are valuable precisely because they resist the temptation to grade the Commission's will. The recurring gap between the Code's text and the Code's effect is a property of the instrument Madhavan describes, visible in election after election regardless of who occupies Nirvachan Sadan.

A further holding deserves record because it disciplines the Commission as firmly as the Code disciplines parties. In the line of cases following Gill, the courts have insisted that Article 324 power, wide as it is, must be exercised in conformity with law, with fairness, and with the rights of candidates to be heard. The Commission enforcing the Code must issue notice, must consider the explanation offered, and must speak through a reasoned order when it acts severely. This procedural discipline is a strength of the system, since power exercised by an unelected body over a live campaign invites scrutiny, yet it also consumes the one resource campaign enforcement cannot spare, which is time. A statutory offence tried after the election can afford deliberation. A censure that must land before polling day cannot, and the procedural obligations the courts rightly impose press hardest on the swiftest sanctions in the Commission's armoury. Design, again, and not dereliction, explains the friction.

## The Enforcement Record: Notice, Censure, Silence, and the Countermand

The Commission's responses to breaches form a ladder, and the rungs are worth climbing slowly because the ladder as a whole is the second component of the design made visible. The lowest rung is the notice: the Commission, acting on a complaint or an observer's report or its own monitoring of speeches and advertisements, calls on a party or candidate to explain conduct said to breach a clause. Many notices end there, with an explanation accepted or an advisory issued, and the file closed without public drama. The next rung is the advisory or warning placed on record, a formal statement that conduct fell short. Above it stands censure, the Commission's public condemnation of a named leader or party, recorded in an order that the press carries and opponents quote. Censure punishes through reputation alone, which is why its severity varies so strangely with the standing of the censured: a reprimand that would end a lesser career can be worn by a major leader as proof of combativeness, and the Commission knows it.

The rung added in assertive practice is the campaign ban, an order forbidding a leader from addressing public meetings or speaking to the media for a stated period, typically counted in hours or days. The general election of 2019 supplied the canonical instances. In April 2019 the Commission, acting on speeches in Uttar Pradesh, barred Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath from campaigning for seventy two hours and Bahujan Samaj Party leader Mayawati for forty eight hours, after both made appeals the Commission held to breach the Code's bar on communal appeals and on appeals in the name of caste and community. In the same week Azam Khan of the Samajwadi Party received a seventy two hour ban for remarks against a rival candidate, and Maneka Gandhi received a forty eight hour ban for a speech in Sultanpur. The orders were swift by the standards of Indian administration, arriving within days of the speeches, and they rested on no statute. They rested on Article 324, on the Code the leaders' parties had accepted, and on the Commission's judgment that silence for a fixed span was proportionate. Their limits were equally visible. The bans expired before polling in the constituencies concerned had closed, the speeches had already travelled through every phone in the state, and each leader's camp presented the order as evidence that the leader had spoken truths the system feared. The ladder's most dramatic rung thus demonstrated the design's truth from the top: enforcement can be real, rapid, and constitutionally grounded, and still remain a cost that a determined campaigner can pay.

Above the ban stand the sanctions that touch the election itself. The Commission may postpone a poll where conditions make a free election impossible, a power the Seshan years made familiar when elections in disturbed areas were deferred until security permitted, and it may countermand an election where the process has been vitiated beyond repair. Two countermands frame the modern record. In April 2017 the Commission countermanded the by-election to the R. K. Nagar assembly constituency in Chennai after evidence of systematic bribery of voters on a scale the Commission held had destroyed the purity of the poll. In April 2019 it rescinded the election to the Vellore Lok Sabha constituency in Tamil Nadu after income tax searches recovered large sums of cash the Commission concluded were meant for distribution to voters, the first occasion on which a parliamentary election was countermanded on grounds of money power. Both orders are landmarks of administrative courage. Both also confess the design's boundary, because a countermand punishes every honest voter and every clean candidate in the constituency for abuses the Code's ordinary sanctions could not deter in advance. The heaviest weapon in the armoury is a weapon against the election itself, and a system driven to use it has already recorded the failure of every lighter rung.

### Why did the Commission countermand the Vellore parliamentary election in 2019?

The Commission rescinded the Vellore election in April 2019 after searches recovered cash on a scale that persuaded it the poll had been vitiated by money power. Countermanding was the one response proportionate to a constituency wide abuse, and the order stands as the first rescinding of a parliamentary election on that ground in the Commission's record.

Between the rungs of the ladder sits the quiet machinery that handles most breaches without headlines. Officials found too close to a ruling party are transferred out of election duty on the Commission's orders, a sanction the party in power provisions of Part VII make routine and which state governments resent precisely because it works. Advertisements issued by governments are scrutinised, and publicity material that crosses into party promotion is ordered withdrawn or charged against the party's account in the Commission's estimation. Announcements scheduled for the campaign window are stopped, foundation stones are postponed, and inaugurations wait for the count. A researcher reading only the Commission's press releases would conclude that the Code governs a large territory of administrative behaviour quite effectively, and the conclusion would be fair. The enforcement record is not a record of impotence. It is a record of an instrument strong across the wide middle of compliance, where officials and parties mostly wish to be seen obeying, and weakest at the hard edge where a campaign calculates that breach pays. That profile, strong in the middle and weak at the edge, is the signature of soft law everywhere it is used, from international undertakings to professional codes, and the Indian election code displays it in textbook form.

The Seshan complication must be faced squarely, because it is the strongest counter to the design reading. Between 1991 and 1996 the Commission's enforcement acquired a ferocity that seemed to refute any claim about built in limits. Ministers were hauled up for announcements, elections were deferred, the identity card programme was driven through state resistance, and the Code's restraints bit into the behaviour of governments that had treated earlier versions as wallpaper. If temperament could achieve all that, a critic asks, why blame design for later softness? The answer lies in what the Seshan record actually changed. Seshan altered the credibility of the sanctions, not their legal nature. The notices he issued derived from the same Article 324, the censures he pronounced carried the same absence of statutory consequence, and the parties he disciplined remained free, in strict law, to treat a breach as no offence. What he supplied was certainty of response and indifference to rank, qualities that raised the reputational price of breach until compliance became the cheaper strategy. When later Commissions responded more slowly or more selectively, the price fell and breach returned, without a single clause of the Code having changed. A design whose effectiveness swings that widely with the personality of its administrator is not refuted by the swing. The swing is the evidence.


## The Statutory Question: Goswami, the Law Commission, and the Standing Committee

If the Code's limits are designed in, the obvious remedy is to redesign it as law, and Indian reform bodies have debated exactly that remedy for three decades. The debate is the best external check on this article's claim, because every participant in it, on every side, ended up describing the same design features and disagreeing only about whether statute would cure or kill them. The Committee on Electoral Reforms chaired by Dinesh Goswami, which reported in 1990, considered the Code among a wide field of reforms and favoured giving its core provisions legal backing, reasoning that a standard important enough to govern every campaign should not depend for its force on the accident of administrative will. The Goswami recommendations arrived just before the Seshan transformation, and the sequence is instructive: Parliament did not enact the statutory proposal, yet enforcement hardened anyway under a determined commissioner, which taught one generation of reformers that law might be unnecessary and taught the next that personality is a frail substitute for law.

The Law Commission returned to the electoral field in its 170th Report on Reform of the Electoral Laws in 1999 and again in its 255th Report on Electoral Reforms in March 2015, the latter chaired by Justice A. P. Shah. The Commission's treatment of the Code across those reports is careful rather than enthusiastic. Statutory force would convert breaches into litigable wrongs, and litigation in Indian conditions means delay, interim orders, and campaigns fought under the shadow of courts asked to rule on speeches while voting continues. The 255th Report's wider programme concentrated on disqualification, party regulation, and the cleansing of candidate selection, fields where legal consequence attaches after due process. On the Code itself the reform literature's centre of gravity settled into a hesitation: the document's speed and flexibility are functions of its non-statutory character, and a Parliament that codified it clause by clause would freeze a text that the Commission has been able to revise, as with Part VIII in 2013, within a single election cycle and with the parties in the room.

The parliamentary standing committee forum produced the clearest statement of the other side. The Department Related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice, in its work on electoral reforms in 2013, recommended that the Model Code be given statutory status, taking the view that the Commission's moral authority, however distinguished its history, was an insufficient foundation for restraints that could silence campaigners and stop governments from announcing policy. The recommendation deserves its full strength. A democracy should be uneasy when an unelected body restricts political speech, even briefly, on the authority of a document no legislature passed. The standing committee's position speaks for the rule of law instinct that coercive power should trace to enacted law, and any honest account of the debate must record that instinct as principled rather than partisan, since members across parties have voiced it in different seasons, usually when their own side has felt a censure's sting.

Against the standing committee's recommendation stands the Commission's own settled position, repeated to successive committees and governments: the Code should not be made statutory. The Commission's reasoning is practical and has never much varied. Elections run on a calendar measured in weeks. A breach on a Monday must be answered before the poll on the following Sunday, and a statutory regime would move the answer into courtrooms where the calendar belongs to litigation, not to the returning officer. The Commission also guards the Code's revisability, its capacity to absorb a new abuse, a new medium, or a new judicial direction without waiting for a parliamentary session and a drafting cycle. Observers of the institution, including Pratap Bhanu Mehta in essays on the Commission's post-2014 trajectory, have noted the paradox this creates. The Commission defends its least legal instrument as its most effective one, and defends it against the very legislatures whose members complain loudest when the instrument is used against them and demand its legalisation mainly in the intervals between elections.

### Which reform bodies proposed giving the Code the force of law?

The Dinesh Goswami Committee of 1990 favoured legal backing for the Code's core, and the parliamentary standing committee on law and justice recommended statutory status in 2013. The Law Commission's reports of 1999 and 2015 treated the proposal with caution, and the Election Commission itself has consistently opposed conversion, defending speed and revisability as the Code's working virtues.

The debate's deepest level is rarely reached in committee rooms, and reaching it is this article's contribution to the argument. Statute would not merely strengthen the Code; it would change the species of the instrument. A statutory code binds through obligation, is interpreted by courts, accumulates precedent clause by clause, and visits breach with defined legal consequence after process. A soft code binds through recognition, is administered by a constitutional authority in real time, accumulates precedent order by order, and visits breach with reputational and administrative consequence inside the campaign. India's parties, administrators, and judges have, across sixty years, repeatedly chosen the second species at the moment of decision while praising the first in the abstract. The Goswami proposal was not enacted. The standing committee recommendation of 2013 was not enacted. The manifesto problem of 2013 was routed by the Supreme Court itself into the soft instrument rather than into a new offence. The pattern of choices is too consistent to be drift. It records a settled, if seldom articulated, judgment by the republic's institutions that campaign conduct is a field where a living compact administered swiftly serves elections better than a litigated code, and that the price of the compact, its weakness against the defiant, is a price the institutions keep deciding to pay.

That judgment connects the Code to the longest arc of the series this article belongs to. The republic's founders, working in the aftermath of [the post-1951 first general election context](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/), built electoral administration on a constitutional commission armed with plenary power and trusted to grow its own instruments. The Code is the purest product of that trust: an instrument the Constitution never mentions, the Commission grew from a Kerala compact, and the courts sustained through Article 324 while refusing to mistake it for law. Read against [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), the Code is a standing negotiation between the state's need for a level field and the campaign's need for free speech, between the centre's single standard and the states' varied conditions, between order and contest. Soft law is the form that negotiation takes when none of its parties will grant the others a final legal victory.

## Stress Tests After 2014: The Design Meets the Modern Campaign

The years after 2014 did not amend the Code, yet they tested every seam in its design, and the tests are best read as experiments the design ran on itself. Campaigning moved decisively into media the document's drafters had not imagined: round the clock television owned by interested houses, social platforms where a speech fragment travels faster than any notice, and publicity machinery in which the line between government communication and party propaganda thins to transparency. The Commission's responses stayed inside the inherited instruments. It ordered, it censured, it banned for measured hours, it stopped announcements, and in two celebrated instances of 2019 it reached for the postponement power in cultural dress, deferring the release of a biographical film about the Prime Minister and restraining a television channel devoted to a leader's image until polling closed. Each response was defensible under Article 324 and the Code. Each also arrived after the material had circulated, which is the design's recurring signature in a faster information order.

The 2019 general election concentrated the stresses into a single season and deserves treatment as the design's fullest modern examination. The Commission's orders against senior leaders in April 2019, described above, were accompanied by controversy over the pace and even handedness of its decisions, including dissent within the Commission itself that became public when one Election Commissioner recorded disagreement with the majority's handling of complaints against the Prime Minister's speeches. The episode matters here for a structural reason that partisans on both sides missed. A soft instrument administered by a multi member body will display its administrators' disagreements in public, because no statute and no precedent library disciplines their discretion into uniformity. Critics who demanded firmer action and critics who alleged partisan softness were, without naming it, demanding that the Code behave like law. The Commission's defenders, pointing to the orders it did issue within days, were defending the proposition that a compact administered at campaign speed will always carry a margin of contested judgment, and that the margin is the cost of the speed. Both descriptions fit the design map. Neither requires a story of institutional collapse or of institutional capture, and the scholarly treatments that have aged best, including E. Sridharan's essays on institutional stress in the period, are those that kept the analysis at the level of architecture rather than motive.

Transfers and announcements supplied the season's quieter data. State officials were moved on the Commission's directions in several states, governments were restrained from launching schemes and from publicising achievements in terms the Commission judged partisan, and the machinery of Part VII operated at a scale that would have satisfied a statutory draftsman. The pattern across the whole season therefore repeats the finding of the enforcement section: strength across the wide middle, contest at the hard edge. Governments complied with the transfers because compliance cost little and defiance would have advertised guilt. Campaigners absorbed the bans because the speeches had already done their work. The Code governed everything except the calculations it was never designed to reach, the calculations in which a breach is a strategy rather than a lapse.

A later companion study in this series takes up the post-2014 application controversies one by one; the present article's narrower point is that none of those controversies produced, or could have produced, a modification of the design itself. The document that governed the election of 2019 was, in its legal nature, the document of 1968 as consolidated in 1979 and enlarged in 2013: codified, Commission enforced, recognition bound. Controversy tested the administrators and the parties. It left the architecture untouched, because architecture changes only when a court rewrites a holding, a Parliament enacts a statute, or the Commission and the parties together renegotiate the compact, and none of those three events occurred. Students who wish to keep the primary record of these debates organised for revision will find that a plain presentation file, kept in a tool such as [the PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), holds the sequence of orders, bans, and countermands in the order the Commission issued them, which is the order in which the design revealed itself.

## Adjudicating the Three Readings

Three readings of the Code compete in the literature, and the article's claim requires a verdict among them. The descriptive reading, strongest in the standard administrative treatments and the encyclopaedia tradition, presents the Code as a list: eight parts, their clauses, the dates of their adoption. Its virtue is accuracy about content; its failure is that content alone explains nothing about effect, and a reader armed only with the list cannot say why identical clauses slept under one commissioner and struck under another. The enforcement effectiveness reading, strongest in journalism and in memoir, grades the administrators: Seshan the titan, successors measured against his shadow, each controversy a report card. Its virtue is narrative force; its failure is that it mistakes the variable for the constant, attributing to character what belongs to structure, and it cannot explain why the titan himself worked entirely within the soft design or why his powers evaporated in court whenever a Code breach was asked to carry legal weight.

The soft law reading defended here absorbs the virtues of both rivals and answers the questions they cannot. From the descriptive reading it takes the text seriously, clause by clause and consolidation by consolidation, because design lives in documents. From the effectiveness reading it takes the administrators seriously, because a compact administered by persons will always bear their impress. It then insists that the pattern connecting text, administrators, and outcomes is the three component design: codified without statute, enforced without judicial enforceability, binding without obligation. Every major episode in the Code's sixty years fits the pattern without forcing. Kerala explains the third component. The 1968 formalisation explains the first. The 1979 consolidation explains the design's reach toward incumbency. Seshan explains how far credibility can stretch the second component and no further. Balaji explains a court choosing the compact over the statute book with its eyes open. The bans and countermands of 2019 explain the ladder's top and its limits in a single season. A reading that orders that much evidence with three components has earned its place against readings that order less with more.

Tenure and appointment complete the frame, because a soft instrument is only as steady as the body that administers it. The questions of how commissioners are chosen and protected, examined in [the architectural-asymmetry-question framework](/2020/06/30/tenure-security-election-commission/), therefore bear directly on the Code's future effectiveness even though they touch not one of its clauses. A Commission whose members hold secure tenure can price defiance high; a Commission perceived as beholden will find its censures discounted before they are issued, and no revision of the eight parts can compensate. Here too the design reading outperforms its rivals, since it predicts in advance where reform energy would actually change outcomes: not in fresh clauses, of which the document has plenty, but in the credibility of the administering institution and in the parties' continuing recognition, the two materials of which the compact is made.

Reform, on this reading, has exactly three honest forms, and the article closes its argument by naming them. The first is statutory conversion, the Goswami and standing committee road, which would trade speed and flexibility for obligation and precedent; it remains available to any Parliament willing to accept campaigns conducted partly in courtrooms. The second is compact renewal, the road of 1968, 1979, and 2013, in which the Commission and the parties revise the document together for the media and money of a new age while accepting the design's limits as the price of its virtues. The third is institutional strengthening without textual change, the road of secure tenure, transparent appointment, and swift published orders, which raises the price of defiance while leaving every clause where it stands. What reform cannot honestly promise is a fourth road on which the Code keeps its speed, its flexibility, and its freedom from litigation while acquiring a statute's teeth. The teeth and the freedom are alternatives. Sixty years of Indian practice, read carefully, is the record of a republic that understood the choice even when it declined to state it, and the Model Code of Conduct, soft law from its first Kerala page to its manifesto part, is where that understanding is written down.


## The Code in the States: One Text, Many Campaigns

The Code is a single national document, yet it is lived in state capitals and district towns, and its state level operation reveals features the national controversies conceal. When the Commission announces elections to a state assembly, the Code operates in that state from the hour of announcement, and the government of the state passes, for election purposes, under a discipline administered from New Delhi through the chief electoral officer and the district machinery. Ministers in the state discover that files they commanded the previous week move under restraint: no new scheme may be announced, no transfer of a district officer with election duties may be made without the Commission's concurrence, and the chief minister's tour programme is read in the Commission's offices with a campaign auditor's eye. State politicians across parties describe the onset of the Code in a phrase administrators repeat with approval, that the government goes into the Commission's custody for the season. The phrase is affectionate and exact. Custody is not ownership, and the state's government continues to govern, to run hospitals and police stations and treasuries, while the uses of government that touch the contest pass under external supervision.

Kerala's place in this geography is more than sentimental. The state that drafted the first compact in 1960 has remained a place where the Code's restraints are treated as civic furniture, quoted by parties against each other in assembly campaigns with a fluency that suggests long habituation. The alternation politics of the state, in which incumbent fronts are regularly voted out and return later, gives every party experience of the Code from both sides of the ministerial chair, and that double experience is the surest school of recognition binding. A party that has suffered under Part VII as an incumbent and benefited from it as an opposition has learned the compact's value in its own record, and such parties defend the document even while protesting individual orders. The design's third component is visible here in its healthiest form, and it suggests why the Code has survived changes of government at the centre without a single ruling formation attempting its abolition. Each formation expects to need the Code's protection in opposition, and the expectation disciplines the temptation to dismantle it in office.

The district level supplies the Code's daily texture. The returning officer and the district magistrate become, for the season, the document's local voice. Flying squads and static surveillance teams, instituted in their modern form to intercept the movement of cash, liquor, and goods meant for voters, operate under the expenditure monitoring machinery that grew beside the Code and draws authority from the same Article 324 root. Their seizures are announced in daily figures during a general election, crores of rupees in cash, liquor by the lakh of litres, narcotics, precious metals, and free goods, a ledger of attempted inducement that no earlier era of Indian elections ever quantified. The ledger matters to the design argument in a way commentators miss. Seizure is an administrative act, swift and reversible on proof of innocence, perfectly suited to a soft enforcement regime. Prosecution of the same conduct as an offence would be slow, contested, and largely posthumous to the campaign. The Commission's preference for interception over prosecution, repeated at industrial scale in every recent general election, is the second component of the design operating as a philosophy of enforcement: act inside the campaign window, by administrative means, under a compact the parties accepted, and let the criminal law handle its narrower categories in its own time.

State elections also expose the Code's dependence on the quality of local observation. A general observer sent to a constituency files reports that travel upward; an expenditure observer shadows the money; a police observer watches the force. Where observers are diligent, the documentary record of a breach exists by evening and a notice can issue while the speech still echoes. Where observers are lax or captured by local hospitality, the same breach leaves no trace but a rival party's complaint, which the Commission must then verify through the very machinery whose failure occasioned the complaint. The Code's text makes no distinction between these two constituencies. Its operation differs completely between them. Here is a limit the design map predicts without naming districts: an instrument enforced administratively inherits the unevenness of administration itself, and the unevenness of Indian district administration, famous in every other field of governance, does not suspend itself for elections.

## Money, Inducement, and the Limits of a Conduct Code

No account of the Code is honest that treats speeches as its main battlefield. The deeper contest of the modern Indian campaign is fought with money, and the Code's provisions on inducement, read with the expenditure machinery, mark both its reach and its boundary. Part I forbids bribery and intimidation in the general language of conduct, and the Representation of the People Act makes bribery a corrupt practice and, in its penal provisions, an offence. Between the Code's generality and the statute's severity sits the practical problem of the campaign's final week, when cash moves at night, through hands that keep no records, to voters whose names appear on no receipt. The Commission's answer, built across the elections after 2010, was to saturate the constituency with observation: squads on the roads, teams at the checkpoints, observers behind the candidates' own accounts, and a complaint apparatus that invited citizens to report distribution as it happened. The Vellore countermand of 2019 and the R. K. Nagar countermand of 2017 stand at the end of that road, the points where observation documented inducement so extensive that the Commission declared the poll itself compromised.

The countermands repay attention as design evidence, because they show the system choosing its own most drastic remedy rather than any legal remedy against the bribers. In neither case did the decisive consequence fall on identified individuals through prosecution. The consequence fell on the election: it was cancelled and ordered afresh. A legalistic reading calls this a failure of enforcement against persons. The design reading calls it the soft regime's logic carried to its conclusion. The Commission's charge is the purity of the poll, not the punishment of the purchaser, and where purity is gone the poll is the thing it can still save. The voters of Vellore, denied their April poll and called again later, paid the price of that logic in inconvenience and in a campaign season doubled. Candidates who had kept the rules paid it in money and exhaustion. The episode therefore belongs in any honest ledger of the Code's costs as well as its powers, and it sharpens the article's claim rather than blunting it. An instrument whose ultimate sanction is the cancellation of the election it exists to protect is an instrument confessing, at its limit, that its lighter sanctions are reputational and administrative, and that against inducement organised as a system those sanctions may not suffice. The confession is structural. It would survive any change of commissioners.

The manifesto part of the Code touches money from the opposite direction, at the level of promises rather than payments. The Balaji litigation arose from Tamil Nadu's competitive distribution of consumer goods by governments formed from rival parties, each honouring manifesto pledges of televisions, mixers, grinders, laptops, and cash assistance. The Supreme Court's refusal to call such pledges corrupt practices, and its direction that the Commission frame manifesto guidelines, placed the discipline of fiscal promising inside the Code and therefore inside the compact system. Part VIII's requirements, that promises carry a rationale and an account of financing, are honoured unevenly by manifestos that grow longer and more generous with each cycle, and the Commission possesses no audit by which to test a financing claim. Parties across the spectrum defend generous pledges as welfare policy and attack rivals' pledges as fiscal recklessness, a debate conducted in the open and settled by voters, which is arguably where the Constitution intends it to be settled. The design reading does not require a verdict on freebies. It requires only the observation that the republic, confronted in 2013 with a demand to discipline promises by law, chose through its highest court to discipline them by compact instead, and that the choice was of a piece with every earlier choice in the Code's history.

## Part VII Read Closely: The Incumbency Clauses in Operation

Because Part VII carries the Code's heaviest political load, its clauses deserve the close reading usually reserved for statutes. The part opens with the principle that the party in power shall not use its official position for campaign purposes, and then enumerates. Government transport, including aircraft and vehicles, shall not be used for the furtherance of the party in power's interests. Official visits by ministers shall not be combined with electioneering work, and where a minister travels on duty the campaign must be separately organised and separately financed. Public places such as maidans, and facilities such as government rest houses, shall be available to rival parties on the same terms, and no monopolisation by the incumbent is permitted. Issue of advertisements at public cost, in newspapers and electronic media, that publicise the achievements of the party in power is forbidden for the season. Ministers shall not announce financial grants, lay foundation stones of projects, or make promises of construction and provision of facilities. Appointments and transfers that may influence the poll are restrained, and the Commission's directions govern officials connected with election work.

Each clause has an operational history. The advertisements clause has required the Commission, in election after election, to order the removal or covering of hoardings displaying a chief minister's portrait beside a scheme's name, and to rule on whether a government campaign about a welfare programme is information or promotion. The foundation stone clause has postponed inaugurations of completed projects whose opening ceremonies would have served as campaign theatre, a small absurdity (a finished bridge waiting for a count) that voters largely accept as the price of fairness. The transport clause has produced rulings on the use of official aircraft by star campaigners holding office, distinctions between the security a chief minister must carry and the convenience a campaigner must fund, distinctions that read like pedantry until one recalls that every earlier republic that let incumbents campaign from the state's vehicles slid toward elections of unequal means. The announcements clause has frozen recruitment processes and scheme launches for weeks, imposing a real cost on administration, and the cost is itself evidence: a purely decorative code would freeze nothing.

The party in power clauses also generate the Code's most delicate judgments, those concerning the head of government who is simultaneously the ruling party's chief campaigner. Indian practice expects a prime minister or chief minister to campaign, and the Code does not forbid it; it separates the offices for the season, requiring that the campaigner travel as a party leader when speaking as one and reserving official machinery for official duty. Speeches by such leaders attract complaints under Part I when their content touches community or caste, and the Commission's handling of complaints against the highest offices became, in 2019, the most contested aspect of its season, with the public dissent of a commissioner marking the strain. The design reading places that strain correctly. A compact policed by an appointed body against the elected head of a government will generate legitimacy questions that no drafting can remove, because the body judges the very executive under which it serves. Statutory conversion would not remove the questions; it would relocate them to courtrooms during the count. The republic has so far preferred its strain in the Commission's open orders, where disagreement can be recorded, published, and argued about, to strain inside litigation. That preference, too, is a design choice, renewed each season by the absence of any enacted alternative.


## Soft Law Beyond Elections: Why the Form Fits the Field

The Code's species has relatives, and placing it among them clarifies why the form suits the field. International law knows instruments that bind states by signature and reputation without a sheriff: declarations, guidelines, and codes of practice whose breach costs standing rather than liberty. Professional life knows codes of medical and legal ethics whose sanctions run through councils and reputation. Financial regulation knows guidance that firms obey as if it were law because the regulator's displeasure is itself a sanction. In each field the soft form is chosen where the regulated activity moves faster than legislation, where the regulated parties' consent is the practical source of compliance, and where the sanction of publicity is swift enough to matter. Election campaigning satisfies all three conditions more completely than any of those fields. A campaign lives for weeks. Its actors are repeat players who will face the same regulator and the same rivals at the next poll. Its currency is public standing, the one currency a censure directly debases. The Code's designers in 1960 and its consolidators in 1968 and 1979 were not failing to write a statute. They were selecting, perhaps without the vocabulary, the regulatory form the field rewards.

The vocabulary arrived later in Indian scholarship, and the sequence of arrival is worth noting. Writers on the Commission in the decades after Seshan tended to describe the Code through personality and episode. The institutional turn in the literature, visible in the work of E. Sridharan on the architecture of democratic institutions and in M. R. Madhavan's patient clause level analyses for PRS Legislative Research, reframed the question from who enforced to what was being enforced. Pratap Bhanu Mehta's essays on the Commission after 2014 added the necessary warning from the other direction: an institution resting on convention and standing can be weakened without any formal change, by appointments that discount its authority and by public attacks that lower the price of defying it. Mehta's warning and the design reading converge. If the Code's force is recognition, then the politics of recognition, who is appointed, whose orders are obeyed, whose censures sting, is the Code's real constitutional politics, and it deserves the attention scholars usually reserve for amendment debates.

Comparative practice strengthens the point, though comparison must be handled with restraint. The United Kingdom regulates campaign conduct through statute administered by an electoral commission with limited sanctioning speed, and British campaigns consequently litigate and negotiate inside legal categories. The United States, with its constitutional protection of political speech at a breadth Indian law does not attempt, leaves most campaign conduct to disclosure and to the courts after the fact. India's arrangement, a constitutional commission wielding a consensual code at campaign speed, is genuinely distinctive, and its distinctiveness explains a fact that puzzles foreign observers: that Indian elections, for all their noise and money, observe a visible discipline about the use of state machinery that many older democracies envy. Foreign observer missions have repeatedly remarked on the quiet that falls on governmental publicity when the Code operates. That quiet is Part VII working, and Part VII works without being law. A complete theory of the Code must explain the quiet as readily as it explains the breaches, and the soft law reading is the only one of the three that does, because it predicts strength exactly where recognition and administrative swiftness are enough, and weakness exactly where they are not.

## Amendment by Accumulation: How a Non-Statute Grows

A statute grows by amendment acts, each dated and numbered. The Code has grown by accumulation, and its growth pattern is itself evidence about its nature. The Commission's archives preserve the succession of texts: the Kerala compact of 1960, the circulated codes of the 1960s, the formalised document of 1968, the revisions that produced the party in power restraints consolidated by 1979, the enforcement practice of the 1990s that hardened interpretation without altering many words, and the manifesto part added in 2013 after the Balaji direction. Between these landmarks lie smaller accretions: clauses on the misuse of official media, directions on the display of photographs of living leaders in government advertisements, clarifications on the use of social media by candidates and parties, instructions on the silence period before polling and on exit poll publication that draw on adjacent statutes. Each accretion followed the same constitutional path. An abuse appeared, or a court spoke, or a medium changed; the Commission consulted; the text or its authoritative interpretation grew. No accretion required a parliamentary majority, a presidential assent, or a gazette notification of commencement, and none could have survived the delay those steps would have imposed between one election and the next.

The growth pattern also explains the document's uneven texture, which descriptive treatments sometimes mock. Parts I to VI carry the cadence of administrative guidance refined across decades of district practice. Part VII carries the cadence of a political settlement, clauses negotiated in the shadow of incumbency's abuses. Part VIII carries the cadence of a judicial direction translated into guidance within months. A statute drafted in one session would read more uniformly and govern less aptly. The Code reads like what it is: the sediment of sixty years of elections, each layer dated by the abuse that deposited it. For the historian of Indian democracy, that sediment is a primary source in itself, a record of what each generation of administrators and parties judged intolerable in a campaign. Read in sequence, the layers tell a story of expanding fairness: first the rival's meeting must not be broken up, then the minister's machinery must be stilled, then the candidate's money must be watched, then the party's promises must carry their financing on their face. The story has no final chapter, because the sediment is still accumulating, and the soft form is what permits accumulation without end.

There is a caution inside the celebration, and the article's charter of evenhandedness requires it to be stated. Accumulation without legislation is accumulation without a legislature's scrutiny, and the same machinery that adds a wise clause can add a doubtful one. The Commission's directions on media, on the portrayal of leaders, on the timing of cultural releases during a poll, have each attracted the charge that an unelected body was regulating speech beyond its writ. The courts remain the check on that expansion, through the Gill discipline that Article 324 power must respect law and fairness, and through the willingness of aggrieved campaigners to litigate orders even mid campaign, as happened with publicity material and film releases in recent cycles. The design's health depends on that check being used, and on the Commission accepting correction without treating every writ petition as an assault on its honour. Soft law and judicial review are, in this field, partners rather than rivals: the compact moves fast because the court stands behind it, and the court can stand back because the compact's sanctions expire with the season.

## The Voter in the Design

Every account of the Code is written from the standpoint of parties, candidates, or the Commission, yet the design assigns the voter a role that completes its logic. The sanctions of the compact are publicity sanctions, and publicity requires an audience that punishes. When the Commission censures a leader for a communal appeal, the order's practical force depends on editors carrying it, rivals quoting it, and voters discounting the leader for it. Where the audience rewards the breach instead, treating the censure as a certificate of authenticity, the sanction inverts and the design's third component shows its dependence on political culture. The Code, in other words, presupposes a voter who values the fairness of the contest enough to tax its violation. Indian electoral history supplies evidence for that voter in the aggregate: parties tainted by the abuse of machinery have been punished at polls often enough to keep the expectation alive, and the rout of governments that campaigned from office with unusual brazenness is a recurring pattern administrators cite when they defend the compact's sufficiency.

The voter also appears in the design as the recipient of the Code's quieter protections. The hundred metre canvassing bar, the silence period, the restraints on vehicles and inducements at the booth, exist so that the act of voting itself occurs in a space cleared of last moment pressure. First time voters in India frequently remark on the calm inside the polling station's perimeter compared with the festival noise outside it, and that calm is manufactured by clauses of Parts IV and V enforced by personnel the voter never names. A theory of the Code that counted only its famous failures, the unpunished speech and the absorbed ban, would miss the millions of ordinary transactions the design successfully governs in every election: meetings held without clash because routes were settled with the police in advance, processions that passed each other because timings were lodged, ministers who cancelled announcements because the custody phrase reminded them of the season. The denominator of compliance is enormous and invisible, and the soft law reading, alone among the three, has a place for it, since a compact's success is measured precisely by the conduct it renders unremarkable.


## A Season Inside the Machinery: How an Ordinary Breach Is Processed

 abstractions about design become concrete when a single ordinary breach is followed through the machinery from complaint to disposal, and the composite picture drawn from the Commission's published orders across recent cycles shows the design at work without drama. A complaint arrives, filed by a rival party's legal cell, or an observer reports a ministerial function at which a new scheme was announced, or the media monitoring cell clips a speech in which a candidate appealed to voters in the name of religion. The complaint is registered, the district machinery is asked for the record: the video of the event, the transcript where one exists, the returning officer's account of permissions and timings. A notice then issues to the candidate, the party, or the minister, reciting the clause said to be breached and calling for a reply within a stated time that is measured in hours or in a small number of days. The reply pleads context, mistranslation, denial, or justification, and the Commission, or the chief electoral officer acting within delegated authority, weighs the record and disposes of the matter: the complaint is dropped, an advisory is recorded, a censure is pronounced, or a severer order follows. The entire journey, in a well run season, occupies days.

Follow the same complaint under the statutory counterfactual and the design's rationale sharpens. A statutory offence would require a charge framed under a section, evidence led to a legal standard, a forum with a queue, and a strong probability of interim protection from a court while the campaign proceeded untouched by the eventual verdict. The complainant's real object, which is to stop the conduct before polling, would be defeated by the remedy's own integrity. This is the consideration that has quietly defeated every statutory proposal since Goswami, whatever the speeches made in its favour. Reformers contemplating conversion must answer the composite case, not the celebrated one. For every Balaji or Vellore that fills a lecture hall, the machinery processes hundreds of complaints about hoardings, announcements, transfers, meetings, and speeches, and it processes them in time to matter because its sanctions are administrative and its standard is a compact. A Parliament that converted the compact into a code of offences would discover that it had legislated the ordinary machinery out of existence in order to punish the extraordinary case, and that the extraordinary case, defended by senior counsel, would still conclude after the count.

The composite picture also explains the Commission's institutional habits that puzzle newcomers: its preference for the video record over testimony, its insistence that complaints carry evidence, its published orders that quote the offending words before condemning them. A body whose sanction is publicity must build a record the public can judge, and the Commission's orders are written for that audience as much as for the parties. The order censuring a leader is a small work of public reasoning, reciting the clause, the words, the explanation, and the finding, so that a voter encountering it in a newspaper can test the conclusion. In a statutory regime the reasoning would be addressed to an appellate court. In the compact regime it is addressed to the electorate, which is the design's final enforcer. The habit is so settled that its absence is itself news, and seasons in which orders arrived without reasoning, or in which complaints against high office appeared to wait while others were answered in days, drew criticism precisely because the compact's legitimacy depends on visible even handedness in the small cases as much as in the great ones.

## What Would Count as Evidence Against This Reading?

An argued article owes its readers the conditions under which its claim would fall, and the soft law reading can state them plainly. The reading would be damaged if a court held a breach of the Code, without more, to be a ground for voiding an election, since that holding would convert the compact into law by judgment and dissolve the second component. No such holding exists; the case law runs the other way, from Gill's careful boundary to Balaji's refusal to create an offence. The reading would be damaged if Parliament enacted the Code and campaign conduct visibly improved in consequence, since the improvement would show the limits had been statutory absence all along. The experiment has never been run, though three decades of proposals gave Parliament its chances, and the consistent refusal to run it is itself a datum the rival readings must explain. The reading would be damaged, finally, if enforcement outcomes ceased to vary with the credibility of the administrators and the recognition of the parties, since the reading predicts that variation as the design's signature. The record from 1991 to 2019 shows the variation in its purest form: the same text sleeping, striking, and sleeping again as credibility and recognition rose and fell around it.

The falsifiability matters because the two rival readings are harder to test. The descriptive reading cannot be wrong, since a list is only a list, and its safety is the measure of its emptiness. The effectiveness reading can explain every outcome by adjusting its grade of the administrators, firmness when breaches fall and laxity when they rise, and a reading that explains everything explains little. The design reading risks itself on a structure, and the structure has survived the hardest cases its critics offer. Seshan, the supposed refutation, changed no text and no holding; he changed the price of defiance, exactly the variable the third component names. Balaji, the supposed invitation to legalise manifesto promises, ended with a court enlarging the compact instead. The 2019 bans, the supposed proof of strength, expired inside the season while the speeches outlived them, exactly the limit the second component names. Critics remain free to prefer another frame, yet the burden of this article is discharged if the frame offered here orders the evidence with fewer exceptions than the frames it replaces, and on the record assembled, from a Kerala conference room in 1960 to a countermanded parliamentary poll in 2019, it does.

One further implication follows for readers who use this article beside the series' other studies of the Commission. The Code should be cited as the central exhibit whenever the institution's character is debated, because it concentrates every feature of that character into a single document: constitutional authority without legislative parentage, administrative swiftness without penal consequence, dependence on standing without formal accountability for its use. Debates about appointments and tenure, about the Commission's relations with governments and courts, about the money and media of campaigns, all pass through the Code on their way to the voter. To understand the document as soft law is to understand why those debates take the shapes they do, and why they recur in the same vocabulary each season, as if the participants were reading from a text none of them wrote. They are. The text is the compact of 1960, still governing, still unwritten into any statute, still waiting each election for the parties to recognise it again.

That waiting is not a flaw in the ceremony. It is the ceremony. Each announcement of an election schedule renews the compact publicly: the document is circulated, the parties acknowledge it, the governments pass into custody, and the season opens under rules every participant has seen before. A statute would not need renewal, and would gain nothing from the ritual of acceptance that reminds each generation of politicians that the restraints are theirs as much as the Commission's. The renewal also performs a quiet census of the republic's political health. When acknowledgment is prompt and general, the design enters the season with its third component replenished. When a major formation signals contempt for the document before the first notice has issued, administrators can read the coming season in that signal and prepare the machinery accordingly. Few statutes announce their own prospects so honestly at the moment of their application.


## Reading the Document as a Citizen: A Short Method

For the reader who meets the Code as a voter rather than as a scholar, the design reading offers a method of use. When a controversy breaks, ask first which component is engaged. If the complaint is that a clause does not cover a new abuse, a fresh platform, a novel inducement, the first component is engaged, and the remedy is revision of the kind the Commission performed in 2013. If the complaint is that a proven breach drew no consequence beyond words, the second component is engaged, and no revision of clauses will answer it, because the consequence gap is the price of administrative speed. If the complaint is that a leader wears a censure proudly and repeats the conduct, the third component is engaged, and the remedy lies with the audience: the voters, the editors, and the rival parties whose recognition gives the compact its price. Sorting controversies by component will not settle them, since seasons are partisan and every sorting will be contested, yet it prevents the commonest error of election commentary, which is to demand from one component the work of another: statute's teeth from a compact, a compact's speed from a court, a court's certainty from a campaign. The Code endures because its makers, across sixty years of revision, mostly resisted that error, and the citizen who reads it on those terms reads it as its makers wrote it.


## The Silence Period and Polling Day: The Design at Ground Level

The last forty eight hours before polling offer the design in its most concentrated form, because the restraints then multiply while the machinery thins. Campaigning stops at the close of the silence period prescribed alongside the Code's operation, public meetings fall silent, and the contest withdraws from the streets into the booth. Candidates who have lived for weeks inside a megaphone discover a regulated quiet in which the voter is meant to be unreachable by fresh pressure. The quiet is never complete. Canvassing moves to doorsteps, messages travel through phones, and the distribution of inducements, where it survives the squads, attempts its final run under cover of the general stillness. The Commission's answer is the machinery Part IV and Part V describe: the hundred metre perimeter around each polling station cleared of canvassing, the booth's entry confined to voters and authorised persons, the observers and sector officers moving between stations with authority to act on the spot. A citizen who has never read a clause of the document experiences its most successful hours as an absence, the absence of the crowd at the gate, of the vehicle ferrying voters, of the agent pressing a slip into a hand at the threshold.

Ground level also reveals the human material the compact depends on, the presiding officers, polling staff, and police constables drawn from state services for a duty that arrives with the election and departs with the count. These officials enforce Parts IV and V without knowing the design vocabulary, and their fidelity is the compact's final delivery mechanism. Training manuals translate the clauses into checklists: verify the perimeter, log the agents, seal the machine, record the complaint. Where the translation is careful and the district leadership is alert, polling day in a hard fought constituency proceeds with a calm that visiting observers describe with surprise. Where the translation is careless, the same clauses exist on paper while pressure gathers at the gate. The variation is not hypocrisy in the document. It is the predictable behaviour of an instrument whose enforcement is administrative all the way down, staffed by borrowed hands, and it explains why the Commission guards the transfer and posting of election officials so jealously during the season. Control of personnel is control of the Code's last mile, and Part VII's restraints on transfers protect the very people through whom every other part must act.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the three components of Model Code soft law?

The three components are codification without statutory status, enforcement by the Election Commission without judicial enforceability, and binding through political recognition rather than legal obligation. The Code is a comprehensive written document in eight parts, yet no Act of Parliament contains it and no offence attaches to its clauses. The Commission enforces it through notices, advisories, censure, campaign bans, postponement, and countermand, yet a breach by itself is no ground for a court trial or an election petition. Parties obey because they accepted the document, beginning with the Kerala compact of 1960, and because voters and rivals price defiance. Each component pairs a power with a limit, and the pairing is the design.

### Q: When was the MCC introduced?

The MCC was introduced in 1960, when the parties contesting the Kerala state assembly election agreed to a code of conduct for the campaign, in a state still marked by the dismissal of its elected ministry in July 1959. The Election Commission then carried the experiment into national practice, circulating a code to recognised parties by the general election of 1962. In 1968 the Commission formalised the accumulated material into a comprehensive document, the ancestor of the eight part text. Revisions consolidated by 1979 added the restraints on the party in power that became Part VII. The document therefore has no single birthday in law. It has a birth as an agreement in 1960, a formalisation in 1968, and a hardening of enforcement after 1991 under T. N. Seshan.

### Q: What does Part VII of the MCC say?

Part VII governs the party in power. It states that a ruling party, at the centre or in a state, must not use its official position for electioneering once the Code operates. Ministers must not combine official visits with campaign work. Government transport, including aircraft, must not carry campaigners on partisan errands, and public money must not fund advertisements publicising the ruling party's achievements. Public grounds and rest houses must be open to opponents on equal terms. Governments must not announce new projects, lay foundation stones, promise grants, or make ad hoc appointments meant to influence voters, and transfers of officials on election duty fall under the Commission's control. The part exists to narrow the gap between a party and a government during the poll.

### Q: Is the MCC legally enforceable?

The MCC is not legally enforceable as a statute is enforceable, and the courts have stated its position consistently. It appears in no Act of Parliament, creates no offence, and a breach by itself cannot sustain an election petition under the Representation of the People Act 1951. Where the same conduct also violates that Act, as an appeal to religion may violate section 123, the legal consequence flows from the Act and not from the Code. Enforcement belongs to the Election Commission, acting under Article 324 of the Constitution and the parties' acceptance of the document, through notices, censure, campaign bans, postponement, and countermand. The Code is therefore binding in practice and powerful inside a campaign, while standing outside the courtroom. That combination is the definition of its soft law character.

### Q: What is the Sun Pharma case?

The Sun Pharma case is the name the brief tradition attaches to S. Subramaniam Balaji v State of Tamil Nadu, decided by the Supreme Court on 5 July 2013. The petitioner challenged the distribution of free gifts by governments in Tamil Nadu and the manifesto promises behind the distribution. The Court held that a promise in an election manifesto does not amount to a corrupt practice under section 123 of the Representation of the People Act, and that the bench could not create a new offence. It then directed the Election Commission to frame guidelines on manifesto content in consultation with recognised parties. That direction produced Part VIII of the Code, requiring manifestos to state the rationale of promises and the means of financing them. A judgment that refused to legislate thus enlarged a non-statutory document.

### Q: Who enforces the MCC?

The Election Commission of India enforces the MCC. It acts through the chief electoral officers of the states, the district election machinery, returning officers, and the observer system, and it answers complaints from parties, candidates, citizens, and its own media monitoring. Its authority rests on Article 324 of the Constitution, which vests the superintendence, direction, and control of elections in the Commission, as read in Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner in 1978. Courts do not enforce the Code as such, and police do not prosecute its breaches as offences. Enforcement is administrative: notice, explanation, advisory, censure, campaign ban, transfer of officials, postponement, and in the gravest cases countermand of the poll. The Commission must follow fairness, hear the person complained against, and speak through reasoned orders.

### Q: What can the ECI do for MCC violations?

For MCC violations the Commission can issue a notice demanding an explanation, record an advisory or a warning, and pronounce a public censure of a leader or a party. It can bar a campaigner from addressing meetings or speaking to the media for a fixed period, as it barred senior leaders for forty eight and seventy two hours during April 2019. It can order the transfer of officials, stop government announcements and advertisements, and require publicity material to be withdrawn. Where conditions make a free poll impossible it can postpone voting, and where inducement or disorder has vitiated a constituency it can countermand the election, as at R. K. Nagar in 2017 and Vellore in 2019. Every one of these responses is administrative rather than penal, delivered inside the campaign window.

### Q: Why did the Code begin in Kerala rather than at the centre?

The Code began in Kerala because the problem it answered appeared there in an acute form and because a state election offered a room small enough for the parties to agree in. Kerala voted in February 1960 after the dismissal of its elected Communist ministry in July 1959, and campaigning carried the grievance of that dismissal. The state administration convened the contesting parties and secured their assent to shared restraints on speeches, processions, and the use of office. A national code imposed from New Delhi in that era would have required either legislation or a direction parties could resent as central pressure. The Kerala compact instead supplied consent first and a text second, and the Election Commission, watching the experiment succeed, carried the form into national practice by 1962 and formalised it in 1968.

### Q: What did the 1979 revision add to the Code?

The revision consolidated by 1979 added the Code's discipline of incumbency, the line of provisions that matured into Part VII on the party in power. Earlier versions concentrated on the conduct of candidates and parties as campaigners: speeches, meetings, processions, and polling day behaviour. The 1979 consolidation addressed the ruling formation as a government, restraining the combination of official tours with campaigning, the use of official transport and publicity, and the announcement of projects and benefits timed to sway voters. The addition followed the Emergency of 1975 to 1977 and the Janata interlude, years that demonstrated how completely a ministry could bend state machinery toward its own re-election. After 1979 the Code regulated the stronger half of the contest as well as the weaker, and no later revision has reversed that judgment.

### Q: What is the difference between censure and a campaign ban under the Code?

Censure is a public condemnation recorded in a Commission order; a campaign ban is an order of silence for a fixed period. A censured leader may keep campaigning, carrying the reprimand as a reputational cost that rivals will quote and voters will weigh. A banned campaigner may not address public meetings or speak to the media for the hours the order specifies, forty eight or seventy two in the instances of April 2019. Censure therefore punishes through publicity alone, while the ban punishes through publicity plus the loss of campaign time inside a short season. Both rest on Article 324 and the parties' acceptance of the Code rather than on any statute, and both expire with the moment they address. The ban is the heavier sanction precisely because time, in the last weeks of an election, is the resource no candidate can replace.

### Q: Why did the Commission countermand the Vellore election in 2019?

The Commission rescinded the election to the Vellore Lok Sabha constituency in April 2019 because it concluded that money power had vitiated the poll beyond repair. Income tax searches in the constituency had recovered large sums of cash which the Commission judged were intended for distribution to voters, and the scale of the recovery persuaded it that the inducement was systematic rather than incidental. Countermanding is the Code regime's gravest response, and the Vellore order was the first rescinding of a parliamentary election on the ground of money power. The consequence fell on the election rather than on identified individuals: voting was cancelled and the constituency waited for a fresh poll. Honest voters and clean candidates paid in delay and in a doubled campaign, which is why the order stands as evidence of both the system's courage and its structural limits.

### Q: How does a complaint about a breach travel through the Commission's machinery?

A complaint reaches the Commission from a rival party, a candidate, a citizen, an observer's report, or the media monitoring cell that clips speeches and advertisements. It is registered and sent for a factual record: the video of the event, a transcript where one can be prepared, and the returning officer's account of permissions and timings. A notice then issues to the person complained against, reciting the clause said to be breached and allowing a short time, often measured in hours or a few days, for an explanation. The Commission or the chief electoral officer weighs the reply against the record and disposes of the matter by dropping it, recording an advisory, pronouncing censure, or making a severer order. The journey is designed to finish inside the campaign window, because a remedy delivered after counting rarely repairs a contest.

### Q: Why has the Election Commission opposed giving the Code statutory force?

The Commission has opposed statutory force because it judges that conversion would destroy the qualities that make the Code work. Elections run on a calendar of weeks, and a breach must be answered before polling; a statutory offence would move the answer into courtrooms, where evidence, interim orders, and appeals follow litigation time rather than the returning officer's clock. Conversion would also freeze the text. The Commission revised the document after the Balaji direction of 2013 within a single cycle, consulting the parties in the room, and it values the capacity to absorb a new abuse or a new medium without waiting for a parliamentary session. Behind both reasons sits a doctrine the Commission repeats to committees: its authority under Article 324, joined to the parties' recognition, already supplies consequence enough, provided the consequence arrives swiftly and is seen to be even handed.

### Q: What did the parliamentary standing committee propose in 2013 about the Code's legal status?

In its work on electoral reforms in 2013, the Department Related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice recommended that the Model Code of Conduct be given statutory status. The committee's reasoning spoke for a rule of law instinct: restraints capable of silencing campaigners and of stopping governments from announcing policy should trace to enacted law rather than to a document administered by an unelected body, however distinguished that body's history. Members across parties have voiced versions of the same unease in different seasons, often after their own side felt a censure. The recommendation was not enacted, joining the Dinesh Goswami Committee's earlier inclination of 1990 in the record of proposals Parliament considered and let rest. The sequence matters to the Code's history, because the institutions that praise statute in the abstract keep choosing the compact at the moment of decision.

### Q: Why do political parties obey a code that no statute enacted?

Parties obey because obedience is, for most actors most of the time, the cheaper strategy, and the design of the compact makes it so. Every recognised party accepted the document, receives it afresh at each election, and invokes it against opponents, so defiance must be explained to an audience the party itself taught to value the Code. The Commission's sanctions are reputational and administrative, priced in publicity and campaign time, currencies in which parties are rich and sensitive. Parties also obey from expectation: a formation in office in one season expects to need Part VII's protection in opposition in another, and dismantling or discrediting the compact would surrender that protection. Finally, habit matters. Sixty years of seasons under the document have made its restraints part of what Indian politicians understand an election to be, and habits of that depth bind without any bailiff.

### Q: Why must the Commission hear a campaigner before imposing a severe sanction?

The Commission must hear the campaigner because the courts have disciplined Article 324 power with the requirements of fairness. Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner in 1978 read the Article as a reservoir of power for areas the enacted law leaves unoccupied, and the case law after it insists that the power be used in conformity with law, with natural justice, and with reasoned orders when the action is severe. A campaign ban silences political speech during an election, the most sensitive speech a democracy protects, and an order made without notice and explanation would invite immediate judicial correction. The hearing requirement is therefore both a right of the campaigner and a protection for the Commission's own authority. Its cost is time, the scarcest resource in a campaign, and the friction between fairness and speed is a designed feature of the regime rather than an accident of any single season.

### Q: How are government advertisements treated once the Code operates?

Once the Code operates, government advertisements that publicise the achievements of the party in power at public cost are forbidden for the season. The Commission scrutinises publicity issued by central and state governments, orders the removal or covering of hoardings and displays that cross from information into party promotion, and distinguishes, sometimes delicately, between a department's duty to inform citizens about a running programme and a ministry's wish to be thanked for it. Portraits of living leaders on government publicity have drawn repeated directions, and material judged partisan can be ordered withdrawn or treated as the ruling party's campaign expenditure in the Commission's estimation. Routine information about services, dates, and entitlements continues, since the Code restrains promotion rather than administration. The advertisements clause is among the most litigated edges of Part VII, because governments defend publicity as governance and oppositions attack the same publicity as the incumbency advantage the seventh part exists to neutralise.

### Q: How long does the Code stay in force during an election?

The Code operates from the moment the Election Commission announces the election schedule until the completion of the process with the declaration of results and the formal notification that ends the poll. Its start is abrupt and famous: a press conference in New Delhi announces dates, and within the hour ministers lose the power to announce projects, governments lose the freedom to transfer election officials at will, and campaigners come under the document's clauses. The restraints do not taper at the edges. Polling may run in phases across weeks, and the Code holds across all of them, through counting, until the Commission's final notification closes the election. Governments chafe at the length in multi phase seasons, when administration feels frozen for the better part of two months, and administrators answer that the freeze is the visible sign of a level field. When the notification issues, the custody ends and ordinary government resumes at full speed.

### Q: What role do observers play under Part VI of the Code?

Part VI provides for the observers through whom the Commission watches an election it cannot personally attend. General observers monitor the conduct of the poll and the campaign in allotted constituencies, expenditure observers shadow the money, and police observers watch the deployment of force. Observers receive complaints, attend the scenes of alleged breaches, and file reports that travel upward to the Commission, where they frequently form the factual foundation of notices and censures. The system converts a promise based code into a watched code: parties know that a speech, a distribution of goods, or an official's partiality may be recorded the same evening by an officer answerable to New Delhi rather than to the district. Observers are drawn from the civil services and serve outside their home states to loosen local ties. Their diligence varies, and with it the Code's practical force varies from constituency to constituency, a dependence on administrative quality that the design accepts as its own.

### Q: What happened to the film PM Narendra Modi under the Code in April 2019?

In April 2019 the Election Commission restrained the release of PM Narendra Modi, a biographical film about the Prime Minister, holding that a biopic serving the purposes of a political formation could not be screened while the Code operated during the general election. The order treated the film as campaign material in substance, since its release on the eve of polling would publicise the ruling party's central figure outside the restraints the Code places on publicity, and the restraint lasted until polling closed. The episode belongs to a family of 2019 orders, including the restraint placed on a television channel devoted to the same leader's image, in which the Commission read the Code's spirit across media the document's drafters had not imagined. Critics charged an unelected body with censoring culture; defenders answered that a level field cannot stop at the cinema door. The film was released after the election, and the order expired with the season, as compact sanctions do.
